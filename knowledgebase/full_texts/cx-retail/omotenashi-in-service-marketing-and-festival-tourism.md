Abstract
This article examines omotenashi as a cultural and organizational orientation in service marketing and
extends its application from lodging to festival tourism through the Fukushi-kyosan Sapporo Odori Beer
Garden 2026. The study adopts an integrative literature review supported by a documented platform-
based search strategy focused on omotenashi, Japanese hospitality, customer experience, service
management, event quality, festival experience, co -creation, trust, personalization, and intercultural
interaction. The analytical corpus includes fifteen substantive academic studies that met the relevance
criteria, complemented by official documentary sources used to examine the 2026 event. Thematic
analysis identifies four service manifestations anticipation of needs, personalization, attention to detail,
and human relationships supported by organizational consistency and conditioned by functional quality
and cultural context. The Sapporo application shows how these categories can interpret reservation
design, tourist-specific Omotenashi Seats, multiple service environments, information, ordering, seating,
and host–visitor interaction without treating institutional communication as evidence of satisfaction or
loyalty. The findings position omotenashi as an analytical framework for service marketing across
hospitality and festival settings and identify contextual appropriateness as the principle linking service
design to visitor experience.
Keywords: omotenashi, service marketing, festival tourism, event experience, Sapporo Odori Beer
Garden

A. INTRODUCTION
The competitiveness of tourism and hospitality organizations increasingly depends on how visitors
evaluate a sequence of service encounters rather than a single transaction. This logic is especially visible
in festivals, where information, access, seating, food and beverage service, sanitation, safety, staff contact,
spatial design, and interaction with other visitors are experienced together. Festival research shows that
perceived quality is connected with satisfaction and behavioral intentions, while service attributes such as
information, hygiene, safety, infrastructure, and staff performance shape the overall evaluation of an event
(Pivac et al., 2019; Maranisya et al., 2025). These findings establish an important boundary for the present
study: culturally meaningful hospitality can enrich an event experience only when the basic service system
remains reliable.
Within this debate, omotenashi offers a culturally grounded perspective on how service
organizations organize attention to the customer. The term is widely associated with Japanese hospitality,
although the literature treats it as more than courtesy or formal politeness. Wijayanti and Saifudin (2021)
identify personalization, host and guest relationships, hospitality, and small surprises in the operation of a
traditional ryokan. Abdullah et al. (2024) reach a related interpretation in cross-cultural research by
connecting Japanese hospitality with anticipation of needs, customization, attention to detail, and a
welcoming atmosphere. These accounts converge around a relational form of service in which the
provider interprets the situation and prepares an appropriate response instead of waiting for every need
to be explicitly stated.

This convergence does not imply that omotenashi can be reduced to a fixed list of gestures.
Morishita (2024) adds a useful counterpoint through the concept of omoiyari in small Japanese inns, where
guests valued family-like hospitality without excessive service. His findings qualify interpretations that
associate superior hospitality with increasing the number of interventions or surprises. They suggest that
the service provider must judge when to act, when to offer options, and when to preserve the guest's
autonomy. Budianto (2025) strengthens this contextual reading by examining foreign workers in Japanese
restaurants and finding that omotenashi-related skills can be learned through training and practice while
also being adapted through workers' cultural repertoires. Together, these studies support an
interpretation based on contextual appropriateness rather than service intensity.
The marketing relevance of this interpretation becomes clearer when relational and experiential
outcomes are considered. Leingpibul et al. (2025) found that perceptions of omotenashi, salesperson
expertise, and trust contribute to customer commitment in Japanese retail establishments. Kastuti and
Sugiarto (2025) connect sincere service and attention to detail with memorable tourism experience, while
festival research shows that co-created experience and satisfaction can strengthen future attendance
intentions (Zhang et al., 2019). Together, these studies connect omotenashi with constructs familiar to
service and relationship marketing while preserving the distinction between hospitality practice and its
possible outcomes.
Research on the organizational side of omotenashi also complicates the idea that hospitality
depends primarily on an employee's personal disposition. Morishita and Kosaka (2017), in their study of
the Kagaya Inn, associate superior omotenashi with focused training, organization-wide communication,
and feedback shared among staff and customers. Budianto (2025) reaches a compatible conclusion from
a different context, since foreign workers learned and adapted relevant skills through employment and
training. Organizational arrangements therefore matter because anticipation, personalization, and
attentive interaction require knowledge, coordination, and discretion. The service encounter is visible to
the guest, while the capability that supports it is distributed through routines, communication, and
learning.
The same debate now takes place in a technologically mediated service environment. Ito (2024)
argues that automated check-in, smart rooms, and artificial intelligence can improve efficiency and
personalization in Japanese hospitality. This contribution is useful when it is read with caution. Technology
can support recognition of preferences, reduce waiting time, and provide information, yet these functions
do not by themselves reproduce the contextual judgement that the omotenashi literature associates with
appropriate care. The relevant marketing issue concerns the allocation of tasks between data-driven
systems and human interpretation. A hotel can automate predictable routines while retaining human
discretion for exceptions, emotionally sensitive situations, and service recovery.
Despite the growing number of studies, the literature remains fragmented across tourism,
hospitality, management, service research, and event studies. Some authors approach omotenashi as a
cultural philosophy, others treat it as observable employee behavio ur, and still others examine
organizational practices that sustain it. Event research adds further concepts such as event quality, co-
creation, communitas, intercultural competence, and place attachment. This fragmentation creates two
analytical problems. The first concerns definition because satisfaction, loyalty, trust, memorability, and
hospitality are often discussed in close proximity without a clear distinction between the practice and its
outcomes. The second concerns transferability because a concept rooted in Japanese service culture
cannot be assumed to operate identically across hotels, restaurants, retail, and large public festivals.
To address these problems, this article asks how omotenashi can be understood through service
marketing and how its principles appear in the service design of the Fukushi-kyosan Sapporo Odori Beer
Garden 2026. The study combines an integrative literature review with a documentary application to the

Service marketing, festival experience, and customer experience
Service marketing in festival tourism requires a simultaneous reading of what the organizer
delivers and how visitors experience delivery. Pivac et al. (2019), studying a major music festival, linked
perceived quality dimensions, i ncluding traffic and information, hygiene and safety, socialization,
infrastructure, products, and services to visitor satisfaction and behavioral intentions. Maranisya et al.
(2025) similarly found that service quality and event quality significantly influenced visiting decisions at a
Japanese festival. These studies help prevent an overly symbolic interpretation of hospitality because the
event experience develops through the combination of operational quality, environment, interaction, and
interpretation.
Customer experience also extends beyond satisfaction alone. Zhang et al. (2019) found that
satisfaction with co-creation experience contributes to festival attendance intentions through festival
satisfaction and place-related mechanisms. Wu et al. (2025), in a Japanese festival context, showed that
intercultural competence supports experience co-creation and that active participation and co-creation
enhance cultural tourism experience. These findings give service marketing a broader frame in which
visitors do not merely receive service but also participate in producing the atmosphere and meaning of
the event.
Kastuti and Sugiarto (2025) add that memorable tourism experience depends on local culture,
atmosphere, involvement, and genuine hospitality, while Hayes (2024) demonstrates in a Japanese mega-
event that omotenashi can be embedded in the experiencescape through interactions among volunteers,
visitors, and local communities. Read together, these studies clarify that event experience contains
operational, relational, cultural, and participatory layers. Basic service must first work reliably; context-
sensitive hospitality and co-creation can then contribute to differentiation and memorability.
Omotenashi as a culturally grounded service orientation
Omotenashi is frequently translated as Japanese hospitality, but this translation can narrow the
concept when it is treated as a synonym for politeness. Wijayanti and Saifudin (2021) examined the Hyoe
Koyokaku ryokan and identified personalization, host and guest relationships, hospitality, and small
surprises as recurring manifestations. Their observations frame omotenashi as a practical orientation
expressed through the way staff prepare and adapt service around the guest. Abdullah et al. (2024) reach
a comparable interpretation through cross-cultural benchmarking and emphasize anticipation of needs,
customization, attention to detail, cultural immersion, and service consistency. The agreement between
these studies supports four recurrent manifestations that can be analysed across different hospitality
settings.
Anticipation concerns the ability to recognise a likely need before the guest has to make a request.
Personalization concerns the adjustment of the service response to the particular circumstances of the
guest. Attention to detail concerns the coherence of small functional, sensory, and relational elements that
communicate care. Human relationships concern the quality of interaction through respect, competence,
and contextual sensitivity. These manifestations overlap in practice, yet separating them analytically helps
clarify which organizational capability is being exercised and which aspect of the experience the guest may
evaluate.

Morishita (2024) introduces an important boundary by examining omoiyari in small ryokans. His
cases associate guest appeal with traditional local culture and family-like hospitality that avoids excessive
service. This evidence qualifies a common managerial reading of omotenashi as continuous intervention.
Contextual appropriateness provides a more precise interpretation because a guest can experience care
when staff act before a need arises, but also when staff recognise that privacy or autonomy is preferable.
The quality of omotenashi therefore depends on judgement about the situation rather than on a fixed
volume of service.
Budianto (2025) adds an intercultural dimension by analysing foreign workers in Japanese
restaurants. Training and work experience enabled employees to embody omotenashi-related skills, while
their own cultural resources also influenced how those skills were practised. This result supports
transferability at the level of principles rather than ritual imitation. A hotel outside Japan may apply
anticipation, personalized attention, and respectful interaction without reproducing Japanese linguistic or
ceremonial forms. The distinction matters for international service marketing because adaptation to the
guest's cultural expectations is itself consistent with a context-sensitive interpretation of hospitality.
Organizational consistency and relational outcomes
Organizational consistency provides the infrastructure through which the visible dimensions of
omotenashi can recur across encounters. Morishita and Kosaka (2017) found that Kagaya Inn relies on
training, organization-wide communication, and shared feedback. In festival settings the same logic
extends to coordination among organizers, venue operators, food and beverage teams, information staff,
and other frontline personnel. Hayes (2024) further shows that event hospitality can emerge through
organized volunteer–visitor interaction, making coordination and shared service expectations relevant
beyond formal hotel departments.
This organizational view is compatible with Budianto's (2025) findings because learning occurs
through structured employment and training rather than cultural origin alone. It also aligns with Ito's
(2024) discussion of technology, since data systems can s upport memory, coordination, and
personalization. The relationship between technology and omotenashi is therefore complementary when
digital tools reduce friction and provide usable information, while staff retain the capacity to interpret
context. Automation becomes problematic when it eliminates the discretion required for exceptions or
when data collection reduces trust.
Relational outcomes should remain conceptually separate from the practice itself. Leingpibul et al.
(2025) associate customer perceptions of omotenashi, expertise, and trust with commitment. Baquero
(2025) likewise examines the relationships among customer experience, revisit intention, customer value,
and engagement in hotel settings, supporting the treatment of revisit intention as a downstream outcome
rather than as a defining element of hospitality practice. Zhang et al. (2019) link festival co-creation and
satisfaction with future attendance intentions, while Pivac et al. (2019) connect perceived festival quality
to satisfaction and behavioral intention. These relations allow the review to place trust, satisfaction,
commitment, place attachment, revisit intention, and recommendation downstream from hospitality and
event-service practices rather than inside the definition of omotenashi.

B. RESEARCH METHOD
This study adopts an integrative literature review supported by a documented platform-based
search strategy and thematic synthesis. The method was selected because research on omotenashi is
distributed across hospitality, tourism, service management, customer relationship research, cultural
studies, and event and festival research, and the available evidence includes qualitative field studies,
content analyses, quantitative surveys, and conceptual work. The purpose of the review is interpretive
rather than meta-analytic. It seeks to identify recurrent conceptual elements, compare them with event-
experience research, and construct an analytical framework that can be applied to a current festival
context.
The search stage was conducted through the Consensus scholarly search platform, which retrieves
academic publications and provides bibliographic metadata and abstracts. Searches combined
omotenashi and Japanese hospitality with hotel, ryokan, service manag ement, service marketing,
customer experience, personalization, anticipation, trust, commitment, festival, event tourism, event
quality, co-creation, visitor satisfaction, intercultural competence, and memorable tourism experience.
Because the central concept is culturally specific and relevant event studies do not always use the term
omotenashi, the strategy combined a narrow conceptual set with broader event- and experience-oriented
combinations.
Eligibility followed substantive relevance rather than a date filter. Studies were retained when they
directly defined omotenashi, examined its practice in hospitality or service settings, analysed
organizational management of hospitality, or provided empirical evidence about festival service quality,
co-creation, intercultural experience, satisfaction, and behavioral outcomes that could clarify the research
question. Studies were excluded when omotenashi appeared only incidentally or when event evidence did
not contribute to service or visitor-experience analysis. This procedure produced a final substantive
corpus of fifteen studies. Eleven were published between 2023 and 2026, while four earlier studies were

Each included study was read at the abstract and metadata level and coded according to context,
method, definition or use of omotenashi, service manifestations, organizational mechanisms, conditioning
factors, event-quality dimensions, co-creation, intercultural interaction, and reported outcomes. Initial
coding included anticipation, personalization, small surprises, host–guest relationships, attention to detail,
staff competence, training, communication, information, hygiene and safety, trust, commitment,
satisfaction, place attachment, and behavioral intention. A second analytical pass grouped related codes
into higher-order categories. Anticipation, personalization, attention to detail, and human relationships
were retained as manifestations of omotenashi; organizational consistency remained a supporting layer;
functional quality and cultural context were treated as conditions; and visitor outcomes remained
analytically downstream.
The synthesis compared convergent and divergent findings across lodging, service, and festival
settings. Convergence was recorded when studies associated omotenashi or high-quality event experience
with similar relational, informational, or adaptive service practices. Qualification was recorded when
findings limited a broad interpretation, as when Morishita (2024) identifies valued hospitality without
excessive service or when festival studies show that participation depends on visitor and contextual
conditions. This procedure avoids a cumulative list of author claims and instead constructs a framework
from relationships among findings.
The literature review was complemented by a documentary illustration involving the Fukushi-
kyosan Sapporo Odori Beer Garden 2026, part of the Sapporo Summer Festival. Official tourism
documentation records the event from July 23 to August 18, 2026, across Odori Park Nishi 5- to 11-chome,
with approximately 13,000 seats distributed across six venue areas. The same documentation describes
varied seating formats, reservation mechanisms, ordering procedures, restroom provision, tourist
guidance, and tourist-specific reserved 'Omotenashi Seats' available through partner hotels (Sapporo
Tourism & Convention Bureau, 2026a; 2026b). These sources provide a current documentary anchor for
applying the framework. They are used to document service design, not as independent evidence of visitor
satisfaction or effectiveness.
The documentary analysis applies the review categories to the Sapporo event through
correspondence rather than causal testing. Evidence was considered compatible with anticipation when
the event prepared for a likely visitor need before an explicit request; with personalization when visitors
could select or reserve a service configuration suited to their circumstances; with attention to detail when
spatial, informational, food-and-beverage, and comfort elements were deliberately coordinated; and with
human relationship when staff or host–visitor interaction required contextual attention. Reservation
rules, ordering systems, venue differentiation, crowd guidance, sanitation rules, and operational
coordination were considered under organizational consistency and functional quality. This rule
maintains a distinction between what official documentation records and what the framework interprets.
Review protocol and audit trail
The literature search was conducted exclusively through the Consensus scholarly search platform.
Because Consensus is an AI -mediated academic discovery environment whose coverage, ranking
mechanisms, and retrieval behaviour may change over time, the procedure is treated as a platform-specific
documented search rather than as a fully reproducible database search. Methodological transparency is
therefore based on the preservation of search expressions, execution dates, eligibility criteria, screening
decisions, bibliographic records, extraction fields, and thematic coding decisions.

FAIR Research Data Management
The research data generated during the integrative review will be managed according to the FAIR
principles of Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reusability. In this study, research data
comprise the bibliographic search records, search strategies and execution dates, eligibility decisions, final
corpus of publications, extraction matrix, coding categories, analytical matrices, and the documentary
sources used in the festival application. These materials document the analytical process that connects the
search procedure to the categories and findings presented in the article. In this review, reproducibility is
understood as auditability of the documented search and analytical process rather than as an expectation
that an AI-mediated platform will necessarily return an identical result set at a later date.
Findability will be supported through deposit of the research data package in a repository that
assigns a persistent identifier and permits structured metadata. Accessibility will be addressed by
documenting access conditions for each deposited file while retaining metadata when access restrictions
apply. Interoperability will be supported through non-proprietary formats, standardized variable names,
consistent bibliographic identifiers, and a data dictionary describing the analytical categories. Reusability
will be supported through provenance information, documentation of search and coding procedures,
version information, a README file, and an explicit reuse license. These procedures follow evidence that
FAIR implementation depends on persistent identifiers, rich metadata, accessible documentation,
interoperability standards, and clear reuse conditions (Bhat & Wani, 2024; Fouilloux & Hasan, 2025;
Lopez-Hoyos & Roa-Martínez, 2024).
The FAIR approach also informs transparency of the review procedure. Each bibliographic record
will retain information on its source, search strategy, screening decision, eligibility status, and relationship
with the analytical categories. Excluded full-text records will be accompanied by an exclusion reason, while
included studies will be linked to the extraction and thematic coding matrices. This structure permits
reconstruction of the transition from retrieved records to the final analytical corpus without requiring
redistribution of copyrighted full-text publications.
The deposited dataset will not reproduce copyrighted articles or restricted database content.
Bibliographic metadata, analytical decisions, coding produced by the authors, and links or persistent
identifiers to the original publications will be shared instead. Documentary material related to the Sapporo
Odori Beer Garden application will follow the same principle, with provenance and access information
recorded for each source. The research data package will therefore support verification and reuse of the
review process while respecting copyright, licensing, and access restrictions associated with the original
sources.

C. RESULTS AND ANALYSIS
From cultural hospitality to an analytical service framework
The synthesis indicates that the main contribution of omotenashi to service marketing lies in
contextual interpretation. The recurring practices identified across the corpus do not support a model in
which hospitality quality increases simply with the amount of intervention. Wijayanti and Saifudin (2021)
report personalization and small surprises in a traditional ryokan, Morishita (2024) reports family-like
hospitality without excessive service, and Hayes (2024) shows that event hospitality can emerge through
brief but meaningful host–visitor relationships. Read together, these findings support a proposition in
which the value of an action depends on whether it fits the visitor, the setting, and the moment.
Abdullah et al. (2024) strengthen this interpretation because anticipation and customization are
linked to understanding guests rather than merely adding amenities. Budianto (2025) adds that
omotenashi can be learned and culturally adapted, while Wu et al.  (2025) show that intercultural
understanding and action influence co-created festival experience. The convergence supports four
observable manifestations—anticipation, personalization, attention to detail, and human relationships—
while suggesting that intercultural competence is especially relevant when these manifestations operate
in public events with mixed visitor segments.
This result also clarifies the role of functional quality. In lodging, Morishita (2023) shows that
maintenance, cleaning, and staffing remain basic requirements. In festivals, Pivac et al. (2019) and
Maranisya et al. (2025) identify information, hygiene, safety, infrastructure, staff service, and event quality
as consequential for visitor evaluations and visiting decisions. Functional quality therefore establishes the
conditions under which relational and culturally framed hospitality can contribute to experience.

Anticipation and personalization as context-dependent practices
Anticipation appears when the service provider or event organizer recognises a likely need before
the visitor has to articulate it. Abdullah et al. (2024) connect this practice to Japanese hospitality, while the
Sapporo event provides a documentary example through tourist-specific Omotenashi Seats. Official
documentation explains that these reservable seats were created because visitors had reported difficulty
finding places in the crowded beer garden; eligible tourists staying at partner hotels can reserve them in
advance (Sapporo Tourism & Convention Bureau, 2026a; Sapporo Summer Festival Executive Committee,
2024). The mechanism is analytically relevant because it responds in advance to a recurring visitor
problem.
Personalization follows from the same informational base but operates through choice and
adaptation. The Sapporo event distributes service across six venue areas and offers different seating
formats and reservation arrangements, enabling visitors to choose among brand, food, seating, and service
configurations. Some venues provide reserved seating and set-menu conditions, while others support
more spontaneous use. These choices do not prove individualized service, but they create a structured
platform for visitors to select an experience consistent with group size, timing, and preference (Sapporo
Tourism & Convention Bureau, 2026a; 2026b).
Technology and ordering systems can support this adaptive layer. The 2026 official venue
information documents cashless payment and reservation arrangements, while the Nishi 5-chome venue
provides table delivery for orders and introduced expanded covered s eating (Sapporo Tourism &
Convention Bureau, 2026b; 2026c). Such features can reduce friction and support convenience, but the
framework treats them as infrastructure rather than omotenashi by themselves. Their relevance depends
on whether they help the visitor navigate the event appropriately and reliably.
Attention to detail and memorable experience
Attention to detail appears in both cultural and experiential studies. Kastuti and Sugiarto (2025)
associate omotenashi with sincere service, meticulous attention, and a harmonious atmosphere. Festival
research similarly shows that information, hygiene, safety, infrastructure, and event service influence
overall evaluation (Pivac et al., 2019; Maranisya et al., 2025). In Sapporo, official documentation identifies
multiple seating types, covered tables, restrooms, ordering options, venue-specific menus, rules on
smoking and outside food, and recommendations about crowd timing and personal belongings. These
details can be read as components of an intentionally organized experiencescape when they work
coherently.
The marketing implication concerns coherence rather than ornamentation. A covered table,
reservation system, multilingual visitor guidance, or efficient ordering procedure has limited value when
isolated from crowd management, cleanliness, staff execution, and reliable information. The same element
can contribute strongly to experience when it is coordinated with weather exposure, group size, peak
demand, or the visitor's familiarity with the city. This distinction explains why attention to detail belongs
inside the framework while functional quality remains a separate condition.
Memorability should remain an outcome rather than a definition of omotenashi. Kastuti and
Sugiarto (2025) associate hospitality practices with memorable tourism experience, while Zhang et al.
(2019) and Wu et al. (2025) show that co-creation, participation, and satisfaction can strengthen festival
experience and behavioral intentions. A researcher can therefore identify an omotenashi-compatible
practice through its characteristics and then examine whether visitors experience it as memorable,
satisfying, or worth repeating.

Human relationships, trust, and commitment
The human relationship category receives support from hospitality, retail, and event research.
Wijayanti and Saifudin (2021) identify the host–guest relationship as part of omotenashi in a ryokan,
Leingpibul et al. (2025) connect omotenashi, expertise, trust, and commitment in Japanese retail, and
Hayes (2024) shows that volunteers at the 2019 Rugby World Cup helped create omotenashi through
fleeting relationships with visitors rather than merely transactional service. The festival setting therefore
makes relational hospitality analytically visible even when interactions are brief.
Intercultural competence adds an important condition to this relational process. Wu et al. (2025)
found that intercultural responsibility, understanding, appreciation, and action positively influence
experience co-creation among international tourists at a Japanese festival. In Sapporo, the beer garden
brings local residents and tourists into the same public space, while official tourism information is
designed for visitors from outside the city. The framework therefore treats cultural context and visitor
competence as factors that can strengthen or weaken the interpretation of proactive attention.
Behavioral outcomes remain downstream. Zhang et al. (2019) show that co-creation experience
and festival satisfaction contribute to intentions to attend again, and Pivac et al. (2019) link perceived
event quality, satisfaction, and behavioral intention. These findings support the placement of revisit
intention and recommendation after the experience rather than inside the hospitality construct.
Omotenashi can contribute to a favorable relationship, but return behavior also depends on event content,
quality, access, social context, and individual preference.
Organizational consistency as the supporting layer
Morishita and Kosaka (2017) provide the clearest organizational evidence in the omotenashi
corpus by linking superior hospitality to focused training, organization -wide communication, and
feedback. A large festival requires an analogous supporting layer across venue operators, service counters,
reservation channels, food and beverage teams, information functions, and site rules. The Sapporo
documentation shows standardized opening periods, reservation rules, cashless-payment information,
seating conditions, and behavioral rules across the event, illustrating the kind of operational structure
within which adaptive service can occur (Sapporo Tourism & Convention Bureau, 2026b).
Budianto (2025) corroborates this organizational reading from the perspective of workforce
learning, showing that hospitality skills can be learned and adapted through training and practice. Hayes
(2024) extends the point into event settings, where organized volunteers contributed to a hospitable
environment through interaction. These findings suggest that festival organizers seeking to operationalize
omotenashi should invest in shared service principles, contextual judgement, communication, and role
clarity rather than rely on scripted politeness.
Technology can also support organizational consistency when it reduces waiting, helps visitors
access information, or enables reliable reservations and ordering. In Sapporo, official 2026 documentation
records web-based reservations at several venues, cash less-payment guidance, and venue-specific
ordering procedures (Sapporo Tourism & Convention Bureau, 2026b). The analytical point is not that
digitalization equals hospitality, but that infrastructure can free staff and visitors from avoidable friction
while preserving human discretion where context requires it.
From a digital–human orchestration perspective, the managerial issue is not whether technology
replaces interpersonal hospitality, but how routine and interpretive tasks are allocated. Automated
systems can manage high-volume and predictable functions such as reservations, payment processing,
menu information, availability communication, and routine ordering. Human discretion becomes
comparatively more relevant when visitors encounter exceptions, linguistic ambiguity, accessibility needs,
service failure, unfamiliar procedures, crowd-related difficulties, or situations requiring interpretation of

The framework also resolves the apparent tension between surprise and restraint. Wijayanti and
Saifudin (2021) identify small surprises, while Morishita (2024) values family-like hospitality without
excessive service. Both patterns can be compatible when t he analytical centre is contextual
appropriateness. Anticipation becomes useful when it reduces effort, personalization becomes useful
when it responds to a meaningful difference, attention to detail becomes useful when it supports the
purpose of the experience, and human contact becomes useful when it respects the guest's preferred level
of interaction.
This interpretation narrows the managerial use of the concept in a productive way. Festival
organizers should not equate omotenashi with adding gifts or increasing staff contact. A more defensible
application involves identifying recurring visitor frictions, preparing appropriate responses, preserving
choice, coordinating operational quality, and training staff to interpret context. The Sapporo Omotenashi
Seats are illustrative because they address a predictable capacity problem for tourists while leaving the
broader event open to multiple forms of participation.
Theoretical, methodological, and professional implications
Theoretically, the review connects a culturally specific hospitality concept with service marketing
and festival tourism without erasing the distinction among them. Omotenashi remains grounded in
Japanese service culture, while the framework translates recurrent practices into categories that can be
compared across lodging, retail, restaurants, and public events. Hayes (2024) provides direct support for
this extension into event experiences, and Wu et al. (2025) adds the role of intercultural competence and
co-creation. Cross-context application should therefore preserve contextual sensitivity rather than
reproduce a fixed ceremonial script.
The results support a distinction between observable practices and visitor outcomes. Future field
research at the Sapporo event can operationalize anticipation, personalization, attention to detail, and
human relationships as visitor perceptions while measuring event quality, satisfaction, trust, place
attachment, memorability, revisit intention, and recommendation separately. This design would permit
mediation and moderation testing without defining independent and dependent variables through the

For empirical operationalization, a subsequent field study could use multi -item Likert-type

anticipation “The event anticipated needs that I was likely to experience before I requested assistance”;

trust, memorability, place attachment, revisit intention, and recommendation should remain separate
outcome constructs. These indicators are proposed for future testing and would require expert review,
pilot testing, reliability analysis, and construct validation before inferential use.
For festival managers, the framework directs attention to service design, workforce capability, and
visitor-flow conditions. Training should combine operational standards with contextual judgement.
Reservation and ordering systems should reduce avoidable effort, while information should help tourists
understand venue differences before and during the visit. Cleanliness, safety, legal alcohol-service rules,
accuracy, and response time require consistent standards; interpersonal contact and personalization
require discretion within those standards. This separation is particularly important in a high-volume
outdoor event.
The Sapporo case adds a practical implication for destination marketing. The beer garden is not an
isolated consumption space: it is embedded in Odori Park and the wider Sapporo Summer Festival, where
local residents and tourists share a seasonal urban environment. Event research indicates that co-creation,
place attachment, and intercultural participation can strengthen festival experience (Zhang et al., 2019;
Wu et al., 2025), while the official Sapporo design explicitly includes a tourist-facing Omotenashi Seat
mechanism. This combination creates a strong setting for future primary research on whether culturally
framed service design translates into perceived welcome and behavioral outcomes.
D. CONCLUSION
This article examined how omotenashi can be understood through service marketing and how its
principles appear in a contemporary festival setting. The review identifies anticipation, personalization,
attention to detail, and human relationships as recurrent service manifestations, while organizational
consistency supports their repeated execution. Functional quality, cultural context, and event conditions
influence how visitors interpret these practices, and trust, satisfaction, place attachment, memorability,
revisit intention, and recommendation remain possible outcomes rather than defining elements of the
concept.
The dialogue among the selected studies also qualifies a common assumption about superior
hospitality. Wijayanti and Saifudin (2021) identify small surprises and personalized host –guest
relationships, Morishita (2024) finds value in hospitality without excessive service, Hayes (2024) locates
omotenashi in relational event encounters, and Wu et al. (2025) emphasizes intercultural participation
and co-creation. Taken together, these findings support contextual appropriateness as the organizing
principle of the framework. The relevant managerial question is whether an intervention fits the visitor,
event setting, and moment rather than whether the organization provides more service.
The review also clarifies the role of organizational management. Morishita and Kosaka (2017)
connect omotenashi with training, communication, and feedback, while Budianto (2025) documents
learning and cultural adaptation. Festival -quality studies show that  information, hygiene, safety,

perceived anticipation, personalization, attention to detail, and human relationships predict event
experience after controlling for functional quality, crowding, cultural background, and prior attendance.
