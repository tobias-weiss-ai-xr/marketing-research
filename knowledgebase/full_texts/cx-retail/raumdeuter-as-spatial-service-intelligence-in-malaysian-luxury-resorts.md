Abstract. This conceptual review proposes Raumdeute r as a spatial service -intelligence lens for
understanding luxury resort service in Malaysia. Raumdeuter is not an established hospitality construct; it
is used here as a metaphor for how frontline employees interpret physical, social, temporal, digital, a nd
cultural cues in resort environments in real time. The review draws on servicescape theory, social
servicescape research, hotel customer experience management, customer experience quality, luxury
hospitality, proactive customer service performance, smar t hospitality and generative artificial
intelligence, and Malaysian cultural communication. On this basis, the paper argues that the luxury resort
experience is influenced not only by environmental design and service standards but also by employees'
capacity to read guest movement, density, timing, privacy expectations, digital signals, and culturally
appropriate service cues, including the indirectness and refinement encoded in budi bahasa. The review
develops a framework that links resort cues, Raumdeuter  interpretation, guest -perceived experiential
value, and behavioural outcomes, and offers four research propositions to guide empirical testing. The
paper contributes by reorienting the servicescape discussion from environmental influence to staff -
mediated interpretation and contextualising anticipatory luxury service within Malaysian hospitality
culture. Practical implications are offered for staff training, guest -flow management, service
personalisation, technology use, and culturally sensitive service design.
Keywords: servicescape, guest experience, proactive service, cultural communication,  guest loyalty
Introduction
Luxury resort experiences are shaped by a complex interplay among physical
settings, social encounters, employee conduct, guest expectatio ns, and symbolic
meanings. Foundational servicescape theory establishes that physical surroundings
influence both customers and employees in service organisations, including hotels and
restaurants (Bitner, 1992). Evidence from Malaysian resort hotels indic ates that the
physical and social environments are meaningful predictors of guest satisfaction and
revisit intention ( Ali and Omar, 2014). Recent work extends this further, showing that
physical, social, and e -servicescape elements influence resort hotel g uest loyalty, with
place attachment serving as a principal mediator for most, though not all, of these
pathways (Ariffin et al., 2025). These findings confirm that the environment matters,
but they also invite a more careful look at how it is activated in real time. The growing
sophistication of luxury resort experiences suggests that the environment is only part of
the explanation. Equally important is the interpretive capability that enables frontline
employees to read the environment in real time. Social  servicescape research shows that
customers form impressions from passive observation of other customers, employees,
and the density of the environment (Line and Hanks, 2019b), with downstream effects
on satisfaction and behavioural intentions in full -service hotels (L ine and  Hanks,

German Raum (space) and Deuter (interpreter), denoting an agent whose value lies less
in occupying a fixed position than in reading available space and arriving where it
matters at the right mom ent. Translated into hospitality, Raumdeuter refers to a spatial
service-intelligence lens through which frontline employees in Malaysian luxury resorts
interpret physical, social, temporal, digital, and cultural cues to anticipate guest needs,
reduce expe riential friction, and deliver personalised yet unobtrusive luxury service.
The Malaysian setting matters because luxury services there are evaluated not only
through efficiency and personalisation but also through culturally appropriate
communication, refined language, indirectness, respect, and care for emotions captured
by budi bahasa (Ramli, 2013), and because frontline cultural intelligence has been
shown to predict service quality in Malaysian four - and five -star hotels (Jiony et al.,
2021).
Building on this definition, the paper pursues three questions. How can Raumdeuter
be translated into hospitality scholarship without being overclaimed as a measured
construct? Which existing literature supports its application in Malaysian luxury
resorts? And how can Raumdeuter -informed service intelligence contribute to guest
experience, proactive service, luxury value, and revisit -oriented outcomes? The
remaining sections review the relevant literature, describe the review method, present
the conceptual synthesis, and discuss theoretical and managerial implications.

how.

Customer experience management and luxury hospitality
Customer experience management research argues that hospitality firms cannot
improve experience through a single functional area. Kandampully et al. (2018)
concluded th at positive experience is the outcome of an orchestrated, multifunctional
approach spanning marketing, operations, design, human resources, strategy,
technology, and social media. Klaus and Maklan (2013) showed that customer
experience is best measured hol istically across product experience, outcome focus,
moments of truth, and peace of mind, and that it predicts loyalty better than service
quality alone. Rahimian et al. (2021) proposed a hotel -specific four -stage process of
identification, design, implemen tation, and monitoring, supported by organisational
factors of culture, structure, leadership, and technology. Veloso and Gómez -Suárez
(2023) consolidated this work and called for clearer conceptual models. For clarity,
CEM denotes in this paper the firm -side managerial framework through which hotels
design, implement, and monitor the guest experience ( Rahimian et al., 2021;
Kandampully et al., 2018 ), whereas customer experience quality denotes the guest -side
construct through which the delivered experience  is evaluated ( Klaus and  Maklan,
2013). The CEM framework thus denotes the managerial architecture, while customer
experience constructs denote the guest evaluations that the architecture is intended to
produce.
Luxury hospitality research has moved in par allel. Luna-Cortés et al. (2022) argued
that the field remains disjointed, while Jain et al. (2023) identified six intellectual
clusters spanning digital interactions, brand attributes, status consumption, service
quality, sustainability, and workforce traits. Christodoulides et al. (2025) introduced and
validated a six -dimensional scale of customer luxury experience comprising service
quality, authenticity, escapism, exclusivity, status, and aesthetic refinement, while Khan
and Rahman (2017) added a comple mentary brand -experience lens spanning location,
ambience, staff competence, website and social media presence, and guest -to-guest
experience. These two streams converge on a question they do not directly answer.
Customer experience management explains the  multifunctional orchestration through
which firms produce experience, and luxury hospitality research explains the
dimensions through which guests evaluate it. Neither stream captures the moment -to-
moment interpretive work through which a frontline employ ee senses that an arriving

Proactive service, technology, and culturally appropriate communication
Research on proactive customer service performance offers the clearest parallel in
hospitality to the proposed Raumdeuter lens. Bai et al. (2026) defined it as frontline
employees' self -initiated efforts to identify and pre -empt guest needs before they are
voiced, and reviewed antecedents, mediators, moderators, and outcomes, positioning
proactive service as a source of reputational differentiation in hospitality and tourism.
However, the construct has been developed largely through employee -level antecedents
such as leadership, climate, and personal resources, and it does not yet speak clearly to
the spatial reading of the resort environment or to the cultural texture through which
anticipatory beh aviour is enacted. Cross -cultural luxury hotel research adds that
frontline cultural intelligence and emotional labour are important resources for such
encounters (Lam et al., 2022).  Technology adds both possibilities and caveats. Smart
hospitality researc h argues that interconnected systems, sensors, and beacons can
support context -aware personalisation across the guest journey ( Buhalis and  Leung,
2018; Marinova et al., 2017), and Internet -of-Things-enabled multisensory
environments in upscale hotels shape  guest emotions and behavioural intentions (Pelet
et al., 2021). Big data analytics capabilities in hospitality and tourism are still maturing
(Mariani and  Baggio, 2022), and Wang (2025) cautions that generative AI raises
concerns regarding complex interac tions, cultural fit, service contingency, and privacy.
In a Malaysian luxury resort, these findings imply that digital data are best treated as
supportive intelligence for human interpreters rather than a replacement for the human
reading of space and cult ure. Ramli (2013) argued that, within Malaysian discourse,
budi bahasa can function as a cultural warrant encompassing politeness, refined
language, indirectness, and care for others' feelings; without essentialising guests or
staff, it offers a culturally  relevant lens for what counts as appropriate service in
Malaysian luxury resorts (see also Jiony et al., 2021).
Materials and Methods
This paper adopted an integrative conceptual review approach to synthesise
hospitality and tourism literature and develop  a theoretical framework. The review did
not attempt a meta -analysis, a PRISMA -type systematic review, or a bibliometric
analysis; it used conceptual relevance as the primary selection criterion because the
purpose was to clarify an emerging conceptual lens rather than estimate effect sizes. The
evidence base was assembled purposively from peer -reviewed journal articles in
hospitality, tourism, services marketing, and cultural communication, identified through
targeted searches in Scopus, Web of Science, Sc ienceDirect, Emerald Insight, SAGE
Journals, and Taylor & Francis Online, complemented by citation tracing from seminal
hospitality sources, and organised around keyword families covering servicescape, hotel
customer experience, luxury hospitality, proacti ve customer service performance, smart
hospitality, generative artificial intelligence in hotels, and Malaysian cultural

Results and Discussion
Defining Raumdeuter in the hospitality context
As defined in the Introduction, Raumdeuter refers to a spatial service -intelligence
lens through which frontline employees interpret physical, social, temporal, digital, and
cultural cues in the resort environment to anticipate guest needs, reduce experiential
friction, and deliver personalised yet unobtrusive luxury service. The definition is
deliberately modest: it proposes no new measurement scale and does not claim to
replace servicescape theory, the CEM framework, or the customer luxury experience
construct; it complements them by shifting analytic attention from what cues exist to
how employees  read and respond to them in real time. The review's findings are
organised around five interpretive dimensions.

Five interpretive dimensions of Raumdeuter in luxury resorts
Spatial cue interpretation refers to employees' ability to read guests' movement
through lobbies, restaurants, pathways, beachfront areas, villa clusters, pool areas, and
activity zones. It is grounded in the servicescape framework, which positions spatial
layout, ambient conditions, and symbolic cues as stimuli influencing both custo mer and
employee behaviour (Bitner, 1992). In the Malaysian resort setting, Ali and Omar
(2014) showed that the physical environment predicts satisfaction and revisit intention,
and Ariffin et al. (2025) demonstrated that spatial layout and signs positivel y affect
place attachment. A Raumdeuter lens reframes these findings by asking what the design
asks employees to notice in real time.  Social cue interpretation refers to employees'
ability to read other guests, group composition, family needs, social densi ty, privacy
expectations, and behavioural appropriateness. It draws on the social servicescape,
defined by the observable characteristics of other customers and employees, including
similarity, appearance, behaviour, and density ( Line and  Hanks, 2019b), an d is
consistent with Ali and Omar  (2014) finding that the social environment significantly
predicts outcomes in Malaysian resort hotels. The Raumdeuter contribution is to
emphasise that employees are themselves readers of the social servicescape, not only its
objects.

Temporal cue interpretation refers to employees' ability to detect critical moments in
the guest journey, such as arrival fatigue, waiting tension, breakfast crowding, activity
transitions, check -out stress, and service recovery windows. This dimension extends
customer experience management, where hotel CEM is built on identifying customers,
designing the experience, and monitoring touchpoints (Rahimian et al., 2021), and
where customer experience quality is a time -extended construct in which m oments of
truth and peace of mind sit alongside product experience ( Veloso and Gómez -Suárez,
2023; Klaus and Maklan, 2013 ). Proactive customer service performance research
supports this reading by defining the proactive capability as anticipating needs bef ore
they surface (Bai et al., 2026). In a Malaysian luxury resort, temporal reading is what
separates scripted service from discreet anticipation.  Digital cue interpretation refers to
employees' ability to use pre -arrival and in -stay digital information et hically to
personalise service. Smart hospitality research argues that interconnected and
interoperable systems, sensors, and beacons can supply employees with context -aware
information at the right moment ( Buhalis and Leung, 2018; Marinova et al., 2017), and
Internet-of-Things-enabled multisensory environments in upscale hotels shape guest
emotions and behavioural intentions (Pelet et al., 2021). Big -data analytics capabilities
are still maturing in hospitality and tourism (Mariani and Baggio, 2022), and W ang
(2025) cautions that generative AI struggles with complex interactions, cultural fit,
service contingency, and privacy. A Raumdeuter lens, therefore, treats digital
intelligence as input to human interpretation, not as a substitute for it; in a Malaysi an
luxury resort, technology should enable the frontline employee to read the guest better
rather than replace the relational work of reading (Lam et al., 2022; Ramli, 2013).
Cultural cue interpretation refers to employees' ability to deliver service in a manner
appropriate to Malaysian culture. Ramli (2013) showed that budi bahasa can function as
a warranty component in Malaysian discourse, reflecting politeness, respect, refined
language, indirectness, and care for others' feelings. As conceptual illustra tions rather
than empirical findings, each component can be translated into a strategy for authentic
service in the luxury resort setting. The halus (refined) preference for indirect
conversation (Ramli, 2013) supports offers that suggest rather than inter rogate, such as
proposing a quieter table when a guest hesitates at a restaurant entrance, rather than
questioning the hesitation. Discretion, listed among the core budi qualities, supports
unobtrusive presence around private moments such as family meals a nd poolside rest.
Care for others' feelings, together with the social emotion of malu (propriety), supports
service recovery by resolving a complaint away from other guests, so that no guest is
made to feel exposed. Politeness, respect, and refined languag e shape greeting, tone,
and the measured pacing of compliments. Malaysian empirical evidence supports the
weight of this dimension: Jiony et al. (2021) found that the cognitive and behavioural
facets of frontline cultural intelligence positively predicted service quality in Sabah
hotels, and Lam et al. (2022) reported that cultural intelligence and emotional labour
shape job satisfaction among luxury hotel employees serving cross -cultural guests in
Asia. Enacted in this way, cultural reading operates as a s trategy for authenticity and
gives Malaysian luxury resorts a distinctive identity in a global luxury market that
increasingly values authenticity and local culture (Christodoulides et al., 2025; Jain et
al., 2023).

Theoretical implications
The review yields three theore tical implications. First, it extends servicescape
research by shifting analytic attention from cues as stimuli to employees as interpreters
of cues; whereas Bitner (1992) discussed how physical surroundings influence
behaviour, the Raumdeuter lens adds a staff-mediated layer that operates in real time,
complementing recent Malaysian resort findings that place attachment mediates the
relationship between most servicescape elements and loyalty (Ariffin et al., 2025) by
clarifying the employee activity throug h which cues become felt experience. Second, it
reframes the customer luxury experience literature by suggesting that the six dimensions
of luxury experience (Christodoulides et al., 2025) and the moments-of-truth measure of
customer experience quality (Klaus and Maklan, 2013) depend partly on employee cue -
reading, particularly spatial reading for privacy, cultural reading for authenticity, and
temporal reading for discreet timing, although the precise mappings await empirical
testing. Third, it contextuali ses anticipatory luxury service in Malaysian cultural
communication: budi bahasa acts as a warranty component guiding the appropriateness
of action (Ramli, 2013), meaning that proactive service in Malaysian luxury resorts
must include refined indirectness, face sensitivity, and discreet non-intrusion as positive
service acts, adding a culturally embedded layer to the proactive customer service
model (Bai et al., 2026; Lam et al., 2022; Jiony et al., 2021).

Practical implications
For resort managers, the R aumdeuter lens implies a training and design agenda.
Frontline staff should be trained in cue reading across the five domains developed
above: space, people, time, digital signals, and culture. High -ambiguity zones such as

Conclusion
This paper has proposed Raumdeuter as a spatial service -intelligence lens for
Malaysian luxury resorts. Framed as an interpretive lens rather than a validated
construct, the proposal clarifies how frontline employees read physical, social, temporal,
digital, and cultural cues to deliver luxury experiences that are personalised yet
unobtrusive. The framework links resort environmental cues, employee interpretation,
guest-perceived experiential value, and behavioural outcomes, drawing on servicescape,
social servicescape, CEM and customer experience quality, luxury hospitality, proactive
service, smart hospitality, and Malaysian cultural communication research. The
contribution is conceptual: the paper offers a vocabulary t o investigate how luxury
performance is produced at the front line. Future empirical work should test whether
guest-perceived Raumdeuter behaviour predicts customer luxury experience
dimensions, place attachment, satisfaction, and revisit intention, and wh ether discreet
anticipatory service differs across domestic and international guests.
