Abstract. Artificial intelligence (AI) agents (software entities that can perceive, decide, and act with
minimal human oversight) are reshaping how consumer s discover, evaluate, purchase, and use brands
across digital channels. In online retail, these agents coordinate search and curation, personalize content,
and accelerate transactions, thereby redistributing influence along the customer journey from retail ers
toward brands and agentic intermediaries. This paper synthesizes current evidence on AI agents’ effects
across pre ‑purchase, purchase, and post‑ purchase stages, highlighting:  (a) hyper‑ personalized
discovery and conversational assistance that compress consideration sets; (b) agent ‑enabled transaction
support (e.g., dynamic pricing, streamlined checkout, real ‑t i m e  h e l p )  t h a t  l o w e r s  f r i c t i o n ; a n d  ( c )
post‑purchase orchestration (recommendations, returns, and service) that shapes satisfaction, loyalty, and
lifetime value. The paper interprets these developments against recent shifts in the information ecosystem,
such as the introduction of AI Overviews in Google Search and the integration of community content via
data‑access agreements – which alter how brand information is surfaced and cited to consumers. Building
on emerging work in generative/agentic search optimization, the paper argues that brands must complement
traditional Search Engine Optimization (SEO) with AI ‑agent optimization (AAO) strategies (e.g., entity
hygiene, structured data, citation seeding, and conversational answerability) to remain
machine‑retrievable in generative results. The paper also notes cross‑ m odel variation in what sources AI
systems cite, implying fragmented visibility requirements for brands. Conceptually, the paper integrates
insights from customer‑journey research with recent AI ‑ in‑ marketing evidence to propose a stage‑ wise
framework and a research agenda focused on trust, transparency, and long‑term brand equity effects.

Introduction
Digital customer journeys are undergoing a structural shift as answer‑first interfaces – large‑scale
generative search experiences and agentic shopping assistants – mediate how consumers discover,
evaluate, and purchase. In May 2024 Google mainstreamed AI Overviews in Search and expanded
globally thereafter, placing model ‑generated answers above links and reframing what counts as
visibility for brands. In parallel, Amazon and category platforms introduced embedded,
conversational shopping agents (for exampl e, Amazon’s Rufus and Instacart’s Ask Instacart),
further compressing search, evaluation, and action into a single, model ‑mediated touchpoint.
These moves reconfigure the practical “unit of competition” from pages and keywords to answers
and citations within opaque model outputs.
While platforms report favorable engagement, early audits and news coverage document
non‑trivial accuracy variance and source‑ selection issues in g enerative answers, hig hlig hting
brand risks associated with misattribution and hallu cination. Google publicly acknowledged
post‑launch fix es after high‑ profile errors;  independent assessments continue to show uneven
performance, with studies and industry analyses describing omission, consensus bias, and error
rates across AI search services. These dynamics suggest that discoverability and trust now depend

Classic customer ‑journey frameworks remain foundational but require reinterpretation

Emerging technical work formalizes the optimization problem created by answer ‑first
interfaces. The Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) literature shows that content style,
structure, and evidence cues can measurably influence whether generative engines cite a source in
their answers, implying a new optimization regime adjacent to – but distinct from – Search Engine
Optimization (SEO ). This reinforces a broader managerial point: as engines fuse retrieval and
generation, firms must optimize  model selection and citation rather than only for page ranking
(Aggarwal et al., 2024).
To organize a brand response, AI ‑agent optimization (A A O) is defined as the
cross‑functional c apability set that makes a brand correctly findable, faithfully represented, and
action‑ready inside model‑ mediated ex periences across the customer journey. Specificall y, A A O
spans (a) content and evidence design for generative engines (e.g., claim ‑level c itations and
structured facts), (b) technical signals and interfaces (e.g., data feeds, schema, product knowledge
accessible to agents), (c) measurement oriented to share‑of‑ answer and citation recall rather than
page rank, and (d) governance aligned with emerging safety and compliance regimes. AAO
complements and extends extant digital‑ marketing and customer ex perience (CX ) capability
frameworks by targeting the selection and reasoning layers of AI systems, not only the interface or
the channel (Aggarwal et al., 2024).
This reconceptualization is timely for at least three reasons. First, agentic surfaces are now
mainstream retail entry points (e.g., Rufus and Ask Instacart), compressing the path from need
recognition to action. Second, the mixture of platform claims, and third‑party audits indicates that
model behavior can be volatile and uneven, raising the stakes for brand accuracy and safety. Third,
regulatory baselines (e.g., the EU AI Act) are crystallizing obligations around transparency and
risk management, increasing the need for explicit governance over how brand data are exposed to
and reused by AI systems.
Although scholars have mapped digital customer journeys and reviewed AI’s role in
marketing and CX, the literature has only recently begun to formalize optimization for generative
engines and has not yet articulated an integrated, managerially actionable capability for brand‑safe
participation in AI answers. Addressing these research  gaps, the paper answers the following
questions:
RQ1. How do model‑mediated, answer‑ first ex periences restructure consumer attention,
trust, and choice mechanisms across journey stages?
RQ2. What specific capabilities constitute AAO for brands (content/evidence design,
technical signals, measurement, and governance), and how do they operationalize visibility and
fidelity in AI answers?

Literature review

Customer‑J ourney F oundations in a Digital, Omnichannel Context
Marketing research has moved from funnel metaphors toward journey ‑centric views that map
dynamic, cross‑channel  episodes of cognition, affect, and behavior over time (e.g., touchpoints
before, during, and after purchase). Lemon and Verhoef’s (2016) synthes is remain an anchor,
formalizing customer experience spanning multiple media and organizational functions. Later
work in Journal of Retailing stresses customer ‑journey management as a strategic capability in

Roggeveen, 2020). At the method level, Rosenbaum et al. (2017) clarify how journey mapping
should be grounded in customer research rather than managerial assumptions, resolving common
pitfalls when journeys become complex. Meanwhile, Kannan and Li (2017) provide a
digital‑marketing framework that ex plicitly situates touchpoints as loci where data, anal ytics, and
automation reshape decisions – an early signal that AI would systematically alter journeys long
before generative systems arrived.

AI in Marketing and Branding: From Augmentation to Agency
Reviews in leading outlets outline how AI alters marketing strategy, structures, and gover nance.
Davenport et  al. (2020) propose a three‑dimensional lens (intelligence level, task type,
embodiment) to anticipate where AI will remake marketing. Huang and Rust (2018) theorize AI’s
impact on service by decomposing tasks into mechanical, analytical , intuitive, and empathetic
“intelligences”, explaining task substitution vs. augmentation in service contexts. Libai  et al.
(2020) examine AI’s consequences for customer‑relationship management and call for safeguards
as automation scales personalization. Field ‑le ve l syn th e se s (Mu sta k e t a l., 2021; Ve rm a  e t a l.,
2021; Vlačić et al., 2021) document the surge of AI ‑in‑ branding scholarship and map research
needs (e.g., governance, explainability, cross ‑functional deployment). Collectivel y, these works
shift attention from isolated tools to systemic, AI ‑mediated market interfaces – the conceptual
bridge to agentic systems that curate, recommend, negotiate, or transact on customers’ behalf.
Implication for AAO: As AI takes over interpretive and coordinative work formerly done
by consumers, optimization must target the information diets of agents (what they ingest, trust, and
surface), not merely human clickstreams.

Conversational and Embodied Agents: Anthropomorphism, Trust, and Compliance
At the interaction layer, research shows that conversational agents (CAs) and chatbots shape
outcomes through social‑cognitive mechanisms. Adam, Wessel, and Benlian (2021) demonstrate
that design choices in AI chatbots influence user compliance, highlighting the role of
anthropomorphism and control cues in service encounters. A multi ‑study meta‑ anal ysis
synthesizing 82 samples finds that chatbot anthropomorphism tends to improve journey outcomes
(e.g., engagement, satisfaction), with moderators such as service result valence and
social‑presence cues (Y anx ia et al., 2024). Complementary work identifies when and why
consumers trust chatbots (e.g ., cognitive ‑appraisal pathways and task framing) and how flow
experiences emerge in AI ‑medi ated service (B aabdullah et al., 2022;  Wang et al., 2023). T he
findings outlined in this study have the capacity to generalize beyond single touchpoints. Indeed,
it has been demonstrated that agent characteristics could re-weight the salience of brand attributes,
alter risk perceptions, and thereby redirect choice architectures across the journey.
Implication for AAO: Because anthropomorphic and social ‑presence cues change how
agents frame brands, AAO must address not only content availability but also presentation
constraints (e.g., structured, answer ‑oriented content that agent user interfaces (U I s) can render
credibly and consistently).

From SEO to answer‑first AI  search:  the case for AI ‑ agent optimization (AAO)
A new, research‑backed thread in information retrieval is Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)
– methodologies for increasing content visibility in LLM‑based “ answer engines”  that synthesize
multi‑source responses (A ggarwal et al., 2024). Controlled ex periments show that structured,
machine‑readable si gnals can lift citation/visibility in generative answers, underscoring that
LLMs do source selection and synthesis rather than rank‑ ordered link retrieval. Complementary
work demonstrates that strategic text sequences on product pages can influence LLM
recommendations – evidence that answer engines can be steered by content design, for better or
worse (Kumar & Lakkaraju, 2024). Managerial literatures describe parallel shifts in
LLM‑powered search behavior and the consequent need to optimize for agents as primary readers,
not only for humans (Puntoni et al., 2024).
Building on that stream, AI‑agent optimization (A A O) for branding can be defined as the
systematic preparation of brand knowledge, evidence, and structures so that autonomous or
semi‑autonomous agents can (a) identify the brand entity, (b) verify claims against trusted
corpora, and (c) select the brand in answer ‑first recommendations across journey stages.
Conceptually, AAO extends GEO beyond “getting cited” to journey ‑stage adequacy (e.g.,
diagnostic facts for pre‑purchase triage, risk‑  and price‑ justification content at purchase, policy
and troubleshooting knowledge post‑purchase), explicitly alig ning  optimization with ag ent tasks
and user intents.
Scholarly guardrails:  Two research strands warn against overclaiming : (1) AI answer
engines can be susceptible to manipulation, implying governance and auditing are integral to AAO;
and (2) philosophical work on artificial agency argues that optimizing for agents adds normative
and design questions distinct from optimizing for intelligence per se (Floridi, 20251). These strands
reinforce that AAO should remain evidence‑first and ethics‑ aware (K umar & L akkaraju, 2024).

Gaps of the Present Study Addresses
Three persistent gaps motivate the AAO lens for online retail branding:
1. Holism across the journey. Much work isolates single touchpoints (e.g., chatbot service;
dynamic pricing) rather than modeling agent ‑mediated continuity from pre‑ purchase to
post‑purchase (cf. reviews in A MJ  and I J E M). A A O reframes op timization as
stage‑contingent and agent‑ aware across episodes (Chen & Prentice, 2025; Rana et al.,
2022).
2. Source preferences and machine ‑readability.  LLMs and agentic systems privilege
structured, trusted, and well‑linked sources when composing answers. T he brand literature
rarely specifies what evidence formats travel well into agent responses (schemas, FAQs,
benchmarks). GEO research begins this spec ification; AAO extends it to branding tasks
(Aggarwal et al., 2024; Kannan & Li, 2017).

Conceptual Framework: The Agent‑Or chestr ated J our ney (A OJ )
The paper conceptualizes AI agents as an orchestration layer that mediates intent, information, and
action across the customer journey. Building on journey theory (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016) and
digital‑marketing frameworks that locate technology’ s influence at touchpoints (K annan & L i,
2017), the Agent ‑Orchestrated Journey (AOJ) reframes how discovery, evaluation, choice, and
post‑purchase memory are produced when model‑ mediated systems summarize, recommend,
and sometimes transact on customers’ behalf. Under AOJ, four stages become analytically distinct
yet interdependent:
Inquiry (need recognition and goal formation). Consumers increasingly articulate needs
conversationally; answer engines fuse retrieval and generation to produce answers rather than
ranked links. Visibility thus hinges on inclusion/citation within the synthesized response rather
than position on a SERP. Early technical evidence from generative ‑eng ine optimization (GEO)
indicates that content design and structure measurably affect whether sources are cited by LLMs,
establishing a new optimization regime adjacent to SEO (Aggarwal et al., 2024).
Agentic mediation (planning and pruning). Agents compare alternatives, apply constraints,
and compress consideration sets using preference, context, and reputation signals. Work on
AI‑enabled CX and CRM anticipates both personalization g ains and discrimination risks as
systems prioritize high‑value customers (Huang & R ust, 201 8; L ibai et al., 2020).
Action (execution and transaction).  Conversational agents shape compliance and
conversion through social presence, anthropomorphism, and control cues; effects are moderated by
task type and result valence (Adam et al., 2021), and meta ‑analytic synth esis in Yanxia et al.
(2024).
Relationship & memory (post ‑purchase service and retention).  Agents manage service
episodes (tracking, returns, troubleshooting), influencing satisfaction, loyalty, and the locus of
relationship (brand ↔ customer vs. agent‑medi ated). R eviews of A I  recommenders show that
algorithmic curation re‑weights ex posure and can reshape long‑ run behaviors – implications for
brand memory and equity (Pappalardo et al., 2024).
Propositions: From this synthesis, the paper advances testable propositions to guide empirical
work:
P1 (Answer visibility). Under answer ‑first interfaces, citation/ inclusion share within generated
answers is a stronger mediator of awareness than rank on traditional SERPs (Aggarwal et al.,
2024).
P2 (Data sufficiency). Agentic pruning increases the premium on structured, trustworthy product
and policy data for inclusion in recommendations (Kannan & Li, 2017).
P3 (Design × trust). Anthropomorphic and transparency cues interact to shape compliance and
satisfaction in agentic recommendations (Adam et al., 2021; Yanxia et al., 2024).
P4 (Attribution shift). As agents manage post‑purchase episodes, loyalty attributions shift from
brand‑customer ties to agent‑ mediated ties, complicating L T V  models (L ibai et al., 2020).

AI‑Agent Optimization (AAO): Definitio n, Capabilitie s, and an Operational
Contrast with SEO
How will brands respond to the  ongoing structural transformations occurring in online retail? At
present, brands are striving to achieve the highest possible  rankings in search engines and online
marketplaces. Empirical studies indicate that consumers are more inclined to choose options
located near the top of a list of results, solely due to their position, regardless of relevance, price,
or quality of the al ternatives (Ursu, 2018). Consequently, securing the top position in a ranking
provides online retailers with a considerable advantage. Likewise, the clickthrough rates of
advertisements on search engines diminish as their positions lower  (Agarwal, et al., 2011) .
Therefore, due to this ranking effect and position bias, brands allocate resources SEO and ranking
optimization as essential tools for boosting brand sales.
In the new AI dominated world this could change. Should AI agents' purchasing decisions
be less swayed by ranking effects and position bias and  instead rely on an objective evaluation of
various factors, it may become less rational for brands and retailers to allocate resources towards
SEO or to secure a higher ranking on online platforms. Consequently, alternative strategies might
need to be implemented. Instead of focusing on enhancing their visibility for search engines, brands
could consider refining their marketing strategies to suit agentic consumption. Although
conventional SEO methods remain important, they typically emphasize indicators t ailored for
traditional search engines (such as keyword density and meta tags), which may not be adequate for
agentic AI that depends on alternative (and more profound) types of information quality. To
maintain machine -retrievability in generative results,  brands must integrate AAO strategies
alongside traditional SEO.
The paper defines AI ‑ag ent optimization (AAO) as the cross‑ functional capability that
makes a brand correctly findable, faithfully represented, and action‑ready i nside model‑ mediated
experiences across the journey. AAO extends GEO’s focus on getting cited by generative engines
to the full AOJ: (a) Content and evidence design (concise claims, verifiable facts, comparative
benchmarks), (b) Technical signals (entity hygiene, schemas, knowledge‑ gr aph alignment,
accessible product feeds/APIs), (c) Measurement (share‑ of‑ answer, citation recall,
agent‑recommendation share), and (d) Governance (provenance, disclosure, safety).

Methodology

Review type and rationale
This study is a structured narrative review that synthesizes peer ‑revi ewed research across
marketing, information systems, human-computer interaction, and retailing to build an integrative
conceptual framework (AOJ) and the capability  construct AI ‑ag ent optimization (AAO). A
structured narrative approach is appropriate when the goal is theory integration rather than
effect‑size estimation and when bodies of evidence are heterogeneous across methods and
disciplines (Snyder, 2019; Tranfield et al., 2003). To enhance transparency and reproducibility, the
research aligned reporting with the PRISMA 2020 guidance and, where relevant to
scope‑mapping, with PR ISMA ‑ ScR  conventions (Page et al., 2021).

Sources and search strategy
The p aper targeted leading bibliographic databases that index marketing and adjacent fields:
Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, Business Source Complete, PsycINFO, and ACM Digital
Library. Searches covered January 2016–October 2025 to capture contemporary AI developments
while retaining foundational pre ‑201 6 works (e.g., L emon & V erhoef, 201 6) for theoretical
anchoring. Representative query templates combined journey, agent, and branding terms (Boolean
operators and truncations used per database rules), for example:
"customer journey" AND (AI OR "artificial intelligence" OR agent* OR chatbot*) AND
(retail OR e-commerce OR branding)
(generative OR LLM OR "answer engine") AND (search OR discovery) AND (brand* OR retail)
(recommender OR "decision support") AND (consumer OR customer) AND (brand equity OR
loyalty)

Eligibility criteria
Inclusion criteria prioritized peer ‑revi ewed journal articl es and scholarl y reviews (E nglish
language) that: (a) theorize or empirically examine AI/agentic systems in consumer journeys,
branding, retail, or service, or (b) provide methodological/technical foundations directly shaping
how AI systems retrieve, synthesize, or recommend brand information (e.g., recommender‑system
surveys; generative ‑engine optimization). Seminal pre‑ 201 6 pi eces were retained when they
provided canonical journey theory or conceptual guidance. Exclusion criteria removed
non‑scholarl y commentary and practitioner pieces unless used to contex tualize platform changes
(kept separate from the analytic corpus). When emergent topics lacked archival coverage (e.g.,
GEO), the research tracked peer ‑reviewed or preprinted sources cautiousl y and labeled them
explicitly.

Screening and selection
Titles/abstracts were screened for topical relevance to AI ‑mediated discovery, evaluation,
transaction, or post ‑purchase phenomena. Full texts were examined for construct clarity
(definitions of “agent,” “assistant,” “chatbot,” “recommender,” “answer engine”), research design,
and direct link to branding or journey outcomes. The research favored journals with established
peer review in marketing, information systems, and service (e.g., Journal of Marketing, Journal of
Retailing, Journal of Service Research), while including adjacent outlets for technical depth (e.g.,
recommender‑system surveys). T he resulting corpus underpins the synthesis in §§2–4 and the
propositions in §3. (Procedural reporting follows PRISMA 2020 headings to facilitate replication
by other scholars.) (Page et al., 2021).

Data extraction and coding
The research extracted bibliographic data, focal constructs, context (industry/setting), methods,
measures, and reported mechanisms. A thematic synthesis procedure was applied to code findings
into first‑order concepts (e.g., anthropomorphism effects, agent transparency, evidence density),
second‑order themes (e.g., trust formation in agentic interactions), and aggregate dimensions
aligned with AOJ stages (Inquiry, Agentic Mediation, Action, Relationship & Memory). Thematic
synthesis is appropriate for inte grating heterogeneous qualitative and quantitative insights while
preserving proximity to primary claims (Thomas & Harden, 2008).

Synthesis logic and conceptual integration
Following guidance on developing conceptual contributions in marketing (MacInnis, 2011) and
the value of conceptual integration to knowledge advancement (Yadav, 2010) , the research used
abductive reasoning to iteratively map coded evidence onto the Agent ‑Orchestrated J ourney
(AOJ) and to specify AI ‑agent optimization (A A O) as a ca pability construct. This process

Findings
AI agents are set to transform how consumers search for branded products. Currently, the customer
journey frequently initiates with a search engine, such as Google, or a major online marketplace
like Amazon, which has effectively evolved into a product search engine. In the foreseeable future,
AI agents may act as novel access points. As a result, brands need to leverage the capabilities of
AAO to provide engaging results to their intended audience, thus improving the overall consumer
experience.
The integration of AAO into marketing and branding strategies has significantly
transformed the conventional consumer decision -making process. Rather than adhering to the
linear path usually linked with decision-making, where consumers navigate through a set sequence
of steps, AAO has introduced a non-linear approach. This shift from traditional methods suggests
that consumers can engage in the decision-making process at different stages (Sharma et al., 2023),
directly influenced by AAO. The widespread impact of AAO highlights the changing dynamics of
consumers' purchasing behavior as a reaction to technological progress.
The commonly accepted stages of the customer journey encapsulate the brand shopping
experiences of customers through three distinct stages: pre-purchase, purchase, and post-purchase.

the AOJ, it should be noted that the stages are connected as follows: inquiry (need recognition and
goal formation) and agentic mediation (planning and pruning) are related to the pre-purchase stage
of the customer journey,  action (execution and transacti on) correspond to the purchase stage and
relationship & memory (post‑purchase service and retention) to the post-purchase. a rough outline
of the AI related cross‑stage regularities that can impact a customer's brand decision journey is
presented below.

Limitations and Future Research

Limitations (brand-specific)
First, as a structured narrative review, this work integrates heterogeneous literatures without
estimating pooled effects; causal magnitudes linking answer -layer metrics  (SoA, CR, ARS) to
brand outcomes (awareness, consideration, equity) remain to be established. Second, fast-evolving
platform behavior introduces recency and opacity risks:  engines change answer policies and
citation practices, and proprietary agent logic limits obs ervability—both complicate brand
inference. Third, instrumentation for SoA/CR/ARS depends on prompt/task design and session
variability; although reliability procedures mitigate noise, construct validity requires  continued
refinement with brand-appropriate gold standards. Fourth, brand heterogeneity (size, distribution,
pricing power, category norms) may confound relationships between answer visibility and equity;
findings may not generalize across B2C/B2B or regulated categories. Finally, adversarial dynamics
(e.g., knowledge-graph poisoning, astroturfed reviews) can distort answer layers in ways not fully
addressed here.

Several additional issues, worthy of future research, are summarized below.

Conclusion
The digital search environment is experiencing a profound transformation due to the swift progress
of AI. Traditional search engines, which previously depended on keyword matching and link-based
rankings, are increasingly incorporating AI -driven technologies. These technologies offer direct
answers, summarize content, and facilitate conversational search, thereby redefining the manner in
which users access information. Often, they provide accurate responses without necessitating clicks
to external websites. This evolution signifies the emergence of AI-agent optimization (AAO) as a
new paradigm that complements established SEO practices, compelling marketers and brands to
adapt. For many years, SEO strategies have been founded on principles such as keyword
optimization, backlink acquisition, and on-page technical improvements. However, the rise of AI-
powered answer engines is reducing the significance of traditional organic search results. Research
indicates an increase in zero-click searches, where users receive answers directly from SERPs. This
trend raises critical questions regarding how brands can sustain visibility when search engines
bypass their websites, what new optimization strategies should accompany traditional SEO, and
what the future may hold for online retail in an AI-centric search ecosystem.
This paper advances the field by (1) synthesizing how agentic, answer ‑first systems
reshape the customer journey; (2) defining AI ‑agent optimization (A A O) as an integrative
capability framework grounded in recent platform developments and technical research; and (3)
proposing measurement constructs and managerial design principles that enable brands to compete
for answers, not just rankings.

A2. Citation Recall (CR) Scoring Rubric (claim-level)
Unit of analysis. A fact 𝑓𝑓 ∈ 𝐹𝐹 scored within a specific generated answer to a prompt –engine (or
prompt–agent) pair. CR can be computed as any- hit (global)  or average per answer (see §6
equations).
A2.1 Coding categories (strict binary for CR; optional soft credit shown)
Code Label Decision rule (apply in order)
C Correct & complete Value matches canonical and units/constraints (region/date) are
correct; faithful paraphrase permitted.
CI Correct but imprecise Semantically correct but rounded or unit converted without material
change (e.g., 9.8 → 10 hrs; inches ↔ mm) and constraint context
preserved.
P Partially correct Mixed claim: includes the correct value and an ancillary inaccuracy
or omits a required qualifier that changes interpretability (e.g.,
“waterproof” without IP code).
O Omitted Fact not stated.
H Hallucinated/incorrect Value contradicts canonical table, wrong unit, wrong region/date, or
invented attribute.
*Use soft credit only for exploratory analysis; report strict CR as the primary metric.

Normalization rules.
Numbers/units. Accept conversions if numerically equivalent (±1% rounding for
consumer specs) and unit is explicit.
Dates/regions. A claim valid only in {REGION/DATE} is incorrect if presented as
universal.
Comparatives. If a prompt requests comparison and an answer embeds a brand fact (e.g.,
“{BRAND} warranty 2 years”), code that fact against the table for the relevant region.
Coder instructions.
1. Read the answer only (ignore external links unless explicitly quoted in the answer).
2. Mark each 𝑓𝑓 ∈ 𝐹𝐹as C/CI/P/O/H.
3. Add brief rationale for any P/H.
4. Submit codes independently; reconcile disagreements by rulebook.
