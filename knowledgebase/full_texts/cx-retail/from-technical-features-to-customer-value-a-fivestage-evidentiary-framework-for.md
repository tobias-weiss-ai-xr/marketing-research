Abstract :  In mature consumer categories, firms frequently introduce technically distinctive features (new
materials, ingredients, components, or processes) and  present them as sources of customer value and
competitive advantage. Yet a documented technical change does not by itself show that customers experience,
value, or will pay for the difference, or that rivals cannot replicate it. This conceptual article de velops a
technology-to-value translation framework comprising five sequential stages (technical attribute, functional
performance, customer experience, customer value, and defensible advantage) and specifies the type and
threshold of evidence required to move from one stage to the next. The framework integrates means-end value
theory, customer value proposition research, jobs -to-be-done and outcome -driven innovation, the resource -
based view, and dynamic capabilities into a single diagnostic audit. A four-category evidence coding scheme
(documented, firm-stated, not evidenced, and ambiguous) makes the audit reproducible, and a hypothetical
illustrative application shows how the scheme separates supported claims from strategic inference. Four
propositions, concerning the distribution of public evidence across stages, competitive density, positioning
narratives, and capability-based defensibility, are advanced for future multi-case, archival, experimental, and
survey research. The paper contributes an integrativ e, evidence -based diagnostic tool for scholars and
managers who must judge whether a technical distinction has become a source of customer value.

1. INTRODUCTION

Mature consumer categories are crowded with products that perform similar core functions, and firms
competing within them often turn to technical differentiation: a new material, ingredient, component, or
manufacturing process that distinguishes a product from its close substitutes. Such features are attract ive to
firms because they are concrete, documentable, and protectable. They are also attractive to communicators,
because a distinctive technology offers a ready-made narrative. Product managers therefore routinely confront
a specific version of a general innovation problem: a firm develops or acquires a technically distinctive
capability and must decide whether, and how, to build a product strategy around it.
The temptation is to treat technical distinctiveness as the value proposition itself. Marketing an d strategy
scholars have long cautioned against this conflation. Levitt (1960) argued that firms that define themselves
by what they produce, rather than by the customer needs they serve, risk strategic myopia even amid short -
term technical success. Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan (2016) restated the caution in terms of “jobs to
be done”: customers do not buy attributes; they hire products to accomplish a job, and a feature matters only
insofar as it changes the outcome of that job. Rogers (2003) adde d that adoption depends on perceived, not
objective, advantage. These contributions, however, address different links in the chain that runs from a
technical change to a sustained commercial advantage, and none supplies a practical means of judging how
far along that chain the available evidence actually reaches.

This paper addresses that gap by asking: What evidence is required before a documented technical feature can
be treated as a source of customer value and defensible competitive advantage? It devel ops a five -stage
technology-to-value translation framework (technical attribute, functional performance, customer experience,
customer value, and defensible advantage) and specifies, for each stage, what must be shown and what kind
of evidence can show it.  The framework is diagnostic rather than predictive: its purpose is to make visible

The paper makes three contributions. First, it integrates means-end value theory, customer value proposition

The paper is conceptual: it does not test the framework with primary data, and its illustrative application uses
a hypothetical product constructed for that purpose.

2. LITERATURE REVIEW

2.1 Feature-Led Innovation and the Perception of Advantage

The risk of equating product features with customer value is long -standing. Levitt (1960) argued that firms
lose sight of customer needs when they define themselves too narrowly by the products or technologies they
produce. The concern  is particularly acute in materials - and process-based innovation, where a distinctive
technical change can become an end in itself rather than a means to a customer outcome.
Rogers’s (2003) diffusion -of-innovations framework adds an adoption perspective. Adoption depends on
perceived relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability, not simply on
objective technical merit. A technically superior solution may therefore diffuse no faster than an inferior one
if its advantage is not perceptible or demonstrable to users. This distinction between technical improvement
and perceived advantage is central to the framework developed here, because it implies that evidence of a
change in the product cannot stand in for evidence of a change in the customer’s experience.

2.2 Customer Value

Zeithaml’s (1988) means-end model of perceived value holds that consumers move from concrete cues, such
as ingredients, specifications, and price, to perceptions of quality, and then weigh those percepti ons against
monetary and non-monetary sacrifice. Gutman (1982) similarly described means-end chains in which product
attributes acquire personal meaning through the consequences they produce for the consumer. Woodruff
(1997) extended this hierarchical logic to value in use, describing customer value as a structure that runs from
product attributes and their performance, through the consequences experienced in use, to the customer’s
goals and purposes. Vargo and Lusch (2004) emphasized that value is realized in use and co-created with the
customer rather than embedded in the firm’s output. A technical attribute therefore enters customer evaluation
only after it has been interpreted as a meaningful difference in quality or consequence.

In business markets, An derson, Narus, and van Rossum (2006) identified a related problem in value
propositions: suppliers often present undifferentiated lists of benefits, or unsubstantiated claims, rather than
quantified value. Their distinction among “all -benefits,” “favorable-points-of-difference,” and “resonating -
focus” propositions carries over to consumer settings. A technical feature becomes a stronger value
proposition when its benefit is tied to a specific customer segment and supported by evidence.

2.3 Jobs to Be Done and Outcome-Driven Innovation

Ulwick (2002) argues that innovation should be organized around measurable customer outcomes rather than
around product solutions or generic statements of need. Christensen et al. (2016) similarly frame products as
being “hired” to accomplish functional, social, and emotional jobs. The implication for the present framework
is that a functional improvement becomes a customer -experience improvement only when it is linked to a
specific, measurable change in a job outcome for a defined customer segment.

2.4 Resources, Capabilities, and Defensibility

Even where a feature produces demonstrated customer value, its strategic significance depends on whether
competitors can reproduce the outcome. Wernerfelt (1984) viewed the firm as a bundle of resources whose
scarcity can support advantage. Barney (1991) formalized this logic: sustained advantage requires resources
that are valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable, and non -substitutable, supported by appropriate organization.
Dierickx and Cool (1989) added that strategic assets accumulated over time are difficult to imitate because of
factors such as time -compression diseconomies and causal ambiguity. For a technical innovation, these
arguments imply that a distinctive solution is not suffi cient in itself if rivals can substitute another solution
that yields the same customer outcome.

Teece, Pisano, and Shuen  (1997) extended the logic to dynamic capabilities, the processes by which firms
sense opportunities, reconfigure assets, and integrate knowledge over time. This distinction separates two
strategic claims that require different evidence: that a specific technical solution is a defensible resource, and
that the underlying technical and manufacturing capability is a defensible, transferable process.

2.5 Positioning of the Framework and Research Gap

Several established perspectives already describe relations hips among attributes, benefits, customer value,
and competitive advantage, and the present framework does not seek to replace them. Means -end and
laddering approaches move from attributes to consequences and values (Gutman, 1982); the framework
retains that attribute-to-outcome logic while adding an explicit evidence requirement at each transition and
extending the chain beyond customer value to defensible advantage. Service-dominant logic locates value in
use (Vargo & Lusch, 2004) and informs the customer -experience and customer-value stages, but it does not
itself specify how to audit whether a technical claim has produced a job-relevant experience or demonstrated
economic value. Customer value proposition research focuses on the credibility and substantiation of benefits
(Anderson et al., 2006), but it does not connect claims backward to technical evidence or forward to
defensibility. The resource-based view and dynamic-capabilities perspective explain why valuable resources
or capabilities may sustain advantage (Barney, 1991; Teece et al., 1997), but they do not provide a customer-
side evidentiary sequence showing how a technical attribute becomes experienced and valued before its
strategic defensibility is assessed.

The framework also differs in purpose  from stage-gate systems (Cooper, 1990). Stage -gate systems govern
internal go/no-go decisions during product development. The present framework audits the evidence behind
a feature-to-value claim, whether that claim is made by a firm about its own product or by an analyst about a
competitor’s.
Taken together, these literatures imply a sequence between a technical feature and defensible advantage: the
feature should produce a measurable functional improvement; the improvement should matter to a defined
customer segment; the resulting experience should create perceived value; and the resulting advantage should
rest on a resource or capability that rivals cannot readily imitate or substitute. Existing work addresses these
links separately, and the full evident iary chain is rarely presented as a single diagnostic tool. The novelty
claimed here is therefore integrative. It lies in the staging, the explicit evidence thresholds, and the
operationalization of the chain as an audit protocol, not in a new theory of cu stomer value or competitive
advantage.

3. CONCEPTUAL METHOD

3.1 Conceptual Approach
This paper is a conceptual article in the sense discussed by Yadav (2010) and MacInnis (2011). Its primary
contribution is integrative: it draws together theory from marketing and strategy into a sequential framework
and derives propositions that can be tested empirically. No primary data were collected.

3.2 Framework Development

The five -stage chain was developed through narrative synthesis of the literatures reviewed i n Section 2.
Constructs that address successive parts of the same phenomenon were ordered so that the output of each
stage forms the input to the next: a technical attribute must exist before it can change performance;

performance must change before customers can experience a difference; an experienced difference is required
before it can be valued; and value must exist before the question of its defensibility arises. Each stage was
then specified in terms of the claim it makes and the evidence required to establish it, so that the framework
can operate as a diagnostic coding scheme.

3.3 Illustrative Application

To show how the framework operates, Section 5 applies it to a hypothetical product. Illustrative cases are
widely used in management research to make abstract constructs concrete and to show how a framework can
be applied (see Siggelkow, 2007). The case used here is constructed rather than observed: it does not describe
any real firm, brand, or product, and the evidence pattern it presents is styliz ed. It is intended to demonstrate
the coding procedure, not to provide empirical support for the framework or its propositions.

3.4 Evidence Coding Scheme

Each claim about a product is classified against the five stages using four evidence categories. Do cumented
claims can be verified in a primary specification, patent, or comparable technical record. Firm -stated claims
are asserted in manufacturer marketing or product -information text without independent verification. Not
evidenced indicates that no qualifying source addressing the stage was located. Ambiguous indicates that the
available evidence is consistent with more than one stage classification or interpretation.
Coding proceeds in three steps: (a) identify the claim; (b) assign it to the earliest stage to which its content is
directly relevant; and (c) assess whether the source meets that stage’s evidence threshold (Table 1). Four rules
govern the procedure. A manufacturer statement remains firm -stated evidence and is never upgraded to
documented or independently verified performance. Absence of a qualifying source is coded as not evidenced,
which is not evidence that the underlying phenomenon does not exist. Where a source supports more than one
interpretation, the claim is coded ambiguous and the inference is stated explicitly. Finally, sources are eligible
only if they are publicly accessible, directly relevant to the product or a functionally comparable one, and
specific enough to support a stage-level claim; general commentary, unsourced summaries, duplicate listings,
and untraceable material are excluded.
In an empirical application, two or more independent coders should apply the scheme and an inter -coder
reliability statistic should be reported. The illustration in Section 5 involves a single c onstructed case and
therefore claims no reliability statistic. The scheme supports two claims: that the five stages can be
independently coded from documentation, and that the coded pattern shows where a feature-to-value claim is
supported. It does not sup port claims about actual commercial performance, undisclosed internal testing, or
generalizability to other innovations.

4.1 Stage 1: Technical Attribute

Stage 1 requires a documented, verifiable change in the product’s composition, design, or manufacturing
process, such as a stated specification value or a patented process step. Acceptable evidence includes product
specification sheets, pate nts, and engineering documentation. Stage 1 answers the question of what has
changed. It is the most readily evidenced stage because the information is generated and controlled by the
firm; for the same reason, documentation at this stage says nothing abou t whether the change matters to
anyone.

4.2 Stage 2: Functional Performance

Stage 2 requires a measurable change in a physical or functional performance property that is attributable to
the technical attribute, for example durability, precision, or ease  of use, ideally observed under controlled,
comparative conditions. Acceptable evidence is independent or third-party comparative testing. Manufacturer
claims alone are insufficient at this stage, because substantiation, not assertion, is what distinguishe s a
documented performance difference from a claim (Anderson et al., 2006).

4.3 Stage 3: Customer Experience

Stage 3 requires a perceptible, job -relevant change in the target customer’s experience of using the product,
mapped to a specific segment and us e case (Christensen et al., 2016; Ulwick, 2002). A performance
improvement that customers cannot perceive, or that does not bear on any job they are trying to do, does not
reach this stage (Rogers, 2003). Acceptable evidence includes user testing, observed  behavior, and self -
reported experience differentiated by segment.

4.4 Stage 4: Customer Value

Stage 4 requires a demonstrated willingness to prefer, choose, or pay a premium for the product because of
the experiential difference, net of the customer’s p erceived sacrifice (Zeithaml, 1988). Acceptable evidence
is revealed preference, such as sales, trial-to-repeat rates, and share shifts, or valid stated-preference data, such
as conjoint analysis and willingness -to-pay studies. Competitor list prices are a  supply-side measure and do
not, by themselves, establish willingness to pay.

4.5 Stage 5: Defensible Advantage

Stage 5 requires evidence that the resource or capability producing the value is valuable, rare, imperfectly
imitable, and non-substitutable (Barney, 1991), or that it derives from a dynamic, transferable organizational
capability (Teece et al., 1997) rather than from a single imitable solution. Acceptable evidence includes patent
scope and freedom-to-operate analysis, competitor imitation timelines, and capability transfer to other product
lines. Because a resource claim and a capability claim require different evidence, the coder should record
which of the two is being asserted.

4.6 Diagnostic Use

The framework is diagnostic rather than predictive. It allows researchers and managers to audit the evidence
stage by stage and to identify where the chain breaks: performance testing at Stage 2, segment and job
evidence at Stage 3, preference or willingness-to-pay evidence at Stage 4, and imitation barriers or transferable
capabilities at Stage 5. The audit also disciplines the language of claims. A firm with Stage 1 evidence alone
may state that its product differs technically; it may not, on that evidence, state that the difference benefits
customers, is preferred by them, or cannot be copied. Each claim is entitled only to the stage at which its
evidence ends.

5. ILLUSTRATIVE APPLICATION

This section applies the framework to a hypothetical product, Product X, introduced by Firm A. The case is
constructed for illustration and does not describe any real firm or product. Product X is a consumer product
in a mature category. It incorporates a technically distinctive feature, Feature F, which is described in the
firm’s product information and quantified in its specifications. Three established competitors (Competitors 1
to 3) sell functionally comparable products with somewhat different formats, specifications, and pack sizes.
Firm A also holds patents on related technical and manufacturing processes that pre-date Product X by roughly
three decades. The audit below codes the evidence that such a firm would typically make public, and Table 2
summarizes the result.

5.1 Stage 1: Documented

Feature F and its quantified specification appear in Firm A’s pro duct information, and Firm A’s patents
document related technical and manufacturing processes. A specific technical attribute and its developmental
lineage are therefore publicly documented, and Stage 1 is coded documented.

5.2 Stage 2: Firm-Stated

Firm A’s communications associate Feature F with improved durability, reliability, and consistent
performance. These are manufacturer-stated claims. No independent controlled comparison with the products
of Competitors 1 to 3 is available, and Stage 2 is therefore coded firm-stated.

5.3 Stage 3: Not Evidenced

Under the jobs -to-be-done perspective, an improvement in durability or ease of use becomes a customer
experience only when it is linked to a specific job, segment, and use case. Firm A’s public communicat ions
provide no segment-and-job mapping supported by quantified evidence. Stage 3 is coded not evidenced.

5.6 What the Audit Shows

The audit reproduces the pattern that motivates the framework. Evidence is strongest where it is cheapest for
the firm to produce (Stage 1), is asserted but unverified at Stage 2, and is absent at Stages 3 and 4. The Stage
5 evidence is compatible with two different strategic claims. On this record, the defensible statement is that
Firm A has documented a technical difference in Product X; whether that difference performs better, is noticed
by customers, is valued by them, or is hard for rivals to replicate has not been established. The audit also
indicates what to do next: commission independent comparative testing (Stage 2), identify the  segment and
job for which the difference matters (Stage 3), elicit willingness to pay (Stage 4), and analyze patent scope
and the transferability of the underlying capability (Stage 5).

6. RESEARCH PROPOSITIONS

The illustrative application suggests that public evidence for feature-based innovations may concentrate at the
early stages of the chain. To make this pattern, and related conjectures, testable, the following propositions
specify directional relationships and measurable constructs. They are theoretically derived and are not tested
in this paper.

Proposition 1. Among feature-based innovations in mature product categories, the proportion of publicly
disclosed evidence associated with Stages 1–2 will exceed the proportion associated with Stages 3–5, as
measured through systematic content analysis of manufacturer disclosures.
Technical specifications and performance claims are generated and controlled by the firm and are inexpensive
to disclose. Segment-level experience data, revealed preference, and imitation analyses are costly to produce,
are often commercially sensitive, and may be unflattering, so firms have weaker incentives to publish them
(Anderson et al., 2006).
Proposition 2.  Holding the innovation’s technical novelty constant, a higher number  of functionally
comparable competing products at launch is associated with a wider evidentiary gap between Stage 2
(performance) and Stage 4 (demonstrated customer value), measured as the presence versus absence of
published willingness-to-pay or comparative-testing data.

In crowded categories, firms have greater incentive to differentiate through technical claims, while price
comparison rather than value elicitation becomes the most visible metric. Evidence of customer value, by
contrast, is competitivel y sensitive. The expected result is a widening gap between what is claimed about
performance and what is demonstrated about value.
Proposition 3.  Between two otherwise comparable products in the same mature category, the one
marketed primarily through a Stage 1 technology narrative (an ingredient- or attribute-led proposition)
will show a higher discontinuation or reformulation rate within five years of launch than the one marketed
through a Stage 3/4 job-outcome narrative.
Following Levitt (1960) and Chris tensen et al. (2016), positioning built on a technical attribute leaves the
product exposed when rivals match the attribute or when customers fail to connect it with a job, whereas
positioning built on a job outcome anchors the product to something customers already value.
Proposition 4. Firms whose Stage 5 evidence consists of a multi -decade, multi-patent technical lineage
(a dynamic -capability pattern) are more likely to extend the underlying technology across multiple
product lines than firms whose Stage  5 evidence consists of a single recent patent (a static -resource
pattern).
Accumulated capability (Dierickx & Cool, 1989; Teece et al., 1997) is by its nature reusable across
applications, whereas a single-resource claim is bound to the specific solution it protects.

7. DISCUSSION

7.1 Theoretical Implications

The framework contributes to theory in three ways. First, it connects literatures that are usually applied
separately. Means -end and value -in-use models explain how  attributes become value; jobs -to-be-done
explains why some attributes matter; resource-based and dynamic-capability perspectives explain why some
advantages persist. Placing them on a single chain clarifies that they answer different questions at differen t
stages and that a complete claim requires all of them. Second, by attaching evidence thresholds to the
transitions, the framework converts conceptual relationships into an auditable protocol and separates
documented evidence from strategic inference. Thi rd, by distinguishing resource claims from capability
claims at Stage 5, it identifies a specific ambiguity that is common in technology -led innovation: the same
patent history can support either claim, and the two have different implications for investment and protection.

7.2 Managerial Implications

For product managers evaluating feature-based innovation in a mature category, the framework suggests four
practical checks:
Establish Stage 2 performance through independent or controlled comparative testing  before relying on
performance claims in communication or pricing.
Identify the customer segment and job for which the difference is relevant, and quantify the improvement in
job-relevant terms.
Base pricing decisions on revealed or rigorously elicited preference rather than on competitor list prices alone.
Determine whether the strategic asset is the specific technical solution or the underlying technical and
manufacturing capability, and align protection and investment accordingly.
The same checks can serve as an evidence checklist for analysts and buyers who assess a supplier’s or
competitor’s technology claims.

7.3 Boundary Conditions

The framework is intended primarily for mature product categories in which firms introduce identifiable
technical or feature-based changes and where functional alternatives can be compared. It is most directly
applicable when the innovation can be traced from a physical or process attribute through observable
performance and customer outcomes. It may require adaptation for radical category creation, pure service

7.4 Limitations and Future Research

This paper is conceptual, and its illustrative application relies on a constructed case; the framework is therefore
untested with real data. Because the coding scheme is applied to public evidence, its findings concern the
public evidentiary record rather than actual c ommercial performance or undisclosed internal testing: a firm
may hold Stage 3 or Stage 4 evidence that it has chosen not to publish. The stage order is a logical rather than
a temporal sequence, and feedback among stages, for example customer experience p rompting further
technical development, is not modeled. Future research should (a) apply the coding scheme to multiple real
innovations with independent coders and report reliability; (b) test the four propositions; (c) develop measures
of evidentiary comp leteness; (d) use primary data to determine whether firms close public evidence gaps
through non -public testing or customer research; and (e) extend the framework to the boundary contexts
identified above.

8. CONCLUSION

A technical feature is not, by its elf, evidence of customer value or competitive advantage. The five -stage
framework proposed here links technical attribute, functional performance, customer experience, customer
value, and defensible advantage, and specifies a different evidentiary burden for each transition. Its
contribution is not to replace means -end, value -in-use, customer -value-proposition, resource -based, or
dynamic-capabilities perspectives, but to integrate selected elements of them into an auditable sequence that
makes missing evid ence visible. The hypothetical illustration shows how evidence can cluster at the
technology-documentation stages and thin at the customer -value and defensibility stages, and the four
propositions translate that diagnostic pattern into testable relationshi ps for future multi -case, archival,
experimental, and survey research. The framework thus offers a disciplined way to ask where a feature -to-
value claim is supported, where it remains inferential, and what evidence is needed before a technical
distinction is treated as a source of customer value or defensible advantage.
