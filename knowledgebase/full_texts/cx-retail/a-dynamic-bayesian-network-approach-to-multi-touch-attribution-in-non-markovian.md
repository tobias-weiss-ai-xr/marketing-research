Abstract: Digital marketing campaigns generate increasingly
complex, multi-channel customer journeys, yet conventional multi-
touch attribution (MTA) approaches, including heuristic rules,
Markov chain models, game-theoretic methods, and deep-learning
approaches—struggle to  represent the cumulative, delayed, and
state-dependent effects that characterize customer behavior. This
article proposes a non-Markovian Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN)
framework for MTA that represents customer journeys as sequences
of observed touchpoin ts generated by latent behavioral
(engagement/intent) states and extends the conventional first -
order DBN by allowing the latent state at time tto depend on
multiple preceding latent states. Channel -level attribution is
computed through counterfactual remo val-effect analysis on the
resulting latent-state dynamics, enabling delayed and cumulative
channel effects to be incorporated into attribution. The framework
is positioned relative to heuristic, Markov -chain, Shapley -value,
deep-learning, and graphical po int-process approaches, and an
experimental design combining synthetic non -Markovian
simulations with real customer -journey data is proposed for
evaluating attribution accuracy, predictive performance, and
computational cost. An illustrative Monte Carlo si mulation
demonstrates the mechanism: the Email removal effect increases
from 0.04207 under a first -order specification to 0.04863 under a
second-order specification, representing an approximately 15.6%
increase when cumulative repeated -exposure effects are
introduced. The proposed framework provides an interpretable
probabilistic alternative to black -box sequence models while
extending the temporal depth of latent -state attribution beyond
conventional first -order DBNs. Future empirical evaluation is
required to establish its effectiveness, scalability, and
generalizability across real-world customer journeys.

1. Introduction
Contemporary digital marketing campaigns unfold across an increasingly dense set of channels —social media, search
engines, email marketing, display advertising, and mobile applications —through which customers encounter a firm repeatedly
before completing a purchase or other conversion event. Understanding how much credit each of these touchpoints deserves for
an eventual conversion is a foundational problem in marketing analytics, because attribution outcomes directly shape how
marketing budgets are allocated across channels1.
Early approaches to this problem relied on simple heuristics —first-touch, last-touch, and linear attribution —that assign

credit according to a fixed rule rather than on the basis of observed data. These rules are easy to compute and explain, but they
implicitly assume that a single touchpoint, or an equal share across all touchpoints, fully determines conversion, an assumption
long recognized as biased and oversimplified 2. Data -driven alternatives emerged to address this l imitation. Markov chain
attribution models represent the customer journey as a sequence of channel “states” and estimate each channel's contribution
through a removal -effect calculation —the predicted drop in conversion probability when a channel is removed  from the
transition graph2. Building on cooperative game theory, Shapley -value and related bankruptcy -based attribution rules instead
treat marketing channels as players in a cooperative game and allocate credit according to axiomatic fairness properties such as
efficiency, symmetry, and the null -player property 3. More recently, deep -learning architectures —including temporal
convolutional networks combined with layer -wise relevance propagation —have been proposed to capture long and complex
B2B customer journeys with many stage transitions 4, and graphical point -process frameworks have been developed to model
the full relational structure among touchpoint types and to compute path-level removal effects5.
Despite this progression, a central limitation persists  across nearly all of these approaches: the Markov property. Markov
chain attribution models, by construction, assume that the probability of the next touchpoint —or of conversion—depends only
on the current state and not on the sequence of states that preceded it. This memoryless assumption iscomputationally convenient
but is at odds with substantial evidence that customer behavior exhibits cumulative, delayed, and state -dependent effects: prior
exposures shape a customer's underlying intent or engagement level in ways that persist and compound over multiple subsequent
interactions6,7. Deep-learning sequence models can, in principle, learn such long-range dependencies, but they do so as opaque,
high-dimensional functions whose internal representations are di fficult to translate into the kind of explanation a marketing
manager can act upon8,9.
This creates a tension that the present article seeks to resolve existing attribution models typically trade off temporal
richness against interpretability. Markov chain and Shapley -value models are interpretable but largely memoryless; deep -
learning and graphical point-process models can represent richer temporal and relational structure but are comparatively opaque
or computationally demanding5. Dynamic Bayesian Networks (DBNs)—probabilistic graphical models that explicitly represent
how observed and latent variables evolve over time—offer a potential middle ground. DBNs have already been used to model
latent customer states underlying sequential behavior in marketing and health contexts, including multistage shopping
journeys6,10, clickstream-based shopping-phase detection and longitudinal engagement dynamics 11. However, standard DBNs
retain a first -order Markov assumption on latent -state transitions, which limits their ability to represent the long -memory
dependencies documented in customer journey research.
This article proposes a Dynamic Bayesian Network framework for multi -touch attribution that relaxes the first -order
Markov assumption on latent states. In the proposed model, each observed touchpoint is generated conditionally on an
underlying latent b ehavioral state, but —unlike a standard DBN —the latent state at time t is permitted to depend on multiple
preceding latent states rather than only on the immediately preceding one. Channel -level attribution is then computed through
counterfactual removal-effect analysis performed on the fitted non -Markovian latent-state dynamics, in the spirit of removal -
effect attribution but operating on inferred behavioral states rather than directly on observed touchpoint sequences 2,5.
The proposed framework makes three contributions to the multi-touch attribution literature. First, it introduces a Dynamic
Bayesian Network architecture for multi-touch attribution that links observed touchpoints to latent customer engagement/intent
states. Second, it extends this architect ure with a non -Markovian, long -memory dependency structure among latent states,
enabling the model to represent cumulative and delayed marketing effects that first -order Markov and hidden Markov model
(HMM)-based approaches cannot capture. Third, it offers  an interpretable probabilistic alternative to black -box deep-learning
attribution models, retaining the transparency associated with explicit transition and emission probabilities while extending
temporal depth beyond what conventional DBNs allow 8.

2. Literature Review
From Heuristics to Algorithmic Multi-Touch Attribution
Heuristic attribution rules —first-touch, last -touch, linear, time -decay, and position -based models —assign conversion
credit according to a predetermined rule applied to a touchpoint's position within the journey, independent of the actual statistical
relationship between that touchpoint and conversion 2. Their main advantages are simplicity and transparency; their main
weakness is that the assigned credit need not correspond to the touchpoint's true contribution.
Markov chain attribution models replace these  fixed rules with a transition -probability model estimated from observed
journey data, in which each unique touchpoint—together with start, conversion, and non-conversion states—is treated as a node
in a directed graph. Channel -level contribution is comput ed via the removal effect: a channel's transition edges are removed
from the graph, the conversion probability is recomputed, and the resulting drop in conversion probability is attributed to t hat
channel2. This approach captures the sequential, path-dependent nature of customer journeys more faithfully than heuristic rules,
but it inherits the Markov property—the assumption that the next state depends only on the current state.
A parallel line of work has approached attribution through cooperative game the ory. Molina et al. 3 formalize the multi -

within a game-theoretic attribution layer. Gaure et al. 2 provides a complementary comparison of heuristic and ensemble-based
attribution approaches, underscoring that the choice of underlying model substantially affects which channels are favored onc e
attribution moves beyond simple rules.
Machine-learning approaches have sought to relax these s tructural assumptions by learning the mapping from touchpoint
sequences to conversion directly from data. Agrawal et al. 4 propose a deep-learning framework for B2B customer journeys in
which each stage transition in the customer funnel is modeled by a Temp oral Convolutional Network (TCN), with a global
Stage-TCN conversion model combining these stage-level models, and channel- and touchpoint-level attribution computed via
layer-wise relevance propagation. Such models can represent long -range dependencies an d complex non -linear interactions
among touchpoints, but the resulting attribution values are derived from relevance scores propagated through a deep network
rather than from an explicit, human-interpretable probabilistic structure8.
Most recently, Tao et al.5 proposed a graphical point-process framework that represents customer -level path-to-purchase
data as a set of asynchronous event streams and learns the full relational structure among touchpoint types using a temporal
point-process model. Building on this graphical structure, they define graphical attribution methods that compute path -level
conversion credit using a probabilistic generalization of the removal effect. Conceptually, this framework is the closest rec ent
precedent to the present proposal in that it explicitly moves beyond pairwise, adjacent -state transitions toward a representation
of how the entire prior history of touchpoints jointly influences conversion. However, it represents this history through the
relational structure of observed to uchpoint types and their mutually exciting effects, rather than through an explicit layer of
latent behavioral or intent states —the mechanism through which the present article's non -Markovian DBN represents long -
memory effects.

Dynamic Bayesian Networks and Latent-State Models of Customer Behavior
A Dynamic Bayesian Network is a probabilistic graphical model that extends a Bayesian network across discrete time
steps, typically represented as a “two -time-slice” structure in which variables at time t are con ditioned on variables at times t
and t − 1 12. DBNs and their close relative, the hidden Markov model (HMM), have a long history of use in marketing for
representing unobservable customer states—such as relationship stage, engagement level, or shopping intent—that evolve over
time and probabilistically generate observable behaviors such as purchases, visits, or clicks.
Recent work has extended this tradition to multistage digital shopping journeys. Wei et al.10 propose a multistage Dynamic
Bayesian Network ( MS-DBN) for product recommendation that explicitly adopts a marketing -funnel perspective: the model
incorporates two latent layers to capture variability in consumers' interest shifts across funnel stages and includes identification
strategies that dynamically detect otherwise-invisible stage transitions and interest shifts, demonstrating superior performance
over baseline recommendation approaches on large -scale real-world data. This work establishes that DBN architectures with
multiple latent layers can r ecover psychologically meaningful, stage -structured representations of consumer journeys —
precisely the kind of representation a DBN -based attribution model needs in order to support counterfactual reasoning about
channel contributions at different funnel stages.
In e -commerce clickstream analytics, Ozyurt et al. 6 develop ClickstreamDMM, an attentive deep Markov model that
combines an attention mechanism for capturing long -term dependencies in clickstream sequences with a latent -variable model
representing different shopping phases (goal-directed search versus browsing), in order to predict the risk of session exit without
purchase. Related work by Hatt and Feuerriegel7 develops a duration-dependent hidden Markov model for detecting user exits,
explicitly addressing a limitation of standard HMMs: that the expected duration of a latent state does not depend on how long
the user has already spent in that state. Both lines of work demonstrate that incorporating richer temporal structure into la tent-
state models m eaningfully improves predictive performance over standard first -order HMMs in marketing -relevant tasks,
providing empirical motivation for relaxing the Markov assumption in the attribution setting as well.
Outside the marketing domain, De La Torre et al. 11 apply a DBN to model the dynamic relationship between user
engagement and physical-activity behavior over a year-long mobile-health intervention, illustrating that DBNs remain a viable
and interpretable tool for modeling longitudinal behavior -change dynamics at scale, including settings with intermittent and
irregularly timed interactions—conditions that closely resemble the irregular, asynchronous touchpoint sequences observed in
digital marketing journeys.
From a methodological standpoint, Shiguihara et al.12 provide a systematic survey of DBN modeling, learning, and
inference techniques, summarizing structure -learning, parameter -learning, and inference algorithms —including exact
inference, sampling -based methods, and approximate or variational approaches —across application domains drawn
predominantly from high -quality journals. Drury and Smith 13 further discuss DBN elicitation and structure specification in
settings where direct estimation from data must be complemented by expert input —a consideration likely to be relevant when
specifying the number and interpretation of latent engagement states in a marketing-attribution DBN, where ground-truth latent
states are, by definition, unobserved.

Beyond First-Order Markov: Non-Markovian and Long-Memory Sequence Representations
The defining limitation motivating this article is the first -order Markov assumption embedded in conventional Markov
chain attribution models, standard HMMs, and conventional DBNs alike: the next state —whether an observed touchpoint or a

latent behavioral state—is assumed to depend only on the current state, not on the broader history of prior states7. Several strands
of research have sought to relax this assumption from different directions. Duration -dependent and semi -Markov extensions
relax the assumption that state durations are memoryless, allowing latent states to become “sticky” and thereby implicitly
encoding a limited form of history -dependence6,7. Higher-order Markov chains extend the dependency window from one to k
previous states, at the cost of an exponentially growing state space.
The graphical point -process framework of Tao et al. 5 represents a further departure from first -order dependence: rather
than modeling transitions between adjacent states, it models the full relational structure—a directed graph of excitation effects—
among all touchpoint types observed across a path, so that a touchpoint occurring early in a journey can be modeled as directly
influencing the conversion intensity much later, independent of intervening touchpoints. This is, in effect, a non -Markovian
representation, but it operates at the level of observed even t types and their mutual excitation rather than via an explicit latent -
state layer.
The framework proposed in this article pursues long -memory representation through a third route: rather than extending
the dependency window of observed transitions (higher-order Markov chains) or modeling relational excitation among observed
event types (graphical point processes), it extends the dependency structure of the latent behavioral -state sequence itself,
allowing the latent state at time t to depend on the latent states at times t − 1 through t − p. This keeps the observation model—
touchpoint generation conditional on latent state —unchanged and computationally tractable, while concentrating the long -
memory, cumulative-effects structure in the latent layer, where, as Wei et al.10 and Ozyurt et al.6 demonstrate, it can be given a
substantively meaningful interpretation in terms of evolving customer engagement or intent.

Interpretability and Explainable AI in Marketing Analytics
As deep-learning and other black -box methods have entered marketing analytics, a substantial literature has developed
around explainable AI (XAI) and interpretable machine learning. Marcinkevics and Vogt8 provide a methods-centric overview
of interpretable and explainable machine learnin g, distinguishing inherently interpretable model classes from post -hoc
explanation methods applied to opaque models, and arguing that the inductive biases built into a model's structure substantially
shape what kinds of explanations are even possible. Ali et al. 9 similarly synthesize the state of XAI research and identify a
persistent gap between technically sophisticated explanation methods and the practical requirement for trustworthy, decision -
relevant explanations in applied domains.
For multi-touch attribution specifically, this distinction matters because the “explanation” a marketing manager needs —
how much a channel's budget should change, and why —is most naturally expressed in terms of a model's own internal
probabilistic structure (transition probab ilities, latent -state occupancy, emission probabilities) rather than via post -hoc
approximations of a deep network's decision boundary. DBNs belong to the class of inherently interpretable models in the
taxonomy of Marcinkevics and Vogt8: their parameters have direct probabilistic meaning, and counterfactual quantities such as
removal effects can be computed by directly manipulating the model's conditional probability tables rather than by
approximating gradients or surrogate models. This is the principal i nterpretability advantage motivating the proposed non -
Markovian DBN as an alternative to deep-learning attribution architectures such as that of Agrawal et al.4.

Customer Journey Complexity and the Omnichannel Context
The practical urgency of richer attr ibution models is reinforced by research on the increasing complexity of customer
journeys themselves. Gao et al.14 show that the way firms integrate channels shapes the customer experience across the journey,
with implications for how and how cumulatively exposures across channels affect downstream outcomes. Vollrath and Villegas1
argue that digital marketing analytics should be organized around the customer decision journey as a strategic framework rather
than around isolated channel -level metrics, cautio ning against an “analytics myopia” that optimizes individual touchpoints
without regard to their role in a longer journey. Mahajan  et al.15, in a systematic review of marketing analytics and consumer
behavior research, similarly identify the modeling of co mplex, multi -stage consumer journeys as an area where existing
analytical techniques remain underdeveloped relative to the complexity of the phenomena being studied.
Taken together, this body of work indicates that (a) customer journeys are becoming longer, more cross-channel, and more
cumulative in their effects; (b) existing attribution methods either assume away this cumulative structure (heuristics, Marko v

rather than latent -behavioral level (graphical point processes); and (c) DBN -based latent -state models  have already proven
effective at representing psychologically meaningful customer states in adjacent marketing application s, but typically retain a

The literature reviewed above suggests that attribution modeling approaches occupy different points along two dimensions
that matter for managerial adoption: interpretability and temporal/relational depth. Heuristic and Markov -chain models sit at

limited temporal depth.
The gap addressed in this study is the absence, within the marketing -attribution literature, of a framework that retains the
explicit, axiomatical ly transparent probabilistic structure of DBN -based latent -state models (Wei et al., 2023; Özyurt et al.,
2022) while extending their temporal depth to represent the cumulative and delayed effects documented in customer journey

architecture linking touchpoints to latent engagement/intent states, (2) a non-Markovian, p-th-order extension of the latent-state
dynamics, and (3) an interp retable counterfactual attribution mechanism operating on this extended latent structure —are
designed to occupy this gap directly, positioning the proposed model at high interpretability and high temporal depth

latent dynamics. Model selection for the order 𝑝and the number of latent states 𝐾can be performed via held-out log-likelihood
or information criteria, ideally combined with substantive input on the expected number and meaning of engagement states , in
the spirit of the DBN elicitation13.

Channel-level attribution is defined as the change in expected conversion probability that results from a counterfactual
intervention on channel 𝑚's presence in the journey. Formally, for channel 𝑚we define the removal effect:

4. Experimental Design
Data Sources
Two complementary data sources are proposed.  First, a real-world, anonymized multi-channel customer-journey dataset
(e.g., from an e -commerce or retail setting) consisting of timestamped touchpoint sequences across the channels under study,
together with a binary conversion label for each journey, w ill be used to evaluate predictive fit and the qualitative plausibility
of estimated attribution patterns. Second, synthetic non -Markovian simulations —generated from a known p -th-order latent -
state process with pre -specified, controllable channel effects —will be used to construct ground -truth attribution weights. As
noted in Section I, real customer-journey data offers no ground truth for “correct” attribution; synthetic simulation is therefore
essential for directly evaluating whether a model recovers know n channel contributions, particularly contributions that arise
only through higher-order, cumulative effects that a first-order model cannot, by construction, represent.

Baseline Models
The proposed non -Markovian DBN will be compared against the following  baselines, spanning the full range of
approaches reviewed in Section II: (1) heuristic models —first-touch, last-touch, and linear attribution; (2) first -order Markov
chain attribution with removal effects 2; (3) higher -order Markov chain attribution, as a non-latent-variable alternative for
capturing extended dependencies; (4) Shapley -value / game -theoretic attribution 3; (5) a deep -learning sequence model in the
spirit of the TCN -based architecture of Agrawal et al. 4; (6) a standard first -order DBN / HMM wi th a single latent layer,
representing the conventional latent-state baseline6,10; and (7), where feasible, the graphical point-process attribution framework
of Tao et al. (2025)5 as the closest recent non-Markovian alternative.

Evaluation Metrics
Three f amilies of metrics are proposed. First, attribution accuracy will be evaluated against the known channel -level
contributions in the synthetic non-Markovian simulations, using the absolute and relative deviation between estimated and true
removal effects. S econd, classification -style metrics —precision, recall, and F1 -score—will be computed for the task of
identifying “high -impact” touchpoints or channels (defined, for example, as those whose true or estimated removal effect
exceeds a threshold), allowing comparison across models on a common discriminative task even where exact attribution values
differ in scale. Third, probabilistic goodness -of-fit will be assessed on the real -world dataset via held -out log-likelihood (or
perplexity) of the conversion outcome  and, where applicable, Kullback -Leibler divergence between model -implied and
empirical distributions of journey outcomes. Computational cost (training and inference wall-clock time, and memory footprint
as a function of journey length, K, and p) will be r eported as a secondary consideration informing the practical trade -offs
summarized in Table 1.

6. Expected Contributions and Positioning Relative to Baselines
Based on the theoretical mechanism described in Sections III and V and the empirical patterns documented in the reviewed
literature, several expectations can be specified for the full empirical evaluation described in Section IV. First, on synthetic non-
Markovian journeys, the proposed model is expected to recover channel -level attribution weights more accurately than first -
order Markov chain, Shapley -value, and standard first -order DBN/HMM baselines, particularly for channels whose true
contribution depends on repeated or delayed exposure —mirroring the mechanism illustrated in Se ction V and consistent with
the documented benefits of duration -dependent and higher -order latent -state models over first -order HMMs in related
clickstream and engagement-prediction6,7,11.
Second, relative to deep-learning attribution models such as the TC N-based architecture of Agrawal et al. 4, the proposed
model is expected to be competitive in predictive fit on real -world data while offering attribution values that are directly
traceable to interpretable transition and emission probabilities, consistent with the interpretability -by-design pro perties
associated with probabilistic graphical models in the taxonomy of Marcinkevičs and Vogt8 and the broader call for trustworthy,
decision-relevant explanations in Ali et al.9.
Third, relative to the graphical point -process framework of Tao et al. 5 the closest recent non-Markovian alternative—the
proposed model is expected to offer a complementary rather than strictly superior representation: where the graphical point -
process approach excels at representing relational excitation among many observed tou chpoint types, the proposed DBN is
expected to offer a more compact representation when the underlying driver of long-memory effects is plausibly a small number
of latent engagement or intent states10, at potentially lower computational cost for journeys with a moderate number of channels.
These expectations are framed as hypotheses to be tested in the full empirical evaluation; they are not presented as establis hed
findings.

7. Managerial Implications
If the expectations outlined in Section VI are borne out empirically, the proposed framework would offer marketing
managers three practical capabilities beyond those provided by existing attribution tools. First, because attribution values are
derived from explicit transition and emission probabilities associated with interpretable latent engagement states, managers can
examine not only how much credit a channel receives, but through which latent -state pathway —for example, whether a
channel's contribution operates primarily by moving customers from “unaware” to “considering,” or by reinforcing an already-
high-intent state—supporting more targeted budget reallocation decisions than an aggregate attribution score alone 1.

Second, the non -Markovian extension directly addresses a recurring practical concern in omnichan nel marketing: that
channels which build engagement cumulatively over multiple exposures (such as email nurture sequences) may be
systematically undervalued by attribution models that consider only the most recent touchpoint or a single preceding state14. By

provides a basis for more accurately valuing such cumulative-effect channels in budget-allocation decisions.
Third, the la tent-state representation offers a natural basis for designing customer engagement strategies that are
conditioned on inferred intent state —for example, by identifying which channel -sequence patterns are most associated with
transitions into high -intent st ates—supporting the kind of journey -level, rather than touchpoint -level, strategic planning

8. Limitations and Future Research
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the computational comp lexity of the proposed non -Markovian DBN
increases with the number of latent states 𝐾, dependency order 𝑝, and journey length, potentially requiring approximate
inference methods such as variational inference or particle filtering. Second, the interpretation and stability of unobserved latent
states require careful validation and appropriate selection of 𝐾and 𝑝. Third, the framework has not yet been validated across
diverse real -world industries, customer segments, or long customer journeys. Future rese arch should therefore evaluate the
model using larger real -world datasets, compare its attribution and predictive performance with established alternatives, and
investigate scalable hybrid approaches that combine latent-state dynamics with graphical point-process representations.

9. Conclusion
This study proposes a non -Markovian Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) framework for multi -touch attribution that
extends conventional first-order latent-state models to a 𝑝-th-order dependency structure. The key novelty i s the integration of
higher-order latent behavioral dynamics with counterfactual channel -level attribution, enabling earlier marketing exposures to
influence subsequent latent states and conversion through delayed and cumulative pathways. Unlike convention al first-order
Markov and DBN models, the proposed framework does not restrict the influence of historical exposures to the immediately
preceding latent state.
Channel attribution is performed through counterfactual removal effects, allowing the contribution of individual channels
to be evaluated through changes in expected conversion probability after removing their exposures. This provides an
interpretable probabilistic mechanism for capturing cumulative effects while preserving the transparency of laten t-state
transition and emission structures. The illustrative simulation demonstrates the proposed mechanism. The Email removal effect
increases from 0.04207 under the first-order model to 0.04863 under the second-order model, representing an approximately
15.6% increase. Although this experiment is illustrative rather than empirical, it demonstrates how higher -order latent
dependencies can capture repeated-exposure effects that are not representable under a first-order formulation.
Overall, the proposed framework provides a novel balance between temporal depth, latent behavioral representation, and
interpretability in multi -touch attribution. Its practical effectiveness, however, requires systematic validation using synthetic
datasets with known attribution ground truth and real-world customer-journey data. Future research should evaluate attribution
accuracy, predictive performance, computational scalability, and generalizability across industries and customer segments.
