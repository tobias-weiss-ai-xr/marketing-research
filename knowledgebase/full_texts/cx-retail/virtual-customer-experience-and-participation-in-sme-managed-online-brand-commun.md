Abstract
Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) increasingly use online brand communities to build
customer relationships, support entrepreneurial marketing activities, and facilitate customer value co-
creation. Yet, mechanisms driving customer participation in SME-managed online communities remain
underexplored. By integrating the Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) theory with behavioral
learning theory, this study examines how virtual customer experience shapes participation through
customer engagement and how online shopping frequency explains participation heterogeneity. Data
from 470 active Indonesian online shoppers involved in SME brand communities were analyzed using
partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM). The ﬁndings show that virtual customer
experience strengthens customer engagement, which serves as the primary affective mechanism linking
virtual experience to participatory behavior. The results also indicate that customers with higher online
shopping frequency report more favorable virtual interaction experiences and are more likely to
participate actively in SME-managed online brand communities. Theoretically, this study contributes to
SME management and entrepreneurial marketing research by positioning virtual customer experience as
a strategic interaction capability through which SMEs can mobilize customer participation. Also, it
extends S–O–R theory by incorporating behavioral learning theory to explain customer heterogeneity in
online customer participation. Practically, this study suggests that SMEs should design meaningful
virtual experiences, foster engagement-oriented interactions, and tailor community strategies to
customers with different levels of online shopping experience.

Introduction
Customer participation in online brand communities has become central to SMEs’ ability to cultivate
customer relationships, support entrepreneurial marketing activities, and facilitate customer value co-
creation in digital markets. Compared with large corporations, SMEs often operate under substantial
resource constraints, which can limit their capacity to build and sustain competitive advantage (Hardi et
al., 2025; Lu et al., 2024; Suryani et al., 2024). Against this backdrop, active participation in SME-
managed online brand communities—including customer reviews, electronic word of mouth (e-WOM),
and other engagement behaviors (Yoo et al., 2013)—is valuable not only because it can encourage
product purchases but also because it supports broader value-creating activities that strengthen SME
competitiveness and market presence (Chan et al., 2010). Simultaneously, by mobilizing customer
participation, SMEs can reduce budgetary pressure, improve the eﬃciency of promotional and marketing
expenditures, and accelerate innovation and product development at lower cost (Chang & Taylor, 2016;
Obal et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2019). Moreover, customer participation can help SMEs align resource-
driven capabilities with market-oriented insights in developing products and services, thereby enhancing
business performance (Menguc & Auh, 2006; Ngo & O’Cass, 2012; Saeed et al., 2015).

However, the key drivers of customer participation in SME-managed online brand communities remain
underexamined in several important ways. First, although prior studies have emphasized the role of
digital platforms in fostering customer participation among SMEs in emerging markets (Mohamad et al.,
2022; Osorno-Hinojosa et al., 2023), they have largely overlooked the virtual experiences of customers
when interacting with SMEs on these platforms. In this sense, digital platforms are primarily channels.
The quality of the interaction and whether it creates a meaningful experience for customers is what
matters more (Suryani et al., 2021). As a result, earlier ﬁndings may not fully explain the underlying
mechanisms and, therefore, require additional empirical validation. To address this gap, this study
examines the relationship between virtual experiences and consumer participation in digital spaces.
Second, the extant literature often treats consumer engagement and consumer participation
interchangeably. However, while the two constructs are closely related, they reﬂect distinct concepts
(Dong & Sivakumar, 2017). Accordingly, additional empirical research is needed to delineate their
conceptual boundaries. Grounded in the S–O–R theory (Mehrabian & Russell, 1974), consumer
engagement and participation are best understood as different types of responses. Consumer
engagement is typically conceptualized as an internal psychological state or process within the
consumer (Bai & Lai, 2025; Herzallah et al., 2025; Ul Islam & Rahman, 2017; Zahran & Aljuhmani, 2025).
In contrast, consumer participation is commonly modeled as an observable behavioral response (Zhang
et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2015). This perspective suggests that engagement is a key element within the
nomological network that causes participatory behavior. More broadly, engagement may function as a
mediator that transmits the effect of external stimuli to subsequent behavior. Therefore, this study re-
examines and re-validates the mediating role of consumer engagement in translating virtual customer
experience into consumer participation behavior to clarify how these constructs function in shaping
consumer participation in SME-managed online brand communities.
Third, prior research on brand communities has rarely explicitly examined context-speciﬁc conditional
factors. However, online consumer behavior literature suggests that purchase frequency (frequent vs.
infrequent buyers) and prior e-commerce experience can lead to meaningful differences in attitudes and
behaviors in online shopping contexts (Gulfraz et al., 2022; Hernández et al., 2010; Martin et al., 2015;
Mofokeng, 2021). Drawing on behavioral learning theory (Rothschild & Gaidis, 1981), more frequent
online shopping helps consumers develop a stronger understanding of the experiential cues they
encounter during shopping. Over time, this learning can produce more habitual behavioral responses,
including repeat purchases, future purchase intentions, and other related activities (Ahmad et al., 2022;
Liu et al., 2020). On this basis, the present study conceptualizes online shopping frequency as a
conditional factor that can strengthen (or weaken) the effect of virtual customer experience on SME
brand community participation. In doing so, the study addresses an important gap by emphasizing
experience-based heterogeneity associated with conditional factors that have received limited attention
in prior models of brand community participation.
Building on its three research objectives, this study is expected to make three contributions to the
literature on SME management, entrepreneurial marketing, and online brand communities. First, this
Page 3/34

study promotes a novel perspective moving beyond viewing digital media simply as a communication
channel and instead conceptualizes virtual customer experience as a strategic interaction capability that
enables SMEs to foster participation and customer value co-creation. Second, this study clariﬁes
consumer engagement’s role as an expression of consumers’ affective responses within the
nomological network of participation behavior and delineates conceptual boundaries that have often
overlapped in prior research. Third, this study extends the S–O–R framework by integrating behavioral
learning theory to account for differences among customers. Speciﬁcally, online shopping frequency
represents accumulated digital marketplace experience that shapes how customers respond to virtual
interactions and how likely they are to participate in SME-managed online brand communities. From a
practical perspective, these ﬁndings provide actionable implications for SMEs seeking to design more
effective strategies to strengthen participation in their managed online brand communities.
Literature Review
Theoretical Orchestration: Stimulus – Organism – Response (S-O-R) and Behavioral Learning Theory
A perspective that integrates multiple theories is needed given the complexity of consumer behavior in
online SME brand communities (Calder et al., 2023; Rahman, 2025). Accordingly, this study used the S–
O–R theory as its primary framework (Mehrabian & Russell, 1974) and supplemented it with behavioral
learning theory (Rothschild & Gaidis, 1981). Although the S-O-R theory is well established and widely
applied to explain related phenomena (e.g., Burnasheva et al., 2019; Carlson et al., 2021; Kuo & Chen,
2023; Naqvi et al., 2021; Ul Islam & Rahman, 2017), it does not fully explain the customer participation
behavior examined in this research. In particular, the S–O–R theory provides limited guidance on the
boundary conditions under which heterogeneity in the organism component shapes consumer
participation. Therefore, incorporating behavioral learning theory strengthens the explanation by
clarifying how experience and reinforcement from repeated online purchasing help form stable patterns
of behavioral responses.
Figure 1 presents the model we propose to predict consumer participation in SMEs’ brand online
communities by integrating two previously discussed theoretical perspectives. According to the S–O–R
theory, environmental stimuli shape individuals’ internal states—particularly cognitive, emotional, and
affective processes (Bai & Lai, 2025)—which then lead to behavioral responses (Mehrabian & Russell,
1974). Consistent with this logic, we conceptualize the virtual experience of consumers as an
environmental stimulus that arises when individuals transact with SME brands through digital platforms.
This stimulus inﬂuences the internal states of consumers, which we represent as affective consumer
engagement. In turn, this engagement motivates behavioral responses expressed as participation in
online communities for SME brands.
Behavioral learning theory further suggests that behavior is formed through environmental inﬂuences
(stimuli) (Haleblian & Finkelstein, 1999), particularly through repeated exposure and reinforcement that
gradually produces stable response patterns (Rothschild & Gaidis, 1981). Successful past transactions
Page 4/34

serve as reinforcers that increase consumers’ tendency to respond favorably to online shopping stimuli
in online retail settings. For this reason, the frequency of online shopping indicates the extent to which
this learned behavioral repertoire has developed. Consumers who shop online more often accumulate
more experience and are more likely to develop stronger, more automatic approach responses to digital
environments. Accordingly, consumers with extensive online shopping experience who encounter
positive virtual experiences within an SME brand ecosystem are more likely to convert those experiences
into active participation, including information sharing, interaction, and co-creation. In contrast,
consumers with limited online shopping experience tend to show weaker responses to the same
stimulus, reducing the strength of the relationship between virtual experience and participation. Overall,
behavioral learning theory provides a strong conceptual rationale for treating online shopping experience
(as reﬂected in online shopping frequency) as a moderating variable that conditions how strongly virtual
consumer experiences translate into participation in SMEs’ brand online communities.
Hypotheses Development
Virtual Customer Experience and Online Participation
According to Xu et al. (2014), the S–O–R theory operates through two pathways. First, stimuli indirectly
inﬂuence behavioral responses by shaping the internal states of the organism, including cognitive
evaluations and affective reactions. Second, stimuli can also exert a direct effect on behavioral
responses without being fully mediated by these internal processes. Consistent with this logic, prior
research has shown that consumer experiences in virtual environments can be important determinants
of consumer behavior (Rose et al., 2012; McLean & Wilson, 2016). Virtual customer experience can be
conceptualized as a primary stimulus in digital contexts. It includes connectivity, interactivity, channel
integration, message consistency, ﬂexibility, and personalization (Salem & Alanadoly, 2024). When
consumers perceive a virtual experience positively, both affective and cognitive responses can be
triggered, which in turn encourage more active behavior. In brand communities and digital SME
platforms, this behavioral response is reﬂected in online customer participation, such as sharing content,
posting reviews, interacting with the brand, and contributing to co-creation activities (Liu et al., 2024;
Taheri et al., 2017). Previous studies have also suggested that enjoyable and value-creating digital
experiences increase consumers’ willingness to engage on a brand’s online platform (Dwivedi et al.,
2021; Rose et al., 2012). Consumers are more likely to respond through voluntary participatory behaviors
when the experience feels relevant and meaningful. Therefore, as depicted in Fig. 1, we hypothesize the
following:
H1. The virtual customer experience positively stimulates online customer participation.
Virtual Customer Experience and Engagement
Based on the S–O–R theory, we conceptualize customer engagement as an internal state (organism)
that arises in response to external stimuli. Customer engagement is a multidimensional psychological
Page 5/34

state that reﬂects the cognitive, affective, and behavioral involvement of consumers with a brand (Brodie
et al., 2011; Hollebeek et al., 2014). In this study, virtual customer experience serves as a key stimulus
that strengthens consumers’ psychological attachment through immersive, personalized, and consistent
digital interactions across multiple touchpoints. Empirical research further suggests that positive virtual
experiences not only improve functional satisfaction but also build stronger emotional bonds and
encourage deeper brand-related consumer attention and cognitive processing (Salem & Alanadoly, 2024;
Sung et al., 2021). Accordingly, based on the S–O–R logic and supported by prior empirical evidence, we
posit the following:
H2. The virtual customer experience positively stimulates customer engagement.
Customer Engagement and Online Participation
Customer engagement serves as a psychological mechanism in the organism component, linking
external stimuli to behavioral responses, within the S–O–R theory. Consumers with higher engagement
levels are more likely to act proactively and participate in a brand’s digital ecosystem (Pansari & Kumar,
2017; Vivek et al., 2012). Engagement also strengthens intrinsic motivation to voluntarily contribute,
such as by sharing experiences, providing feedback, and participating in online community activities. In
this setting, online customer participation represents a behavioral response that stems from strong
psychological attachment to the brand (Taheri et al., 2017; Jaakkola & Alexander, 2014). Prior research
consistently shows that highly engaged consumers are not only more loyal but also more active in co-
creating value through online participation (Hoyer et al., 2010; Laud & Karpen, 2017). Accordingly, in line
with the S–O–R theory and supported by prior empirical evidence, we hypothesize the following:
H3. Customer engagement positively drives online customer participation.
The Mediation Role of Customer Engagement
According to the S–O–R theory, customers’ digital experiences act as stimuli that shape internal
psychological states and subsequently lead to behavioral responses. In digital SMEs, virtual customer
experience—as reﬂected in interactivity, social presence, and interface quality—constitutes a key
stimulus that inﬂuences the quality of customers’ digital interactions with the brand (McLean & Wilson,
2016; Rose et al., 2012). Then, these stimuli affect the organism component, namely, customer
engagement. Customer engagement is a multidimensional psychological state that includes customers’
cognitive, emotional, and behavioral involvement with a brand (Brodie et al., 2011; Hollebeek et al., 2014).
Research across digital retail, tourism, ﬁnancial services, and platform-based SMEs consistently shows
that customer engagement is an internal mechanism that converts digital experiences into value-adding
behaviors (Dwivedi et al., 2021; Pansari & Kumar, 2017; Vivek et al., 2012). Customers are more likely to
participate online by sharing content, providing feedback, and engaging in co-creation activities as
engagement increases (Jaakkola & Alexander, 2014; Hoyer et al., 2010). Therefore, the inﬂuence of
Page 6/34

virtual customer experience on online customer participation may be indirect. Speciﬁcally, higher
customer engagement may occur as a mediating mechanism. Based on this argument, we hypothesize
the following:
H4. Customer engagement mediates the positive effect of virtual customer experience on online
customer participation.
Moderation Role of the Online Shopping Experience
This study conceptualizes online shopping frequency as a reinforcement mechanism that strengthens
consumers’ cognitive and behavioral responses through repeated learning (Rothschild & Gaidis, 1981;
McLean et al., 2020). In the digital SME ecosystem, higher shopping frequency signals both habituation
and greater experience maturity, enabling customers to process and interpret digital stimuli more
eﬃciently (Tran, 2021; Rose et al., 2012). As customers gain experience, cognitive barriers to
internalizing these stimuli tend to decline. Consequently, the translation of virtual customer experience
into customer engagement and online customer participation becomes stronger (Brodie et al., 2011;
Dwivedi et al., 2021). Accumulated experience may also act as a catalyst that accelerates the conversion
of internal psychological states—particularly engagement—into observable behavioral contributions,
such as co-creation activities and the voluntary posting of reviews (Pansari & Kumar, 2017). In contrast,
customers with lower frequency of shopping are more likely to remain at the level of functional
satisfaction and may not progress toward stronger engagement and more proactive participation
(Jaakkola & Alexander, 2014). As such, we hypothesize that
H5a-c. Online shopping frequency strengthens the positive effect of (a) virtual customer experience on
online customer participation, (b) virtual customer experience on customer engagement, and (c)
customer engagement on online customer participation.
Method
Instrument Development
The research instrument was developed by drawing on established measures from previous studies.
Measures of virtual customer experience and customer engagement were adapted from Salem and
Alanadoly (2024), while measures of customer participation were adapted from Taheri et al. (2017). The
original instrument was translated into Indonesian using a back-translation procedure to ensure
accuracy and semantic equivalence (Mohatlane, 2014; Yu et al., 2004). To ensure that the instrument ﬁts
the current research context, we conducted a comprehensive validation through a focus group
discussion (FGD) with 20 participants, including academics in marketing and information technology,
business practitioners, and consumers. The instrument (see Appendix 1) was then pilot-tested with 40
representative respondents in Surabaya to conﬁrm that all questionnaire items were clear and easy to
understand (Hulland et al., 2018).

Control Variable
We included three control variables: gender, age, and educational attainment to strengthen internal
validity and support more robust causal inference in this non-experimental study (Atinc & Simmering,
2021; Spector & Brannick, 2011; York, 2018). Previous research suggests that differences in

reduces the likelihood that the observed relationships simply reﬂect differences in the characteristics of
the respondents.

Population and sample size
The study population consisted of Indonesian consumers who had purchased products online from local
brands—typically operated by SMEs—and who had visited online communities managed by those brands
or companies. We used a non-probability sampling strategy, speciﬁcally purposive sampling because the
population size was unknown (Memon et al., 2024). Data were collected in Jakarta and Surabaya, two
metropolitan cities on the island of Java, based on two considerations: economic conditions and the
digital environment. Both cities are major drivers of Java’s regional economy and substantial
contributors to Indonesia’s gross domestic product (GDP), indicating high levels of economic activity
(BPS-Statistics Indonesia, 2025a; BPS-Statistics Indonesia, 2025b). Digitally, both cities are located in
regions with high Internet penetration (Indonesian Internet Service Providers Association (APJII), 2025)
and have digital competitiveness indices above the national average (East Ventures, 2025). The
minimum required sample size was determined using an a priori calculator for SEM, assuming an
anticipated effect size of 0.5, statistical power of 0.9, and a signiﬁcance level (α) of 0.05 (Soper, 2025;
Westland, 2012). The resulting minimum sample size was 347.
Data collection procedure
Data were collected with the support of trained enumerators who administered the questionnaire
through two modes: online and in person. For the online mode, the questionnaire link was shared with
the target respondents through digital channels, primarily WhatsApp, Instagram, and email. For the in-
person mode, enumerators distributed paper questionnaires in public areas—such as shopping malls
and city parks—that commonly serve as sites for community activities and gatherings. Data collection
was conducted from July to September 2025 and yielded 498 responses. Because responses were
gathered through both modes, some responses contained missing values. Missing data were addressed
using series mean imputation (Collier, 2020; Eekhout et al., 2014; Guenther et al., 2023; Schafer &
Graham, 2002). We then screened for outliers using the Mahalanobis distance to protect data quality
from potential straight-lining or bot-generated responses (Dupuis et al., 2019). This procedure identiﬁed
28 outliers, resulting in a ﬁnal analytic sample of 470 responses. The characteristics of the respondents
are summarized in Table 2.

Results and Analysis
Common method bias (CMB)
The results of the zero-order partial correlation (see Appendix 2) show that controlling for the two
marker variables—fashion consciousness and attitude toward the color blue—did not change the
magnitude or direction of the primary correlations in the research model. This pattern indicates that the
relationships among the constructs remain stable after accounting for the effects of both marker
variables. Therefore, these ﬁndings support the integrity of the data and strengthen the conclusion that
the study does not show evidence of CMB (Lindell & Whitney, 2001; Simmering et al., 2015).

Assessment of normality data
As shown in Appendix 3, Mardia’s test of multivariate normality (Mardia, 1970), computed using the
WebPower calculator (Cain et al., 2017), showed signiﬁcant skewness (β = 242.84, z = 19030.60, p = .00)
and kurtosis (β = 1425.66, z = 115.42, p = .00). This result indicates that the data do not meet the
multivariate normality assumption. However, the deviations from normality are within acceptable limits
because the univariate skewness values are below 2 and the univariate kurtosis values are below 7
(West et al., 1995). Therefore, the data are still suitable for drawing conclusions (Guenther et al., 2023).
This evidence also supports the use of PLS-SEM for model estimation, as the method is robust and can
accommodate non-normally distributed data (Hair et al., 2019).
Measurement model evaluation
Table 4 shows that all indicator loadings exceeded the recommended minimum thresholds (> 0.708),
indicating the reliability of the measurement items. Meanwhile, one of the three constructs—virtual
customer experiences (VCE)—has a composite reliability value above the recommended upper threshold
of .95. This result indicates that the VCE construct has a strong internal consistency. However, such a
high value may also suggest multicollinearity or excessive overlap among the measurement items. This
concern is consistent with the VIF values observed for several VCE items. However, because none of the
VIF values for the VCE items exceed the commonly used cutoff of 5, multicollinearity is unlikely to be a
substantive problem in this study (Guenther et al., 2023; Hair et al., 2019; Kock, 2017; Kock & Lynn, 2012).
Therefore, the reliability of the construct can be regarded as adequately maintained. Moreover, AVE
values above .50 provide evidence of adequate convergent validity for each construct. Table 5 also
reports HTMT values below .85 for all construct pairs, which supports discriminant validity. Overall, these
results indicate that the study’s measurement model demonstrates satisfactory reliability and validity.

= .36, t = 5.16), supporting H2. In turn, CET has a strong positive effect on CPN (β = .73, t = 6.17),
conﬁrming H3. The results also show a signiﬁcant indirect effect of VCE on CPN through CET (β = .26, t 
= 3.86, 95% CI [.14, .41]), supporting H4. The direct VCE →  CPN path is not signiﬁcant, whereas the VCE
→  CET and CET →  CPN paths are signiﬁcant. Thus, CET fully mediates the relationship between VCE and
CPN.
Although the direct effect of VCE on CPN is not supported, the moderation analysis indicates that the
VCE × OSF interaction is marginally signiﬁcant (β = .08, t = 1.80), supporting H5a. This result suggests
that OSF functions as a boundary condition that shapes the extent to which VCE promotes CPN.
Consistent with this interpretation, the conditional effects in Table 6 show that the effect of VCE on CPN
is weak when OSF is low (β = .01, t = 0.15). However, at higher levels of OSF, the effect becomes stronger
and statistically signiﬁcant (β = .10, t = 2.30). As illustrated in Fig. 2, among customers with high OSF, a
stronger VCE is associated with a higher CPN. In contrast, among customers with low OSF, VCE is not
positively related to CPN; participation is even slightly higher when VCE is low. Overall, these ﬁndings

Robustness Checks
The study’s ﬁndings’ credibility was assessed using several robustness checks, including tests of data
normality, potential nonlinearity, endogeneity, and unobserved heterogeneity (Sarstedt, Ringle, et al.,
2020; Vaithilingam et al., 2024). First, the Mardia test reported in the previous section indicates that the
data are not normally distributed. However, the deviation from normality remains within acceptable
limits, suggesting that the data are suitable for the analyses conducted in this study. Second, the
quadratic effect test (Table 7) indicates that none of the hypothesized nonlinear relationships are
signiﬁcant (p > .05). This result supports the linearity assumption for the relationships among constructs
and provides empirical justiﬁcation for retaining the linear model speciﬁcation. Third, the Gaussian
copula approach (Eckert & Hohberger, 2023), applied across seven model combinations (Table 8), points
to potential endogeneity that could reduce estimation accuracy. This concern is most evident for the
VCE →  CPN path (β = .29, p = .00) in three speciﬁcations (Models 3, 5, and 7). Fourth, unobserved
heterogeneity was examined using FIMIX-PLS with one- to six-segment solutions (Hair, Jr. et al., 2016l;
Matthews et al., 2016). This procedure accounted for the sample size (n = 470) and the minimum
recommended sample per segment (77 respondents) (Memon et al., 2020). As shown in Tables 9 and
10, the joint evaluation using AIC3–CAIC, AIC3–BIC, and AIC4–BIC does not indicate a single, stable
segmentation solution. This pattern suggests that unobserved heterogeneity is unlikely to be a major
threat to the ﬁndings’ credibility. Overall, although the analyses ﬂag potential endogeneity for one
speciﬁc path, the results remain credible because the other diagnostics indicate (1) acceptable non-
normality, (2) no meaningful evidence of nonlinearity, and (3) no substantive indication of unobserved
heterogeneity.

Discussion
Page 20/34

This study evaluates the proposed consumer participation model using data from 470 consumers in
Indonesia and identiﬁes several important insights into how consumer participation develops in SME-
managed online communities. Consistent with the S–O–R framework that underpins the model, the
ﬁndings show that virtual consumer experiences act as a key stimulus shaping consumers’ affective
states, primarily by fostering strong psychological engagement. This result extends prior studies that
have suggested that simply using digital platforms is suﬃcient to encourage participation (Mohamad et
al., 2022; Osorno-Hinojosa et al., 2023). In contrast, the present study indicates that a digital presence
alone is no longer enough unless SMEs also manage these platforms in ways that create meaningful
and memorable experiences. Therefore, SMEs should not only maintain a presence on social media and
other online channels but also strategically use these platforms to build deeper consumer experiences
(Sung et al., 2021).
Furthermore, this study provides additional support for the S–O–R model. The results show that
consumers’ affective engagement is the primary mechanism through which perceived virtual
experiences (the stimulus) are translated into actual consumer participation behaviors (the response).
This ﬁnding contrasts with earlier studies that reported a direct effect of virtual experiences on
consumer behavior (McLean & Wilson, 2016; Rose et al., 2012). At the same time, it highlights the
importance of psychological processes in explaining why consumers engage in voluntary participation.
Speciﬁcally, when consumers develop a strong attachment to a ﬁrm’s brand or products, they are more
likely to enthusiastically participate in a company’s products (“enthused participation”). This
participation is reﬂected in greater engagement across digital channels. For example, consumers may
spend more time on social media, visit the platform more frequently, build social connections in virtual
spaces, and make greater efforts to understand the SME’s platform or applications. In turn, these
behaviors encourage information seeking, information sharing, and positive interactions with the ﬁrm
(Taheri et al., 2017).
This study also suggests that consumers differ in their engagement in participation behaviors. Although
the analysis did not conﬁrm a direct relationship between virtual consumer experience and consumer
participation behavior, an important boundary condition was identiﬁed. Participation becomes stronger
when consumers have a high frequency of purchasing. The behavioral learning theory helps explain this
pattern. As consumers shop online more often, they have more opportunities to interact with the brand
and experience reinforcement from those interactions. More frequent purchases increase the likelihood
of positive reinforcement, which, in turn, strengthens consumers’ participation motivation, including their
intention to reengage with the brand. Although the moderating effect of online purchasing frequency is
modest, this result indicates that a single theoretical lens cannot adequately explain consumer
participation. Therefore, a multi-theory approach is more appropriate, as demonstrated in this study
through the integration of the S–O–R model and BL theory. This integration highlights the need to
account for heterogeneity at the organism level when predicting behavior. Researchers can produce
more precise insights and help ﬁrms develop more accurate strategies based on consumer
segmentation.

Theoretical Implication
This study advances knowledge of SME management, entrepreneurial marketing, and online brand
communities in three ways. First, prior research has often treated the use of digital platforms as the
primary stimulus for encouraging customer participation (Mohamad et al., 2022; Osorno-Hinojosa et al.,
2023). This study extends that view by showing that a central stimulus is meaningful customer
experiences within virtual environments. These meaningful virtual experiences shape customer
engagement as an affective response, which leads to participation. Second, although prior literature
suggests that customer engagement and customer participation overlap conceptually (Dong &
Sivakumar, 2017), this study uses the S–O–R framework to clearly distinguish between them. The
ﬁndings indicate that customer engagement is a key element in the nomological network that helps
explain customer participation. In this model, engagement represents a psychological state (organism),
whereas participation represents an observable behavioral response (response). This distinction
strengthens the applicability of the S–O–R theory for explaining participation in online brand
communities. Third, while S–O–R theory is effective for explaining how customer participation emerges,
this study improves the framework by integrating behavioral learning theory. This integration provides a
more comprehensive account of consumer heterogeneity within the organism component (internal
psychological states), which the foundational S–O–R framework does not fully capture. Overall, the
study highlights the value of integrative theorizing in S–O–R-based research to explain the variation in
consumers’ internal states and how those differences translate into behavioral responses (Calder et al.,
2023; Lim, 2026).
Practical Implication
This study offers two practical recommendations for SMEs aiming to increase customer participation in
their managed online communities. First, SMEs should focus on designing and managing digital stimuli
and aligning them with other marketing mix elements. This alignment ensures a consistent and
memorable virtual customer experience. Such experiences matter because they can strengthen
customers’ affective responses, particularly customer engagement, which can in turn increase voluntary
participation in SME-managed online communities. To do so, SMEs should prioritize ﬁve attributes:
connectivity, integration, consistency, ﬂexibility, and personalization (Salem & Alanadoly, 2024).
Connectivity and cross-platform integration should enable customers to access the community quickly
and easily, thereby promoting a positive experience. Consistency extends beyond messaging and
includes consistency in promotional communications, customer responsiveness, product quality, and
service performance. SMEs should also provide ﬂexibility, especially in terms of ordering processes,
channel selection, and delivery or fulﬁllment options. Finally, SMEs can enhance personalization by
tailoring information to customers’ needs, such as through product recommendations, rewards,
appreciation expressions, or other relevant content.
Second, SMEs should design strategies to encourage customer participation using behavioral
segmentation, with particular attention to online shopping activity. A frequency-based approach is
Page 22/34

important because more frequent online purchasing provides ﬁrms with more opportunities to reinforce
positive experiences during virtual interactions. For customers who shop online infrequently, SMEs
should use tactics that directly encourage higher purchase frequency. These tactics may include
discounts, product bundles, and other promotions intended to reduce purchase barriers and make
transactions more appealing. In contrast, SMEs can use purchase history data to combine loyalty
programs with personalization for customers who shop online frequently. Loyalty or reward programs
seek to strengthen loyalty by offering structured incentives based on customers’ prior purchasing
behavior (Ebrahimi et al., 2024). Customers who purchase more often should receive greater recognition
than those who do not. However, these initiatives should be designed not only to express appreciation
but also to motivate future purchases, thereby improving ﬁrm proﬁtability. This approach is justiﬁed
because retaining existing customers typically costs less than acquiring new ones. Additionally, prior
research indicates that loyalty programs can increase customer retention and satisfaction (Fook &
Dastane, 2021).
Limitations and Future Research Directions
Despite the important insights generated by this study, several limitations should be acknowledged, and
these limitations point to clear directions for future research. First, the ﬁndings should be generalized
with caution. This study examines consumer participation models only in online communities managed
by SMEs in an emerging market context (Indonesia), using a sample drawn from urban areas. Because
Indonesia is geographically extensive and consumer behavior and preferences can differ between urban
and non-urban groups (Fauzi & Sheng, 2021; Schütte & Ciarlante, 1998), future research should examine
consumer participation in suburban settings. This focus would support a more comprehensive
understanding of consumer behavior in this emerging market. Second, the research model may be
subject to endogeneity, particularly in the relationship between virtual consumer experience and
consumer participation behavior. Although the linear association is supported, if the relationship is
bidirectional, endogeneity may arise. Therefore, a reciprocal relationship should be tested in future
studies using a two-stage least squares (2SLS) approach (Cooper et al., 2020).
