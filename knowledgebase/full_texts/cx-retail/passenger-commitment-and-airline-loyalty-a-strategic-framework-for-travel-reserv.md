Abstract
Purpose
This paper develops a commitment-backed approach to airline loyalty in which a customer voluntarily
places funds in a protected travel reserve, and the airline reciprocates with transparent, capacity-aware
beneﬁts. The central question is not whether advance funding can create cash, but when an advance
commitment can create durable customer value and economically sound demand without weakening
trust, operational resilience or regulatory compliance.
Design and methodology
The paper integrates research on loyalty programs, relationship marketing, customer journeys, service
recovery, fairness and dynamic capabilities with the operating conditions of passenger airlines. It uses
theory synthesis and design logic to deﬁne the construct of commitment quality, specify a product
architecture, derive ﬁve propositions and set out an executive governance and pilot-testing framework.
No airline, passenger or transaction data are presented.
Findings
Advance commitment is valuable only when the reserve is useful to the customer, beneﬁts can be
fulﬁlled, pricing remains fair and incremental contribution survives the costs of rewards, servicing,
safeguarding, cannibalization and recovery disruption. The proposed architecture separates reserve
beneﬁts from elite status, introduces capacity and disruption gates, assigns component-level
accountability, and treats customer balances as obligations rather than unrestricted capital.
Practical implications
Airlines should begin with a capped, opt-in pilot; preserve a visible cash-purchase alternative; provide
simple withdrawal and expiry rules; measure contribution against a control group; and stop expansion
when trust, fulﬁlment, liability coverage or complaint thresholds deteriorate.
Originality and value
The article moves airline loyalty from retrospective recognition alone toward reciprocal, forward-looking
commitment. It contributes the concept of commitment quality and explains how ﬁnance, commercial,
digital, operational and customer-care decisions must be joined before a travel reserve can become a
credible loyalty instrument.
1 Introduction
Airline loyalty programs are remarkably good at recording the past. They recognize ﬂown activity, card
spending and partner transactions, then convert those records into status, points or privileges. What they
do less well is accommodate a customer who is willing to make a credible commitment to future travel
Page 2/21

but wants more than another layer of points in return. That gap matters because travel decisions are
made under uncertainty: fares move, capacity tighten, schedules change, household budgets ﬂuctuate
and disruption can erase the value of a beneﬁt at the moment it is most needed.
This paper asks whether an airline can invite a voluntary advance commitment without turning loyalty
into customer lock-in. The proposed answer is a commitment-backed travel reserve: a customer places a
chosen amount in an account restricted to eligible travel and related services, while the airline offers a
bounded set of beneﬁts such as fee ﬂexibility, fare-hold windows, baggage, seat selection, connectivity,
lounge access, partner mobility discounts or disruption support. The balance is neither a deposit product
nor a license to promise scarce services indiscriminately. It is a contractual obligation whose design
must respect accounting, consumer, payments, privacy and insolvency rules in each jurisdiction.
The distinction is important. A large balance may look attractive to treasury, yet it can destroy value if
customers would have purchased anyway, rewards are too generous, refund requests create liquidity
stress, beneﬁts fail during peak periods, or the program is perceived as coercive. A credible model
therefore begins with customer value and fulﬁlment, not with the size of the cash balance. It also
separates observed cash movement from incremental economics. Under IFRS 15, advance
consideration ordinarily gives rise to a contract liability until the promised good or service is transferred;
questions involving stored value or transferable funds may also enter ﬁnancial-regulation territory (IFRS
Foundation, 2024; European Parliament and Council, 2009).
The article makes three contributions. First, it deﬁnes commitment quality: the degree to which an
advance travel commitment is voluntary, usable, protected, economically incremental and operationally
fulﬁllable. Second, it explains reciprocal commitment as the mechanism connecting a reserve account to
trust and loyalty. Third, it translates the concept into an implementable operating model with decision
rights, capacity controls, disruption rules, unit economics and testable propositions. The intention is not
to report validation that has not occurred, but to provide a disciplined platform for empirical testing.

2 Theoretical foundation
2.1 Loyalty is behavioral and relational
Repeat purchasing is not the same as loyalty. Customers may remain because schedules are convenient,
alternatives are weak or accumulated rewards make switching costly. Oliver (1999) distinguishes
attitudinal commitment from repeated behavior, while Uncles et al. (2003) caution that loyalty schemes
often reinforce existing patterns rather than create deep allegiance. Longitudinal evidence also suggests
that program effects differ across customer segments and purchasing histories, and that membership
and service experiences jointly shape retention and customer value (Bolton et al., 2000; Liu, 2007).
Reviews of the ﬁeld likewise show that loyalty-program outcomes depend on program design, customer
characteristics and market context (Dorotic et al., 2012). A reserve account should therefore not be
judged by enrolment or balance alone. The relevant outcomes are incremental share of wallet, retention,
trust, reduced journey effort and contribution after all program costs.
Relationship marketing adds a second requirement. Trust and commitment sustain exchange when the
customer believes that the provider is reliable, creates enduring value and does not exploit vulnerability
(Morgan and Hunt, 1994; Sirdeshmukh et al., 2002; Kumar and Reinartz, 2016). Prepayment increases
vulnerability because the customer gives up liquidity before receiving travel. The airline must reciprocate
with control, clarity and dependable remedy. This is why non-refundable balances, vague expiry clauses
and beneﬁts described as “subject to availability” without meaningful access rules are weak foundations
for loyalty.
2.2 Customer journeys and service fulﬁlment
Page 4/21

Air travel is a chain of interdependent episodes rather than a single transaction. Search, booking, airport
processing, ﬂight, connection, baggage and recovery shape the total experience (Lemon and Verhoef,
2016). A reserve beneﬁt that works at booking but fails at the airport transfers effort back to the
customer. Service quality research has consistently linked reliability and recovery to behavioral
intentions (Zeithaml et al., 1996). Recovery can rebuild conﬁdence, but the so-called service recovery
paradox is conditional and should never be used as a justiﬁcation for preventable failure (McCollough et
al., 2000).
Airline evidence reinforces this emphasis on service performance. Passenger loyalty is shaped by
experienced service quality, perceived value and satisfaction, and those relationships can differ across
carrier models and traveler expectations (Park et al., 2004; Forgas et al., 2010). A reserve therefore
cannot compensate indeﬁnitely for weak core operations. Its strategic role is to make a reliable journey
easier to plan, purchase and recover, not to purchase tolerance for service failure.
Accordingly, every reserve beneﬁt needs an inventory source, an owner, a service standard and a remedy.
A lounge pass requires access capacity; a priority-security promise requires an airport agreement; a rail
connection requires timetable, ticketing and disruption data; and an insurance inclusion requires
eligibility and claims ownership. Commercial teams cannot sell these elements as one seamless
experience unless operational teams can identify where responsibility begins and ends.
2.3 Fair exchange and customer control
Customers evaluate fairness by comparing outcomes, procedures and explanations. Perceived price
unfairness can weaken trust even when a price is legally permissible (Xia et al., 2004). The reserve
should therefore inﬂuence beneﬁt composition before it inﬂuences personalized pricing. Two customers
buying the same fare under the same public conditions should not unknowingly face different base
prices because one has placed more funds with the airline. Any bonus or preferential offer should be
explicit, bounded and available under published eligibility rules.
Mental accounting offers further caution. Customers may treat a travel balance differently from cash
and may increase purchase incidence, but that does not make unused value a legitimate objective
(Thaler, 1985). The program should measure successful use, not celebrate inactivity. Easy balance
visibility, reminders, partial redemption, accessible refunds where promised, and humane treatment of
illness or bereavement protect the relationship and reduce the temptation to rely on breakage.
2.4 Dynamic capability and cross-functional coordination
A reserve account touches pricing, revenue management, ﬁnance, loyalty, digital, operations, customer
care, risk and external partners, consistent with the cross-functional process view of customer
relationship management (Payne and Frow, 2005). Its advantage lies less in the idea than in the
organization’s ability to sense demand, seize viable opportunities and reconﬁgure resources without
losing control (Teece, 2007). This makes the program a dynamic capability problem. A marketing launch
cannot substitute for a reconciled ledger, a capacity gate or a disruption playbook.

3 Commitment to quality and reciprocal commitment
Commitment quality is deﬁned here as the extent to which a customer’s advanced travel commitment
provides a reliable basis for mutual value after adjustment for customer control, legal protection,
expected fulﬁlment, incentive cost, cannibalization and operational risk. Its ﬁve dimensions are
voluntariness, usability, protection, incrementality and fulﬁlability. A high balance with weak refund rights
or low beneﬁt access has poor commitment quality. A smaller balance that shifts future choice, remains
useful and can be served reliably and may have high commitment quality.
Reciprocal commitment is the proposed mechanism. The customer commits purchasing power and
provides a better signal of intended travel. The airline commits to rules that are intelligible before
purchase, beneﬁts that reﬂect genuine needs, and recovery when a component fails. Each side’s
commitment remains bound. The customer does not promise to ﬂy regardless of price or service, and
the airline does not promise unlimited scarce inventory. The relationship is strengthened when both
parties can see what has been committed, what remains available and what happens if conditions
change.
The model does not assume a direct path from prepayment to loyalty. Advance commitment ﬁrst has to
acquire quality through voluntariness, usability, protection, incrementality and fulﬁlability. Customers
must then perceive a credible reciprocal response from the airline. Trust and lower journey effort are the
nearer outcomes; retention, share of wallet and contribution are more distant outcomes and should be
tested against counterfactual behavior. Fairness and fulﬁlment of reliability operate as boundary
conditions, while cannibalization and liabilities constrain the economic outcome.

4 Product and service architecture
The travel reserve should be designed as a modular account rather than a new elite-status ladder. Status
recognizes demonstrated relationship value; reserve beneﬁts compensate for the customer’s forward
commitment. Combining the two can dilute established tiers, create resentment among frequent ﬂyers
and encourage ﬁnancially capable customers to buy around behavioral criteria. The two systems may
share identity and redemption rails, but their qualiﬁcation logic should remain distinct.
At enrolment, the customer chooses a capped commitment band, validity period and refundability
option. A fully ﬂexible version may carry modest beneﬁts; a partially restricted version may offer more
value but must preserve clearly deﬁned exit events. The account shows available balance, pending
transactions, expiry dates, earned beneﬁts and any capacity conditions. Funds should not be
transferable or redeemable beyond the approved design unless the airline has conﬁrmed the resulting
payments and stored-value obligations.
Beneﬁt selection should be need-based. A family may value adjacent seating, checked baggage and a
shared wallet. A small business may prefer name changes within a traveler pool, reporting and ﬂexible
rebooking. A frequent short-haul traveler may prefer same-day change options and rail or airport-transfer
integration. A long-haul leisure customer may value insurance, lounge entry, connectivity and an
instalment plan. These are propositions to test, not entitlements to bundle indiscriminately.

The integrated mobility module should solve a real connection problem rather than decorate the offer.
Mobility-as-a-service research similarly emphasizes the need to rethink transport contracts and
institutional arrangements as services become digitally integrated (Hensher, 2017). An airline can
combine air with rail or ground transport where schedules, minimum connection times, ticket
responsibility and recovery rules are available. A referral link is not through journey assurance. The
customer interface must say whether the journey is separately contracted, protected by a commercial
agreement or serviced under a single order. Expansion should proceed from referral to coordinated
booking, to protected connection only as operational maturity increases.
At referral level, the airline limits its role to discovery and must avoid language implying connection
protection. Coordinated booking adds shared itinerary data, customer-service hand-offs and settlement
rules, although contracts may remain separate. Protected journey status should be reserved for
arrangements with veriﬁed connection times, real-time status exchange, rebooking authority, ﬁnancial
settlement and one clearly identiﬁed recovery owner. This staged maturity model keeps strategic
ambition aligned with operational accountability.
5 Economic discipline and revenue quality
The business case cannot rest on gross balances. Customer funds normally create an obligation and
may require safeguarding, liquidity reserves or other protections. Any treasury beneﬁt must be measured
Page 9/21

after those requirements and must not be confused with revenue. Breakage is similarly unsuitable as a
strategic objective. Accounting recognition depends on enforceable rights and expected entitlement,
while deliberate friction around redemption can damage trust and invite regulatory scrutiny (IFRS
Foundation, 2024).
The correct decision unit is incremental contribution. For a deﬁned cohort and period, management
should estimate incremental travel margin, attributable ancillary and partner margin, permitted funding
beneﬁt and any avoided acquisition or payment costs. It should then deduct reward cost, servicing,
payment, safeguarding, technology, fraud, cannibalization and expected recovery liabilities. Every term
requires a counterfactual. If a participant had made the same purchase at the same cost without the
reserve, the program has not created incremental demand.
Net incremental value = incremental travel margin + attributable ancillary and partner margin + 
permitted funding beneﬁt + avoided cost − reward cost − servicing and protection cost − 
cannibalization − expected recovery liability
Revenue quality adds a timing and risk perspective. A program that accelerates cash but concentrates
redemptions in peaks, raises refund volatility or creates underfunded beneﬁt promises has poor revenue
quality. The ﬁnance case should therefore include balance ageing, redemption seasonality, liquidity-at-
risk, liability coverage, cohort proﬁtability and stress scenarios. No universal reward percentage is
defensible. The reward ceiling should be derived from measured incremental contribution and the
probability that promised beneﬁts can be delivered.
The program should also protect the existing loyalty economy. Points may be offered as one beneﬁt, but
their cost, expiry and redemption capacity must be visible. Elite recognition should not be displaced by
deposit size. The strongest design may give reserve customers convenience and certainty rather than
large point bonuses: a longer fare hold, one fee-free change, family pooling, automated refund or
disruption assistance may create more perceived value per unit of airline cost.
6 Operating model and governance
The reserve needs one accountable executive sponsor and several explicit control owners. Commercial
leadership owns the value proposition and cohort economics. Finance owns accounting classiﬁcation,
liquidity treatment and liability coverage. Operations have fulﬁlment standards and protection mode.
Technology owns ledger, identity, reconciliation and resilience. Legal, privacy and compliance teams
determine where the design becomes a regulated stored value, credit, insurance or payments product.
Customer care owns remedies and contact demand. Internal audit should test controls independently
before expansion.
The hierarchy deliberately places classiﬁcation and customer protection ahead of commercial
optimization. A positive demand forecast cannot cure an unresolved regulatory perimeter, an
unreconciled liability or an unfulﬁllable promise. Likewise, technology development should follow a
Page 10/21

conﬁrmed product boundary rather than force the customer proposition to ﬁt an existing wallet or loyalty
platform. Each lower level therefore depends on evidence from the level above.

A separate reconciled subledger is essential. It must connect each customer balance to funding,
redemption, refund, expiry, beneﬁt issuance and partner settlement. Daily exception reporting should
identify negative balances, unmatched transactions, delayed refunds and failed fulﬁlment. Cybersecurity
should cover account takeover, synthetic identity, fraudulent transfer, promotion abuse and credential
risk. Controls must be designed before launch because a reserve combines loyalty value, payment value
and personally identiﬁable travel data.
The architecture is intentionally modular. The reserve subledger remains the ﬁnancial source of truth,
while order, inventory and partner services conﬁrm what can be sold and fulﬁlled. Fulﬁlment events
return actual delivery status rather than if issuance equals service. Refund and recovery services close
the operational loop, and ﬁnance, audit and monitoring receive reconciled events. A pilot may use a
limited integration layer, but it should not rely on manual balances or unreconciled promotional codes.
Personalization should select relevant beneﬁts within approved rules, not infer vulnerability or charge
hidden individual prices. Models may rank likely usefulness of baggage, ﬂexibility, lounge, connectivity or
Page 11/21

surface transport, but the customer should be able to edit preferences and understand the principal
reason for an offer. Sensitive attributes should be excluded unless clearly justiﬁed and lawful. Model
drift, proxy discrimination and feedback loops require monitoring. Where automated decisions have
material effects, human review and contestability should be available.
Artiﬁcial intelligence is useful only when assigned to a bounded decision. It may forecast reserve
utilization, rank beneﬁt relevance, detect fraud, predict fulﬁlment risk or identify customers needing
proactive recovery. It should not covertly individualize the public base fare, manufacture urgency,
obstruct refunds or infer vulnerability for commercial exploitation. Research on AI in marketing and
consumer response shows that effectiveness depends on the task, the customer’s experience of agency
and the governance surrounding data use (Huang and Rust, 2021; Puntoni et al., 2021). Privacy is
therefore part of value creation rather than a compliance appendix (Martin and Murphy, 2017).
7 Resilience fairness and regulatory ﬁt
Disruption is the hardest test of reciprocal commitment. When operational control declares protection
mode, the system should suspend non-essential selling, suppress beneﬁts that cannot be fulﬁlled and
prioritise re-accommodation, refunds and statutory care. Customers should never be encouraged to
spend more from a reserve to obtain assistance already owed under passenger-rights rules. The
program’s value in disruption lies in faster identiﬁcation, clearer options and fewer hand-offs, not in
creating a privileged route around legal obligations.
Fairness requires attention to customers who cannot or do not wish to prepare. Ordinary fares and core
service must remain accessible. Reserve beneﬁts should compensate for a chosen commitment without
degrading non-participant treatment. Marketing must state material restrictions with the offer rather
than behind a link. Expire reminders should arrive early enough for action, and exceptional-circumstance
policies should be consistent. These safeguards reduce complaints, conduct risk and reputational
exposure while strengthening perceived legitimacy.
Regulatory classiﬁcation will vary. A closed-loop, non-transferable travel balance may be treated
differently from a transferable or widely redeemable wallet. Adding interest, cash withdrawal, third-party
acceptance, credit, insurance or foreign-exchange features can change the perimeter. The European
electronic-money framework, data-protection rules and local consumer protections illustrate why a
global program needs a modular legal design rather than a single worldwide contract (European
Parliament and Council, 2009, 2016). Local approval is a launch gate, not a post-launch clean-up activity.
8 Propositions
The framework yields ﬁve propositions suitable for empirical testing. They are deliberately conditional: a
reserve account is not assumed to produce loyalty merely because customers enroll or funds arrive.
Proposition 1
Page 12/21

The positive relationship between a travel reserve and customer adoption will be stronger when
refundability, validity, eligible uses and remedies are transparent and controllable than when value is
delivered primarily through a larger headline reward.
Proposition 2
The effect of reserve participation on share of wallet and retention will be mediated by perceived
reciprocal commitment, reﬂected in customer control, beneﬁt relevance and conﬁdence that the airline
will honor the arrangement.
Proposition 3
Fulﬁlment reliability will positively moderate the relationship between perceived reciprocal commitment
and loyalty outcomes; repeated denial or substitution will weaken or reverse that relationship.
Proposition 4
Need-based, capacity-aware beneﬁts will produce higher customer value per unit of airline cost than
uniform point bonuses, particularly when beneﬁts reduce journey effort or uncertainty.
Proposition 5
A reserve will create positive net incremental value only when incremental margin and permitted funding
beneﬁts exceed reward, servicing, protection, cannibalization and expected recovery costs at cohort
level.
9 Pilot and evidence plan
A pilot should answer whether the mechanism works, for whom and under what constraints. It should
not be designed to manufacture a favorable uptake number. A practical ﬁrst test would select one or two
markets with stable operations, known repeat demand and manageable regulatory scope. Participation
should be opt-in, deposits capped and beneﬁt inventory limited to components with reliable data. A six-
to nine-month enrolment and observation window can capture booking, redemption and service behavior,
although retention effects require longer follow-up.
Where feasible, eligible customers should be randomly assigned to an invitation group and a business-
as-usual control group. If randomization is impractical, matched cohorts and difference-in-differences
analysis can improve inference, provided assumptions are tested. Offer variants can compare ﬂexibility,
convenience and point-based value without changing the public base fare. Pre-registration of primary
outcomes and stop rules reduces the risk of selecting only favorable results.

The scale decision should be asymmetric. Strong enrolment cannot compensate for weak liability
coverage, opaque pricing or poor fulﬁlment. Conversely, modest enrolment may still justify reﬁnement if
a clearly deﬁned segment achieves strong incremental contribution and trust. Expansion should occur
one capability at a time: new market, new beneﬁt or deeper partner integration, but not all
simultaneously. This preserves causal learning and limits operational exposure.
10 Discussion
The proposed model changes the unit of loyalty from accumulated activity to a negotiated future
relationship. That shift can be strategically useful because it gives customers a way to convert planned
travel into convenience and protection while giving airlines a better, though still imperfect, demand
signal. Its value does not depend on replacing existing programs. A reserve can sit beside points and
status, serving customers whose travel is predictable enough to commit but not frequent enough to earn
elite recognition quickly.
The model also reframes advance cash. The managerial temptation is to value liquidity ﬁrst and
customer obligation second. Commitment of quality reverses that order. Balance is valuable only to the
extent that it represents a healthy future exchange. This view aligns marketing accountability with
Page 15/21

ﬁnance and operations: marketing cannot count balances as loyalty, ﬁnance cannot count liabilities as
revenue, and operations cannot treat beneﬁts as promotional language detached from capacity.
For integrated mobility, the same principle prevents overreach. Airlines need no ground transport. They
may orchestra it was doing so lowers journey effort and where responsibility can be made explicit. The
strategic boundary should be drawn around customer continuity rather than asset ownership. A
protected air–rail connection can deepen trust; an unprotected referral presented as seamless can do
the opposite.
For loyalty theory, reciprocal commitment offers a more precise explanation than lock-in. Lock-in
predicts persistence because exit is costly. Reciprocal commitment predicts preference because the
customer expects the airline to act reliably after receiving an advance commitment. The distinction is
empirically important: high retention accompanied by low trust, complaints and unused balances may
indicate captivity, not loyalty.
11 Limitations and research agenda
This is a conceptual paper and does not establish causal effects, market demand or ﬁnancial returns.
The propositions require passenger research and controlled ﬁeld evidence. Regulatory treatment,
insolvency protection and accounting may differ materially across jurisdictions and product features.
Airline network structure, customer mix, brand position and operational reliability will also affect results.
The model should therefore not be implemented from illustrative examples alone.
Future work should test willingness to commit under alternative refund, validity and beneﬁt structures;
estimate heterogeneous effects across leisure, visiting-friends-and-relatives, small-business and
corporate traveler s; and examine whether commitment quality predicts behavior beyond satisfaction
and existing status. Field studies should measure long-run retention and share shift, not only initial
deposits. Research should also test whether recovery beneﬁts create value during disruption without
producing unequal access to statutory care. Comparative studies across network, low-cost, regional and
hybrid carriers would clarify where the architecture travels well and where it does not.
A staged validation program would provide the strongest evidence. First, a two- or three-round Delphi
study could seek convergence among airline loyalty, commercial, revenue-management, ﬁnance,
operations, technology and regulatory specialists on the construct deﬁnition, controls and pilot
thresholds. Second, a preregistered choice experiment across several passenger markets could estimate
how refundability, validity, reserve amount, ﬂexibility, points, journey beneﬁts and disruption protection
affect willingness to participate, perceived fairness and intended share shift. Third, a controlled airline
pilot could compare invited customers with a business-as-usual group and measure actual funding,
booking, redemption, fulﬁlment, complaints, recovery and net contribution. The three stages answer
different questions: organizational feasibility, customer preference and operational-economic
performance. None should be represented as completed until its protocol, sample and results are
independently documented.

12 Conclusion
A commitment-backed travel reserve can become a credible airline loyalty instrument, but only if it is
designed as a reciprocal service promise rather than a cash-collection device. The customer commits
future purchasing power; the airline commits transparency, useful choice, fulﬁlment and remedy.
Commitment quality determines whether that exchange produces preference or merely locks-in.
The practical implication is disciplined restraint. Beginning with a narrow customer problem, capped
exposure and beneﬁts that operations can fulﬁl. Treat balances as obligations, measure incremental
contribution against a counterfactual, protect non-participants, and stop when trust or control
deteriorates. Under those conditions, a travel reserve may strengthen customer relationships and
revenue quality. Without them, the same idea can magnify ﬁnancial, operational and reputational risk.
