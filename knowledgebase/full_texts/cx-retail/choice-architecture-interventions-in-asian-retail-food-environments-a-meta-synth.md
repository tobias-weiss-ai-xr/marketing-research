Abstract. Public health strategies are using choice architecture
interventions such as retail product allocation layouts and default swaps
to change consumer food choices without constraining economic
autonomy. However, how varied customer segments accept and mana ge
these low -agency procedures in structurally complex Asian retail food
landscapes is unclear. This study consolidated qualitative data on
customer experiences, environmental realities, and subsequent equity
implications in this fast-modernising setting. Methodology: Using PRISMA
principles, signiHicant electronic datasets were reviewed up to 2026. We
isolated nine empirical primary qualitative research studies (n = 9) that
met tight inclusion criteria: direct participant interviews, focus group
discussions, or ethnographic Hield observations. The Critical Appraisal
Skills Programme (CASP) qualitative checklist assessed methodological
quality. A continual comparative meta -ethnographic translation method
gathered and synthesised primary narrative texts and interpretive codes.
Results: CASP scores of 9 to 10 indicate good methodological quality. Four
themes emerged from meta -ethnographic translation: (1) Intervention
Modalities and Mechanisms of Action; (2) Consumer Experiences and
Acceptability; (3) Equity Implications for Vuln erable Populations; and (4)
Contextual Factors in Asian Retail Environments. In addition, the Hindings
suggest that low -income consumers use conventional wet markets as an
important equity buffer due to their easily accessible micro-quantities and
informal credit networks. On the other hand, contemporary convenience
stores outside of school raise the issue  of persistent diets that are not
supervised by parents.  Discussion: These Hindings show that centralised
choice architecture interventions often fail because they assume equal
cognitive agency without considering socioeconomic constraints,
household risks, and regional policy patterns. Therefore, multi -level
structural strategies need to be used within a pub lic health framework,
rather than relying solely on micro -environmental impulses. To ensure
equitable health outcomes, policies should actively protect informal food
systems, address time poverty among the working class, and enforce
stricter spatial zoning  regulations on high -density commercial corporate
chains near vulnerable communities.

Asian retail environments are distinctive in that contemporary supermarkets and high -density convenience chains mix

experiences,  equity  boundaries,  and  environmental realities are the focus of this qualitative systematic review. This study
maps these variables to improve public  health frameworks and ensure that  future structural interventions are effective
behavioral cues and equitable, contextually  appropriate instruments.

Research Problem

Uncritically applying Western-centric choice architecture models to structurally divided Asian retail food settings without
considering local socio-economic inequalities and infrastructural difMiculties  is the main research concern in this review.
Modern  behavioral  "nudge"  theories  imply  that  micro -environmental  changes  like  changing  point -of-purchase  food
visibility or default meal selections operate on a universal consumer agency baseline (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). On  the
contrary, top -down city sanitisation and retail modernisation  initiatives in developing Asian countries actively replace
street  vendors and informal open -air wet markets with corporate supermarkets and convenience store chains  (Banwell
et al.,  2012; Wertheim -Heck et al., 2019).  Current intervention designs neglect socio -economically excluded cohorts by
focusing solely on statistical purchase trends  or high -agency Western assumptions. In low -income groups, acute time
poverty and structural Minancial  constraints impair  healthy  goals,  generating  behavioral  overrides  that  make  pure
visibility  modiMications  ineffective  (Colozza,  2022).  Modernisation  strategies  can  also  create  localised  "supermarket
deserts"  that  prevent  vulnerable  communities  from  accessing fresh food by replacing low -cost informal food centers
with corporate businesses (Wertheim-Heck et al., 2019).  Public  health  strategies  may  increase  health  inequalitie s  by
applying  choice  architecture  treatments  without  a  robust  metasynthesis of empirical qualitative insights into how these
varied groups manage these environmental crosscurrents  (Thomas & Harden, 2008).

Research Questions

The following major research issues guide this review to carefully address literature gaps:
1. How do choice architecture interventions and micro -environmental default alteration affect varied Asian retail  food
consumer cohorts?
2. What  impact  do  household  socio -economic  situations,  gendered  labor  factors,  and  structural  Mlaws  have  on
localised choice architecture?
3. What  roles  do  traditional wet markets and modern  retail  venues  play as  structural equality  buffers  or  spatial  health
hazards in rapidly modernising urban landscapes?

Assumptions of the Study

Our qualitative synthesis assumes that food environments are active, socio-culturally formed spaces with power dynamics,
historical  habits,  and  economic  limits,  not  just  retail  outlets. Qualitative  primary  research is thought  to  offer  a  unique
perspective  on  consumer  decision-making,  particularly  why  environmental  interventions  work  or  fail  among  excluded
groups. Despite national differences, Asian subregions seem to have structural undercurrents. Rapid urbanisation, working
class time poverty, and retail modernisation policies also inMluence diets.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

We  searched  major  electronic  databases  for  relevant  peer-reviewed  academic  articles  published  up  to  2026. Choice
architecture  terms  were  combined  with  Boolean  operators  in  the  search  string (e.g.,  "choice  architecture",  "nudg e",
"placement",  "default"),  retail  settings  (e.g.,  "supermarket",  "grocery",  "wet  market",  "convenience  store"),  qualitative
methodology (e.g., "interview", "focus group", "ethnography", "qualitative"), and geographical focus (e.g., "Asia", "Southeast
Asia", "East Asia").

Initial database search revealed 4,265 unique records. After deleting 89 duplicates, 4,176 entries were independently title
and abstract screened, excluding 3,758 records that did not match fundamental theme criteria. The remaining 418 full-text
publications were extensively examined against these clear inclusion criteria: (a) Asian retail food surroundings or choice
modiMication  elements;  (b)  applying  empirical  primary  qualitative  data  gathering  methods, including  semi-structured
interviews,  focus  group  discussions,  and  ethnographic  observation  to  stakeholder  or  participant  narratives;  and  (c)
reporting explicit equity or socioeconomic context. Methodological issues eliminated 409 papers from full-text evaluation:
non-Asian context  (n = 187), non -retail settings  (n = 98), inappropriate study design or quantitative methods  (n = 75),
insufMicient data mapping to equity dimensions (n = 42), and secondary literature reviews, scoping overviews, or expert

Core Dynamics of the Framework
o The Core Environmental Dialectic (Theme 4): Food procurement is roiled by a fractured retail market (Banwell et al.,
2012). Two systems coexist: traditional, low -cost open-air wet markets and rapidly expanding commercial convenience
store networks and supermarkets (Banwell et al., 2012; Kelly et al., 2014).

o The  Micro-Level  Nudge  EfMicacy  (Theme  1):  In  formal  institutional  environments,  decision  architecture  changes,
including  placement  defaults,  drive  low -agency  cognitive  pathways  (Seah  et  al.,  2020).  It  meets  nudging's  initial
behavioral assumptions by in Mluencing decisions without conscious strain (Kawabata et al., 2024; Thaler & Sunstein,
2008).

o The Household Vulnerability Override (Themes 2 & 3): The theory suggests that micro -environmental nudges function
together  (Marteau  et  al.,  2012). When  a  customer  exits  a  structured institutional  setting,  severe  time  poverty  and
structural Minancial constraints change their behavior immediately (Colozza, 2022).

o The  Spatial  and  Policy  Cross -Currents  (Theme  3):  Localised  "supermarket  deserts"  result  from  top -down
modernisation programs that eliminate informal markets (Wertheim -Heck et al., 2019). This affects fresh food supply,
proving that municipal zoning determines structural equity buffers more than cognitive choices (Hofman & Trevenen Jones,
2024; Wertheim-Heck et al., 2019).

Thematic Narrative and Metasynthesis

The synthesised conceptual model (Figure 2) maps complex structural factors on health navigation and dietary compliance
across distinct Asian communities. Rather than interpreting food choices as a mere result of willpower, this metasynthesis
reveals a complex hierarch y in which macro -policies actively inMluence micro -environmental retail factors. All four
resulting  thematic  domains  have  strong  GRADE -CERQual  conMidence   indicators  (Table 4),  proving  the  translated
qualitative  metadata's empirical validity and structural consistency across regions.

Theme 1: Intervention Modalities and Mechanisms of Action

Primary  qualitative  literature  shows  that  structural  micro -environmental  alterations  support  or  challenge  cognitive
processes for eating choices. Primary qualitative datasets contained 14.29% of this theme. It addresses one local topic: 1)
Environmental Restructuring & Low-Agency Placement Defaults.

Subtheme 1.1: Environmental Restructuring & Low-Agency Placement Defaults

Formal institutions and automated procurement systems dominate point -of-purchase food options, according to line -by
line coding. Institutional design elements and default shifts steer choices without conscious consideration, proving  that
behavioral shortcuts work in daily shopping routines (Seah et al., 2020).

"Many participants noted that they choose healthier dishes when they are placed at eye level or at the  front of the  food
counter... due to the visual salience of the healthy options, they did not have to spend time searching for alternatives." (Seah
et al., 2020)

Theme 2: Consumer Experiences and Acceptability

The  main  statistics  reveal  that  complicated  household  survival  strategies  and  severe  environmental  trade-offs  drive
consumer navigation via modern and traditional shop locations. This theme was 28.57% of the major synthesis. It covers
two interrelated parts: 1) Channel Segmentation and Strategic Traditional Market Utilisation; and 2) Economic Counter
Pressures and Time Poverty Bottlenecks.

Subtheme 2.1: Channel Segmentation and Strategic Traditional Market Utilisation

The qualitative synthesis contained 14.29% of this subtheme. Line-by-line coding shows that household work demands and
cultural buying habits cause channels to be deliberately split up. As a result, customers refrain from transferring all of their
purchases to modern corporate venues. Maintain sensory trust and lower prices by using modern nodes for pre -packaged
dry items and active ties to local open-air wet marketplaces (Kelly et al., 2014).
"We go to supermarkets once a month for dry goods like milk, oil, and canned stuff because it's organized and clean. But for

Subtheme 2.2: Economic Counter-Pressures and Time Poverty Bottlenecks

The  qualitative  synthesis  contained  14.29%  of  this  subtheme. The  line -by-line  coding  shows  that  acute  household
vulnerability  and  immediate  family  hunger  often  trump  long -term  nutritional  aspirations. Environmental  visibility
modiMications fail to change working-class customers' habits because of long work hours, low Minancial margins, and time
restrictions that impede fresh food production (Colozza, 2022).

"Even if I know processed foods are not good for health, when I <inish my factory shift at night, I am too exhausted to cook.
The  roadside fried food stalls and convenience items are cheap, instant, and satisfy my family's immediate hunger without
taking  up time I don't have." (Colozza, 2022)

Subtheme 3.1: Spatial Access Disparities and Modernization-Driven "Supermarket Deserts"

The qualitative synthesis contained 14.29% of this subtheme. Line-by-line coding shows that top-down modernisation and
severe  municipal  sanitisation  cause  localised  zoning  exclusions. Distancing  informal  vendors  from  low-cost  food  hubs
isolates low-income households physically and Minancially (Wertheim-Heck et al., 2019).

"Since  the city government closed down  the informal street market in our neighborhood for modernization,  I have  to
walk  double the distance to the new supermarket. The food there is packaged beautifully but costs much more, which
means I can  buy fewer fresh vegetables for my children." (Wertheim-Heck et al., 2019)

Subtheme 3.2: Traditional Retail Channels as Structural Equity Buffers

The qualitative synthesis contained 14.29% of this subtheme. Line -by-line coding shows that informal business networks
provide  vital  social  protection. Since  corporate  structures  exclude  low -income  customers,  wet  markets  and  small
community s ellers provide Mlexible micro -quantities and localised Minancing mechanisms to maintain food security
(Hofman  & Trevenen-Jones, 2024).

"The wet market vendors know our <inancial  situation; if I am short on cash before the monthly payday, they let me take
the  rice and vegetables on informal credit and write it down in their book. A corporate supermarket would never allow me
to walk  out with food without paying <irst." (Hofman & Trevenen-Jones, 2024)

Line-of-Argument Synthesis

Following Noblit and Hare's meta -ethnographic framework, this synthesis uses a line -of-argument technique to explore
beyond themes and rebuild an integrated narrative from the nine primary qualitative datasets. Essentially, a country's retail
development s tage and household socioeconomic constraints dictate the public health efficacy of low -agency choice
architecture interventions. This qualitative synthesis has multi-layered behavioral relationships:

• The Structural Environment Baseline: Traditional fresh venues (wet markets) and sophisticated corporate
networks (supermarkets and convenience stores) exist together in growing Asian retail landscapes (Banwell et
al., 2012; Kelly et al., 2014).
• The Micro -Level Nudge Effect: Microenvironmental changes in formal institutions and corporations. Change
point-of-purchase displays or optimise healthy defaults. Use low-agency cognitive pathways to influence customer
behavior without conscious strain (Seah et al., 2020; Thaler & Sunstein, 2008).
• The Household Vulnerability Override: Once people leave highly controlled micro -environments, profound
socioeconomic restrictions overrule their behavior. Acute household time poverty and systemic financial
restrictions interrupt positive nutritional inten t in marginalised people, making pure visibility interventions
ineffectual (Colozza, 2022).
• The Policy -Driven Access Disparities: Municipal sanitisation programs that phase out informal marketplaces
generate "supermarket deserts" that deny low -income customers fresh food. Traditional open -air networks

This synthesis concludes that choice architecture interventions fail when they presume uniform cognitive agency without

Conclusion and Recommendations

This meta-ethnography and qualitative systematic review show choice architecture mechanisms. Low -agency storefront
defaults can drive customer choices within organised institutional environments, but their real-world impact on Asian food
landscapes is substantially shaped by structural restrictions. The meta -ethnographic synthesis shows that socioeconomic
factors often override localised  micro-environmental efforts. Low -income consumers are often hampered by systemic
factors like acute household time poverty, severe financial constraints, and top-down municipal modernisations.  Top-down
retail formalisation schemes that abolish informal marketplaces can cut off low -income residents from flexible, low -cost
food centers, compromising food security. Traditional fresh wet markets are structural equity barriers, but high -density
corporate convenience shop chains near school perimeters pose a dietary concern for youth.

Implications for Public Health Policy and Practice

Public Health Policy

Food environment treatments must go beyond cognitive agency and choice optimisation. Food policy must enable informal
commerce to address systemic issues. Instead of abolishing wet markets, upgrading their sanitary infrastructure and
helping low-income households with finances and time.

Spatial Environmental Zoning

Local governments should restrict spatial zoning around sensitive areas. Lowering the density of harmful retail convenience
stores near schools reduces youth ultra -processed food exposure. Thus, strengthening parental oversight and school
nutrition.

Clinical and Community Research

In routine health evaluations, community health researchers and clinical nurses should analyze structural food insecurity
and home restrictions. Health professionals should give contextually appropriate dietary guidance rather than nutrition
education that presumes universal lifestyle management. It recognises time poverty and social division in real life.
