ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated a transition that was already under way in m arketing:
the migration of consumer attention, search and purchase activity to digital channels. This
narrative review examines how consumer behavior changed in the new scenario  and how
firms can adapt their marketing strategies to remain competitive and sustain sales. Peer-
reviewed studies and reference works published up to 2023 were analyzed qualitatively and
organized into three dimensions: changes in consumer behavior, the reconfig uration of the
customer journey across channels, and the organizational capabilities required for
adaptation. The literature indicates that many habits formed during the pandemic—such as
online purchasing, contactless service and reliance on digital information sources —have
persisted, while consumers now expect seamless experiences across physical and d igital
touchpoints. Firms that responded effectively combined omnichannel integration, data-driven
customer understanding, agile business-model adjustments and a stronger foc us on
customer experience. Conversely, approaches that treat digital marketing as an isolated
communication tool, rather than as part of a broader business transformation, tend to yield
limited results. The review concludes that adaptation to the new scenario is less a ma tter of
adopting specific platforms than of building the capabilities needed to understand customers,
integrate channels and continuously test and refine marketing actions.

1 INTRODUCTION
Digital technologies have been reshaping marketing for more than two decades,
altering how consumers search for information, evaluate alternatives, purchase and  share
their experiences [1, 2]. Digital marketing is now understood not merely as adve rtising o n
online media, but as an adaptive, technology-enabled process through which firm s
collaborate with customers and partners to create, communicate, deliver and su stain value
[3]. This broader view places digital marketing at the center of firms’ commercial strategy.
The COVID-19 pandemic, which began in 2020, acted as an abrupt accelerator of this
transition. Mobility restrictions, the temporary closure of physical stores and health concerns
pushed consumers and companies toward digital channels in a matter of weeks [4, 5 ].
Businesses of all sizes were forced to reconsider their value propositions, channels and
customer relationships in order to survive [6, 7]. For many organizations, the pandemic
exposed the gap between the digital expectations of customers and the digital capabilities of
the firm.
As restrictions eased, a central question emerged for managers: which changes were
temporary and which represent a lasting new scenario? Understanding this distinction is
essential for deciding where to invest and how to reorganize marketing activities. The present
study therefore examines how consumer behavior changed in the post-pandem ic scenario
and identifies the strategic adaptations that allow firms to remain competitive and to su stain
sales growth.

1.1 THEORETICAL BACKGROUND
Two complementary perspectives frame the analysis. The first concerns consumer
behavior. Habits are formed and maintained by context; when the context ch anges, habits
are disrupted, and new behaviors may emerge or old ones may return once  conditions
normalize [4]. Consumption patterns observed during the pandemic —hoarding,
improvisation, pent-up demand, the embrace of digital technology and th e store coming
home—illustrate how quickly behavior can adapt when constraints change [4].
The second perspective concerns the customer journey. Customer experience is
formed across multiple touchpoints during the pre-purchase, purchase and post -purchase
stages, and many of these touchpoints are not directly controlled by the firm [8]. The evolution

from multichannel to omnichannel retailing reflects the need to manage these touchpoints in
an integrated way, so that customers can move freely between channels during  a single
journey [9]. In this context, the shift from traditional to digital marketing is described as a
combination of online and offline interactions rather than a substitutio n of one by the other
[10].

2 MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1 REVIEW DESIGN AND SOURCE SELECTION
This study adopts a narrative literature-review approach. Peer-reviewed articles and
reference books in marketing, consumer behavior and business strategy published up to 2023
were selected for their relevance to three questions: how consumer behavior changed during
and after the pandemic; how the customer journey and channel structure were reconfigured;
and which organizational capabilities support adaptation. Priority was given to frequently cited
conceptual frameworks, systematic research agendas and empirical studies published in
established marketing and management journals.

2.2 ANALYSIS PROCEDURE
The selected sources were analyzed qualitatively and organized by theme. Beca use
the studies differ in context, sector, method and country, their findings were not combined into
a quantitative estimate. Instead, convergent conclusions were identified and distin guished
from those that depend on specific sectors or conditions. The review does not generate new
empirical data; its contribution lies in organizing existing evidence into a structure th at
managers can use to guide adaptation. No ethical approval was required, as the study relies
exclusively on published sources.

3 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1 PERSISTENT CHANGES IN CONSUMER BEHAVIOR
The literature indicates that the pandemic did not create digital consumption b ut
compressed into months a shift that would otherwise have taken years. Consumers who had
never purchased online were compelled to do so, learned the process and, in m any cases,
kept the habit once the convenience of digital channels became evident [4, 5]. Activities that

were previously performed outside the home, such as shopping, education, work and
entertainment, were partially brought into the home, increasing the time consumers spend in
digital environments [4].
At the same time, not every behavior changed permanently. Sheth argue s that some
habits return once constraints disappear, while others are replaced by new habits that prove
more convenient, affordable or accessible [4]. For firms, the practical implication is th at the
post-pandemic consumer is hybrid: they value physical experiences but expect digital
convenience at every stage, including information search, price comparison, payment ,
delivery and after-sales support [5, 11].
Social media also consolidated its role as a space for discovery, evaluation an d
recommendation. Consumers increasingly rely on peer reviews, creators and online
communities when forming opinions about brands, which shifts part of the persuasive power
from the firm to the network of consumers [12, 13]. This change requires firms to listen,
respond and participate in conversations rather than simply broadcast messages.

3.2 RECONFIGURATION OF THE CUSTOMER JOURNEY
The customer journey in the new scenario is non-linear and distributed across
numerous touchpoints. A customer may discover a product on social media, research it on a
search engine, compare prices on a marketplace, visit a physical store and complete the
purchase through a mobile application [8, 9]. Each of these touchpoints co ntributes to the
overall experience and to the decision to buy.
Omnichannel management addresses this complexity by integrating channels so that
information, inventory, prices and service are consistent wherever the customer interacts with
the firm [9]. Technologies such as mobile applications, contactless payment, click-and-collect
services and real-time inventory visibility have become part of customers’ expectations [11,
14]. Firms that maintained separate, uncoordinated channels during the  pandemic
experienced friction—for example, products advertised online that were unavailable in store—
which undermined customer trust.

3.3 STRATEGIC RESPONSES OF FIRMS
Studies of business-model adaptation during the pandemic show that firms responded
along different paths. Some focused on preserving their existing business model, others
adjusted specific components such as channels or revenue streams, and some redesign ed
the model more deeply [6]. Among small and medium-sized enterprises (SM Es), digital
transformation often followed a staged path, beginning with the use o f digital channels for
sales and communication and progressing toward broader digitalization of processes  [15].
Family firms, in turn, frequently combined short-term cost containment with an acc elerated
adoption of digital tools [7].
Across these studies, the firms that adapted most effectively shared several
characteristics, summarized in Table 1. They used data to understand chang es in demand,
integrated channels around the customer, adapted their offerings to new needs and adopted
an agile approach based on rapid experimentation [6, 16]. These re sponses are consistent
with the broader view that digital transformation involves changes in strategy, o rganizational
structure and capabilities, not only the adoption of technology [16].

3.4 ORGANIZATIONAL CAPABILITIES FOR ADAPTATION
The literature converges on the idea that technology alone does not prod uce results.
Digital transformation requires digital resources, but also organizational structures, skills and
decision processes that allow the firm to use them effectively [16]. In marketing, this includes
the ability to collect and analyze customer data, coordinate teams across cha nnels and
translate insights into action [1, 17].
Leeflang and colleagues identified, even before the pandemic, a  talent gap in
analytical capabilities and the difficulty of measuring the effectiveness of digital actio ns as
major challenges for marketing in the digital era [1]. The pandemic made  these challenges
more urgent. Firms that had invested in analytical capabilities could detect changes in
demand quickly and redirect investments, whereas those that depended on intuition and fixed
annual plans struggled to respond [5, 17].

3.5 PRACTICAL IMPLICATIONS FOR SALES GROWTH
For managers, the findings suggest a sequence of practical steps. First, the firm sho uld
understand its customers’ current journey, identifying which channels they use at each stage
and where friction occurs [8]. Second, it should integrate channels so that the cu stomer
experiences a single brand, regardless of the touchpoint [9]. Third, it shou ld strengthen its
digital presence in the spaces where customers search and interact, including search
engines, social networks and marketplaces [3, 12]. Fourth, it should establish indicators that
link marketing actions to commercial outcomes, allowing continuous adjustment [17].
These steps are accessible to firms of different sizes. For SMEs, the priority is usually to
establish basic digital sales and service channels and to use simple customer da ta
consistently, before advancing to more sophisticated tools [15]. The literature also c autions
that the adaptation must preserve the human dimension of the relationsh ip: technology
should facilitate, not replace, trust and empathy in customer interactions [14, 18].

3.6 LIMITATIONS
The studies reviewed cover different sectors, countries and stages of the pande mic,
and many were published while the situation was still evolving. Consequently, the persistence
of specific behaviors may vary by market and product category. The conclusions should

therefore be interpreted as general strategic orientations rather than precise forecasts for any
particular industry.

4 CONCLUSION
The post-pandemic scenario is characterized by a hybrid consumer who combines
physical and digital channels, relies heavily on online information and peer opinions, and
expects consistent, convenient experiences throughout the customer journey. The pandemic
accelerated these trends, and a significant part of the habits formed durin g that period has
persisted.
Firms that wish to sustain and increase sales in this environment need to treat d igital
marketing as part of a broader business transformation. Integrating channels, understanding
the customer journey, building analytical capabilities and adopting agile, tes t-and-learn
approaches are more decisive than adopting any single platform. Future  research should
examine, with longitudinal data, which post-pandemic behaviors remain  stable in different
sectors and how SMEs can develop digital marketing capabilities with limited resources.
