Abstract
The retail industry is receiving more attention than ever in the context of corporate social
responsibility (CSR), when customer trust and loyalty towards retail systems become more fragile.
The study aims to investigate the impact of CSR on customer loyalty (CL) via customer experience
(EXP) and perceived satisfaction (PS), thereby decoding what is happening in the minds of
customers when they encounter CSR information. Additionally, this study examines the moderating
effects of brand authenticity (BA) on the associations in the conceptual model in the context of CSR.
By analyzing data from 332 customers from retail systems, including supermarkets, shopping malls,
and retail agents in Southern Vietnam, the study conducted measurement and structural model
evaluation using SmartPLS software to explain the connections in the conceptual model. The
research findings show that CSR is positively connected with PS and both EXP attributes, utilitarian
experience (UE) and HE (hedonic experience). EXP is highly associa ted with PS, while PS is
positively connected to CL, proving PS's mediatory role. Notably, BA moderates most of the
relationships in the theoretical model, except for the relationship between CSR and EXP. The
research results provide a clearer understandin g of the mechanism of CL formation, enabling
managers to develop effective policies related to CSR and BA activities at current retail systems.

1- Introduction
The explosion of digital platforms has dramatically changed consumer shopping behavior and loyalty to retail brands
[1]. Shopping via apps has become increasingly popular in most countries, especially in emerging economies like
Vietnam, following the Covid-19 pandemic [2]. Although the form of direct purchasing has existed for a long time, the
appearance of online purchasing has created a significant shake -up in terms of trust and brand loyalty among retail
customers [3-5]. Retail systems encompass the entire network, processes, and technologies connecting manufacturers to
end consumers, including the supply chain, wholesalers/distributors, retailers (stores, supermarkets, online), and
management software to efficiently deliver products to customers, meeting  diverse shopping needs, from traditional
stores to modern models [6]. To retain customers, retailers not only pay attention to their competitiveness [7] but also
demonstrate to customers their social responsibility [3, 5]. In practice, buyers can readily forsake their familiar consumer
brand if they encounter a product or service that they believe is superior to similar products or services, and is consistent
with the company's commitments [8]. Notably, most retail brands make announcements about corporate  social
responsibility (CSR) to their customers, but they rarely address problems with customers' product and service
experiences because customers often ignore them when they find the complaint process cumbersome [9]. Additionally,
firms typically disclose their CSR voluntarily, and the verification of these standards is contentious due to the various

Former researchers have contributed a variety of views and situations to explore the link between CSR and CL [12,
13]. Previous research on the nexus between CSR and CL often applies background theories such as Stakeholder theory
[12, 14, 15], Social identity theory [13, 16, 17], and Signaling theory [12, 15, 18], and has the similarity of considering
CSR as an independent variable, while CL is the dependent variable [19 -21]. On the other hand, these s tudies mostly
focus on intermediary mechanisms [ 22-25] while limiting the exploration of moderating mechanisms [26, 27]. The
mediating factors mentioned for the relationship between CSR and CL are customer satisfaction (CS) [22-25], customer
trust (CT) [14, 25, 28], corporate image (CI) [17, 29, 30], and corporate reputation (CR) [23, 25, 31]. According to Zhou
et al. [32], CS is the intersection between customer expectations (CE) and their perceptions of the utilitarian value they
experience, while perceived satisfaction (PS) is the intersection of CE and psychological experiences (e.g., emotional or
hedonic experience). Notably, previous studies rarely address the EXP factor or do not fully represent the attributes of
EXP related to utilitarian and hedonic (emotional) experiences under the impact of CSR [28], leading to CL. According
to Yen & Chen [33], CSR significantly impacts CL through customer moral identity, service quality, and relationship
quality; however, the processes by which CSR affects consume r loyalty may be more complex, involving other aspects
such as values, risk perceptions, and emotions. In line with the findings mentioned above, the research gap pertains to
the relationship between EXP, which includes both utilitarian and hedonic values, and PS, influencing CL in the context
of CSR.
In another approach to CL, brand authenticity (BA) and brand experience are considered factors that have a direct
positive impact on CL [34]. According to Tran [35], BA is considered one of the factors affecti ng the relationship
between customer recovery satisfaction and attitude loyalty. Up to now, no study has examined the role of BA in the
context of CSR, leading to CL. On the other hand, the moderating mechanism in studies on the impact of CSR on CL is
currently quite modest and less explored. Ghaderi et al. [36] argued that CSR significantly influences CL via CT and
CS; nevertheless, later research can investigate these dimensions in relation to concerns such as return visits or
difficulties with the brand, including corporate image and brand attitude. In line with this, Nguyen et al. [37] called for
papers on CL (e.g., intention to reorder) to consider brand -related factors such as brand image, brand equity, and brand
loyalty. Therefore, the research gap is  identified as the role of brand (e.g., BA) in the context of CSR, especially the
moderating role of BA leading to the formation of CL.
To address the above research gaps, this study aims to investigate the impact of CSR on EXP and PS, leading to CL.
In addition, this study examines the moderating effects of BA on the associations between CSR and EXP, CSR and PS,
EXP and PS, and PS and CL to clarify the role of BA and enhance the explanatory power of the mechanism of CL
formation in the context of CSR. In doing so, the social cognitive theory (SCT) of Bandura [38], the social identity
theory (SIT) of Tajfel et al. [39], and the signaling theory (ST) of Spence [40] were applied in this study to clarify the
impact of CSR on both EXP and PS, leading to CL. In addition, EXP was built based on the perspective of Ponsignon
[41], including utilitarian experience (UE) and hedonic experience (HE)  [41, 42]. From the perspective of Ponsignon
[41], PS, adapted from Pham et al. [43], enables explaining individual satisfaction in terms of both actual and emotional
experiences. Consequently, the research findings are anticipated to clarify how CL formation occurs through the
mediation of PS and EXP within the framework of CSR, thereby contributing to the sustainable develop ment of
enterprises via the relationship between CSR and EXP in contemporary retail systems.
The remainder of this study comprises five Sections. Section 2 is the theoretical basis, which includes the theories
applied in the study, definitions related to the concepts in the theoretical model, and the development of hypotheses.
Section 3 outlines the research methodology, encompassing data collection, scale development, and data analysis
techniques. Section 4 presents the research results. Section 5 present s the discussion, including both academic and
practical contributions, while Section 6 provides the conclusion, limitations, and further research directions.
2- Theoretical Framework and Hypothesis Development
2-1- Theoretical Framework
The social cognitive theory (SCT) of Bandura [38] was developed to explain individual behavior via social influences
(SI) and individual perception (including rational and emotional aspects). In the context of CL, CSR is viewed as an
environmental factor that influences individual p erception, leading to customer loyalty behavior [ 44]. Although this
theory has not been widely applied in the CSR context, its relevance to explaining individual behavior has been
demonstrated [45]. However, CSR disclosures are voluntary, and many stakehol ders influence the recognition of these
standards. As a result, viewing CSR as a contextual factor influencing customer perceptions and CL is not enough, so in
addition to SCT theory, it is necessary to combine it with some other theories to build a more p erfect mechanism to
explain CL in the context of CSR.

ST was developed by Spence [40] to clarify information asymmetry in the labor market. In the marketing context, it
was developed and used to explain the benefits of information symmetry and messages [40]. Signals are often expressed
through social responsibility, product/service quality, and branding [46]. ST suggests that being responsible can make a
company perceived as trustworthy by sending signals about corporate fairness and responsibility [ 47]. Wh en firms
allocate resources to CSR initiatives (e.g., education, health), these activities act as signals to customers about the
organization, promoting long-term loyalty [21, 24]. Unlike ST, SIT, with its main factors including social categorization,
social identification, and social comparison, allows for explaining the process by which individuals feel connected to a
firm's CSR disclosure and what they actually experience, shedding light on CL [14, 17].
According to Kotler & Armstrong [48], “loyalty is the attitude of consumers who are satisfied with a service and
purchase products/services repeatedly and repeatedly”. In line with this, Alves et al. [ 49] indicate that “CL is the
commitment of a customer to buy at a particular store”. In other words, reta iler loyalty happens if a customer makes
regular purchases at the same retail location and promotes the shop to their colleagues [ 50]. In the current study, we
developed the CL based on Oliver’s perspective [ 51]. CL is defined as “a deep commitment to cons istently repurchase
a preferred product/service in the future, which leads to repeated purchases of the same or similar brand, despite
situational influences and marketing efforts that have the potential to cause switching behavior” [51]. In the context of
CSR, most previous research focuses on the intermediary mechanisms that lead to CL [15, 22-25, 31]. The similarities
in previous studies also stem from the mediating mechanisms for the relationship between CSR and CL, and these
mediating factors mainly fo cus on CS [ 22-25], CT [14, 25, 28], CI [17, 29, 30], and CR [23, 25, 31]. Notably, studies
that focus on the mediating role of CS for the nexus between CSR and CL often fail to capture the process by which
individuals experience a product or service in its hedonic aspect, instead primarily focusing on the utilitarian aspect [22-
25, 28]. On the other hand, the differences in CL research primarily focus on the attributes that constitute CSR [14, 22,
24, 26]. The reason for the differences in CSR attributes st ems from the research contexts related to CL, such as the
jewelry industry [52], the banking industry [15], the pharmaceutical industry [14], and the hospitality industry [42].
CSR was first mentioned by former scholars, such as Van Marrewijk [53], Hopkins [54], and Dahlsrud [55], with five
dimensions: environmental, social, economic, stakeholder, and voluntariness. According to Van Marrewijk [ 53], CSR
refers to “voluntary company initiatives that demonstrate the integration of social and environmental issues into business
operations and stakeholder relations”. Fatma et al. [ 56] indicated that CSR is “a concept in which firms voluntarily
incorporate social and environmental issues into their business operations and interactions with their stakeholders”.
Literature indicates that definitions of CSR share certain similarities in meaning, but the dimensions may vary depending
on the research context, such as the inclusion of all five dimensions mentioned above or fewer [55]. In the current study,
the voluntary d imension can be reduced because CSR disclosure is almost always a voluntary behavior of enterprises
[56]. In line with this, we identified four CSR dimensions based on the perspective of Dahlsrud [ 55]:
 Environmental CSR (ENV) refers to “the organization’s responsibility to protect and preserve the natural
environment and promote green behaviors”.
 Social CSR (SOC) refers to “the responsibility of the organization to have a positive impact on society”.
 Stakeholder CSR (STA) refers to “the organization’s responsibility to all stakeholders , such as customers,
employees, shareholders, investors, government, community, and the environment”.
 Economic CSR (ECO) refers to “the organization’s obligation to generate profits for the shareholders and
investors”.
EXP is viewed as a holistic interaction process, driven by physical and emotional experiences, mediated by customer
and contextual characteristics, which creates unique, enjoyable/unforgettable memories [ 57]. Meyer & Schwager [58]
indicated that “EXP” is “the subjective response of customers to direct or indirect contact with a business, comprising
all areas of the service: customer care, promotion, packaging, features, simplicity of use, and dependability”. In the
context of CSR, Cuesta -Valiño et al. [28] ment ioned EXP but limited it to the practical experience aspects rather than
the emotional ones, which positively and negatively impacted CS and CL, respectively. Based on the perspective of
Ponsignon [41], previous research on EXP has typically explored utili tarian and hedonic services separately as two
distinct types of environments that provide radically different forms of client experiences. In the current study, we
developed EXP as a unified mechanism, encompassing both UE and HE, which is directly affecte d by CSR related to
the products and services that customers use. The reason for constructing a second -order construct for EXP is that EXP
provides a more complete conceptual explanation while still keeping the customer clearly aware of each attribute of EXP
when answering questions.
PS refers to “a psychological experience in which a client perceives the degree to which a product's or service's stated,
frequently assumed, or necessary performance is met” [32, 51]. PS can also be related to intangible issue s, such as
feelings of happiness related to the service experience [32]. Since prior research has consistently utilized CS instead of
PS in the context of CSR, attention to PS-related elements has been confused. Likewise, in terms of definition, CS refers
to how effectively certain products or services align with CE [59], while PS refers to not only CE but also the perceived

values of UE and HE [32]. Although no study has highlighted PS in the context of CSR leading to CL, this approach is
entirely appropriate and relevant for investigating the impact of CSR, EXP on CL via the mediating role of PS.
According to Beverland [60], authenticity is one of the fundamentals of current advertising techniques. Authenticity
is a crucial part of developing and maintaini ng a successful brand, as it produces a distinct brand identity [10] and
establishes a strong link, fostering a favorable relationship [ 61]. In line with this, BA refers to “the extent to which
consumers perceive a brand to be faithful and true toward itself and its consumers, and to support consumers being true
to themselves” [62, 63]. In the context of CSR, BA has functioned as a dependent variable, influenced directly by service
quality [ 64]. In another approach, BA mediates the relationship between orga nizational purpose and stakeholder
perception of a conscientious corporate brand, with CSR acting as a moderator for this relationship in the model [ 65].
Although BA has been examined in the context of CSR, no study has investigated the moderating effects of BA on the
direct relationships between CSR and EXP, between EXP and PS, or between PS and CL.
To address the shortcomings of previous studies in the context of CSR, this study examines the impact of CSR on CL
via the mediating roles of EXP and PS in the retail system. CSR was adapted from Van Huy et al. [19] with four attributes
such as SOC, ENV, STA, and ECO. EXP was developed based on the viewpoint of Ponsignon [41] and inherited from
Yoo et al. [66] with two attributes, UE and HE. Notably, PS, adapted from Zhou et al. [32], is considered a mediator for
the associations between CSR and CL, as well as between EXP and CL. In addition, BA, adapted from Tran [35], is
considered to have moderating impacts on the relationships in the conceptual model, opening  up a new approach in the
context of CSR. In terms of the theoretical approach, this study combines the ST, SIT, and SCT to explain the process
by which customers become loyal to retail systems. The ST paves the way for shaping input signals, specifically CSR,
delivering positive messages from these organi zations regarding SOC, ENV, STA, and ECO. The SCT serves as the
foundation for the social awareness process, which begins with CSR messages that lead to EXP and PS, ultimately
resulting in loyal behavior. Furthermore, the SIT serves as the foundation for constructing the BA element, which is a
factor related to the social perception of image and quality, with the capacity to influence other relationships within the
conceptual model.

enhancing EXP. Although no study has examined the connection between CSR and PS, numerous scholars have found
that CSR has a positive connection with CS [24, 68, 69]. Based on these findings, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H2: CSR is positively associated with PS;
As mentioned, EXP is the subjective response of customers when they directly or indirectly interact with a b usiness
[58]. Consumers have the opportunity to access products and experience them in their own way, from which they can
have perceptions of product satisfaction and brand prejudice [70]. Additionally, HE refers to the joy and enjoyment that
customers derive from using a product/service [71]. Therefore, there is evidence to suggest that EXP will have a positive
impact on PS.

3-1- Data Collection and Measurement Scale
The current study selected customers from retail systems such as supermarkets, shopping malls, or retail agents as
survey subjects. Respondents were presented with a number of familiar retail brands and published CSRs related to these
retail systems. The survey link was then sent directly to participants and immediately administered with the guidance of
interviewers. All questionnaires were translated into Vietnamese via an interpreter with a bachelor's degree in English
to e nsure the accuracy of the questionnaire items. Subsequently, the questionnaire was also translated back from
Vietnamese to English by a member of the research team with a bachelor's degree in English to verify any discrepancies
in the content. Using the convenience sampling method, the survey was conducted at several retail systems in southern
Vietnam. Regarding retail systems, the research focuses on selecting large regional systems with a certain degree of
similarity in scale, services, and products to ensure comprehensive customer experiences. Besides, this study applied the
ten times rule of Hair et al. [ 72] to estimate the sample size. In line with this, the current study approached more than
400 consumers across five retail systems and collected 332 va lid responses. Respondents are clearly and specifically
informed about the risks associated with participating in the survey and can request to stop the survey at any time. Survey
participant information is presented in Table 1.

3-2- Analytical Process
To ensure the feasibility of the theoretical model and answer the hypotheses, evaluation  of the measurement and
structural models is necessary. According to Hair et al. [ 72], “convergent validity (outer loading ≥ 0.708; AVE ≥ 0.5),
composite reliability (CR ≥ 0.6 or Cronbach’s alpha ≥ 0.7), and discriminant validity (Heterotrait -monotrait rat io
results)” are the criteria that need to be evaluated in the measurement model. Next, a check for common method biases
(CMBs) should be performed before evaluating the structural model. Specifically, if the VIF values are less than or equal
to 3.3, the m odel does not have multicollinearity and is not violated. To evaluate the structural model, the 5000
bootstrapping procedure will be performed to assess direct, indirect, and moderating relationships [ 72].

and CL (βCSR  EXP  PS  CL = 0.027; βCSR  PS  CL = 0.047, p < 0.05). Similarly, the positive indirect impact of EXP
on CL is confirmed (β EXP  PS  CL = 0.082, p < 0.05). Similar to the direct effects, the confidence ranges from 2.5%
to 97.5% all have scores that do not go through 0, indicating that the mediating connections in this model were
significant.
Regarding the moderating impacts, BA was expected to moderate all the connections in the conceptual model.
The research findings in Table 6 indicated that BA did not moderate the relationship between CSR and EXP (p >
0.05), while it moderated other relationships, such as between CSR and PS (β BA x CSR  PS = -0.157, p < 0.05), EXP
and PS (βBA x EXP  PS = 0.175, p < 0.05), and PS and CL (β BA x PS  CL = -0.070, p < 0.05). Therefore, the hypotheses
(H5b, H5c, H5d) were supported, while H5a was rejected. Additionally, Figure 4 shows that BA weakens the
connection between CSR and PS, while Figure 5 shows that BA also stren gthens the connection between EXP and
PS. Similarly, BA also weakens the connection between PS and CL in Figure 6, but to a lesser degree compared to
the relationship between CSR and PS .

5- Discussion
By integrating the SCT, ST, and SIT to explain the CL in the context of CSR for current retail systems in Vietnam, a
comprehensive theoretical model has been provided to address the current research gaps. Likewise, this approach is
appropriate when the explanatory level of the variables in the theoretical model for CL is acceptable (R2 = 54,1%). This
study has shed light on the process by which customers become loyal to retailers through EXP and PS, influenced by
CSR, as well as the moderating role of BA. To clarify the research findings, this discussion will focus on the direct,
indirect, and moderating effects in the conceptual model.
In terms of the direct effects, H1 and H2 were supported, demonstrating the positive impacts of CSR on both EXP (β
= 0.333, p < 0.05) and PS (β = 0.232, p < 0.05). These results demonstrate that all CSR attributes, including SOC, STA,
ECO, and ENV, significantly influence UE and HE of customers as well as their PS. Compared with previous research,
the positive connection between CSR and EXP is similar to the results of Cuesta-Valiño et al. [28], but the current study
is more conceptually complete in considering four aspects of CSR (SOC, ENV, ECO, and STA) and two aspects of EXP
(HE and UE). Specifically, CSR in the study by Pham & Nguyen [15] only considers aspects of ENV or STA, without
considering other aspects such as SOC or ECO. Likewise, Cuesta-Valiño et al. [28] only address the utilitarian aspect of
EXP without considering the emotional aspect of customers in relation to CSR. Notably, previous research has primarily
focused on the impact of CSR on CS rather than PS [48, 68]; however, the positive relationship between CSR and PS is
consistent. The highlight of this approach is considering PS instead of CS, which enables us to explain the impacts of
two attributes of EXP (HE and UE) on PS within the context of CSR. Similarly, H3 and H4 were supported,
demonstrating the positive impacts of EXP on PS (β = 0.400, p < 0.05) and PS on CL (β = 0.204, p < 0.05).
These findings demonstrate that EXPs, incl uding UE and HE, both have a significant positive impact on customer
perception, emphasising the relevance of emotional aspects to PS rather than just UE. Compared to the study by Pham
and Nguyen [15], the research results are consistent with the positive impact of UE on PS; however, the present study
provides more comprehensive information on the properties of EXP, especially HE. On the other hand, PS has a positive
relationship with CL, indicating that customers are willing to continue returning to retail systems if they are truly satisfied
with CEs through CSR-related activities, as well as EXP, in both utilitarian and hedonic aspects. The positive connection
between PS and CL is completely consistent with previous studies, despite the somewhat different research contexts [22-
25]. Therefore, it can be concluded that PS is a perfect link to demonstrate the important role of experience for CL in the
context of CSR in current retail systems.
Related to the indirect effects, EXP acts as a partial mediator for the impact of CSR on PS (β CSREXPPS = 0.133,
p < 0.05). Likewise, PS mediates the connections between EXP and CL (β EXP  PS  CL = 0.082, p < 0.05) and between
CSR and CL (β CSR  EXP  PS  CL = 0.027; β CSR  PS  CL = 0.047, p < 0.05). These findings demonstrate that EXP
significantly enhances PS, while PS enhances CL in the CSR context, and are fully consistent with previous research
results examining the mediating roles of EXP and PS in the connection between CSR and CL [19, 23, 24, 28]. Notably,
the current research findings demonstrate that PS not only directly influences CL but also mediates the indirect effects
of UE and HE on CL.
As previously noted, moderating mechanisms in CL studies focusing on CSR are limited. In the current study, BA is
viewed as a moderating variable in the conceptual model, and the research findings support this. H5b was supported,
confirming that BA weakens the connection between CSR and PS (β BA x CSR  PS = -0.157, p < 0.05).  According to the
research results, BA has a positive direct impact on PS (β = 0.274, p < 0.05) while negatively moderating the connection
between CSR and PS. To explain this outcome, the practice shows that genuine product brands s old at retail systems
easily satisfy user needs; however, unauthenticated or non -genuine brands weaken the CSR that this point of sale
announces to increase PS. H5c was supported, confirming that BA strengthens the connection between EXP and PS (βBA
x EXP  PS = 0.175, p < 0.05). Accordingly, if a brand is authenticated (e.g., a genuine brand), the relationship between
EXP (UE and HE) and PS will be strengthened. H5d was supported, confirming that BA weakens the connection between
PS and CL (β BA x PS  CL = -0.070, p < 0.05). Although BA moderates the connection between PS and CL, this finding
differs from earlier research, which suggests that BA enhances the link between customer recovery satisfaction and
attitude loyalty [35]. The distinction in the moderating effect of BA on the connection between PS and EXP of the current
study compared to previous research is due to the difference in research context, for example, a study of service failure
in the airline industry [35] versus this study on CSR in retail systems.
Theoretically, the study contributes to further refining current theories of CL in the context of CSR. The study
offers a more comprehensive understanding of CL creation under the influence of CSR, encompassing both
mediating and moderating mech anisms. Approaching the research in this manner helps to fill existing gaps in
research on the mediating functions of EXP and PS under the influence of CSR, leading to the formation of CL. The
highlight of the study comes from two attributes of EXP that le ad to PS, emphasizing the hedonic aspect, while
other studies often only consider the utilitarian aspect [28]. Besides, PS again acts as a mediator for the effects of
both CSR and EXP on CL, emphasizing the role of perception leading to return or reorder b ehavior (loyalty). This
mediating role of PS in the connection between CSR and CL is consistent with previous research, such as Chi &

Phan [22], Ali et al. [23]. According to the SCT of Bandura [38], CSR not only acts as an independent variable but
also co ntributes to individual cognition (EXP and PS), leading to behavior (CL). Notably, considering BA as a
moderating element in the conceptual model is a novel approach  that explains why, despite retail systems'
announcements of CSR to improve PS, certain circumstances create unexpected results. BA also helps explain why,
in different industries, the connection between EXP and PS, or between PS and CL, changes depending on the level
of BA in the business. Compared to the research of Tran [35], this study indicates a similarity in the moderating role
of BA on customer satisfaction and loyalty; however, it reveals that BA weakens the relationship between PS and
CL, whereas Tran [35] demonstrates that BA promotes this relationship.
Practically, research contributes to supporting managers in building CL via CSR and BA activities. Undoubtedly, all
four CSR attributes are positively correlated with EXP and PS. These findings demonstrate that publicizing or
strengthening CSR actions can assist organizations to boost EXP and PS, ultimately leading to CL. Retailers need to pay
attention to the alignment between CSR and EXP disclosures, considering both utilitarian and hedonic aspects. In
addition, brand authentication is necessary because it not only positively impacts EX P, PS, and CL but also contributes
to moderating the nexuses between CSR and PS, EXP and PS, and PS and CL. In alignment with these findings, some
implications were proposed for retail systems:
 CSR activities must include four aspects: social, economic, environmental, and stakeholder, in which the adoption
of these standards must include the participation of customers or users.
 Create a customer feedback channel for retail systems to comment or complain about the CSR after experiencing
the product or service.
 Conduct customer satisfaction surveys related to CSR and EXP after customers use or purchase products/services
 Brands in retail systems must be authenticated, and attention must be paid to the level of authenticity through
evaluation criteria that involve customers.
6- Conclusion
In accordance with the initial objectives of addressing the current gaps of  CL in the context of CSR, the study
successfully modelled CL using mediation and moderation mechanisms. The theories employed in the study are
congruent with the inheritance and development of concepts linked to CSR, EXP, and PS. The development of EXP
attributes, such as UE and HE, makes a significant addition to our understanding of the connection between CSR and
PS, which leads to CL. This outcome enables  managers to gain a deeper understanding of what is going
through customers' minds in response to CSR announcements from enterprises, as well as the experiences customers
have. Notably, BA significantly moderates the relationships in the model, opening up new perspectives in the CSR
context, leading to CL. These findings provide practical implications for managers to develop appropriate policies,
including activities related to CSR, EXP, and PS, to enhance CL.
The limitations of the study are also clarified  alongside the contributions. First, the study was cross-sectional, which
has certain limitations in predicting CL in current retail systems. Second, the small sample size of the study did not
significantly affect the estimates; however, future studies sho uld expand the sample size to re -evaluate the variables in
the conceptual model. Third, the study limited the moderating variable to BA, while other factors, such as control
variables, service quality, brand image, and reputation, require further exploration. Finally, the study focuses on the retail
sector; further research is necessary to expand the field and validate the conceptual model .
