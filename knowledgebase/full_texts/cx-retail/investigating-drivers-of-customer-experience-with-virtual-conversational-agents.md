Abstract
Building on the perspectives of the uses & gratification (U&G) theory and stimulus-organism-response (S–O-R) model, this
article develops and tests an integrative framework to examine the underlying factors influencing customers’ experiences
with chatbots as a form of virtual conversational agent (VCA) in the UK and Vietnam. In addition to utilitarian and hedonic
factors, anthropomorphism and social presence are also investigated, which are considered important experiential dimen-
sions in a customer-machine relationship. We also explore how stimuli such as functionality, communication style similarity,
and aesthetics indirectly affect outcomes like customer satisfaction and reuse intention, mediated by four types of customer
experiences. Data collected from a sample of 417 and 359 participants in the UK and Vietnam respectively revealed that, in
general, perceived informativeness, credibility, enjoyment, functionality, and communication style similarity are crucial for
customer satisfaction in both countries. Interesting differences in the effects of customer experience between developed and
developing countries were observed. For instance, the effects of anthropomorphism and social presence on satisfaction are
only effective for customers from developed country, while those from developing country only need information provided
by chatbots be transparent. Our findings offer a novel way to understand customer experience with chatbots and provide
important theoretical and managerial implications.
Keywords Artificial intelligence · Chatbot · Virtual Conversational Agent · Customer experience · Anthropomorphism ·
Social presence
1 Introduction
Diverse applications of artificial intelligence (AI) in busi-
ness and social endeavors ranging from autonomous cars
(Goodall, 2014) to caring for an aging society (Wu et al.,
2014) have spurred interest among marketing researchers in
exploring various facets of human-AI interactions (Huang &
Rust, 2018). The quality of such interactions shape customer
experience, which acts as a fundamental premise shaping
customer loyalty and augmenting business competitiveness
in today’s rapidly evolving marketplace (Becker & Jaakkola,
2020). In customer service research, it has been well-estab-
lished that positive experience can be triggered not just by
frontline human employees and surrounding environment,
but also by appropriate technology interfaces (e.g. self-ser -
vice machine, AI-enabled tools, etc.) (Verhoef et al., 2009).

Delivering superior customer experiences by using various
AI-based applications demand a paradigm shift in our think-
ing and business practices by moving away from viewing
them as merely autonomous tools to seeing them as intel-
ligent teammates and partners (Ling et al., 2021). AI-enabled
technologies appear in several forms, including voice user
interface (e.g. Siri), embodied conversational agent (e.g. ser-
vice robot), and text-based virtual conversational agent (VCA)
(e.g. chatbots), which are increasingly used by several service
providers across the globe (e.g. booking.com, Skyscanner,
Amazon), etc. A VCA, or a chatbot, can be simply understood
as an online robot that can interact with customers just like
a human agent (Mauldin, 1994), and it is able to learn and
respond to users via text based on algorithms evolving with
experience. Chatbots can handle repetitive tasks faster than
frontline employees, and their 24/7 availability and instant
response were reported to significantly reduce customer wait-
ing time, create seamless experience, and enhance customer
satisfaction (Behera et al., 2024; Shweta, 2022). With such
benefits, it was reported that about 2 billion customer interac-
tions were handled by chatbots from June 2023 to May 2024
(Livechat, 2024) while 64% of service agents had more time
solving complex cases (Salesforce, 2019). In a survey con-
ducted by Gartner (2022), it was observed that over half of the
surveyed customers had already interacted with VCAs during
the information-seeking process, and it was predicted that a
quarter of organizations will adopt this AI technology by 2027
to improve customer experience and achieve cost benefits.
Designing new interactive technologies usually required
designers to overcome multiple challenges in regards with
user adoption, therefore, developers and industry practition-
ers need to understand the motivation why people use spe-
cific technology (Malhotra et al. , 2008). Previous studies
have investigated benefit-related aspects (e.g. productivity
and convenience, etc.), and inquisitive interest (e.g. curios-
ity and social influence, etc.) as the main motivations of
chatbots use (Brandtzaeg & Følstad, 2017; Cheng & Jiang,
2020; Rese et al., 2020), however, they have largely ignored
myriad customer experiences affecting customer satisfaction
derived from using such VCAs that ultimately determine
reuse intention (Mostafa & Kasamani, 2022). Prior studies
have once emphasized hedonic and utilitarian elements of
customer experience as motivation to use chatbots (Rizo-
myliotis et al., 2022; Rzepka et al., 2022). However, they do
not always hold true, for example, hedonic elements (e.g.
enjoyment) in chatbots are not the main elements leading to
positive customer responses because AI cannot effectively
capture ones’ emotions during information seeking process
(Longoni & Cian, 2022 ; Zhang et al., 2022 ). In addition,
customers expect informativeness (an important constructs
of utilitarian experience) from chatbots, however, too much
information could lead to information overload (Ramesh &
Chawla, 2022).
VCAs embedded with intuitive intelligence are increas -
ingly replacing frontline human employees in many fields
(Huang & Rust, 2018). Yet, the literature has not adequately
considered the characteristics seemingly unique to humans
(i.e., anthropomorphism and social presence) of this AI-
powered tool which are important dimensions of customer
experience leading to customer satisfaction (Bleier et al.,
2019). While Blut et al. (2021) argued that anthropomor -
phism is key for customer satisfaction, and Lu et al. (2024)
encouraged enabling chatbots to behave in as human-like a
manner as possible, it is not always true (Ramesh & Chawla,
2022). In particular, individuals might experience negative
emotions when chatbots too closely resemble human (Ciech-
anowski et al., 2019), and adding more human-like factors to
AI-powered tool can trigger fear of social judgement (Kim
et al., 2022a, 2022b). Additionally, the importance of social
presence was acknowledged, however, its effects are not con-
sistent. For instance, social presence enhances positive cus-
tomer responses (Konya-Baumbach et al., 2023) while it does
not trigger customers’ favorable behavior (Li & Mao, 2015).
In line with previous studies (Ahn et al., 2022; Lemon &
Verhoef, 2016; Li & Mao, 2015), it is important to under -
stand various drivers that influence diverse customer expe-
riences, as they determine how people feel and think when
encountering a technology. From an in-depth literature review,
we observed that functionality and aesthetics are important
stimuli of customer experiences within a tech-based setting
that have never been explored in previous studies focusing on
VCAs (Huang & Rust, 2018; Kim et al., 2020; Lee, 2022; Lin
& Hsieh, 2011). Further, while communication style similar-
ity was indicated as one of the potential drivers of utilitar -
ian and hedonic experience (Li & Mao, 2015), its impact on
social presence remains less understood (Bleier et al., 2019).
Lastly, research by Li and Mao (2015) was conducted within
a health-related context in China, which triggers inquiries
about whether these effects stay unchanged in commercial
customer service contexts in a different culture (e.g. the UK).
In particular, how customers accept the information being
delivered is heavily influenced by the endorsers (Kwon et al.,
2015), therefore, we anticipate that experience (e.g. informa-
tion credibility, transparency, etc.) a person receives while
interacting with a chatbot acting as a medical adviser differs
from a chatbot functioning as a retail store staff member. Fur-
ther, because of differences in cultural orientation (Frost et al.,
2010), we also believe that Chinese respondents (collectiv -
ists) in Li and Mao (2015) had higher trust in their in-group
human community, rather than feeling the need to build rela-
tionships with a chatbot, which explains the neutral effects
of social presence in their research. In contrast, this effect is
more likely to be positive for individualist customers (e.g. the
UK) because they tend to interact with technology slightly
differently to meet their personal needs (Winsted, 1999). For
those reasons, we need to test the constructs adopted from

Li and Mao (2015) to compare results in different context as
well as between developed and developing countries. Despite
increasing attention being paid to the potential influence of
the physical and non-physical aspects of anthropomorphism,
whether communication style similarity is an important ante-
cedent of anthropomorphism has also not been confirmed to
date (Blut et al., 2021).
Lastly, but most importantly, past studies investigating
VCAs have largely focused on developed countries (e.g.
the US), often ignoring customers residing in developing
countries. In particular, Blut et al. (2021) found positive
effects of anthropomorphism using data from the US via
meta-analysis, however, they suggested primary data from
other countries should be collected for comparison, because
customers from different countries experience AI differently.
Charles et al. (2024) also called for more research on the
adoption of AI in emerging economies to offer rich insights
from different countries to marketers operating in different
markets. Therefore, based on the differences in the way cus-
tomers are exposed to chatbots, the United Kingdom (UK)
was chosen as the representative for developed countries
on which hypotheses were established, and Vietnam was
selected as the representative for developing countries to
cross-validate the result. The UK, with its commitment
to leading the world in building AI-enabled technology,
has developed diverse applications of VCAs that offer the
potential to improve people’s lives, transforming industries
and delivering first-class public services (Kwarteng & Dor-
ries, 2021). In the UK, many organizations have developed
their VCAs to provide optimized service support (e.g. EDF
Energy, UPS, Vodafone, the NHS, and Barclays) helping
customers to interact with well-developed VCAs. With the
widespread use of the internet, developing countries like
Vietnam have more or less equal access to new technology,
and the use of AI-enabled tools such as VCAs is rapidly
gaining momentum (Government News, 2021; World Bank,
2021). Even though firms in Vietnam have started imple-
menting chatbots in various industries (Government News,
2021), this AI-powered tool is usually built upon, and its
features inherited from well-established platforms (e.g.,
Facebook Messenger, Instagram, and WhatsApp) rather than
being a standalone purpose-built application developed by
the local organization. In this instance, customers from these
two countries may interact with chatbots that may differ in
terms of features and application, making us to anticipate
that differences in findings between these two countries
may be observed (Elmashhara et al., 2024). Importantly,
Vietnam, with its high potential for economic growth, is
believed to be an important hotspot for information technol-
ogy outsourcing for the world, including the UK (Forbes,
2018; PwC, 2018).
In addition, cultural differences between the UK and Viet-
nam may also lead to different experiences with chatbots.
Research has proved that customers from developed coun-
tries tend to pay more attention to easy-to-use functions of
technology, while customers from developing countries are
likely to highly evaluate the usefulness of technology (Choi
& Totten, 2012). Furthermore, there is also a significant dif-
ference in how customers from different cultures process
information. In particular, Taylor et al. (1997) found that
customers from the US responded more favorably to con-
tent containing a high level of information while customers
from Korea are believed to rely more on the mood and tone
of the content. Additionally, Eastern customers tend to rely
more on the endorsers of the messages being delivered while
Western customers tend to treat information and endorsers
independently (Kwon et al., 2015). Thus, we expected that
users in the UK and Vietnam have different experiences
while using chatbots during information-seeking activities.
Studying chatbot user experience in these two countries
would help us to broaden our theoretical understanding
of AI and the customer experience in the wider context of
developed and developing countries (Rahman et al., 2017).
Overall, we have developed and empirically tested an
integrative framework to understand the drivers and effects
of customer experiential needs of a chatbot on customer
satisfaction ultimately leading to reuse intention, pull -
ing together the perspectives of the U&G theory and SOR
model, and applying it to the context of the UK and Viet-
nam. The article has four important contributions. Firstly,
taking cues from U&G theory, we have explored four main
dimensions of customer experience as motivations to use
chatbots rather than benefit-related needs and inquisitive
interest, which were previously investigated in the VCA
context (Brandtzaeg & Følstad, 2017; Cheng & Jiang, 2020;
Rese et al., 2020). Secondly, instead of merely looking at
customer experience from the unidimensional perspective
(Rizomyliotis et al., 2022), we expand insights into four
dimensions of customer experience (i.e., hedonic, utilitar -
ian, anthropomorphism, and social presence). Thirdly, we
utilized the SOR model to propose that the effects of func-
tionality, communication style similarity, and aesthetics on
customer satisfaction, and their relationships can be medi-
ated via four key customer experiences. Finally, interesting
similarities and differences between the UK and Vietnam
were discussed to provide insights to industry practitioners
and AI developers.
2  Theoretical development and hypotheses
2.1  Chatbot and customer experience
In the past few years, customer experience has become a
focus for academic researchers and industry practitioners,
as it drives the core competitiveness of firms and acts as the

1874 Information Systems Frontiers (2025) 27:1871–1897
fundamental basis for marketing decision-making (Becker &
Jaakkola, 2020). Lemon and Verhoef (2016) highlighted that
customer experience closely associates with service quality,
leading to increased customer satisfaction and loyalty. With
advancements in AI, researchers have shown greater inter -
est in investigating the role of chatbots in customer service
(Behera et al., 2024; Kushwaha & Kar., 2024; Rzepka et al.,
2022). At the same time, it is equivocally important to iden-
tify a set of stimuli that affect customer experience while
interacting with chatbots and their effects on subsequent
satisfaction as well as reuse intention.
This study adopted U&G theory to explain how four key
dimensions of customer experience influence customer sat-
isfaction with chatbots. U&G theory (Katz et al., 1973) has
been widely applied to understand the reasons individuals
seek to use a particular technology to satisfy their needs
(Rubin, 2009). According to Lin (1999), the concept of uses
deals with the type of media chosen for exposure (chatbot
in this context), while gratification describes cognitive and
emotional responses (utilitarian, hedonic, social presence,
and anthropomorphism for this study) of an individual pro-
voked from their actual uses of the chosen media (Weibull,
1985). Lin (1999) also highlighted that the level of individu-
als’ satisfaction is determined by gratifications sought and
obtained using specific media; therefore, overall satisfac -
tion and reuse intention will be used as the outcomes in this
study.
However, the literature noted that the U&G theory is
effective when exploring customers’ post-adoption behavior
(Nikhashemi et al., 2021). To understand antecedents driv -
ing such behavior, we used the SOR model as suggested by
Nikhashemi et al. (2021) to explain how functionality, com-
munication style similarity, and aesthetics work as stimuli
affecting four dimensions of customer experience, leading
to customer satisfaction. The SOR model was originally
propounded by Mehrabian and Russell (1974) to study how
specific cues (S) affect individuals’ cognition or emotion
(O) causing behavioral responses (R) (Bitner, 1992). In this
study, specific cues (S) are primary stimuli of customer
experiences (i.e. functionality, communication style simi -
larity, and aesthetics); individual’s emotion (O) is customer
experiences (i.e. utilitarian, hedonic, social presence, and
anthropomorphism); and behavioral responses (R) are cus-
tomer satisfaction and then reuse intention. The application
of the SOR model has the potential to offer both theoretical
and practical implications with regard to how chatbots may
be designed to trigger better customer experiences leading
to higher customer satisfaction and ultimately driving reuse
intention. Distinctive from Li and Mao (2015), who exam-
ined the direct effects of hedonic and utilitarian experiences
of dealing with virtual health advisory services on reuse
intention, the SOR model allows this study to specifically
focus on key features of chatbots (as stimuli) influencing
customer satisfaction (response) mediated via customer
experiences (organism). Figure  1 below presents the con-
ceptual model of the study.
2.1.1  Utilitarian and hedonic experience
Utilitarian and hedonic experiences are important fac-
tors driving user satisfaction within information systems
research (van der Heijden, 2004). Utilitarian experience
has been described as task-related and rational (Batra &
Ahtola, 1991), representing the cognitive aspect of user
attitude (Lim, 2014). Robey (1979) has propounded that,
if the technology is not usable, the adoption rate reduces
significantly regardless of how well other supportive market-
ing strategies are deployed. Researchers in both marketing
and information systems also explore the effects of hedonic,
which is usually characterized by experiences that are fun,

pleasant, and exciting (Lim, 2014). Here, we agree with the
views that hedonic experience should not be connected only
to temporal leisure activities and fun aspects but also results
in ultimately prolonged use of the system (Li & Mao, 2015;
van der Heijden, 2004). Based on the previous studies (Hor-
váth & Adıgüzel, 2018; McLean et al., 2018), we have taken
a multidimensional perspective for utilitarian and hedonic
experience as elucidated below.
Taking the perspective that the utilitarian aspect of VCAs
should provide users with relevant and reliable information
(Li & Mao, 2015 ), in this research, perceived credibility
(i.e. VCAs’ ability to deliver information which is believable
or worthy of trust) (Kim et al., 2022a, 2022b; Li & Mao,
2015) and perceived informativeness (i.e. VCAs’ ability to
deliver relevant information) (Li & Mao, 2015) are used
for measuring the utilitarian experience. In line with the
trust-commitment theory, Morgan and Hunt (1994) opined
that trust could enhance the confidence of a person during
communication, which therefore creates a positive relation-
ship between parties. In retailing, customers are more likely
to feel satisfied when frontline human employees provide
trustworthy information about products/services (Mohr &
Sohi, 1995). Importantly, customers also find it helpful to
use reliable information on social networking sites to sup-
port their decision-making (Asamoah & Sharda, 2021). Even
though the linkage between credibility and satisfaction was
not tested, Chakraborty et al. (2024) found that credibility
has positive impact on customers’ attitudes. Therefore, we
hypothesize that:
H1a Perceived credibility with chatbot positively influences
customer satisfaction.
In addition, we expect that the relevance of information is
a key to customer satisfaction with chatbots. This is congru-
ent with previous research that customers are more likely to
click on ads when the messages being delivered are person-
alized and relevant; otherwise, they tend to ignore the in-app
ad (Mattke et al., 2021). Similarly, during the information-
seeking process, customers are likely to look for content that
is closely connected to their needs (Sánchez-Franco et al.,
2019). Prior study already proved that information accuracy
could maximize customer satisfaction (Chung et al., 2020),
offering stronger evidence for perceived informativeness-
customer satisfaction relationship. Therefore, it is hypoth-
esized as below:
H1b Perceived informativeness with chatbot positively
influences customer satisfaction.
Empirical studies in both conventional and tech-based
service contexts often regard enjoyment as the singular
construct for hedonic experience (Childers et al., 2001 ).
However, hedonic experience is not only related to leisure
activities; it also aligns with engagement and transparency as
it encourages users to spend more time on information sys-
tems (Li & Mao, 2015; van der Heijden, 2004). Therefore,
we envisage hedonic experience much more broadly, not just
limited to enjoyment, but also including engagement and
transparency, which could play a significant role in human-
chatbot interactions.
Engagement is the perceived ability of the VCA to com-
municate well by acting and responding to users’ input
thoughtfully (Holtgraves et al., 2007). Engagement is con-
ceptually separate from flow experience, which has been
defined as a positive state that occurs when the performer
relates to performance (Csikszentmihalyi, 1997), and
includes three common elements: absorption, enjoyment,
and intrinsic motivation (Trivedi & Teichert, 2017). To date,
engagement with a chatbots’ avatars has been considered key
for customer satisfaction (Esmark Jones et al., 2022); how-
ever, little is known about the effect of engagement, which
can also be determined by linguistic cues (Kull et al., 2021),
on customer satisfaction. Ashley et al. (2011) believe that
engagement expresses the highest interest of a person toward
a product or service. In the VCA context, such interest could
arise from a chatbot’s intelligence and problem-solving abil-
ity via a text-based interface. Therefore, we hypothesize as
below:
H2a Perceived engagement with a chatbot positively influ-
ences customer satisfaction.
Customers are not only looking forward to performing
a specific tech-enabled task smoothly but also receiving a
fun and exciting experience (Horváth & Adıgüzel, 2018),
as these aspects provide additional value to customers
while getting tasks done (Kim et al., 2007). Enjoyment is
an important experience gained from interaction with tech-
nology, because it enables customers to continue with tasks
for longer and to explore new products as well as services
(Marinkovic & Kalinic, 2017). In the AI context, Li and Mao
(2015, p. 233) have already proved that perceived enjoy -
ment, which is defined as an “…affective belief that refers
to the extent to which the activity of using the system is
perceived to be enjoyable in its own right”, can motivate a
person to obtain new information and reuse VCAs. There-
fore, we hypothesize that:
H2b Perceived enjoyment with a chatbot positively influ-
ences customer satisfaction.
Transparency is the notion of accuracy and truthfulness
of relevant information. In retailing, transparency stimulates
customers to evaluate whether the retailer is open, under -
standing, and honest (e.g. in pricing) (Keller et al., 2022).
Petter et al. (2008) also found that users usually require

1876 Information Systems Frontiers (2025) 27:1871–1897
systems to provide relevant, easy-to-understand, accurate,
and concise information. Li and Mao (2015) have also estab-
lished perceived transparency as one of the key elements
of hedonic value derived from health advisory bots. It is
associated with a user’s ability to understand this AI-enabled
tool and share information with greater confidence. With-
out the feeling of perceived transparency about the content
being delivered by chatbots, customers would find it difficult
to absorb relevant information, which might create a nega-
tive experience and fail to trigger their sense of satisfaction.
Therefore, it is hypothesized that:
H2c Perceived transparency with a chatbot positively influ-
ences customer satisfaction.
2.1.2  Anthropomorphism experience and customer
satisfaction
Anthropomorphism is the human-like experience individu-
als have when they interact with non-human entities with
human–like characteristics, and is a type of feeling ones seek
to satisfy a goal of social connection (Epley et al., 2007).
Several scholars have highlighted chronic loneliness or
social disconnection as key reasons triggering a person’s
need to anthropomorphize during interactions with smart
technologies (Mishra et al., 2021). Past studies investigat-
ing customer experience with AI-enabled tools have taken
anthropomorphism as a unidimensional construct that
measures the human-likeness of AI based on appearance
and behavior (Lu et al., 2022; Moriuchi, 2021). However,
such measurements are difficult to apply in dialogue via chat
with a VCA. Even though other scholars have also evalu-
ated anthropomorphism via an AI’s thoughts, intention, and
consciousness (Blut et al., 2021), these aspects are more
applicable to physical AIs, which can explicitly express their
human-likeness via their movement and voice. Researchers
also identified warmth and competence as sub-constructs
of anthropomorphism (Li et al., 2023), however, they are
normally regarded as independent constructs in most cases
(Van Doorn et al., 2017). Therefore, to increase theoreti -
cal clarity, improve parsimony, and reduce complexity of
the model (Koufteros et al., 2009), we used and tested the
effect of anthropomorphism as the second-order construct
of mindful and mindless anthropomorphism for this study
(Araujo, 2018). Mindful anthropomorphism is a person’s
overall evaluation of the human likeness of a chatbot
(Araujo, 2018), while Kim and Sundar (2012) link mindless
anthropomorphism with a person’s emotion toward personal
characteristics of the system such as being likable, sociable,
and friendly.
For the mindful anthropomorphism experience, empiri-
cal research has emphasized human likeness as one of the
key dimensions of chatbot quality (Chen et al., 2022), and
it has proven to be crucial to trigger a person’s intention
to adopt (Lu et al., 2024; Sheehan et al., 2020). Blut et al.
(2021) opined that anthropomorphism is a psychological
process that can induce human–nonhuman interactions,
and their investigation of physical robots revealed that
human likeness (e.g. embodiment, gesture, gaze, etc.)
plays a significant role in maximizing a person’s satisfac-
tion and intention to use this AI, even though effects of
anthropomorphism are not always consistent (Ramesh &
Chawla, 2022). Physical appearance is not available for
VCAs; however, it is expected that their visual appearance
(e.g., avatars, uses of emoticons) holds the keys to cus-
tomer satisfaction. This is congruent with Kang and Watt
(2013), which reveals that a human-like avatar in mobile
conversation could essentially stimulate the outcome of
communication which increases user satisfaction. Moreo-
ver, the relationship between frontline human employees’
friendliness and customer satisfaction is largely acknowl-
edged (Keh et al., 2013; Lim et al., 2012). In a similar
context with VCAs, evidence has already proven that the
expression of live chat agents via emoticons would impact
on customers’ perceived likability and sincerity, leading to
customer satisfaction and purchase intention (Ma & Wang,
2021). Overall, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H3 Perceived anthropomorphism with chatbot positively
influences customer satisfaction.
2.1.3  Social presence experience and customer satisfaction
According to Biocca and Harms (2002, p. 10), social pres-
ence is “the sense of being with another”, which is a sort of
feeling a person has when interacting with another being.
This need has a long history which goes back to the time
when humans made the first stone statue. According to Hajli
et al. (2017), the sense of being with another is important
because it can activate a person’s feeling of being cared for,
which essentially increases their trust and enjoyment. There-
fore, social presence has become an important experience
customers would expect while interacting with chatbots.
In online interactions, people need to feel that their com-
panions are present, otherwise, they may think that others
have ended the conversation (Morahan-Martin & Schu-
macher, 2003). An experiment conducted by Gnewuch et al.
(2018) highlighted how important it is to have the feeling of
being cared for while interacting with chatbots. Van Doorn
et al. (2017) propounded that social presence would make
an AI more competent and attractive, ultimately affecting
satisfaction. In a similar context such as mobile instant mes-
saging applications, texts and/or emoticons were proven to
enhance social presence, which could create a link with
customer satisfaction (Ogara et al., 2014). This leads us to

2.2  Drivers of customer experience with chatbots
2.2.1  Functionality, customer experience, and satisfaction
Functionality signifies an opportunity for action afforded
by a product (Ziamou & Ratneshwar, 2003 ). Functional -
ity is one of the important elements for most technological
devices because it would be difficult for customers to accept
and use technological devices which exhibit low function-
ality. When using a specific technological device, custom-
ers usually expect it to be error-free and of low complexity
(Stevenson et al., 2000). The relationship between function-
ality of VCAs and customers’ perceived credibility is still
to be investigated; however, their influences were widely
anticipated by several research scholars. Self-determination
theory has already proven that competency is one of the
three fundamental needs for driving self-motivation (Ryan
& Deci, 2000). The competency of conventional technology
(e.g. self-service technology) is known as the key to enhanc-
ing its credibility as it allows users to complete tasks with a
minimum number of clicks (Collier & Kimes, 2013). Even
though customers tend to trust human employees more than
technology, if technology shows its ability to assist custom-
ers with relatively fewer errors, they are likely to express
firm belief in its reliability and adopt that technology (Mori-
uchi, 2021). Therefore, it is hypothesized that:
H5a1 Perceived credibility mediates the relationship
between functionality and satisfaction.
Interestingly, it has been proven across many studies that
a well-performing technology would facilitate users in find-
ing relevant content easily during the information-seeking
process. In particular, the process of self-directed movement
while using an online retail website is known to assist users
to seek out product-related information (Childers et al.,
2001). Similarly, Bridges and Florsheim (2008) asserted
that customers might find it distracting if there is too much
information on a system (e.g. website); however, if the sys-
tem enables customers to navigate smoothly, they might find
it easy to obtain relevant information for their online pur -
chase. In addition, research in the AI context also reveals
that customers are more interested in communicating with
chatbots that can help them obtain relevant information
with less effort (Hildebrand & Bergner, 2021). Therefore,
we hypothesized that:
H5a2 Perceived informativeness mediates the relationship
between functionality and satisfaction.
Van Doorn et al. (2017) believed that social presence is
triggered during the interaction with an automated machine
as the performance of frontline human employees has been
replaced by the functionality of machines in various service
contexts. It has been proven that VR’s (virtual reality) per -
formance (via narrative interaction) could create the feeling
that users are experiencing the presence of another person
in the virtual environment (de Regt et al., 2021). Gnewuch
et al. (2018) also revealed that chatbots’ functions, e.g., typ-
ing indicators and the use of languages, could make the cus-
tomers feel as if chatbots are there to handle their problems.
Therefore, it is hypothesized that:
H5b Social presence mediates the relationship between
functionality and satisfaction.
2.2.2  Communication style similarity, customer experience,
and satisfaction
The language style is the way people write or speak, and,
when a person finds a match in language style while com-
municating with others, they tend to have a positive experi-
ence (Littlejohn & Foss, 2010). Similar communication style
can increase someone’s willingness to trust the person with
whom they are interacting (Al-Natour et al., 2011). When
individuals find similarities with others, they spend less
effort to process the information being delivered (Littlejohn
& Foss, 2010). Moreover, communication style similarity
can make individuals feel as if they belong to a group, which
could trigger their perceived credibility during interactions
(Brewer, 2001). Interestingly, communication style can help
low-equity brands to better compete with high-equity brands
(Schindler et al., 2024). Previous studies already highlighted
the importance of the communication style in human–human
communication in a retail context (Holmqvist et al., 2019),
and, as chatbots are increasingly used as a replacement for
frontline human employees demonstrating the ability to
socially interact with users via text-based communication,
it has been hypothesized that:
H6a1 Perceived credibility mediates the relationship
between communication style similarity and satisfaction.
Language plays a crucial role in daily human–human
communication and it helps convey content that expresses a
social meaning (Liu et al., 2019). Littlejohn and Foss (2010)
emphasize in the theories of human communications that,
when a person finds that their language style matches with
others, mutual attraction goes up and there will be a less
apparent need for extra information. In the online environ-
ment, individuals tend to seek content which shares some
similarities (e.g., location, linguistic style, etc.) during the

information-seeking process because they believe that such
similarities are more relevant to their needs (Deng et al.,
2021; Hoskins et al., 2021). In a context that is closer to the
current research, Li and Mao (2015) argued that communi-
cation style similarity would create a user’s sense of reduced
uncertainty, hence, making them feel as if the information
they are seeking can be fulfilled easily during their interac-
tion with a virtual advisor. Therefore, it is hypothesized as
below:
H6a2 Perceived informativeness mediates the relationship
between communication style similarity and satisfaction.
In addition, communication style similarity plays a signif-
icant role in assisting individuals to easily absorb the content
as linguistic features have the potential for simultaneously
enhancing both cognitive information processing and emo-
tional response generation. Even though texts composed by
some online reviewers are complex, the match in language
style would help customers, as online users understand the
information being delivered easily, and enhance the trust-
worthiness of the content shared (Hoskins et al., 2021). Li
and Mao (2015) have further provided evidence that the
alignment in the use of language style could reduce con-
fusion, enforce trust, and enhance the ability to interpret
the meaning of wording delivered by the chatbots. A recent
study also reveals that the ability of this AI-enabled tool to
deliver personalized messages, which are highly similar to
human languages, can make customers understand the con-
tent easily, leading to an increased buying intention (Whang
et al., 2022). Therefore, it is hypothesized as below:
H6b1 Perceived transparency mediates the relationship
between communication style similarity and satisfaction.
Deng et al. (2021) supported that similarity in linguistic
features would make customers stay engaged while interact-
ing with the agent as they would perceive the communica-
tion as less serious and more friendly. In addition, it can also
make it easy for them to pick up different ideas within social
media contexts (Herhausen et al., 2019). Bleier et al. (2019)
also asserted that alignment in word choice and use of ques-
tions, pronouns, and adjectives would also draw customers’
attention to the information being delivered. Furthermore,
the ability of chatbots to maintain a flow in the conversa-
tion via the use of language style could stimulate custom-
ers’ interest in the information being delivered, whilst they
may become distracted if chatbots are unable to engage like
human agents do (Schuetzler et al., 2020). Therefore, it is
hypothesized as below:
H6b2 Perceived engagement mediates the relationship
between communication style similarity and satisfaction.
Grigoryan (2020) asserts that similarity in language style
could potentially create a chemistry of attraction between
the two parties. In social media settings, online reviewers
who use figurative language which is similar to the reader’s,
would trigger the reader’s favorable attitudes in a hedonic
context (Labrecque et al., 2020). Similar to human-to-human
interaction, AI can also tweak and adapt its communica-
tion style, which can stimulate customer enjoyment, e.g., the
chatbots can use profanities, shorthand, and emotional icons,
recite a poem and tell a story to amuse users during the inter-
action (Mou & Xu, 2017). Tay et al. (2016) also highlight
the ability of robots to crack situation-specific jokes in a con-
versation with a user. Therefore, it is hypothesized as below:
H6b3 Perceived enjoyment mediates the relationship
between communication style similarity and satisfaction.
Linguistic cue is one of the key elements that stimulate
customers’ perception of human resemblance when inter -
acting with non-human agents (Moriuchi, 2021). Empiri-
cal studies in text-based interfaces highlighted the positive
effects of tone of written content on the anthropomorphism
experience (Barcelos et al., 2018). It was also proved that
the way chatbots use active/passive voice in sentences would
influence individuals’ perception on this AI-powered tools’
human-likeness (Sah & Peng, 2015). In addition, empiri-
cal research also emphasized similarity in the way compa-
nies use emoticons during communication with customers
to enhance their likability (Das et al., 2019). Furthermore,
Moriuchi (2021 ) asserts that linguistic cues are identified
as one of the most important human-like qualities for non-
human agents which would enhance a person’s perception
of their friendliness. Therefore, it is hypothesized as below:
H6c Anthropomorphism mediates the relationship between
communication style similarity and satisfaction.
Additionally, similarity in how the communication is
conducted was found to influence the social presence expe-

typing indicator of a chatbot, which is like human–human
communication in an online chat environment, can make
users feel as if their questions are looked after and eventu -

technology that can respond in the form of text, they tend
to put less emphasis on the machine part of the technology
and perceive it as if they are engaging with a social entity
(Xu & Lombard, 2017). Similarly, when humans find an
avatar that shares similar characteristics with them, they are
more likely to develop a higher feeling of empathy (Belk,

positive interaction outcomes between human and chatbots

2.2.3  Aesthetics, customer experience, and satisfaction
Aesthetics is described as effect, beauty, art or a combination
of the three together (Tindale, 2014). According to Barbera
et al. (2018), the way people perceive their immediate envi-
ronment is usually influenced by what they see, therefore, it
is an important sense for information processing and inter -
pretation (Kim et al., 2020). Research further reveals that
color, size, illustrations, and shape can express the informa-
tion necessary to facilitate customers in their product choice
(Martinez et al., 2021). In e-tailing, the layout of online
stores also helps customers navigate easily (Krasonikolakis
et al., 2018), and understand the content with minimal effort
(Wang et al., 2010). Appropriate use of layouts, texts, and
fonts would reduce information overload, enabling custom-
ers to easily absorb content from information systems (Kahn,
2017). We hypothesize as below:
H7a1 Perceived transparency mediates the relationship
between aesthetics and satisfaction.
MacInnis et al. (1991) suggest that visual appeal could
evoke customers’ emotional responses, which ultimately
influence attention and positive intentions. Visual appeal is
shown to significantly draw customers’ focused attention as
it stimulates their curiosity (Shukla et al., 2022). Text, which
is one of the main elements of visual design in many tech-
nologies, requires more eye fixation, the appropriate design
of text could result in higher concentration from customers
(Kahn, 2017). Similarly, if the design of a VCA is visually
appealing, customers are more likely to immerse themselves
during interactions. This point is in line with Pengnate et al.
(2019): that visual appeal within the web atmospheric could
trigger a person’s curiosity, arousal of imagination, and
interest. Therefore, we hypothesize as below:
H7a2 Perceived engagement mediates the relationship
between aesthetics and satisfaction.
Even though the influence of a chatbot’s visual design
on the enjoyment experience is less known, research in a
conventional context has already proved the connection
between these two variables. In web atmospherics, visual
design could be represented by how different elements
(e.g., fonts, size, colors, layouts, etc.) are combined to yield
an impression of beauty, triggering a person’s feelings of
arousal and enjoyment (Wang et al., 2011). In research on
wearable technology, aesthetics is viewed as a crucial ele-
ment for customer evaluation of overall product quality (Lee,
2022). Similarly, a human-like salesperson avatar in a virtual
store can enhance a customer’s perception of enjoyment,
product involvement, and brand attitude (Moon et al., 2013).
Therefore, we hypothesize as below:
H7a3 Perceived enjoyment mediates the relationship
between aesthetics and satisfaction.
Aesthetics has also played an important role in early
humans anthropomorphizing ambiguous objects by
evaluating their human-like characteristics (Damiano &
Dumouchel, 2018). Although there is limited research on
the link between AI’s aesthetics and customers’ perceived
anthropomorphism, previous studies in non-technology
settings claimed that, when seeing strangers (e.g. human
frontline employees), humans tend to evaluate their visual
appearance (e.g. dress color) to learn whether they are socia-
ble or hostile (Bruder et al., 2021). In addition, the appear -
ance of frontline employees serves as an important indicator
for customers to judge non-verbal interpersonal interactions,
leading to customers’ enhancement of perceived sociability
and friendliness (Bruder et al., 2021). Research in a similar
setting such as physical robots has already proved that the
facial appearance of a technology agent is strongly linked
with users’ perception of the AI’s friendliness (Tinwell &
Sloan, 2014), while other studies found that gender presenta-
tion of robots also holds the key for customers’ positive emo-
tion, e.g. warmth and friendliness (e.g., Borau et al., 2021).
Overall, it is hypothesized that:
H7b Anthropomorphism mediates the relationship between
aesthetics and satisfaction.
According to van Doorn et al. (2017), the physical appear-
ance of robots can stimulate the feeling of social presence
during interactions. However, in the case of disembodied AI
agents like chatbots, as physical appearance is unlikely to
exist, the display of chatbot avatars can emerge as an impor-
tant factor that determines customer experience. Past studies
focusing on live chat also confirm that the pictures of service
providers can stimulate users’ sense of being with another
(McLean & Osei-Frimpong, 2017). When interacting with
a VCA, it is expected that the presence of the VCA’s avatar
could help users feel that the chatbots are present. Therefore,
it is hypothesized as below:
H7c Social presence mediates the relationship between aes-
thetics and satisfaction.

3.2  Measurement
A structured questionnaire was used for data collection with
measurement scales adopted from past studies (Table  2).
For all the constructs measured in the study, a 7-point Lik -
ert scale with anchors such as “1 = strongly disagree and
7 = strongly agree” was used. Utilitarian (12 items), hedonic
(12 items), social presence (5 items), and communication
style similarity (3 items) were adapted from Li and Mao
(2015). Anthropomorphism was used as the second-order
construct for mindful and mindless anthropomorphism,
which were measured using four and three items respec -
tively, adapted from Araujo (2018). Measurement scale of
10 items for functionality was adapted from Lin and Hsieh
(2011). After confirmatory factor analysis, 7 items were
dropped due to reliability and validity. The aesthetics scale
was measured with a total of three items adapted from Balaji
and Roy (2017). Satisfaction was adopted from Chung et al.
(2020), with six items, and reuse intention was measured
using three items adapted from Kim and Hyun (2016).
3.3  Common method bias
To test the presence of common method bias (CMB) in the
data, Harman’s (1976) single-factor test was applied using
unrotated exploratory factor analysis (EFA) on all the vari-
ables used in this study. The single factor explained 38.89%
(the UK) and 41.75% (Vietnam) of variance, which is less
than the 50% threshold, indicating the absence of the issue of
CMB (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Then, a marker variable test
was carried out (Lindell & Whitney, 2001; Williams et al.,
2010). Perceived risk, which is not the main construct in this
study, was used as the marker variable. Results revealed that
the commonly shared variance was achieved with 19.36%
(UK) and 40.96% (Vietnam), which meet the cut-off thresh-
old of less than 50%. Therefore, common method bias is not
a significant concern in this research.

urement model and (b) testing the structural model. Firstly,
confirmatory factor analysis was used to assess the validity
and reliability of the scales, and, secondly, structural model
analysis was used to test all the hypotheses (Byrne, 2013).

5  Discussion and implications
We investigated the effects of utilitarian, hedonic, anthropomor-
phism, and social presence on consumer satisfaction, leading to
reuse intention. We also explored the effects of three drivers of
customer experiences, i.e. functionality, communication style
similarity, and aesthetics, on satisfaction. Overall, this study
provides some of the important evidence supporting relation-
ships prescribed within the scope of literature about U&G
theory (Katz et al., 1973) and the SOR model (Mehrabian &
Russell, 1974), highlighting the direct effects of perceived cred-
ibility, informativeness, and enjoyment on customers’ satisfac-
tion in the context of VCAs in both the UK and Vietnam.
In addition, we also fulfilled the need to conduct more AI
research in emerging country which was enquired by Charles
et al. (2024) and Blut et al. (2021). Interestingly, only cus-
tomers in Vietnam are interested in chatbots which can pro-
vide easy-to-understand information (transparency) while
human-likeness (anthropomorphism) and social presence
are more important for those in the UK. We further found
results regarding the mechanisms through which functional-
ity affects satisfaction, mediated by perceived credibility for
the UK. In addition, communication style similarity can also
influence satisfaction via the mediation of perceived cred-
ibility, informativeness, and enjoyment. In contrast, for Viet-
nam, functionality has an indirect effect on satisfaction via
perceived informativeness, and communication style simi-
larity was found to be a determinant of customer satisfaction
via perceived transparency and enjoyment. Finally, the study
assessed the direct role played by customer satisfaction in
infusing the chatbot reuse intention. This research makes
several contributions toward VCAs, customer experience,
and artificial intelligence literature, as discussed below.
5.1  Theoretical implications
This article makes four main theoretical contributions.
The first contribution is to the theory of U&G. Unlike
previous VCA research, which usually focused on users'
benefit-related needs (Cheng & Jiang, 2020), we investigated
four key dimensions of customer experience as motivations
to use chatbots. The second contribution is associated with
unravelling the multidimensionality of customer experiences
while dealing with chatbots. In addition, we have used the
SOR model to address the drivers of customer experience,
which has been raised as an interesting future research topic
by several scholars (Bleier et al., 2019; Lemon & Verhoef,
2016; Li & Mao, 2015). Finally, following research agenda
proposed by Charles et al. (2024) and Blut et al. (2021), we
found that customer experience has different effects in devel-
oped (the UK) and developing countries (Vietnam), thus
highlighting the need for careful consideration when apply-
ing this AI-powered tool in different economies and cultures.
Such differences were discussed in conjunction within the
perspectives of U&G theory and SOR model below.
First and foremost, in terms of specific theoretical insights
drawn from U&G theory, we found that informativeness,
credibility and enjoyment are the main reasons why cus-
tomers in both the countries use chatbots. Our research has
responded to Ramesh and Chawla (2022) by confirming that
chatbots’ informativeness leads to satisfaction, and aligns
with Chung et al. (2020), showing that chatbots’ accuracy
and reliability are key to customer satisfaction. Our finding is
also similar to Chakraborty et al. (2024) as we found positive
effects of perceived credibility. We further found contradict-
ing results from previous research in regard with the effect
of enjoyment (Longoni & Cian, 2022; Rizomyliotis et al.,
2022; Zhang et al., 2022). Surprisingly, contrary to previous
IS literature, data from both the UK and Vietnam did not
support perceived engagement as the motivation to use this
AI-enabled tool. While little is known about the effect of
engagement on satisfaction triggered by linguistic cues of a
chatbot (Kull et al., 2021), we have established this relation-
ship based on the sense that engagement signifies a strong
interest of a person in a product or a service, hence, develop
a higher satisfaction (Ashley et al., 2011). In fact, engage -
ment is resulted not only from the visual appearance of a
technology (e.g. chatbot’s avatar) (Esmark Jones et al., 2022)
and a flow of information (Schuetzler et al., 2020), but it also
links with long waiting time (Ilk & Shang, 2022 ). While
chatbots can provide instant responses, most are unable to
handle complicated questions, which increases customer
wait time for appropriate resolution (Forbes, 2022). Further
investigation should be carried out, however, engagement
which links with long waiting time could be the cause for its
neutral (Vietnam) or negative effect (the UK) on customer
satisfaction within the domain of our research.
Evidence also shows that the motivations to use chatbots
vary in both the countries. In the context of the UK, anthro-
pomorphism is shown to influence customer satisfaction,
which is aligned with empirical research within developed
economies (Blut et al., 2021; Chung et al., 2020; Sheehan

1887Information Systems Frontiers (2025) 27:1871–1897
et al., 2020). In addition, based on prior studies (Ciecha-
nowski et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2022a, 2022b), which found
adding advanced human-like characteristics to AI-powered
tools would lead to negative customer responses, the positive
effects of anthropomorphism in our study signified that our
UK participants might prefer to interact with text-based chat-
bot interface to seek information and resolve service issues.
We also confirmed the importance of chatbots’ social pres-
ence (Bleier et al., 2019) from our UK data. However, we
observed that our Vietnam result aligns more closely with
Li and Mao (2015) as social presence is not the key moti-
vation for customers to use chatbots. We anticipate that this
inconsistency is primarily based on the difference in cultural
orientation. In particular, customized service takes on greater
importance for customers belonging to countries with stronger
focus on individualism (the UK) (Winsted, 1999), therefore,
chatbots’ ability to fulfil their needs via human touch (e.g.
warm, personal and social conversation) play important role
to maximize their satisfaction. On the other hand, customers
from collectivist society (Vietnam) tend to establish strong
bonds with in‐group members (e.g. friends and family mem-
bers) (Frost et al., 2010), therefore, no matter how social pres-
ence a chatbot displays, it is unable to replace the value that
customers in countries such as Vietnam and China place on
human–human interactions. Additionally, types of chatbots
greatly influence customer behavior (Elmashhara et al., 2024).
Upon reviewing the responses of customers from the UK and
Vietnam, for example, we also observed that within the bank-
ing service, customers in Vietnam can interact with Vietin-
Bank's virtual agent through Facebook Messenger, which can
provide their customers with ease of access; however, they
lacked clear human-like features, e.g. delivering messages too
quickly, providing excessive information, and relying more
on multiple-choice options instead of natural language. On
the other hand, customers in the UK can access the Barclays
chatbot via the bank’s app, which can provide its customers
with an optimized service with more “human” features, e.g.
the use of typing indicator […], smaller amount of informa-
tion being delivered, and acceptable interval between mes-
sages. We anticipate that could be some of the potential rea-
sons why there was no strong link between anthropomorphism
and social presence with customer satisfaction from Vietnam.
The perceived transparency-satisfaction link is positively
significant only for customers from Vietnam. The difference
could be due to the un-similarity of consumer aliteracy (the
phenomenon of choosing not to read despite the cognitive
ability to do so) among different cultures (DelVecchio et al.,
2019). Further investigation should be carried out, however,
based on the difference in how individualistic (the UK in our
study) and collectivistic customers (Vietnam in our study)
process written information (Kwon et al., 2015; Taylor et al.,
1997), we assume that customers in Vietnam have lower
ability to independently read and judge information being
delivered by chatbots, therefore, information transparency is
only necessary for customers in Vietnam. This phenomenon
may also explain why the indirect link between communi-
cation style similarity and satisfaction via transparency is
only significant for Vietnam data, while such relationship
through credibility and informativeness is established for
the UK data.
Secondly, from the perspective of SOR model, we
expected drivers of customer experience, including com-
munication style similarity, functionality and aesthetics
indirectly lead to customer satisfaction. However, in both
the countries, only communication style similarity can trig-
ger customer satisfaction via enjoyment, which confirms
the hedonic role of language in communication (Grigoryan,
2020; Labrecque et al., 2020). Based on our findings, we
confirm that communication style similarity not only gener-
ates attraction among people in human–human conversation,
but it is also crucial in enhancing ones’ enjoyment feeling
with AI-tools like chatbots, which thereafter influences their
satisfaction. Furthermore, linguistic cues are usually linked
with an AI-enabled tools’ human-likeness, however, we
found non-significant relationships between communication
style similarity and customer satisfaction via engagement,
anthropomorphism and social presence in our research. It is
unclear why the results conflicted with our expectation; how-
ever, the reasons could be stemmed from various customers’
emotional states during interaction with chatbots (Ozuem
et al., 2024). For example, an angry customer interacting
with a chatbot which highly resembles human’s natural
language is more likely to develop a negative satisfaction
(Crolic et al., 2022).
Furthermore, because performance of human employees
on chat interface has been replaced by chatbots’ functions
(e.g. typing indicators, and the use of human languages, etc.),
which could make customers feel as if they are there to pro-
vide proper resolutions (Gnewuch et al., 2018; Van Doorn
et al., 2017), we expected that functionality would trigger
social presence, and ultimately lead to customer satisfaction.
However, the indirect link between functionality and cus-
tomer satisfaction via social presence was not established in
our study. Although in-depth investigation should be carried
out for better understanding of this insight, we anticipate that
customers usually know exactly that they are interacting with
a chatbot (vs. a live chat agent) via initial disclosure mes-
sages. And because a chatbot can display obvious mechanis-
tic functions (e.g. provide instant, unbiased response, and be
available 24/7), customers may not expect to have a social
connection from such interaction. While the effect of human-
like features in a chatbot may only become significant when
there is a high need for human interaction (Sheehan et al.,
2020), in this instance, customers may primarily focus on
whether or not the chatbot can fulfil their functional needs
and ignore its ability to display social presence. We also

anticipate that due to aliteracy differences between individu-
alistic and collectivistic customers (DelVecchio et al., 2019;
Kwon et al., 2015), perceived credibility only mediates the
relationship between functionality and satisfaction only in
the UK data, while perceived informativeness serves as the
mediator for the relationship between functionality and sat-
isfaction only in the Vietnam data.
Similarly, the indirect links between aesthetics and satis-
faction were not established. This could be that the design
of a chatbot is mostly based on simple text; therefore, its
visual looks are not strong enough to trigger transparency,
engagement, enjoyment, anthropomorphism and social pres-
ence, leading to satisfaction during the information-seeking
process. This explanation is similar to the view that custom-
ers usually seek a chatbot with ability to deliver relevant and
reliable information, and disregard their aesthetic aspects
(Jeon, 2022).
Thirdly, this research is novel in the way we view cus-
tomer experience in the VCA domain. Customer experience
in the current research stream was indistinguishable from the
enjoyment aspect (Rizomyliotis et al., 2022). In addition, we
have adopted the stance that there would be multiple dimen-
sions of customer experience. Therefore, instead of measur-
ing how customers feel, sense, think, act, and relate (Lemon
& Verhoef, 2016), and beyond merely referring to the enjoy-
ment as unidimensional customer experience (Behera et al.,
2024; Rizomyliotis et al., 2022), the four dimensions of cus-
tomer experience with chatbots were incorporated.
Overall, customers need to experience chatbots as a form
of VCA which can demonstrate the ability to provide rel-
evant and trustworthy information without encountering
errors. In addition, the leisure/fun factor is one of the main
motivations for customers to use chatbots. Interestingly,
there are some differences regarding motivation and drivers
to use a chatbot in the UK and Vietnam. In particular, the
effects of anthropomorphism and social presence on satis-
faction are only effective for UK customers, while Vietnam-
ese customers need information provided by the VCA to
be transparent. Functionality can indirectly influence sat -
isfaction via perceived credibility only for the UK, while
this link is only effective via informativeness for Vietnam.
Lastly, for the UK, language style similarity can stimulate
perceived informativeness, credibility, and enjoyment, which
consequently enhance the user’s satisfaction from using a
chatbot. However, for Vietnam, communication style used
between human and chatbot can enhance satisfaction only
via perceived transparency and enjoyment.
5.2  Practical implications
Based on the U&G theory, we found that informative-
ness, credibility and enjoyment are key motivations that
people expect from chatbots in the UK and Vietnam.
Therefore, to maximize customer satisfaction in both
developed as well as developing countries, AI develop-
ers should consider designing VCAs that deliver informa-
tive (e.g. providing relevant information that can solve
customers’ enquiries) and credible response (e.g. pro-
viding several options based on customers’ preferences
with neutral manners, and acknowledge their limitation
when they cannot assist customers in specific requests).
Furthermore, it’s important to design a chatbot with
which customers enjoy interacting, for example, equip-
ping chatbots with ability to crack a joke or use humor
where appropriate.
Based on the behavioral differences of customers of
both the countries in our findings, we recommend that
chatbots in developing countries should be designed to
deliver transparent messages, for example, providing
short answers, and avoid excessive use of jargons. How -
ever, this may not be a priority in developed countries.
Additionally, AI developers and industry practitioners
in the developed countries should design chatbots that
resemble human agents and show that they are always
‘there’ to offer support. For example, chatbot developers
for the UK might consider incorporating natural language
processing to make chatbots ‘more human’, and use typ-
ing […] indicator to inform customers that chatbots are
currently processing their queries. Since human-likeness
and the sense of human contact are not found to directly
enhance satisfaction for customers in Vietnam, AI devel-
opers and managers may input fewer scarce resources to
enhance such features to maximize these experiences,
unless it is felt necessary to do so.
We also believe that some of non-significant results are
helpful for industry practitioners and AI developers in both
the countries. Specifically, our data show that perceived
engagement does not link with customer satisfaction, and it
even has slightly negative impact from the UK data. While
the results are surprising, we encourage AI developers to
consider and improve unfavorable causes of engagement,
e.g. customers have to wait for a long time to get a satisfied
answer.
From the perspective of SOR model, we expected that
design aspects of chatbots, including functionality, aes-
thetics and communication style similarity are the main
drivers that indirectly influence customer satisfaction via
utilitarian experience (informativeness, credibility, and
enjoyment), hedonic experience (enjoyment, engagement,
and transparency), anthropomorphism and social presence.
Based on our data, even though it is not necessary for
industry practitioners and AI developers in both the coun-
tries to use functionality as a trigger for social presence,
it serves an important determinant for perceived cred-
ibility for customers in the UK and informativeness for
customers in Vietnam, for example, a focus on chatbots’

function to provide clear references to the sources of infor -
mation for the UK customers (to enhance credibility),
and filter large volumes of information to only deliver the
most relevant content for Vietnam customers (to enhance
informativeness).
For the UK customers, we recommend that chatbots
should be designed to use language, which is similar to their
target customers’ where appropriate, including the use of
emojis (e.g.  ), shorthand (e.g. btw – by the way, tbh – to be
honest, etc.) to trigger customers’ enjoyment, leading to an
increased overall satisfaction. Depending on the contexts, we
also encourage that chatbots should have ability to adjust the
communication style to increase its target users’ perceived
credibility (e.g. using professional language to make the
conversation more serious and well-intention for customers)
and informativeness (e.g. aligning chatbots’ response time to
customers pace – chatbots’ responses should not be too quick
or too slow when comparing with customers’ responses).
For customers in Vietnam, chatbots’ language style should
be designed to not only mimic customers for an increased
enjoyment, but AI developers should also focus on the match
of language style to enhance customers’ perceived transpar-
ency (e.g. enhancing chatbots’ proficiency of Vietnamese
language). In both the countries, we encourage AI develop-
ers and industry practitioners not to heavily pay attention on
chatbots’ visual appearance.
6  Limitations and future research
This study has provided substantive findings, but they do
not come without certain limitations that may seed future
inquiry. Firstly, the participants were asked to recall their
previous experiences with VCAs. Even though this method
has been widely used, the participants might not recall
exactly their actual interaction with this AI-enabled tool,
especially for the construct regarding visual appearance (e.g.
aesthetics). Therefore, researchers may want to consider
collecting real-time data using the experimental method to
cross-validate the results of this study. In addition, our find-
ings reveal that customers in Vietnam tend to highly evalu-
ate transparency and informativeness more as compared to
customers in the UK. This could be due to the difference in
customer aliteracy (Kwon et al., 2015); a separate investiga-
tion is necessary to provide further insights into this behav-
ioral phenomenon among different economies and cultures.

Samuel Adomako is an Associate Professor of Strategy at the Univer-
sity of Birmingham, UK. His research examines the nexus of strategy
and innovation. His research has appeared or forthcoming in leading
journals including Journal of Management Studies, Research Policy,
British Journal of Management, International Business Review, Inter-
national Small Business Journal, Journal of Business Research, Jour-
nal of International Management, among many others. He received his
Ph.D. from Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, United
Kingdom.
