Rural entrepreneurship and ecommerce interlinkage: A multi-country exploration in the
African context

Abstract.

Purpose: To explore the challenges facing rural entrepreneurs in adopting ecommerce in an under-
researched African context, this study adopts a multi-country exploration involving five countries and
offers valuable managerial and theoretical implications.

Design/methodology/approach: With samples covering significant geographical regions – North,
South, Northeastern, and West Africa  – this research  adopted a qualitative method with an
interpretivist approach. The empirical data was collected via in-depth semi-structured interviews with
30 rural entrepreneurs drawn from a wide range of sectors in Egypt, Nigeria, South Africa, Sudan and
Zimbabwe. The data analysis followed Gioia’s suggestions for qualitative rigor.

Findings: The findings reveal institutional voids – political instability and lack of technology
savviness, social infrastructure and financial resources . Participants further revealed that t hese
challenges result in loss of valuable opportunities for learning, communication, productivity, and
expansion, as well as  inability to compete with their urban counterparts. Findings also highlight
specific challenges facing rural women entrepreneurs – socio-cultural barriers and gender stereotypes.
Despite these challenges , participants who invest in ecommerce revealed significant benefits –
improved customers sourcing, customer retention, customer experience, and customer satisfaction.
The findings also reveal  business expansion, enhanced brand visibility , and improved supply chain
management because of ecommerce adoption.

Originality/value: Extending prior theories – the Digital Entrepreneurship Ecosystem (DEE) and the
Technology-Organisation-Environment (TOE) frameworks – the current paper demonstrates how
technological, organisational, and environmental factors interact in an institutionalized setting , and
suggest a hybrid model unique to resource-constrained rural Africa.

Keywords: Rural entrepreneurship, ecommerce, institutional voids, Africa, emerging markets.

1. Introduction
Due to its capacity to stem urbanization , address (rural) poverty and minimize unemployment (Yu et
al., 2024; Brenya et al., 2023), rural entrepreneurship is gaining a burgeoning scholarly interest. Rural
entrepreneurship (mainly agribusinesses) is one of the most potent tools  to address unemployment,
unplanned urbanization and poverty challenges facing most African countries (OECD, 2023; African
Union, 2022; UN Women, 2021; Sumberg & Hunt, 2019 ). Yet , significant obstacles include poor
access to capital, digital illiteracy and poor connectivity  (Garg et al., 2025; Benya et al ., 2023 ).

2
Researchers have linked these issues to institutional voids in the region  (Alo et al ., 2024, 2023) .
Despite these barriers , rural entrepreneurship has shown great potential in reducing rural poverty in
Africa, as evidenced in agribusinesses (Komolafe et al., 2022). Consequently, there is a renewed call
for increased access to financial resources by rural entrepreneurs (Magdalena et al., 2024) to help
address digital illiteracy, digital divide, poverty, unemployment and geographical barriers in Africa
(Alo et al., 2025;  Nambisan, 2017). Researchers link investment in ecommerce to sustainability in
rural entrepreneurship (Arslan et al., 2022; Barragan & Becker, 2024; Zahra et al., 2023).
However, research examining the challenges and benefits of adopting ecommerce  in rural
entrepreneurship in African is extremely limited if not absent. Notwithstanding the important
foundational work on entrepreneurs’ challenges in Africa (e.g., Mhlanga, 2021) and ecommerce
adoption (e.g., Mhlanga, 2020), a focused, qualitative multi-country inquiry into the challenges and
benefits of ecommerce for rural entrepreneurs in Africa remains rather scarce. This is because most
prior studies have focused on a single country, which limits the potential generalization of their
findings. Recognizing these gaps, this paper undertakes an exploratory qualitative assessment in a
diverse set of African countries – Egypt, Nigeria, South Africa, Sudan and Zimbabwe  – and tries to
answer the following two research questions.
1. What challenges limit ecommerce adoption by rural entrepreneurs in Africa and how do these
entrepreneurs navigate these challenges to minimize their impact on their business activities?
2. How do these rural entrepreneurs benefit from adopting ecommerce in their businesses?
The above research questions are complementary and deliberately paired. Research question one
addresses the contextual and systemic barriers to adoption, a known but complex area requiring further
qualitative insight across varied African settings. Research question  two focus o n the outcomes and
value creation post -adoption, a less explored aspect that reveals strategies , adaptive practices  and
advantages. Together, they provide a holistic view of the ecommerce journey from barriers to gains.
In doing so, this paper offers three critical contributions to the extant literature. Firstly, this study offers
a timely and comparative analysis of similarities and differences in ecommerce adoption across several
African countries in the context of rural entrepreneurs . Secondly, the study not only focuses on
challenges in ecommerce and adaptation but also specifies strategies that help rural entrepreneurs to
overcome these challenges. Finally, by examining the readiness level of  rural entrepreneurs in these
five diverse locations to match their local practice s with global standards in ecommerce innovation,
this paper makes a complementary but distinctive contribution to the Africa-focused digital ecosystem
literature, as existing literature has focused mainly on Asia and developed countries. Theoretically, the

3
current paper extends the Digital Entrepreneurship Ecosystem (DEE) framework (Nambisan, 2017;
Mafimisebi & Ogunsade, 2022) and the Technology -Organisation-Environment (TOE) framework
(Baker, 2012) , as it examines how the interplay between digital infrastructure, entrepreneurial
resources, and institutional voids shape ecommerce adoption outcomes in rural Africa. This integrated
theoretical lens helps to advance understanding beyond merely application of ecommerce to
understanding the underlying challenges and their benefit in rural African entrepreneurship setting.
2. Literature Review
2.1 Major Challenges in entrepreneurship development in rural Africa.
While countries like Ghana, Nigeria, Cameroon, Kenya, Zambia, Zimbabwe, and South Africa have
had a long history of investment in their rural entrepreneurship development (Konczacki, 1999), the
digital divide facing the continent has not allowed Africa to exploit the full potentials of its rural
entrepreneurship (Allen, 2019) . Yet, w e also still lack research examining challenges facing rural
entrepreneurs in Africa (Utete & Zhou, 2024). Consistent with existing research examining constraints
to entrepreneurship development in rural African setting (e.g., Brixiova & Kangoye, 2015) and their
impacts on entrepreneurial self -efficacy (e.g., Arshed et al ., 2021), this study provides a detailed
review of the main challenges facing rural entrepreneurs in Africa.
Researchers have found some obstacles facing rural African entrepreneurs, such as  lack of funding,
acute skill shortage, lack of  digital infrastructure, lack of business support, poor legislation,
bureaucratic hurdles, legitimacy, negative attitudes from the society , cultural issues, and unfriendly
business climate  (Harit, 2015 ; Fal, 2013 ). For instance, r ural South Africa n entrepreneurs  must
overcome inadequate electricity supply, poor road networks, transportation issues, lack of funds, and
acute skill shortages (Lekhanya & Mason, 2014) . Yet, the greatest challenge to entrepreneurship
development in Africa is the institutional voids  – the absence of supporting institutions, unreliable
market intermediaries, unsystematic contract-enforcing mechanisms, and inefficient transportation and
communication networks (George et al., 2016). Limited technological infrastructure and low internet
services have also been highlighted in West Africa (Madichie et al., 2021). Furthermore, recent studies
have noted that African entrepreneurs face systemic barriers related to technological fragmentation
and institutional misalignment. Similarly, Magdalena et al. (2024) proposed that while digital tools
lead to financial inclusion , rural entrepreneurs often lack access to proper training which limits their
digitization capabilities. In African context, these challenges could be exacerbated by other challenges
(Oliveira & Martins, 2011), such gender discrimination.

2.1.1. Gender Barriers and rural entrepreneurship development.
While the above challenges are common throughout Africa, empirical evidence shows that men and
women face different constraints  in entrepreneurship development  (Nziku & Struthers, 2017) .
Researchers have linked challenges facing female entrepreneurs in rural Africa to lack of access to
digital technologies resulting on lack of business opportunities (Halkias et al.,  2011), family
commitments and lack of funding options (Isaga, 2018; Mordi et al ., 2010) , unlike their male
counterparts. Similarly, Rahman et al. (2022) revealed that women entrepreneurs in Bangladesh face
higher resistance to technology use. Yet, addressing digital inclusion for women requires solving both
infrastructural access and established socio -cultural barriers simultaneously (Soluk et al ., 2021;
Salemink et al., 2017). Although studies have explored some of the constraints facing rural Albanian
women entrepreneurs in North Macedonia (Ramadani & Ramadani, 2021), women entrepreneurs in
rural India (Pathak & Varshney, 2017; Garg et al., 2025), rural women entrepreneurs in Bangladesh
(e.g., Rahman et al., 2022), small rural businesses in a rural region of Canada (Siemens, 2010) and
rural female farmer entrepreneurs in China (Qing et al., 2021; Feng et al., 2024), we still lack research
examining challenges facing rural women entrepreneurs in Africa(Utete & Zhou, 2024).
2.1.2. Rurality, digital divide and ecommerce adoption by rural entrepreneurs
In sharp contrast with urbanization which is characterized by more densely populated areas with a
different way of life and a focus on commerce and industry, rurality implies rural characteristics
– geographical areas situated outside of cities and towns with often lower population density of usually
less than 10,000 people, a slower pace of life and relatively wider open spaces (Wallin, 2003). As rural
areas include mainly villages, isolated dwellings, hamlets, rural towns and open countryside (Hall et
al., 2006) with fewer homes or other buildings, rural communities are home to mostly agriculturist s,
mountains, forests, farmlands and other natural resources . Consequently , they suffer from digital
divide – the gap between people with sufficient access to information communication technology
(ICT) and people with inadequate or no access to ICT (Soomro et al., 2020).
            Since digital divide is influenced by factors that determine the digital connectivity levels  –
location, distance to urban area and the level of digital skills available (Gerli & Whalley, 2022) – rural
communities struggle to  keep up with digital connectivity developments ( Velaga et al.,  2012).
Consequently, rural communities pay the price for living in rural areas (Marsden, 2016), which is also
known as ‘the rural penalty’  (Malecki, 2003 ; Hite, 1997 ). The rural  penalty implies significant
challenges for rural entrepreneurs in their efforts to adopt ecommerce, unlike their urban counterparts,
even in developed countries of the world . For instance, despite improved digital connectivity across

rural areas of Wales, many businesses in rural Wales a re still without reliable digital connections
(Morris et al., 2022). This limit s business opportunities, restricts creativity and innovation capacity
and exacerbates the difficulty in overcoming location constraints and industrial challenges, especially,
in agribusiness entrepreneurial activities (Bowen & Morris, 2019).
            Salemink et al. (2017) highlighted that rural entrepreneurs in fragile states face disrupted
supply chains and unreliable ICT. Alo et al. (2024) highlighted the hyperinflation and sanctions in
Zimbabwe that hindered ecommerce adoption. Although Zahra et al. (2023) added that digital tools
can increase resilience and decentralize the risks , there are very few studies linking unpredictable
regulatory changes with ecommerce adoption. As Africa is mostly plagued by huge informal economy,
high unemployment rates, endemic poverty and political instability (Mpofu, 2023), researchers argue
that t hese circumstances create barriers to ecommerce adoption  by rural entrepreneurs in African
(Baalbaki & El Khoury, 2024).
2.2 Other Challenges in the adoption of ecommerce
Digital entrepreneurship literature highlights poor internet services a significant barrier to ecommerce
adoption within the rural West African entrepreneurship ecosystems (Madichie et al., 2021). Oblivious
of new standards, lack of adequate electricity supply, inability to integrate smart gadgets, high cost of
mobile internet devices, and risk of cybercrimes are the top five constraints that hinder ecommerce
adoption by rural entrepreneurs in Namibia (Kamutuezu et al., 2022). Ecommerce adoption challenges
in rural Africa are also exasperated by inadequate infrastructure, digital illiteracy, digital divide,
unaffordable cost of ICT infrastructure, and a fragile political economy (Bakibinga-Gaswaga et al.,
2020).
            Furthermore, a lack of IT proficiency limits the developmental capacity of women
entrepreneurs in sub-Saharan Africa, including their capacity to exploit existing opportunities (Savira
& Fahmi, 2020; Irene, 2019). Despite the recent revolution in digitization and the resultant improved
business innovation capacity in sub -Saharan Africa (Tagne, 2021) , there is still a lack of research
examining the capacity of rural entrepreneurs in the region to maximize the benefits of digitization in
adopting ecommerce (Bellucci & Otenyo, 2019) . For instance, some rural areas in South Africa still
lack ICT support, which makes it difficult for small and medium -sized enterprises (SMEs) to expand
through the use of digital marketing (Lekhanya, 2015). Similarly, the farming communities in Sub -
Saharan Africa still lack reliable mobile phone services to drive their operations (Kabbiri et al., 2018).

            Despite a growing body of research linking digital technology, ecommerce adoption and rural
entrepreneurship development, there is still a lack of research focusing on rural African communities.
While Feng et al., (2024) examined how to widen the digital accessibility divide by arguing that urban-
rural digitalization should evolve from divide to inclusion, their study focused on China. Although
Morris et al. (2022) examined how digital inclusion and bridging the digital divide could boost the
resilience level of rural entrepreneurs, their research focused on rural Wales. Consequently, there is a
lack of research focusing on rural Africa. Specifically, there is a lack of research examining how digital
technologies can influence ecommerce adoption by rural entrepreneurs in Africa. While there may be
a few studies in this regard, most prior studies have focused on a single country, which limits the
potential generalization of their findings. Recognizing these research gaps, this paper undertakes an
exploratory qualitative assessment in a diverse set of African countries including Egypt, Nigeria, South
Africa, Sudan and Zimbabwe to explore the interlinkages between rural entrepreneurship and
digitalization.
2.3 The Role of Digitization in ecommerce adoption by rural entrepreneurs in Africa.
The role of digitization entrepreneurial orientation, strategic visioning and in ecommerce adoption,
have been  emphasized (Niemand et al ., 2021; Vadana et al ., 2019) . Researchers have found that
adopting information communication technology ( ICT) positively affects rural entrepreneurship in
Africa, especially, in agriculture , forestry (Musingafi & Zabroon, 2014), economic and commercial
ventures (Iddrisu & Chen, 2022; Adomako et al., 2021). Digitization in Sub-Saharan Africa has also
been linked to  improved e-commerce in the region, due to  improved communications and mobile
banking (Myovella et al., 2020). Yet, the application of ICT in rural entrepreneurship has a significant
impact on women entrepreneurs . Adopting digital infrastructure in entrepreneurship boosts African
women's participation in rural agriculture (Rutashobya et al., 2021), and transforms their capacity to
innovate, create jobs, and collaborate with relevant stakeholders, especially in the agriculture sector
(Sambrani & Jayadatta, 2020) . Furthermore, through e-commerce, women entrepreneurs from rural
Africa can circumvent traditional barriers and provide access to global markets (Abeysinghe & Malik,
2021; Lloyd & Vengrouskie, 2019).
           Furthermore, the role of digital entrepreneurship in fostering the bonds between local networks,
attracting new investment and people, and bridging geographical barriers in rural areas have been
emphasized (Alo et al., 2025; Rodrigues & Franco, 2021). Yet, due to the digital divide and resource
constraints, a robust digital economy in Africa requires a systematic approach to unlock the
opportunities and foster digital, socio-cultural and socio-economic growth (Mafimisebi & Ogunsade,

Theoretical Framework
This study integrat es two complementary theoretical frameworks: the Digital Entrepreneurship
Ecosystem (DEE) and the Technology-. Organisation-Environment (TOE) framework. This integrated
lens allows us to systematically analyze the multi -level factors influencing digital adoption among
rural African entrepreneurs and to articulate a clearer theoretical contribution. By examining their
interdependencies and how they manifest in the presence of significant institutional voids and gendered
constraints, we move beyond simply applying these frameworks to a new context, an underexplored
theoretical intersection (George et al., 2016; Soluk et al., 2021).
Digital Entrepreneurship Ecosystem (DEE)
The DEE framework focuses on understanding how the interrelation between digital infrastructure,
institutional policies, stakeholder dynamics and socio -cultural norms  influence entrepreneurial
outcomes (Mafimisebi & Ogunsade, 2022; Nambisan, 2017). This framework helps situate this study
within the broader scholarly discourse on how ecosystem fragmentation in emerging economies
creates unique challenges (Autio et al., 2018). The rural Africa’s entrepreneurs operate in a fragmented
ecosystems characterized by lack of access to digital tools, weak policy support and cultural resistance
(Prasetyo & Setyadharma, 2022; Rahman et al.,  2022). For instance, studies show that Sudan’s
political instability and Nigeria’s infrastructur al gaps lead to heterogeneous digital ecosystems that
restrict technology adoption (Lekhanya, 2015).  Like the current  research, previous research that

8
adopted the DEE theory also utilized multi-country design to discuss how localized gaps in ICT,
education and regulation interact with global standards (Baker, 2012). This is also shown for the case
of Africa’s context (Satyro et al., 2024; Mafimisebi & Ogunsade, 2022) as well as research examining
how ecosystem actors like governments and NGOs engage in cross-sector collaboration to increase
digital literacy and financial inclusion (Prasetyo & Setyadharma, 2022).
Technology-Organisation-Environment (TOE) Framework
The TOE Framework also complements the DEE by differentiating between technological,
organisational and environmental factors in digital adoption (Kraus et al., 2021; Huete-Alcocer, 2017).
This three-factor lens (technological, organisational and environmental) directly links to this study’s
identified challenges – poor internet, limited digital skills and socio -cultural barriers, respectively.
More importantly, it provides a structured way to analyze the research questions: research question
one – constraints in adopting  digital technologies across all three TOE contexts, while research
question two – reveals the benefits derived by rural  entrepreneurs within given technological ,
organisational and environmental constraints. This framework effectively integrates considerations of
human capital ( organisational knowledge/skills) and the environmental context of gendered norms,
thereby addressing theoretical perspectives. This framework also addresses the rural -urban digital
divide discussed by (Salemink et al., 2017).
3. Research Methodology
Grounded in an interpretivist philosophical stance, this research adopts a qualitative methodology.
The research approach is appropriate for examining how rural entrepreneurs in resource -constrained
and institutionally fragile contexts experience the adoption of ecommerce  (Hennink et al. , 2020;
Stebbins, 2001). This aligns  with the Digital Entrepreneur Ecosystem (DEE) and Technology -
Organisation-Environment (TOE) frameworks, which jointly emphasize the role of institutional
conditions, technological capabilities, organisational resources, and socio -cultural influences in
shaping entrepreneurial behaviour ( Nmbisan, 2017 and Tornatzky, et al , 1990 ). Specifically, th is
research focused on participants’ capacity to adopt ecommerce adoption, the challenges they face and
the ensuing benefits. Attaining such subjective data requires storytelling through semi-structured
interviews (Knight & Jarzabkowski, 2022). In the interview approach, participants were recognized as
the key players, whose side of the story needed to be captured uninterruptedly by allowing them to
reflect freely on their experiences with ecommerce adoption (Liu & Rong, 2015 ; Dick & Collings,
2014; Ezzamel & Willmott, 2008). To contact these key players, we relied on the authors ’ contacts

9
(Knight & Jarzabkowski, 2022; Harvey, 2011) – as the authors are from the five participating countries.
30 interviews, i.e. six from each country were conducted and consents were sought through a gate
keeper from each of the countries. Through snowballing (also known as chain sampling or network
sampling) each gate keeper helped us to reach (and build rapport with) additional five participants
from their communities. This also enhanced willingness to share their views with us (Patton, 2015).

3.2 Data analysis
To explore the research topic in depth t his study utilized Braun’s thematic analysis approach to
investigate rural African entrepreneurs' challenges in adopting ecommerce. The data analysis started
by carefully listening to the recorded oral interview to develop familiarity with the primary data before
data transcription. This was followed by data transcription , and then comparing the transcribed data
with the recorded oral data to ensure no single word was missing. Consistent with the thematic analysis
approach, the data analysis has  followed previous discursive analyses of subjectivity (e.g., Knight &
Jarzabkowski, 2022; Mantere & Whittington, 2021), by the authors’ constantly going back and forward
between the dominant literature and the transcribed raw interview data. A thorough understanding of
participants' responses allowed for an easier identification of the common patterns in the data and the
emergent themes. Based on the identified patterns attempt s were made at grouping the data. At this
stage it was observed that these initial groupings of data were overwhelming (Gioia et al., 2013), and
thus the authors needed to make sense of these data sets by using  open coding (Strauss and Corbin,
1998), a process akin to Gioia’s (2004) idea of hanging them together  by tagging these sets of data
together to generate the 1st-order concepts (Gioia et al., 2010). The next step was to compare each of
the coded sets of data against their corresponding interview transcript (Patton, 2015). Having
completed the 1st-order analysis at this stage, these sets of data needed to be further condensed down
to a more manageable number by tying up as many categories as required , based on their differences
and similarities. This process required the adoption of axial coding (Strauss and Corbin, 1998) . The
axial coding matched the large sets of initial codes (i.e., the set of first -order concepts) against the
corresponding existing literatures on ecommerce and entrepreneurship development, resulting in a
range of second -order themes. To enhance meaning making, the second order themes were  further

Figure 1 below . In the findings section below, data  presentation has followed the set -up, quote and

communities' accessing and affording technology. Thirdly, frequent changes in governmental policies

technology adoption. Lastly, armed conflicts and population displacement disrupt technology access

5. Conclusions
Like previous studies on the role of entrepreneurship in transforming modern societies, this research
has found that entrepreneurship development in rural Africa plays a crucial role in creating
employment opportunities, improving food security, and supporting overall sustainability. However,
across all five countries studied, rural entrepreneurship still exists predominantly within the informal
sector (aka, the grey economy), but limited integration of modern technology limits their scalability.
These entrepreneurs face financing difficulties, bureaucratic red tape, and poor infrastructure, which
challenge business growth, particularly in politically unstable regions.  Colonial legacies have
influenced their political systems, often resulting in authoritarianism, centralized power, and
corruption. Social unrest is widespread (Vines, 2023), fueled by economic struggles and governance
issues. Ethnic and religious diversity contributes both to cultural richness and to conflicts in the studied
regions. Although modernity and gender equality movements are emerging, their traditional values of
patriarchal societies remain strong and still affecting rural women entrepreneurs in these regions. Yet,
these countries also have large youth populations, offering both opportunities for growth and risks of
instability. As g lobalization is increasingly shaping their economies and cultures, this offers both
benefits and challenges, including rising inequality and dependency (African Centre for Economic
Transformation, 2023). However, participants highlighted the transformative effects of mobile phones
and internet access, especially for rural entrepreneurs. Specifically, these technologies have improved
access to information and opened new market opportunities, with  rural entrepreneurs reporting
increased sales and revenue due to e -commerce and digital financial services. Mobile banking and
payment platforms have addressed financial exclusion, while technology in agriculture has boosted
productivity. Despite these gains, challenges remain, such as inadequate infrastructure, cybersecurity
risks, and socio-cultural resistance to technology adoption, which limit its full entrepreneurial potential

24
in rural areas.  The findings offer several contributions to the extant literature along with offering
specific practical implications, as discussed in next sub-sections.
5.1 Theoretical implications
This paper contributes to the current debate on the role of rurality and spatial contexts in rural
entrepreneurial activities (e.g., Tuitjet & Thompson, 2025 ; Müller & Korsgaard, 2018 ). Although
researchers have highlighted some aspects of rurality (e.g., Calispa Aguilar, 2021) which affects both
entrepreneurship in the rural, and rural entrepreneurship (Korsgaard et al., 2015). Also, rural
entrepreneurs’ capacity to respond to increasing dynamism and complexity in social, cultural, and
economic environments throu gh rural innovation (e.g., Hunt et al., 2021), and the impact of such
entrepreneurship activities on rural communities (Barber III et al., 2021), have also been explored. The
role of place-sensitive conditions as key determinants of strong entrepreneurial ecosystems has also
been explored (e.g., Muñoz & Kimmitt, 2019). Despite this burgeoning literature on rural context and
theory, there is a dearth of research examining the practical challenges (and opportunities) facing rural
entrepreneurs in rural Africa, especially, in a digital era. The paper addresses this gap by exploring the
interlinkages between rural entrepreneurship and digitalization in rural Africa , and the findings offer
theoretical implications for rural entrepreneurship , digitalization and African management literature
streams. First, it advances the DEE and TOE frameworks by contextualizing and testing their
integrated applicability in a setting of pronounced institutional voids. The paper demonstrates how
technological factors (infrastructure deficit), organisational factors (low human capital, gendered self-
efficacy), and environmental factors (unstable governance, patriarchal norms) are not merely additive
but interact in complex, multiplicative ways to constrain or enable digital adoption (George et al.,
2016; Soluk et al., 2021). These finding challenges prevailing assumptions in digital entrepreneurship
literature that treat these factors as independent  and instead it reveals their contingent interplay in
under-institutionalized contexts , thus contributing  a novel theoretical insight beyond empirical
replication. This application moves beyond applying known relationships to revealing their contingent
interplay in  institutionalized contexts. Second, by integrating gender, the study enriches gendered
entrepreneurship frameworks (Brush et al., 2009) within digitalization discourse. It empirically shows
how digital tools can both circumvent and reinforce traditional gender constraints, suggesting that the
relationship between technology and empowerment is mediated by entrenched socio -cultural
environmental factors.  Therefore, expanding on recent literature (Ghouse et al., 2017, 2021), this
detailed insight clarifies that digital inclusion is ineffective for rural women unless socio -cultural
barriers are addressed at the same time. Moreover, by revealing the readiness level of rural

25
entrepreneurs in five different African countries , this paper presents evidence on pitfalls of
generalizing research findings in African context, as significant differences exist in countries despite
some similarities, including in the areas of digitalization and digital readiness. Hence, theorization
needs to incorporate these locational differences, including in the context of rural entrepreneurship ,
particularly in Africa. A major implication relates to contextual theorization of the role of digitalization
for rural entrepreneurship as several factors including cultural characteristics, infrastructure
limitations, lack of training opportunities, political instability and lac k of awareness, among others
emerged as major challenges. Hence, while theorizing digitalization’s role in rural entrepreneurship,
the focus should be on contextual attributes and limitations, as well, along with discussing potential
benefits associated wi th it. Finally, the paper contributes to the literature on entrepreneurship as an
interplay between tradition and modernity, where innovation plays a critical role (e.g., Yamauchi &
Hjorth, 2024). It provides empirical evidence of ‘frugal’ or ‘bricolage’ innovati on, where rural
entrepreneurs adapt limited digital tools within traditional business models and social structures. This
model questions the belief that technology always evolves in a predictable, step -by-step manner. It
introduces a fresh conceptual angle that differs from previous studies, which typically assumes that
full digital transformation is the only viable direction. This reveals a hybrid model of digital
entrepreneurship unique to resource -constrained, rural settings, challenging assumptions of linear
technological progression.  Rural entrepreneurship in Africa in many cases have traditional roots
including inherited businesses, while at the same time, increased digitalization is bringing in modernity
elements along with ensuring (frugal) innovation, which influence both operatio ns and strategies in
several ways.

5.2 Practical implications
Digitalization represents a significant growth option along with being a tool to circumvent resource
and access limitations for rural entrepreneurs in Africa. Hence, to overcome the myriads of challenges
found in this paper, there is a need for cross-sector collaboration between public sector bodies (policy
institutions), third sector organisations (including NGOs and CSOs), and businesses to ensure that rural
entrepreneurs can use these technologies for economic empowerment and growth. Specifically,
policymakers and development agencies should move beyond generic broadband rollout. Initiatives
must be integrated, combining infrastructure investment (Technology) with targeted digital literacy
programs that build human capital and self-efficacy (Organisation), and regulatory reforms that create
stable, gender -inclusive business environments (Environment), as guided by the TOE framework .

26
Also, rural entrepreneurs, despite having some similarities, can differ a lot based on the sector they are
operating in, e.g., agricultural entrepreneurship , tourism, construction retailing, logistics, or other
services. Consequently, the need for training and intervention by other stakeholders, including
policymakers, tends to be different across sectors. Hence, these stakeholders should recognize these
differences and develop targeted intervention and support mechanisms ensuring rural entrepreneurs
gain competitiveness in their sector, rather than generic policies, whose effectiveness tends to be lower
in most cases.  For ecosystem builders (e.g., incubators, NGOs), the findings indicate the need for
gendered support programmes . Initiatives must address not only skill gaps but also socio -cultural
barriers, perhaps by engaging community leaders and designing women -only training spaces to build
confidence and mitigate the ‘penalty’ identified in the DEE. Financial inclusion effort s should be
coupled with digital tool training, as access to mobile money alone is insufficient without the skills to
leverage it for business growth (Koomson et al., 2022). Drawing directly from the findings on socio-
cultural barriers ( e.g from Participant 15, Nigeria), the paper recommends that financial institutions
design gender -sensitive digital lending products that do not require male guarantors, alongside
community-based digital literacy workshops specifically for married women to address patriarchal
constraints. For entrepreneurs, the study highlights the tangible benefits of incremental digital
adoption—even basic tools such as WhatsApp can enhance customer sourcing and supply chain
efficiency. The success stories within the data serve as relatable models, demonstrating that
strategically, context -appropriate technology use can yield significant returns despite systemic
constraints. Forming local peer networks can facilitate knowledge sharing and collective bargaining
for better digital services. For entrepreneurship education, the findings suggest curriculum redesign to
include “frugal digital entrepreneurship” modules that teach low-cost, low-bandwidth digital solutions
rather than assuming high -speed internet access. Ultimately, building a supportive digital
entrepreneurship ecosystem in rural Africa requires a synchronized, multi -stakeholder approach that
aligns technological solutions with deep organisational and environmental realities.

5.3 Limitations and future research directions
The paper has limitations as well. Firstly, there are sectoral differences due to the nature of businesses
in which rural entrepreneurs are operating. For example, agricultural entrepreneurship is different than
retailing which is different compared to other services such as construction, logistics or tourism, as
mentioned above. The paper did not consider these sectoral differences, which can be considered a
limitation and influences the findings' generalization. Future studies can build on this work and explore

27
these sectoral and other operational differences in rural entrepreneurs to offer more specific insights
to this under -researched phenomenon in African context. Also, gender plays a critical role in rural
entrepreneurship, and female entrepreneurs may face some extra challenges as well, along with the
identified ones.  Specifically, future research should integrate human capital and self -efficacy
perspectives (Ghouse et al., 2024) with institutional and financial inclusion frameworks to examine
how digital literacy and entrepreneurial confidence interact with structural constraints in rural Africa.
Longitudinal and comparative designs (e.g., across African regions or between Africa and Asia) would
capture entrepreneurial dynamics over time and enable cross -contextual theory development.  Future
studies can explore this aspect also and  see if the interlinkage between digitalization and rural
entrepreneurship differs among male and female entrepreneurs in Africa. The Sudanese war, while
mentioned in one theme, had minimal impact on its entrepreneurial development due to its ongoing
nature and limited impact beyond Sudan, except for Egypt. Additionally, the study lacked
representation from East Africa, despite covering significant geographical regions like North, South,
Northeastern, and West Africa. The refore, the conclusions drawn from this study do not encompass
the entire African continent, indicating the need for further extensive research.  Such further studies
could incorporate samples from the entire continent and utilize a mixed method to make up for the
weaknesses in purely qualitative research.  Finally, there is a need to undertake more studies on how
modernity including digitalization is influencing traditional business especially in African rural
context. Hence, specific studies on artisans, animal herders, micro-retail, logistics and services in this
concern, as such studies have potential to enrich several literature streams including rural
entrepreneurship.
