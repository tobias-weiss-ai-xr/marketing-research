INTRODUCTION
Globally, banking and insurance distribution are undergoing significant transformation as
digital technologies, changing customer expectations and new financial ecosystems redefine
how financial products reach consumers. Bancassurance, traditionally unders tood as the
distribution of insurance products through banks and their customer networks, has become an
important component of this transformation. Its principal advantages include established
customer relationships, cross -selling opportunities, shared dis tribution infrastructure and
potentially lower distribution costs (Rubio-Misas, 2022). However, conventional branch-based
bancassurance is increasingly giving way to digitally enabled models involving omnichannel,
embedded and personalized distribution. Th e scale of this transformation is evident in global
markets. McKinsey & Company (2025) reports that digital channels accounted for 28% of non-
life insurance distribution through European bancassurance in 2022, compared with 14% in
2017, while digital insur ance sales in Australia increased from 16% of premiums in 2019 to
31% in 2023. These trends demonstrate that the future of bancassurance increasingly extends
beyond the physical bank branch to interconnected digital ecosystems.
Within this evolution, omnichannel distribution involves coordinating physical and digital
channels so that customers can move seamlessly between mobile applications, websites,
branches, call centres and advisers. Embedded insurance, in contrast, integrates insurance into
the purchase or use of another product or service at the point at which the associated risk
emerges. McKinsey and Company (2023) estimates that the Asian embedded-insurance market
could reach approximately US$270 billion in gross written premiums by 2030. Personalization
further strengthens these models by applying customer and transactional data to identify the
most relevant product, timing and communication channel. Proactive market ing instead
anticipates customer needs using financial behaviour, life events, transaction patterns and other
permitted customer information to deliver relevant solutions at appropriate moments. Global
banking evidence indicates that combining the right offer, timing and distribution channel can
produce conversion prop ensity almost 20 times higher than that of peers (McKinsey &
Company, 2024). Data -driven personalization therefore creates an opportunity to transform
bancassurance from passive cross-selling into continuous needs-based customer engagement.
Nevertheless, this development also requires responsible data governance because customers’
willingness to share information influences insurers’ ability to provide personalized products
and services (Schmidt et al., 2024).
This transformation is particularly relevant to Kenya, where a highly digitized banking system
coexists with relatively low insurance penetration. Bancassurance gross written premiums
increased from KES 19.5 billion in 2019 to KES 35 billion in 2023, repre senting growth of
79.4%, while its insurance distribution market share increased from 8.4% to 10% (Association
of Kenya Insurers [AKI], 2024). Despite this growth, Kenya’s overall insurance penetration
remained only 2.44% in 2024, with total premiums of KE S 395.30 billion (AKI, 2025). Low
penetration is driven largely by the predominance of informal agricultural livelihoods and
micro-enterprise activity, segments that often lack formal bank transaction profiles and
therefore remain only partially reachable through conventional bancassurance channels. Digital
and omnichannel approaches, especially when combined with mobile -money ecosystems,
agent networks and embedded insurance at the point of everyday transactions offer a pathway
to extend coverage beyond already-banked customers into these informal segments.
This paper therefore advances the proposition that banks and insurance companies operating
within bancassurance partnerships must adopt a proactive rather than reactive approach to
marketing. Operationally, proactive marketing is defined here as the systematic use of customer

LITERATURE REVIEW
Theoretical Review
Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)
The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) was developed by Fred Davis in 1989, drawing
from the Theory of Reasoned Action developed by Fishbein and Ajzen (1975). Davis (1989)
proposed TAM to explain and predict individuals’ acceptance and use of information
technology. The theory argues that technology adoption is primarily determined by two beliefs:
perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Perceived usefulness concerns the extent to
which individuals believe that using a particular technology will impro ve their performance,
whereas perceived ease of use concerns whether the technology can be used with minimal
effort (Davis, 1989). Subsequent proponents expanded the model. Venkatesh and Davis (2000),
through TAM2, incorporated social influence and cognitive instrumental processes to explain
perceived usefulness and technology adoption. Venkatesh and Bala (2008) subsequently
developed TAM3 by integrating determinants of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of
use and highlighting interventions that organ izations can employ to enhance technology
adoption.
Despite its extensive application, TAM has attracted criticism. Benbasat and Barki (2007)
argued that excessive reliance on TAM diverted researchers from other important determinants
of technology adoption and that numerous extensions had contributed to th eoretical
fragmentation. Turner et al. (2010), through a systematic literature review, further established
that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use were less consistently associated with
actual technology usage than with behavioural  intentions, raising questions regarding TAM’s
predictive capacity beyond intentions.
A further limitation arises in the context of embedded insurance ecosystems. Classical TAM
assumes that a user intentionally evaluates a standalone technology before deciding to adopt it.
In embedded insurance, however, the customer frequently purchases a primary good or service
such as an airline ticket, a bank loan, a mobile -money transfer or a retail purchase where
insurance is bundled invisibly or near-invisibly via APIs at the point of transaction. Adoption
is therefore often passive, zero-click or low-friction rather than the result of deliberate cognitive
evaluation. Under these conditions, the traditional constructs of perceived usefulness and
perceived ease of use operate differently: usefulness is inferred from the relevance and timing
of the embedded offer rather than from active appraisal of an insurance platform, while ease of
use collapses into the near -absence of additional effort required to accept the cover.
Consequently, TAM must be adapted for embedded environments by incorporating constructs
such as contextual relevance, friction lessness, trust in the primary provider, and post-purchase
awareness. Perceived usefulness becomes a function of how well the insurance address the risk
inherent in the primary transaction, and perceived ease of us e is redefined as the degree to
which the customer experiences the cover as an automatic or one -tap extension of an already-
accepted purchase journey.

may be insufficient unless customers perceive the resulting channels, or the embedded offers

Relationship Marketing Theory
Relationship Marketing Theory is principally associated with Leonard Berry, who introduced
the concept of relationship marketing in 1983 within the services -marketing literature. Berry
(1983) conceptualized relationship marketing as an approach concerned n ot merely with
attracting customers but also with maintaining and enhancing customer relationships. The
theory consequently shifted marketing thinking away from isolated transactions towards
continuous, long -term customer engagement. An important subsequen t development came
from Morgan and Hunt (1994), who proposed the Commitment -Trust Theory of Relationship
Marketing. They argued that successful relational exchanges depend fundamentally on trust
and relationship commitment, which operate as key mediating v ariables leading to outcomes
such as cooperation and successful long -term relationships. Palmatier et al. (2006) further
demonstrated through meta -analysis that relationship -marketing activities influence
performance through relational mediators, particula rly commitment, trust and relationship
satisfaction.
Relationship Marketing Theory has nevertheless been criticized. O’Malley and Tynan (2000)
questioned whether relationship marketing principles developed principally within service and
industrial contexts could be transferred straightforwardly to mass consu mer markets,
identifying unresolved conceptual, practical and empirical issues surrounding its expanded
application. A further challenge arises in contemporary bancassurance environments
characterized by hyper -automation, algorithmic decision -making and em bedded distribution.
Classical Relationship Marketing Theory relies heavily on relational mediators such as trust
and commitment that are typically built through repeated interpersonal interactions between
bank or insurance staff and clients. In zero -click or low -friction embedded models, and in
highly automated omnichannel journeys, human touchpoints are minimized or eliminated.
Trust must therefore be reconceptualized as algorithmically mediated rather than
interpersonally generated. In these settings, tr ust is established and sustained through
transparent data-governance practices (clear consent mechanisms, visible privacy controls and
accountable use of customer information), reliable and frictionless claims processing,
consistent delivery of relevant em bedded offers, and demonstrable system integrity.
Commitment, in turn, is signaled by the customer’s continued acceptance of automated
recommendations and by the provider’s consistent fulfilment of algorithmic promises.
Relationship satisfaction emerges from the perceived fairness, predictability and usefulness of
the automated experience rather than from personal rapport.
Despite these adaptations, the theory remains central to the present review. It provides the
theoretical basis for examining how customer data, personalization, timely communication,

Literature Review
Recent global literature demonstrates that insurance distribution is moving from reliance on
individual channels towards combinations of physical, digital and embedded channels. Barwitz
and Maas (2020) investigated multichannel customer journeys within the motor insurance
market to establish the determinants of customers’ channel choices. The study adopted a
quantitative cross -sectional design and obtained 338 valid responses through a w eb-based
customer survey. Customer journeys were classified according to combinations of digital and
personal channels, while multinomial logistic regression was used to identify factors
influencing channel selection. The study established that customers f requently combined
digital and personal channels during their insurance journeys. Digital channels were
particularly important during information search, but customers often moved to personal
channels when completing purchases. The findings demonstrate tha t digitization does not
necessarily eliminate traditional channels but increases the importance of integrating them into
seamless customer journeys.
Meyers and Van Hoyweghen (2020) examined data -driven personalization within motor
insurance by investigating a Belgian insurer’s experimentation with behaviour -based
insurance. The study adopted a qualitative case -study approach involving an experiment wit h
approximately 5,000 participants whose driving behaviour was monitored through smartphone
technology. Semi -structured interviews and documentary evidence were analysed to
understand how insurers developed personalized products from behavioural data. The findings
showed that smartphones, behavioural  data and analytics enabled insurers to progress from
conventional risk classifications towards individualized pricing and customer propositions.
However, successful personalization required considerable experimentation, technological
capability and organi zational learning. The study is relevant because it demonstrates that
personalized insurance is not merely a marketing technique but depends on insurers’ capacity
to transform customer data into appropriate products and propositions.
These European studies reflect advanced digital infrastructures and strict GDPR -compliant
regulatory ecosystems in which data protection, consumer consent and channel integration are
already highly formalized. Kenya’s financial landscape differs markedly: it is dominated by
mobile-money ecosystems (particularly M -PESA), high mobile penetration and distinct
regulatory oversight by the Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) for banking and payments and the
Insurance Regulatory Authority (IRA) for insurance conduct and p rudential matters.
Consequently, while the European findings on multichannel integration and data -driven
personalization remain conceptually transferable, their practical application in Kenya must
account for mobile -first customer journeys, lower formal ba nking density in informal and
agricultural segments, and a regulatory environment still evolving to accommodate embedded
and API-driven distribution models. This contrast justifies the selective applicability of the
European evidence while underscoring the need for context-sensitive adaptations.

Rubio-Misas (2022) investigated bancassurance and the coexistence of multiple insurance
distribution channels within European markets. The quantitative study used longitudinal
insurer-level data and applied cost - and profit-efficiency measures together wit h a Heckman
two-stage random -effects regression model. The findings established that using banking
channels improved insurers’ cost efficiency, although distribution -channel choice did not
significantly influence profit inefficiency. Importantly, the study found no universally optimal
distribution channel; instead, different channels possessed comparative advantages in
particular customer and product segments. Rubio -Misas (2022) therefore supports a flexible
approach in which bancassurance operates alongsid e alternative distribution mechanisms
rather than attempting to replace them completely. This finding is particularly relevant to
contemporary omnichannel bancassurance, where branches, advisers, mobile applications and
other digital platforms increasingly coexist.
Pham et al. (2022) examined customer adoption of insurance applications within a developing-
market context. The study combined preliminary qualitative interviews with a quantitative
customer survey. Ten insurance -app users and five developers/managers part icipated in the
exploratory stage, while convenience sampling produced 209 questionnaires, of which 166
were valid for quantitative analysis. Cronbach’s alpha, exploratory factor analysis, Pearson
correlation and multiple regression were employed. The find ings demonstrated that trust,
perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use significantly influenced customer attitudes and
intentions towards insurance applications. The study illustrates that future insurance
distribution requires more than technologica l availability: platforms must also provide
convenient, trustworthy and useful experiences capable of encouraging customers to adopt
digital insurance services.
Within Sub -Saharan Africa, digitalization is increasingly associated with insurance -market
development and financial inclusion. Sibindi (2022) investigated whether information and
communication technology adoption influenced life -insurance market developme nt in Sub -
Saharan Africa. The study employed longitudinal secondary data and panel -regression
techniques to analyse the relationship between ICT indicators and life-insurance development.
The results established important relationships between technological adoption and insurance-
market development and emphasized the potential contribution of fintech and insurtech  to
overcoming conventional distribution constraints. The study recommended ICT -friendly
policies alongside appropriate insurance regulation to facilitate market development. For
bancassurance, these findings suggest that African banks’ increasingly sophis ticated digital
platforms could provide infrastructure through which insurance companies expand customer
reach beyond conventional branches.
Similarly, Sodokin  et al. (2022) investigated digital transformation, banking stability and
financial inclusion using panel data covering 36 Sub -Saharan African countries from 2004 to
2017. The researchers applied simultaneous panel -model estimates to examine the
relationships among technological transformation, banking stability and financial inclusion.
Digital transformation and banking stability produced positive and statistically significant
effects on financial inclusion, while the effect of digitalization became strong er when the
banking sector was stable. The findings are relevant to bancassurance because they demonstrate
the potential of established banking systems to serve as platforms through which digitally
delivered financial products can reach broader populations.
FSD Africa (2022) examined insurance innovation and regulation across eight Sub -Saharan
African markets: Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, Malawi, Nigeria, Rwanda, Uganda and Zimbabwe.

In Kenya, Magati (2021) investigated the influence of distribution channels on life -insurance

purchasing behaviour  and that electronic and bank -led channels have become important

channel. Its limitation, however, lies in its focus on a single insurer and a relatively narrow set

Nyabera (2022) examined the influence of digitalization on insurance penetration in Kenya
using a descriptive research design. The study specifically considered big-data technology, data
storage and protection technology, and insurance communication technology as dimensions of
digitalization. The research established the relevance of digital technologies to expanding
insurance access and highlighted the importance of technological infrastructure in addressing
Kenya’s persistently low insurance penetration. While u seful, the study remains largely
descriptive and does not fully unpack the organisational or channel -integration mechanisms
required for sustained performance gains.
Naggayi (2023) subsequently examined digitalization and operational performance among all
61 insurance companies operating in Kenya. A cross -sectional descriptive design was
employed, with operations managers serving as respondents. Descriptive statistics and multiple
regression were used in analyzing the data. Digitalization was positively related to operational
performance, with 84.2% of variation in operational performance attributed to digitalization of
policy-administration and procurement functions in  the reported model. However, claims,
underwriting, peer-to-peer processes and customer onboarding were not significant predictors,
partly reflecting incomplete automation. This finding is particularly instructive: it reveals that
Kenyan insurers have achi eved only partial digital maturity. Core customer -facing and risk -
assessment functions remain insufficiently integrated, indicating that superficial digitization of
back-office processes is insufficient. True performance gains require deeper omnichannel
integration that seamlessly connects front-end customer journeys (including mobile-money and
embedded touchpoints) with automated underwriting, claims and service processes. Naggayi’s
results therefore strengthen the argument that banks and insurers must mov e beyond
fragmented digital initiatives toward coherent, end -to-end omnichannel architectures capable
of delivering both efficiency and customer-centric value.

Africa and global markets. A scoping review was appropriate because contemporary
bancassurance spans banking, insurance, marketing, and financial technology, with relevant

seminal studies were retained for theoretical grounding. Studies were included where they
addressed bancassurance, insurance distribution, digitalization, personalizati on, embedded

demonstrated sufficient methodological or institutional credibility. Duplicates, unverifiable

Thematic Findings
Building the Future of Bancassurance Requires Foresight and Flexibility
Reviewed literature consistently showed that bancassurance can no longer be treated as a static
arrangement in which insurance companies simply gain access to a bank’s branches and
customer database. Rather, bancassurance is becoming an evolving distribution ecosystem
requiring banks and insurers to  anticipate technological, regulatory and behavioural  changes.
Rubio-Misas (2022) demonstrates that although banking channels generate efficiency benefits,
no single distribution mechanism is universally optimal. Different channels possess different
comparative advantages, making flexibility an important characteristic of sustainable insurance
distribution.
This need for foresight becomes particularly important when regulatory environments change.
Across Sub-Saharan Africa, regulators face the dual responsibility of promoting insurance -
market innovation while protecting consumers. FSD Africa’s (2022) examinat ion of eight
African insurance markets revealed that low penetration persists despite considerable untapped
demand, particularly among informal workers, rural populations and MSMEs. Regulatory
systems must consequently facilitate innovation without weakeni ng market integrity or
consumer protection. In Kenya, this issue is particularly relevant following regulatory reforms
affecting bancassurance intermediaries and the institutional arrangements through which banks
participate in insurance distribution.

their physical branch networks. Banks increasingly possess customer transaction histories,
mobile platforms, payment infrastructure, lending relationships and digita l communication
channels. These capabilities potentially enable banks and insurers to identify changing

The Move to Omnichannel Distribution
The reviewed literature further showed the transition from multichannel availability to
genuinely omnichannel distribution. Multichannel distribution means providing several
channels through which customers can interact with an insurer, whereas omnichannel
distribution requires those channels to operate as an integrated customer journey (Barwitz  &
Maas, 2020). A customer might therefore discover an insurance product through a mobile -
banking application, obtain further information online, consult a bank adviser and eventually
complete the purchase digitally without repeatedly providing the same in formation. Barwitz
and Maas (2020) findings show that insurance consumers frequently combine digital and
personal channels rather than relying exclusively on one channel. Digital platforms were
important for searching for information, while human interaction remained influe ntial during
purchasing. This implies that the appropriate strategic response to digitization is not necessarily
eliminating branches and advisers but connecting them effectively with digital platforms.
This has significant implications for bancassurance because declining branch dependency
reduces the effectiveness of the traditional model in which bank customers encounter insurance
representatives while conducting unrelated banking activities. Evidence f rom Sibindi (2022)
found that ICT development has important implications for life -insurance-market
development, while Sodokin et al. (2022) demonstrated positive relationships between digital
transformation and financial inclusion across 36 Sub -Saharan African countries. Kenya is
especially well positioned for this transformation because digital banking has become deeply
embedded in everyday financial activity. However, omnichannel distribution should extend
beyond acquisition. Digital and physical channels need to remain connected throughout policy
servicing, claims, renewals and customer support. Kenyan evidence from Angima and Jebiwott
(2022) indicates a strong positive relationship between claims digitalization and service
delivery, with claims automation, audit controls and back -end claims management emerging
as significant predictors. Thus, an effective omnichannel strat egy must cover the entire
customer lifecycle rather than simply digitizing the initial sale.
An important caveat concerns channel cannibalization and inclusive access. Aggressive
migration toward digital and omnichannel models risks alienating traditional or older
demographic segments that continue to prefer face -to-face interaction, paper-based processes
or branch-based service. These customers, often characterized by lower digital literacy, limited
smartphone access or established relational trust with branch staff, may experience exclusion
or reduced satisfaction if physical channels are scaled back too rapidly. Institutions must
therefore design omnichannel strategies that preserve inclusive access rather than simply
substituting digital for physical touchpoints. This can be achieved by maintaining adequately
staffed branches and agent networks as fully integrated components of the omnichannel
architecture, offering assisted digital journeys in which advisers help customers navigate
mobile or online platforms, and ensuring that data continuity allows a customer who begins a
journey digitally to complete it in-branch (or vice versa) without friction or information loss.

Beyond Traditional Bancassurance: Personalization and Embedded Insurance
The transition from standardized cross -selling towards personalized and embedded insurance
was also another theme from reviewed literature . Meyers and Van Hoyweghen (2020), for
example, demonstrated how smartphone -generated behavioural information could support
individualized insurance propositions. The wider implication for bancassurance is substantial
because banks already generate extensive information th rough customers’ transactions,
savings, borrowing and payment activities. Where legally and ethically permissible, such
information can enable more precise identification of customers’ insurance needs.
Embedded insurance moves this principle further by placing insurance directly within another
transaction. Evidence from Swiss Re Institute (2023) describes embedded insurance as
integrating insurance protection into the purchase of another product or service, bringing
coverage closer to the point at which the underlying risk arises. Digitalization has enabled
insurers to es tablish partnerships with financial and non -financial platforms and offer
customized or white -labelled insurance products.  Nevertheless, personalization introduces
concerns surrounding privacy, consent, transparency and potential inappropriate use of
customer data. Banks and insurers must therefore balance commercial personalization with
responsible data governance. Without cu stomer trust, increasingly sophisticated
personalization may become counterproductive.
Buying Health Insurance at the Bank
The literature also points towards diversification of bancassurance beyond products
traditionally linked to banking transactions. Credit life, mortgage protection and motor
insurance have natural connections with lending, but emerging bancassurance models
increasingly incorporate health, wellness and living -benefit products. This development is
important because significant health-insurance gaps remain across Sub-Saharan Africa. Digital
and alternative distribution models provide an opportunity to reach pop ulations inadequately
served by traditional employer -sponsored or intermediary -based insurance. Banks occupy a
strategically useful position because they maintain continuing financial relationships with
customers, including self-employed people, entrepreneurs and small-business owners who may
not obtain health insurance through conventional employment arrangements.
The development of health bancassurance also changes the nature of the customer relationship.
Unlike insurance products that become visible primarily when a claim occurs, contemporary
health products can involve frequent customer interactions. Telemedicine , wellness
applications, pharmacy services, health monitoring and preventive -care benefits create
opportunities for continuous engagement. Bancassurance can consequently evolve from selling
protection against an uncertain future event towards providing an ongoing service ecosystem.
For banks, such services can strengthen the broader customer value proposition, while insurers
benefit from access to established digital platforms and customer relationships. For customers,
integration may simplify the process o f discovering, purchasing and using health protection.
However, effective health bancassurance requires careful product design because customers’
health needs, affordability and coverage expectations differ substantially.
In the Kenyan context, any expansion of private health bancassurance must be situated within
the evolving public universal health coverage (UHC) architecture. Kenya is currently
transitioning from the National Hospital Insurance Fund (NHIF) model toward the Social

needs-based rather than product-led. Instead of asking which existing insurance product can be
sold to a bank customer, institutions should identify the customer’s p rotection needs, taking
into account both public entitlements and residual private gaps and determine how appropriate
coverage and related services can be incorporated into the existing banking relationship.
Flexibility for the Future
From the reviewed literature, it is evident that o mnichannel, personalized and embedded
insurance cannot function effectively when banks and insurers operate fragmented legacy
systems that cannot exchange information or modify processes quickly.  Automation is
particularly important in underwriting, onboarding, policy administration and claims. Naggayi
(2023) found that Kenyan insurance firms had implemented digitalization only to a moderate
extent and identified important differences across insura nce processes. Although digitalized
policy administration and procurement contributed to operational performance, areas including
underwriting and customer onboarding remained constrained, partly because appropriate
systems had not achieved full automation . Similarly, Angima and Jebiwott (2022) found that
claims digitalization improved service delivery but remained incompletely implemented.
These findings indicate that digitizing the customer -facing interface without transforming
underlying insurance processes may create a superficially digital system that remains
operationally slow. A customer might apply for insurance through a mobile application but still
experience manual underwriting, document submission or claims processing behind the
interface. Genuine omnichannel bancassurance therefore requires end -to-end integration.
Flexible technological architecture is equally important for embedded insurance because
banking and insurance platforms must communicate in real time. The same flexibility is
required organizationally. Banks and insurers must agree on data governance, prod uct
development, customer ownership, service responsibilities and performance measures.

customers enter a branch for unrelated banking activities. As banking  behaviour migrates
rapidly toward mobile applications, USSD and online platforms, the dominant touchpoint
architecture is shifting from infrequent physical interactions to continuous digital micro -
moments. Customers now interact with their banks multiple times a day through brief, context-
rich digital encounters —checking balances, transferring funds, applying for credit or paying
bills. These micro -moments constitute the new primary sites of opportunity. Capturing the
remaining penetration gap therefore requires banks and insurers to redesign distribution around
these digital touchpoints rather than continuing to rely on declining branch traffic.
For licensed banks, the strategic value of bancassurance consequently depends on treating
insurance as a significant component of the customer relationship rather than as an ancillary
product occasionally introduced by branch staff. Kenyan banks already po ssess much of the
infrastructure required to effect  this transition. In 2024, 98% of surveyed banks had adopted
mobile applications or USSD services, while 79% had adopted APIs and approximately 40%
reported adoption of big -data and data -analytics technologies (CBK, 2025). The practical
question is therefo re how these technologies can be converted into insurance -distribution
capability that operates within digital micro -moments. Banks should integrate insurance
propositions into existing digital customer journeys instead of expecting customers to
deliberately navigate to a separate insurance marketplace. A customer applying for vehicle
financing, for example, presents an immediate opportunity to offer appropriately priced motor
insurance within the same process. A mortgage application can similarly trigger p roperty,
mortgage-protection or life -insurance options, while international travel -related transactions
can provide an appropriate context for travel insurance.
Second, insurers need to design products specifically for banking journeys instead of simply
transferring products originally developed for agents and brokers into banks’ digital channels.
Embedded insurance is effective when the product is simple enough t o understand,
appropriately priced and capable of being issued with minimal interruption to the primary
transaction. Underwriting automation is therefore critical. Kenyan insurers partnering with
banks should identify products and customer segments for whi ch underwriting decisions can
be automated or substantially simplified ( Angima & Jebiwott, 2022). Straight -through
processing should become feasible for relatively standardized products where sufficient
information is available and regulatory requirements permit it. Where additional assessment is
necessary, digital journeys can be s upplemented with telephone underwriting or specialist
human assistance. The objective should not be to remove people entirely but to deploy human
expertise where it adds value rather than requiring human intervention at every stage. The same
principle applies after the sale. A customer who purchases insurance through a bank should not
experience a seamless digital purchase followed by a completely disconnected claims process.
This is what distinguishes genuine omnichannel bancassurance from merely selling i nsurance
online. There is also considerable opportunity for product diversification, particularly in health
and living-benefit insurance. Kenyan bancassurance has historically benefited from products
naturally associated with credit, mortgages and motor fi nancing. Bancassurance platforms
could therefore offer appropriately designed health products supplemented by telemedicine,
wellness support and other services. This would shift the relationship from occasional policy

awareness, consideration, conversion, retention and advocacy funnel —now operating
predominantly within digital micro-moments rather than physical branch encounters. Only by
aligning distribution architecture with the evolved touchpoint reality can the sector convert
bancassurance growth into meaningful gains in overall insurance penetration.
Conclusion
As bancassurance continues to evolve in the wake of COVID -19, the winners will be those
institutions that position themselves now . This will be possible through flexible tools,
embedded product design, and adaptable underwriting technology to meet consumers where
they are, rather than waiting for consumer behaviour to revert to pre-pandemic, branch-centred
patterns. Kenya's 2022 regulatory reforms have narrowed the field of licensed bancassurance
intermediaries, but the underlying global and regional evidence points toward continued, and
potentially accelerating, importance of bank -distributed insurance. Therefore, b anks and
insurance companies that adopt a proactive, omnichannel, and increasingly embedded
approach to marketing , extending into personalization, health and wellness benefits, and
automated underwriting, will be best placed to capture this opportunity.
Implications for the Study
The study’s implications underscore that sustainable bancassurance growth in Kenya requires
a fundamental shift from branch -dependent cross -selling to proactive, omnichannel and
embedded distribution aligned with digital micro -moments. Banks must integrate  insurance
into existing mobile and API -driven journeys, while insurers redesign simple, automatable
products suited to low-friction banking contexts. Regulatory bodies (IRA, CBK and the Data
Protection Commissioner) need to update conduct, disclosure and data-governance rules so that
embedded offers remain transparent and trustworthy. Industry associations should develop
shared standards for digital journeys and post -sale service. Health bancassurance can
complement public UHC frameworks by offering top -up cover and wellness services.
