Purpose: This work aims to resolve this research fragmentation around the
concept of adoption in FinTech and to present an empirically validated novel
quadripartite integrated framework that integrates constructs from
Technology Adoption (UTAUT2), Service Quality (DBSQual/SERVQUAL),
Information Systems Success (DeLone & McLean) and Customer Experience
(CX) theory. The framework and its validation are based on objective
behavioural data, distinguishing it from the current literature that has been
limited to self-report measures.
Design/Methodology/Approach: We surveyed 450 active FinTech users
across India from mobile payment applications, neobanks and traditional
banks digital platforms, a cross -sectional survey. To analyse the data we
applied PLS -SEM, drawing inferences from 5,000 resampling iterations.
Convergent validity was established with three different objective measures
of behaviour based on the platform usage logs (transaction intensity, adoption
of a feature, and a Behavioural Engagement Index).
Results: All except two of the eight hypothesised pathways are statistically
significant. The highest Predictors of Emotional Engagement are
Personalisation (β = 0.148, p < 0.001), Performance Expectancy (β = 0.105, p
< 0.05) and Hedonic Motivation (β = 0.092, p =  0.051 marginal). There is a
strong association between Emotional Engagement and User Satisfaction (β =
0.109, p < 0.05) and Behavioural Loyalty (β = 0.230, p < 0.05). Information
Quality is the strongest direct driver for User Satisfaction (β = 0.155 and p <
0.01). Trust—rather than Security alone—drives Habit (β = 0.148, p < 0.001).
Convergent validity is confirmed with behavioural validation: self -reported
usage is correlated with objective transaction logs (r = 0.895); and self -
reported satisfaction with the Behavioural Engagement Index (r = 0.862).
Practical Implications:  FinTechs need to build emotional connection with
the users by first figuring out how to enhance trust, then the next most
effective way to keep users (i.e. data -driven personal experiences and better
information quality). It has been shown that investing in trust results in the
greatest return on investment with regards to user satisfaction. Therefore,
trust is not simply a security measure, it's a ground level expectation prior to
users' ongoing interaction with platforms.

1. Introduction
A new generation of FinTech solutions has transformed the way consumer’s access, interact and
transact with banking services. The Indian government is also promoting digital payments with its
infrastructure of UPI and the country has 870 million internet u sers, which makes it one of the
FinTech marketplaces with the Reserve Bank of India that is expanding the quickest in the world
estimating transactions processed by UPI reach over 13.7 billion transactions a month. In addition to
functionality, the competi tion for consumers is happening on the total experience they offer on
FinTech platforms, which include neobanks, mobile wallets, stock brokers and digitizing the
traditional banking business. Although the number of FinTech services is increasing, the theor etical
foundations for the relationship between FinTech adoption behaviours with the adoption
frameworks is still limited. Prior studies on the adoption behaviours of FinTechs have used a variety
of models, such as the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), th e Unified Theory of Acceptance and
Use of Technology 2 (UTAUT2), the DeLone and McLean Information Systems (IS) Success Model, and
some service quality (SQ) models (including SERVQUAL and DBSQual), which focus on different but
limited aspects of FinTech ad option behaviours. For instance, the cognitive -based acceptance
determinants (ease of use and performance expectancy) are not sufficiently captured in TAM and
UTAUT2 to capture the importance of emotional, experiential, and service quality related
determinants of acceptance. The quality -based models, on the other hand, do not sufficiently
account for the ‘hedonic' and ‘habit' behaviours that help to sustain long -term service usage. The
development of FinTech in the various waves of digital -finance innovatio n have been well
documented (Arner, Barberis, & Buckley, 2016; Gomber, Koch, & Siering, 2017; Lee & Shin, 2018),
while mobile-money and digital -payment channels have repeatedly been linked to wider financial
inclusion across emerging economies (Jack & Suri, 2016; Ozili, 2018).
One of the frequently mentioned challenges with the adoption of Fintech is the extensive use of
surveys to collect data. This results in the data being skewed and difficult to independently confirm
whether attitudinal constructs result in actual behaviours on the platform. Until now, there has been
no study to validate a multi -theoretical model of Fintech adoption with objective behavioural data
from Fintech platforms in India at the same time.
This study offers a double solution and proposes a four -part integrated framework combining
constructs from four theoretical fields: (i) Technology Acceptance Model (UTAUT2), (ii) Service
Quality (SERVQUAL/DBSQual), (iii) IS Success (DeLone & McLean, 2003) and (iv) Customer Experience
(CX). The framework is evaluated against three levels of objective behavioural data by using
transaction volume, feature adoption breadth, and Behavioural Engagement Index (BEI) and
compares it to survey data from 450 functional Fintech users in India.

Three specific objectives are set up to guide the study:
1. To build an integrated multi -theoretical model encompassing cognitive, emotional, technical
and experiential influences on the usage and satisfaction of Fintech.
2. To evaluate the framework employing PLS -SEM with resampling significance testing and
conclude all path coefficients with 95% confidence interval.
3. To test the relationship between self -reported measures and objective, platform -based
behaviour data to gain methodological rigor and mitigate issues of common method variance
in the data.
The research contributes three aspects to the field of research on the acceptance of FinTech. First,
on the theoretical side, this research substantiates that multi -theoretical integrated approaches are
better than the single model approaches. Second, on a n empirical level, this study provides a
behavioural validation architecture which has not been provided in any other survey studies to date.
Last but not least, this study is practically relevant for the FinTech practitioners and regulators as well
as platform architects who will work with emerging markets contexts in the design of FinTechs.
2. Literature Review and Hypothesis Development
2.1 Limitations of Single-Model Approaches.
Each of the most common frameworks to study fintech adoption have systemic inadequacies all on
their own. Yet the technology acceptance model (davis, 1989) provides little consideration of the
boundaries that may exist when a person adopts technology in the broader financial context, such as
the adoptee's social environment, hedonic motives, and more. Utaut2 (venkatesh et al., 2012) does
better to add hedonic motivation to tam, but it does not sufficiently incorporate the person's service
experience or level of emotional involvement in that service's use. The delone and mclean (2003) is
a success model that considers the aspects of system quality and information quality but not aspects
of the adoption environment's affective and experiential aspects of an ad optee's service use. The
servqual scoped models (parasuraman et al., 1988; bauer et al., 2005), describe the quality of service
experienced by a service recipient, but were not designed to account for an adoptee's cognitive
processes while they are going through the process of adopting the service and long-term behaviour
as a result of that adoption. Each of these traces back to a shared family of intention-based theories
— among them the Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen, 1991), the decomposed model of IT  usage
(Taylor and Todd, 1995), the extended technology acceptance model (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000),
the original unified theory of acceptance (Venkatesh et al., 2003), and diffusion of innovations
(Rogers, 2003) — several of which framed adoption through  a single explanatory lens, as numerous
systematic reviews of digital-banking acceptance have noted (e.g., Hanafizadeh et al., 2014).
Based on recent meta-analyses, the integrated theoretical frameworks have significantly more power
in explaining FinTech phenomena as compared to single theoretical models (Alalwan et al., 2020;
Patil et al., 2021). Hence, the argument in this study is tha t such an idealized one combined
framework exists and is better than any alternative, including all the drivers of adoption as well as

2.2 Performance Expectancy and Emotional Engagement
The relationship between PE (performance expectancy) and a user's future intention to use Fintech
products based on results from the UTAUT -based study conducted by Venkatesh et al. (2012) and
Alalwan et al. (2020) received strong support form user's percep tion of a strong financial effect by
using a Fintech technology platform. More specifically, when a user feels a clear functional benefit
from using a digital payment platform or gain from a digital investment platform, they will also create
emotional connections with the platform as well as solidify the initial expectation of their gain from
the platform (Hossain & Quaddus, 2021). The expectation/conformation process as being a motivator
for continued use and, therefore, emotional engagement (Oliver, 1980).
H1: Performance Expectancy has a significant positive direct effect on Emotional Engagement.

2.4 Hedonic Motivation and Emotional Engagement
Hedonic Motivation (HM) represents the pleasure or happiness which a user feels during the usage
of a platform (Venkatesh et al., 2012). As such, users of mobile financial services where they perceive
positive feelings during their transactions, gamification, aesthetic design, and reward systems, build
a stronger emotional attachment to the platform (van der Heijden, 2004; Lu et al., 2022). This is an
emotional pathway to involvement, in addition to the utilitarian mechanism of adoption.

2.5 Security, Trust, and Habit Formation
Luo et al (2020), and Bauer et al. (2005), demonstrated that security plays a significant part in
facilitating the user's experience when interacting with a FinTech platform. Based on the study
conducted by Venkatesh et al., (2012), Habit Forming (habitual  behaviours of a user using how they

thinking about it. Trust and Security are expected to increase the amount of perceived risk, however,
according to t he literature on trust, Institutional Trust (trust in the organisation demonstrating
integrity and wanting to assist) would have a direct impact on what behaviour a user would have
during their development because it would alleviate the need to evaluate th e risk multiple times
(McKnight et al., 2002). It has been well established that perceived risk influences the adoption of
DFS and continuation of use of the services in general (Featherman and Pavlou, 2003) and even when
adopting and using mobile payment in particular (Zhou, 2013).
H4: Trust positively, directly affects Habit Formation.
2.6 Information Quality and User Satisfaction
The Information Quality (IQ) which is defined as the accuracy, timeliness, completeness and
relevance of the information provided by a product/service determines user satisfaction with any IS
success model (e.g., DeLone & McLean 2003). Accurate, real -time account balances, transaction
records and market data are essential requirements for end-users of Digital Financial Services (DFS).
If the information is of poor quality, the value proposition of the service will be affected, thus
reducing the user satisfaction (Hossain & Quaddus 2021).
H5: Information Quality has a significant positive direct effect on User Satisfaction.
2.7 Effort Expectancy and Actual Usage
Effort expectancy (EE) is user's perception of ease in operating the DFS (Venkatesh et al. 2012). The
effect of EE has been extensively researched regarding behaviour and intentions but limited research
has been conducted to examine the effects on actual (rather than intended) use. If lots of options do
the same thing, keeping the interface simple and the cognitive load in working through a task are
huge factors of user behavior. That is, the users are expected to opt for the easiest option (Lu et al
2022). Look at the multilingual environments across India with different levels of digital literacy which
would like to use one and the same platform to transact. Other studies based on comparisons and
multi-country analysis also refer to the ease of use and eff ort-related factors as some of the key
determinants that influence the actual adoption of mobile -payment and mobile -banking
technologies (Liébana-Cabanillas, Muñoz-Leiva, & Sánchez-Fernández, 2018; Oliveira, Faria, Thomas,
& Popovič, 2014; Oliveira, Thomas, Baptista, & Campos, 2016).

2.8 Emotional Engagement as Mediator: User Satisfaction and Behavioural Loyalty
There is a notion of Emotional Engagement (EEG) - the feeling that people have towards the platform
with which they interact - which has been identified as being important within the customer
experience (CX) research (Klaus & Maklan, 2013; Phan et al., 202 0). Users who are emotionally
engaged with the platform report higher levels of customer satisfaction, which can be correlated with
their increasing levels of customer loyalty - retention, recommendations and depth of their usage of
the platform. The emotional pathway towards gaining loyalty is separate from the functional aspect
of customer satisfaction, and provides the mechanism by which the FinTech provider can gain a

4. Methodology
4.1 Research Design
The design used in this study is quantitative research design with cross -sectional approach and the
main method for data analysis used in this study is PLS -SEM. Four reasons that make PLS stronger
than CB-SEM are (a) the studying nature of the study involv es forecasting and building up a theory,
which demands a PLS method to face an area where there is less prior parameteration; (b) the
number of constructs in the model is relatively large (13 constructs) with numerous mediating
pathways; (c) PLS proves to be a useful tool when the models are of a reflective type with a large
number of constructs (13), which is numerous for the use of CB -SEM; (d) pls can handle both mixed
reflective measurement types (perceptual and objective behaviours) without the need to apply
distributional assumptions (hair et al., 2017; hair et al., 2019). Following the two -stage logic of
Anderson and Gerbing (1988), we first examined the reflective measurement properties and only
then estimated the structural paths. Modest R² values were expected due to the exploratory, theory-
building approach of this study and in keeping with past multi-construct studies on FinTech adoption.
For the purpose of the structural model, the goal was not to maximize the amount of variance
explained since the integration was based on the theory of the researched variables; for the case of
a complex behavioural model with a large number of variables influencing it and of small effects,
values between 0.02 and 0.12 are accepted in social-science studies using PLS-SEM (Hair et al., 2019).

4.3 Measurement Instrument
In this study all the constructs were operationalized on a five-point Likert Scale (1 = Strongly Disagree
and 5 = Strongly Agree). The survey items were adopted from previous validated scales which
included Performance Expectancy (Venkatesh et al., (2012) UTAUT2), Effort Expectancy (Venkatesh
et al., (2012) UTAUT2), Hedonic Motivation (Venkatesh et al., (2012) UTAUT2), Habitual Use
(Venkatesh et al., (2012) UTAUT2), System Quality (DeLone and McLean, 2003), Information Quality
(DeLone and McLean, 2003), Sec urity (Bauer et al., (2005) DBSQual), Personalization (Bauer et al.,
(2005) DBSQual), Trust (McKnight et al., (2002)), Emotional Engagement (Klaus and Maklan, 2013)
and Journey Fluency (Klaus and Maklan, 2013), Actual Usage (Oliver, 1980 and DeLone and McL ean,
2003), and User Satisfaction (DeLone and McLean, 2003 Each construct had three reflective items
with a total of 39 items for the constructs. Before the full data was collected, a pilot test was done
with a sample of 30 respondents to find the clarity of the items and their comprehension of the scale.

4.5 Common Method Bias and Ethical Compliance
Harman’s single-factor test was run across the 39 items. The first unrotated component accounted
for only 12.86% of the variance (well under the 50% mark), pointing away from any dominant factor
that might induce common method bias. Collinearity was likewise not a concern: predictor VIF values
fell between 1.056 and 1.105, comfortably below conventional limits. Each participant supplied
digital informed consent before taking part, and the study was conducted in line with the institution’s
ethical guidelines.

6. Discussion
6.1 The Primacy of Personalisation and Emotional Engagement
Personalisation and emotional engagement emerged as the two most consequential levers in this
study, and the discussion below unpacks why.

Volume 3(2), (2026) 809-832

The most important result of structural model research is that Personalising is a significant predictor
for Emotional Engagement (β = 0.148, p < 0.001). This corroborates previous Customer Experience
studies (Klaus & Maklan, 2013; Phan et al., 2020) on the role of Personalisation in the FinTech space,
demonstrating that how Personalisation helps a business to be competitive is predominantly through
Emotional Engagement, rather than Functional Engagement. Personalised interface, contextual
spending patterns and customised financial recommendations create an emotional bond with the
platform for the user, extending beyond the satisfaction of the transaction.
Performance Expectancy consistently and significantly positively affects the Emotional Engagement
(β = 0.105, p<0.05). This aligns with expectation -confirmation theory (Oliver, 1980; Hossain &
Quaddus, 2021), which states that once the user can see the fin ancial productivity gains, they elicit
positive feelings from their experience with the FinTech platform which forms an emotional
connection to the platform, extending the primary functional benefit. This suggests that there are
different pathways that lea d to emotional engagement through Personalisation and Performance
Expectancy and that both affective and cognitive pathways are needed to achieve both Functional
and Emotional Engagement.
The marginal effect of hedonic motivation (β = 0.092, p = 0.051) should be considered with caution
because it is at the limit for statistical significance. This intrinsic luster -gain of dealing with the
platform seems to play a secondary role with regards to engagement within this group of users, who
were active users in financial transaction activities (task oriented). This difference in findings from
other digital contexts, such as an entertainment-focused one (van der Heijden, 2004), indicates that
the relationship between the hedonic route and other engagement felt may be stronger (have more
force) in FinTech platforms that featured more gamification elements or investment advisory
platforms (Lu et al., 2022).
6.2 Information Quality as the Dominant Satisfaction Driver
The research in the FinTech area found that Quality of Information is the significant direct factor that
influences User Satisfaction as it entered the research with low p -value of 0.01 and high beta value
of 0.155 as also indicated by DeLone and McLean’s (2003) model of Information Systems Success
outside the pathway of Emotional Engagement.
When user levels of Emotional Engagement increased, but there was low levels of Quality Data
(accurate account information, current transaction information, and reliable market data), it was a
hygiene issue that decreased U.S. satisfaction with the banks. The studies indicate that Satisfaction
Drivers are found in two different levels: (1) Information Quality as a threshold to User Satisfaction,
and (2) Emotional Engagement (expressed in differentiated enhancements) as a way to obtain high
User Satisfaction  more than if only reliable information was provided without Emotional
Engagement.
6.3 Trust as the Primary Driver of Habit Formation
With the trust in habit formation within online platforms, it has been found that there are two
different aspects of trust: Trust and Security. McKnight et al. (2002) specifically describe trust as "an

operating mechanism that facilitates an unconscious (the lack of conscious deliberation at every
point of transaction) 'disinhibition' of the service's use, encouraging the user to do so again. With this
difference in mind, service and product providers should prioritize a broader story of setting trusted
expectations for transparency in data management, client service responsiveness, and adherence to
legal requirements to inculcate clients into a habit of trusting the provider for security; mere
marketing security components will not do the trick for the client.
6.4 Emotional Engagement as a Dual-Outcome Mediator
The results of EEG data show a significant effect on user satisfaction (β = 0.109, p < 0.05) and a
stronger effect on behavioural loyalty (β = 0.230, p < 0.05). The results indicate that the emotional
engagement had a stronger relationship to observable, l ong-term behaviour (re -engagement,
referral, and continued subscription) than to immediate attitude towards user satisfaction. This
confirms the affective loyalty literature (Oliver, 1997; Shankar & Jebarajakirthy, 2019) claiming that
loyalty to a service brand is created through emotional bonding and not through logical (cost-benefit)
analysis of whether to use a service.
6.5 Behavioural Validation: Methodological Implications
The presence of extremely high correlation coefficients (e.g., r = 0.895 for AU compared to
Transaction Intensity; r = 0.862 for User Satisfaction compared to BEI) between self -reported
measures and objective behavioural measures demonstrate one form of convergent validity that has
been exceptionally rare in the FinTech literature regarding adoption. Moreover, these correlations
have two important methodological implications. First of all, it is important to note that the measure
of usage and satisfaction d erived from self -reported response data is not just a construct of social
desirability, it is true usage and satisfaction patterns. Second, the findings provided by the PLS -SEM
analysis can only obtain modest results based on R², however, the findings represent real behaviour
variation and not only inflated covariance between variables because of the design of the
questionnaires used is within one survey. Studies that stir up a hat of concern over CM bias in PLS -
SEM survey designs, generally, don't have this type of cross-method validation.

8. Limitations And Future Research Directions
This research goes on and comes up with four major discoveries. The reason for the first is that the
current analysis is limited to a cross -sectional study and no longitudinal study has been performed,
so no causal analysis of the relationships found between the variables of personalization, emotional
engagement and loyalty for the users in the study can be done. Future studies should run a
longitudinal study, to clarify the directionality and the effects these platforms may have on the user
after long term usage, because of their long time on use.
Second, the R 2 values for all endogenous constructs ranged from 0.021 to 0.064 and are not high
but acceptable when compared to the norms of PLS-SEM standard norms for multi-construct model
which is found in social-science behavioural research from Hair et al. (2019). The values are indicative
of the characteristics of behaviour in digital financial services that tend to be diffuse, affected by a
multitude of antecedents that aren't captured in any one score, such as DFAscapacity, switching
costs, income level and regulatory awareness. Importantly, this concurrent behavioural validation (r
= 0.895 for Actual Usage; r = 0.862 for User Satisfaction against the Behavioural Engagement Index)
confirms that the amount of variance explained was relatively low, and indicates that this variance is
real and measurable and not just common method inflation. Antecedent setting extended to include
background variables to enhance predictive ability but keeping the behavioural cross -validation
methods presented in this study is recommended.
Thirdly, the participants in the sample were largely urban -based, educated and digital epi -entitled,
also belonging to the 26-45 age group that represents 63% of the sample, so the findings may not be
easily replicated with other age groups or user types w ithin India, like the rural, older generations
and those not technologically new to a digital banking platform. Research should be designed so that
sampling methods will be established that will capture these under-represented groups of users.
Finally, the number of respondents in the analysis (pre - vs. post-stratification) did not significantly
affect any of the R² values of the structural model.
This study has helped shed some light on the perceptions of the personalization, emotional
involvement and loyalty in the context of digital FIs, but there is space for future research that can
help provide understanding of other constructs that can influe nce user perceptions on FIs through
digital channels.
In fourth, the research highlights that different countries have different infrastructures between
them as well consider the Indian regulatory and cultural environment (as they are regulated
differently than other countries) and the Indian FinTech industry  to ascertain whether the The
FinTech Integrative Framework will be suitable to the other places where the industry could be
implemented other than India like Southeast Asia (SEA) and Latin America (LATAM).
Future studies could/ should: (1) apply the longitudinal/experience sampling methodology to
different types of payment processes to gauge changes in adoption over time for the different types
of payment processes; (2) extend the Framework to include investment channels (e.g. Robo-Advisory
and Stock Trading Apps) where the constructs of Trust/Information Quality could prove to behave

9. Conclusion
via the development, testing, and verification (via experimentation) of an integrated,
multidisciplinary theoretical framework of FinTech usage with 450 real users (financial tech) in India,
this research contributes to our knowledge of FinTech usage. The UTAUT2, DeLone & McLean's IS
Success Model, DBSQual/SERVQUAL, and Customer Experience were all integrated into this model's
quadruple framework. When these ideas are combined, they provide a more comprehensive
understanding of how customers use digital fin ancial services than when they are used separately.
The findings provide statistical evidence for each of the eight proposed links. There is a
personalisation dimension that affects the user's autonomous cognitive route and emotional
component of engagement. satisfaction with the quality of the information. The study demonstrates
that the development of habitual FinTech use —which goes beyond security—is only made possible
by trust. Emotional engagement mediates the dual outcomes of seen behavioural loyalty and attitude
satisfaction. This study's methodological contribution is its triple concurrent validation using three
objective platform -based behaviour indicators, which raises the bar for psychometric validation
rigour in FinTech research. The significant correlations between SRS and the BEI (r = 0.862) and
between SRU and the OSU (r = 0.895) suggest that the survey captures behaviour rather than
measurement error.
FinTech practitioners will find it useful to adhere to this study's hierarchy of FinTech characteristics,
techniques, and advantages. The practitioner is aware of the most crucial factors in a customer's
decision to utilise a FinTech product or service.
