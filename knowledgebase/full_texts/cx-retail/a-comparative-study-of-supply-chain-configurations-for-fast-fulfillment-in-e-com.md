Abstract
In e -commerce, delivery speed has become a core dimension of customer experience and platform
competitiveness. Same-day and next -day delivery services do not rest solely on the efficiency of last -mile
transportation. Their performance also depends on how warehousing, inventory positioning, order fulfillment,
and delivery resources are coordinated with one another. JD.com and Amazon have developed large-scale fast
fulfillment systems in China and the United States, respectively. Both firms seek to shorten the spatial distance
between inventory and customer demand. Multi-node warehousing and inventory localization are the common
means, yet the ways in which fulfillment networks are organized and logistics resources are allocated diverge
considerably between th e two firms. The study draws on a literature review and a comparative case study
approach. JD.com' s mainland China operations and Amazon's U.S. market are placed side by side. The
comparison is organized around three analytical dimensions: fulfillment net work and inventory positioning,
logistics governance, and resource configuration. Order density, labor costs, and the broader logistics service
environment enter the analysis not as explanatory variables in a strict sense. They function instead as contextual
conditions that help interpret the observed differences. The case evidence points toward a stronger reliance on
internally coordinated fulfillment operations at JD.com. Amazon, by contrast, follows a hybrid governance
approach, combining in-house infrastructure with external logistics resources. There is little ground, based on
the two cases examined here, for claiming that a single fast fulfillment configuration can suit all market
conditions. Speed, cost efficiency, and resource flexibility have to be weighed against one another. By bringing
together supply network configuration and logistics governance perspectives, the study provides an analytical
framework for understanding why fast fulfillment configurations vary across markets.
Keywords
fast fulfillment, supply chain configuration, last-mile delivery, fulfillment network, logistics governance

1. Introduction
1.1 Research Background and Research Questions
Delivery speed and service reliability have become core competitive capabilities for major e-commerce
platforms. Same -day and next -day delivery services cannot be reduced to last -mile transportation alone.
Network design, demand distribution, and delivery strategies also shape fast fulfillment performance [1–4].
Fast fulfillment rests, in general, on moving inventory closer to demand before customer orders are placed.
Regional demand has to be forecast, products must be assigned to storage locations, and decisions need to be
made about which fulfillment nodes proc ess which orders and how delivery resources are allocated.
Warehousing decisions, inventory positioning, delivery commitments, and order fulfillment are thus tightly
interdependent rather than separable [5–6].
JD.com and Amazon were chosen as comparative cases because both operate as large e -commerce
platforms within their primary domestic markets and both have built mature fast fulfillment networks. At the
same time, the boundaries of their logistics governance differ considerably. This combination offers a common
operational basis for comparison while still providing clear organizational differences. JD.com depends on a
two-level distribution network and inventory pre-positioning to support local fulfillment. Amazon, for its part,
has pushed the regionalization of its U.S. ful fillment network, so that a larger share of orders can be fulfilled
within customers' local regions [15 –18]. The two companies share the same general goal of shortening the
distance between inventory and demand, but their network organization and their mix of internal and external
logistics resources differ in practice.
The literature has approached e-commerce logistics from several directions. Last-mile delivery, third-party
logistics integration, and differences between Chinese and U.S. e -commerce firms have all received attention
[1–5,8,10]. What remains less developed is a systematic comparison of fulfillment configurations, governance
boundaries, and market conditions for JD.com and Amazon within a single analytical framework.
The present study therefore concentrates on fast fulfillment supply chains and compares the major
configuration differences between JD.com and Amazon. Three questions are addressed:
(1) What are the main similarities and differences in the fast fulfillment supply chain configurations of
JD.com and Amazon?
(2) How might order density, labor costs, and the logistics service environment relate to different fulfillment
configurations?
(3) Under what conditions might different configurations show advantages or face limitations?
1.2 Research Objectives and Significance
The question behind this study is straightforward: why do two firms with similar fulfillment goals end up
with different supply chain configurations? JD.com and Amazon both seek to position inventory closer to
demand, yet their networks, governance choices, and resource arrangements look quite different. One way to
make sense of this is to bring inventory positioning, fulfillment networks, and logistics governance into a single
analytical frame. That frame draws on several bodies of work: last -mile delivery [1 –4], supply network
configuration [5], third-party logistics integration [8], data-driven logistics strategies [9], and transaction cost
economics [12–13]. Much of this work examines these elements from separate  perspectives. The comparison
between JD.com and Amazon is therefore useful not because one firm is more successful, but because the two
cases show how similar objectives can be pursued through different organizational paths.
1.3 Research Method and Analytical Framework
This is a comparative case analysis [14] built around three dimensions: fulfillment network and inventory
positioning, logistics governance, and resource configuration. The analysis draws on peer-reviewed academic
studies, corporate annual reports, and pub licly available operational information. Market conditions —order
density, labor costs, and the logistics service environment—are not treated as explanatory variables. They enter
the analysis as contextual factors that help interpret the differences between the two configurations. The study

1.4 Research Scope and Definition
The comparison is limited to JD.com' s mainland China market and Amazon's U.S. market. Fast fulfillment,
as the term is used here, refers to short-lead-time e-commerce services such as same-day and next-day delivery,
which depend on inventory pre-positioning, order fulfillment, and last -mile delivery. The focus is not on the
entire e-commerce operations of either firm, but on the supply chain structures behind these short -lead-time
services.
2. Literature Review
2.1 Fast Fulfillment and Last-mile Delivery
Last-mile delivery refers, in general, to the movement of goods from the final logistics node to the end
customer. This stage is characterized by highly dispersed delivery locations, small order sizes, and customer
expectations for both speed and convenience. It is therefore widely recognized as a complex operational
challenge in e-commerce logistics [1–4].
A substantial part of the existing work approaches last -mile delivery as a network design and routing
problem. Delivery node configuration, route planning, and the spatial distribution of demand have received
considerable attention, and last-mile efficiency is often traced back to these three factors [1–2]. Once delivery
commitments tighten, however, this framing becomes harder to sustain. Warehousing and inventory allocation
decisions further upstream begin to constrain what the last-mile network can accomplish. Improving last-mile
transportation alone may not be enough for reliable fast fulfillment if the inventory is simply not where it needs
to be.
Order density matters here because it affects how many customers a given facility or route can serve within
a fixed delivery window. Higher order density can raise utilization. Dispersed demand may have the opposite
effect. This is why the present study uses order density rather than population density to characterize market
demand conditions. Customer density and delivery time windows have been shown to shape the economic
performance of last-mile delivery [11], but the spatial distribution of actual orders is treated in this study as a
more direct constraint.
2.2 Supply Chain Configuration and Logistics Governance
Inventory positioning is one of the key decisions that shape fast fulfillment. Placing nodes closer to
customers can shorten delivery distances, but it may also scatter inventory across more locations and lower the
utilization of individual facilities. The problem is therefore not how many warehouses a firm operates, but how
products are distributed among them and whether that distribution matches real demand patterns [5–6].
A second decision concerns who carries out logistics activities. Prior research has linked the integration of
third-party logistics providers with internal supply chains to overall supply chain performance [8]. Self-
operated logistics, third -party logistics, and hybrid configurations each involve different trade -offs among
service control, resource investment, and operational flexibility.
Data capabilities feed into both decisions. Digital technologies and data -driven methods can support
demand forecasting, inventory planning, and logistics strategies [9,15–16]. From a transaction cost perspective,
activities with high asset specificity and strong coordination requirements may require greater internal control.
Where external service markets are mature, cooperation can provide greater resource flexibility [8].
This study therefore avoids treating logistics governance as a binary choice between self -operated and
outsourced models. The focus is on how firms combine internal logistics capabilities with external resources
under different market conditions.

2.3 Literature Gap and Research Positioning
E-commerce logistics has been studied from a range of angles. Last-mile delivery, inventory management,
logistics governance, and digital transformation have all received considerable attention [1 –13]. More recent
work has looked specifically at JD.com 's RDC -FDC inventory configuration and Amazon's regionalized
fulfillment network in the U.S. market [15–17].
What remains relatively limited, however, is research that compares the fulfillment configurations,
governance boundaries, and market conditions of JD.com and Amazon within a unified analytical framework.
The present study addresses this gap by drawing on three bodies of literature. Work on supply network
configuration and inventory decisions provides the basis for analyzing fulfillment networks and inventory
positioning [5–6]. Transaction cost economics and research on third-party logistics integration provide a basis
for interpreting differences in logistics governance [8,12 –13]. Studies on customer density and delivery
efficiency connect market conditions to logistics operations [11].
On this basis, the comparison between JD.com and Amazon is structured around three analytical
dimensions: fulfillment network and inventory positioning, logistics governance, and resource configuration.
Order density, labor costs, and logistics service environments are further considered as contextual factors used
to interpret differences in these configurations.
3. Fast Fulfillment Supply Chains of JD.com and Amazon
3.1 JD.com’s Fast Fulfillment System
3.1.1 Warehousing and Inventory Allocation
JD.com operates a two -level distribution network in which selected products are pre -positioned closer to
customers. The fulfillment network is built mainly around Regional Distribution Centers (RDCs) and Front
Distribution Centers (FDCs). RDCs hold broader product assortments, while FDCs have relatively limited
storage capacity but are located closer to end markets [16].
The central difficulty of this system lies in deciding which products should be placed at FDCs. If too much
inventory is pre-positioned at FDCs, inventory fragmentation and overstocking risks rise. If too little is pre -
positioned, a larger share of orders must be fulfilled from distant RDCs. Product selection, demand forecasting,
and inventory allocation therefore need to be optimized together rather than in isolation.
In numerical experiments reported for JD.com, assortment planning was associated with a local order
fulfillment rate 0.54 percentage points higher, from 58.29% to 58.83%. Inventory allocation was associated
with an FDC demand fulfillment rate 1.05 percenta ge points higher [16]. These gains are not only about
distance reduction. Which SKUs belong at FDCs, and how much inventory each node holds, also shape how
well the RDC-FDC structure performs.
3.1.2 Fulfillment Coordination and Technology Support
Warehousing, inventory management, and order fulfillment at JD.com are kept largely within the
company's own coordination structure [10]. Information and logistics systems link product planning, inventory
allocation, and order fulfillment together [16].
Data analytics support demand forecasting, product selection, and inventory allocation decisions [15,16].
3.2 Amazon’s Fast Fulfillment System
3.2.1 Regionalized Fulfillment Network
Amazon's U.S. fulfillment network was not always organized the way it is now. The earlier model relied
on a highly interconnected national system. That design offered greater allocation flexibility, but cross-regional
transportation and coordination became more complicated [17].

The company responded by dividing the U.S. network into eight interconnected but largely self -sufficient
regions. The share of orders fulfilled inside the customer's region rose from roughly 62% to 76% after the
change, and the average distance between fulfillment nodes and customers fell by about 15% [17].
3.2.2 Delivery and External Resources
Amazon operates large -scale fulfillment centers, sorting facilities, and delivery infrastructure of its own.
Transportation and last-mile delivery, however, draw on delivery partners and commercial carriers [17–18].
Regional demand, fulfillment capacity, inventory levels, and order allocation decisions have to be
coordinated [17–18].
4. Comparative Analysis of JD.com and Amazon
4.1 Comparison of Fulfillment Networks and Inventory Positioning
The clearest difference between the two firms lies in how local fulfillment capacity is created. JD.com
creates it through the RDC -FDC hierarchy: selected products are moved forward to FDCs before customer
orders are placed [16]. Amazon creates it by reorganizing the larger network geographically, so that a higher
share of orders can be fulfilled within the customer's local region [17]. Both designs respond to the same spatial
problem, but they solve it at different levels of the network.
The two approaches also differ in what they require from upstream decision -making. JD.com 's model
depends on product-level decisions: which SKUs should be placed at FDCs, and in what quantities. Amazon's
model depends more on regional -level decisions: how demand and fulfillment capacity should be matched
within each region. Reliable fast fulf illment therefore depends not only on transportation speed, but also on
demand forecasting, product selection, and inventory positioning.
4.2 Comparison of Logistics Governance
The governance boundary is drawn differently in the two systems. JD.com keeps warehousing, inventory
management, and order fulfillment under internal coordination. Product planning and inventory allocation are
linked through the company's own information a nd logistics systems. Amazon places the boundary further
toward external cooperation: large-scale fulfillment centers remain in-house, but transportation and last -mile
delivery draw on delivery partners and commercial carriers [16–18].
Each arrangement carries different operational consequences. Internal coordination supports cross -stage
alignment and service control, but it requires sustained investment in infrastructure, technology, and
organizational capabilities. A hybrid arrangement  can add flexibility in transportation capacity, yet it also
increases the need for information coordination and service management across  participants [8]. The relevant
question for large e-commerce firms is therefore not whether to choose self-operated logistics or outsourcing.
It is where to draw the line between internal control and external cooperation, given the operational
requirements and market conditions a firm actually faces [12–13].
4.3 Resource Configuration Trade-offs
Both firms operate logistics infrastructure at considerable scale. What separates them is not whether they
invest heavily, but how fixed and flexible resources are put together. JD.com 's integrated system can support
higher facility utilization when order volumes are high enough, but that comes with continued investment in
warehousing, technology, and delivery capabilities. Amazon also maintains substantial investment in
fulfillment infrastructure. At the same time, it uses external resources to add flexibility in certain transportation
activities [17–18].
The two firms also direct their technology efforts toward different areas, and these areas are related to the
network structures the two firms rely on. JD.com 's RDC-FDC model requires ongoing choices about which
SKUs belong at FDCs and how much stock each node should carry. SKU-level assortment planning and FDC
inventory allocation matter as a result [15 –16]. Amazon's regionalized network places greater weight  on
matching regional demand with fulfillment capacity, so its operational focus falls on regional demand-capacity
matching and transportation network optimization [17].

The two cases do not differ much in whether trade-offs exist. They differ in where the weight falls. JD.com's
configuration leans toward service control and internal coordination. Amazon's leaves more room for
combining resources flexibly. These different emphases can be understood in relation to the market conditions
each firm operates in, including order density, labor costs, and the surrounding logistics service environment.
4.4 Market Conditions and Configuration Logic
4.4.1 Order Density
The economics of inventory pre -positioning are not the same everywhere. Holding inventory at more
locations adds cost, and whether those costs are justified depends on how many orders flow through a given
node. Order density helps capture this condition.  Where density is high, facilities and delivery resources can
be used more efficiently. Where demand is thin, the same positioning may push up fulfillment costs per order
[7,11].
Population density alone may be a less direct proxy for the demand a specific facility actually faces. A city
can be densely populated while a specific facility inside it handles too few orders to justify the inventory placed
there. What matters is the flow of actual orders through the node, not the number of people living nearby. This
is why the study treats order density, rather than population density, as the more relevant condition for pre -
positioning decisions. Customer density and delivery time windows have been linked to the economic
performance of last -mile delivery [11], bu t the choice of order density as the focal market condition is an
analytical decision made in this study.
4.4.2 Labor Costs
Labor costs do not operate in isolation. Warehousing, transportation, and last -mile delivery remain labor-
intensive, so higher labor costs  may increase firms' incentive to automate [19]. Whether firms respond by
seeking external providers depends on the maturity of available logistics services and on the governance trade-
offs discussed earlier [8,12 –13]. The response is not uniform. It depen ds on demand characteristics,
technological capabilities, and the availability of external logistics resources.
Labor costs are therefore better understood as a contextual constraint rather than a direct explanation for
the differences between JD.com and Amazon. They may shape resource configuration decisions, but they do
not by themselves determine governance choices. What matters is how labor cost pressures interact with other
conditions a firm faces.
4.4.3 Logistics Service Environment
External cooperation becomes more feasible when the surrounding logistics service market can support it.
Activities with high asset specificity and strong coordination requirements may require greater internal control
[12–13]. Where external services are mature, cooperation becomes a practical option and can increase resource
flexibility [8]. The logistics service environment therefore helps shape the feasible range of governance choices,
rather than dictating which choice a firm should make.
A market with well-developed third-party logistics providers can make external resources easier to use in
transportation and delivery.  Where external services are less developed, firms may face a narrower set of
workable options for external capacity. The logistics service environment matters for cross-market comparison
because it affects which options are realistically available, without directly determining governance choices.

4.5 Comparative Discussion
The two cases do not point to a single superior configuration. JD.com places greater emphasis on internal
coordination, but that approach requires a larger internal resource commitment. Amazon retains substantial
infrastructure of its own while keeping more room to draw on outside transportation capacity. These strategic
differences are accompanied by differences in the conditions each firm faces.
Order density, labor costs, and the surrounding logistics service environment do not determine these choices.
They affect which options are realistically available. Where order volumes are sufficiently high, facilities and
delivery resources may be used mo re efficiently [7,11]. Where labor costs are high, automation may become
more attractive [19]. Whether automation is feasible in practice may also depend on technology and scale.
Where third-party logistics markets are mature, external resources may take on a larger share of transportation
and delivery work [8]. Governance boundaries, in turn, are related to the transaction cost considerations
discussed earlier [12 –13]. In neither case i s the configuration a pure reflection of market forces. Strategic
choices still matter, and the two firms have made different choices about how much control to retain and how
much flexibility to obtain through external resources.
The broader lesson is not that one model is superior. Fast fulfillment configurations involve trade -offs
among delivery speed, cost efficiency, and resource flexibility. The balance among them depends on conditions
that differ across markets.
5. Conclusions and Future Research
5.1 Conclusions
This study has compared the fast fulfillment supply chain configurations of JD.com in mainland China and
Amazon in the United States.
Both companies have pursued demand forecasting and inventory localization as ways to strengthen
fulfillment efficiency. The RDC -FDC hierarchy gives JD.com a way to move selected products closer to
customers before orders arrive. Amazon reaches a similar outcome by regionalizing its U.S. network, so that
a larger share of orders can be fulfilled within customers' local regions. The two firms differ less in what they
are trying to do than in the network level at which they do it.
JD.com keeps its core fulfillment activities under internal coordination. Amazon pairs internal
infrastructure with external transportation and delivery resources. These choices are not independent of market
conditions. Order density, labor costs, and the surrounding logistics service environment affect how suitable a
given configuration is likely to be, but they do not determin e it. The two cases do not point to a single
configuration that suits all markets. The balance among delivery speed, cost efficiency, and resource flexibility
shifts with the conditions a firm faces.
5.2 Managerial Implications
A logistics model that works in one market will not necessarily work in another. How much inventory to
pre-position, and where to draw the line between internal and external capabilities, should reflect the demand,
cost, and logistics resource conditions of the target market.
5.3 Limitations and Future Research
This study draws on publicly available secondary data. The two companies report operational indicators
differently, so cost and performance comparisons remain limited. The comparison also involves only two firms,
and market -level conditions are entangled with firm -level choices. These limits constrain generalization.
Further work could add more firms and city -level data on order density, delivery distance, labor costs, and
fulfillment costs. The effects of artificial intelligence, warehouse robotics, and autonomous delivery
technologies also deserve closer study.
