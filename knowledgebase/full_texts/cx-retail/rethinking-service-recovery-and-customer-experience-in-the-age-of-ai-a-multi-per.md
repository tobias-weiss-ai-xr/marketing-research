# Rethinking Service Recovery and Customer Experience in the Age of AI: A Multi-Perspective Review

Chen, Youchun Mao and Chunlin TaoAbstract
Artificial
intelligence (AI) is gradually integrating into service systems and
beginning to play an important role in service recovery following service failures. As
AI systems take over tasks originally performed by frontline employees, the design
and evaluation methods of AI-enabled service recovery, as well as its positioning in
the overall service scenario, have become urgent issues to explore. This chapter
provides a comprehensive and structured review of existing research on AI-led
service recovery, clarifies its theoretical foundations, and integrates scattered
research findings from various disciplines. Based on an analytical framework, the
chapter organizes prior studies from three complementary perspectives: technology,
customer perception, and service context. The technical perspective highlights how
AI appearance, expressive styles, and functional boundaries shape recovery
interactions; the customer perception perspective summarizes attributional,
perceptual–affective, and relational mechanisms through which customers evaluate
AI-led recovery; the service perspective analyzes how service processes, contextual
contingencies, and relationship norms condition the effectiveness and legitimacy
of AI-enabled recovery. By mapping dominant research streams and key constructs,
this chapter advances a holistic understanding of AI-driven service recovery and
identifies directions for future research. This chapter defines AI service recovery
as a dynamic service model jointly influenced by technical design, customer cognitive
interpretation, and service system characteristics, enriching relevant research in the
service field.

IntroductionArtificial
intelligence (AI) is spreading fast across industries, and it has transformed
how service systems operate and how customers interact with businesses. AI-led
technologies are increasingly deployed at critical customer touchpoints, particularly in
handling complaints, failures, and post-purchase problems [1–3]. As service recovery
is a critical moment for regaining customer satisfaction and trust [4], the increased 1

dependence
on AI to conduct recovery activities poses key theoretical and practical
challenges regarding customer experience in the age of intelligent services [5–7].
For businesses, AI-powered service recovery sounds like a perfect solution. It can
make operations faster, easier to scale, and reduce labor costs. But real-world
experience tells a more complicated story: customers have very mixed feelings about
AI handling their complaints. Simple issues get fixed quickly through automation,
but when customers need emotional support, have complex problems, or their
situation requires understanding context, AI often falls short [ 8]. This is not just
a technical upgrade, but also a complete shift in how customers experience
responsibility, empathy, and human connection during service interactions.
Although this topic is becoming more important every day, academic research
on AI in service recovery is still limited [ 9–11]. Studies are dispersed across
marketing, service management, information systems, and human–computer
interaction, often focusing on one aspect, such as algorithmic decision,
anthropomorphic cues, customer trust, or final service results [ 12–15]. As a result,
there is a lack of a consolidated framework that integrates technological features,
customer perceptions, and specific service contexts to explain how AI reshapes
service recovery and customer experience.
Because research is so fragmented, a structured review of the literature is
necessary to synthesize existing knowledge, identify dominant research streams, and
highlight unresolved issues. This study fills that gap. This study conducted
a comprehensive review of AI-led service recovery research and organized prior
findings using an integrated “technology–customer–service” perspective. By doing
so, the study seeks to give readers a clearer picture of how AI influences recovery
processes and outcomes, as well as to offer guidance for future research and real-
world practice.
To make the review systematic and thorough, this study followed a structured
literature search and screening procedure. The literature search was conducted in
January 2026 using two major academic databases: Web of Science Core Collection
and Scopus. This study chose these two because they cover the most influential peer-
reviewed journals in marketing, management, and information systems.
The review focused on studies published between 2019 and 2025. This time
period was chosen for a specific reason: 2019 was when AI technologies, particularly
conversational agents and service robots, began to move beyond small experiments
and started being used widely in commercial services. Then, the explosion of
generative AI in the early 2020s made even more researchers pay attention to
AI-powered service interactions. Therefore, the selected period captures both the
emergence and the consolidation of AI-driven service recovery research.
The search was conducted in the title, abstract, and keyword fields.
A combination of Boolean operators was used to ensure precision and coverage.
The main search terms were: (“artificial intelligence” OR “AI” OR “chatbot*” OR
“service robot*” OR “intelligent agent*”) AND (“service recovery” OR “service
failure” OR “complaint handling” OR “recovery strategy” OR “service encounter”).
To avoid missing relevant studies, supplementary terms such as “human–AI
interaction,” “automated service recovery,” and “technology-mediated service”
were also applied in separate queries. Truncation symbols (e.g., chatbot*) were used
where needed to include different forms of the same word.

stages. First, duplicate entries across the two databases were removed. Next, titles AI

service recovery and customer experience in the AI age.2.
An integrated framework of AI-driven service recoveryService
recovery refers to the actions taken by firms after a service failure to solve
customer problems, compensate for losses, and repair customer relationships [16].
In traditional service research, service recovery has been viewed as a critical stage of
the service encounter. Frontline employees are expected to restore customer
satisfaction and trust through apologies, explanations, and compensation [12, 17–19].
However, the rapid growth of digital technologies has changed how recovery is
delivered and who takes part in the process.
AI is now widely used across service industries, and its role in service recovery
has expanded considerably. Rather than relying solely on human employees, many
firms use AI systems to handle complaints, identify the causes of failures,
recommend solutions, and implement recovery decisions. Examples include
chatbots, virtual assistants, intelligent customer service systems, and automated
compensation tools [2, 20, 21]. Compared with human-led recovery, AI can process
large volumes of requests more quickly and deliver more consistent responses,
making it particularly useful for routine service failures.
As service technologies have developed, recovery practices have moved from
fully human interactions to human–AI collaboration and, in some cases, AI-led
recovery. Service recovery is, therefore, no longer only an interaction between
customers and employees. It has become a broader process shaped by customers,
technologies, service procedures, and organizational rules. This shift also affects how
customers judge responsibility, experience care and understanding, and evaluate
recovery outcomes [22, 23].
Although AI-driven service recovery is increasingly common, there is still no
widely accepted definition. Drawing on the shared features identified in existing
studies, this chapter defines AI-driven service recovery as a set of recovery activities
in which AI directly participates in or leads customer interactions and recovery
decisions after a service failure, with little or no involvement from human staff.
These activities focus on problem resolution, explanations, and compensation.Rethinking

A review of the literature suggests that these studies mainly fall into three related
perspectives: technology, customer perceptions, and service context. Together, these
perspectives capture the key elements of AI-driven service recovery.
The technology perspective examines AI as a service tool and focuses on
characteristics such as autonomy, anthropomorphism, transparency, and decision-
making capability, as well as their influence on recovery outcomes [ 20, 24, 25].
The customer perception perspective explores how individuals think and feel when
interacting with AI during recovery. Common topics include perceived warmth,
perceived competence, trust, responsibility attribution, and perceived fairness
[2, 22, 26]. The service context perspective considers the conditions under which
recovery takes place, including failure severity, recovery timing, interaction
sequence, and the fit between AI roles and service objectives [ 27–29].

customer–service framework (see Figure 1). The framework highlights the links
between AI characteristics, customer interpretations, and service conditions. AI-
driven service recovery is, therefore, not simply a technological issue or
a psychological response. Its effectiveness depends on the interaction of technology,
customers, and the service context.

AI service recovery from a technology perspectiveAdvances
in AI technology are transforming traditional service recovery models.
With companies increasingly demanding higher service efficiency, large-scale
operations, and 24/7 responsiveness, AI tools like chatbots and virtual assistants are
being widely used in handling customer inquiries and resolving service failures [ 22].
This change has also attracted sustained academic attention, making AI-driven
service recovery a prominent research area [ 12, 20].
From a technical perspective, research mainly focuses on anthropomorphic
theories, AI Device Use Acceptance (AIDUA) models, attribution theories, and
social response theories. Anthropomorphic theories suggest that when technology
exhibits human-like characteristics, users will understand and respond to it as if
interacting with a human. In service recovery scenarios, characteristics of AI, such as
its appearance, communication style, and emotional expression affect customers’
expectations of the service and their responses to service failures [ 30–32]. The
AIDUA model focuses on how customers evaluate the capabilities of AI systems.
Customers’ perceptions of the usefulness, performance, and effectiveness of AI
affect their attitudes toward AI recovery services [ 30, 33, 34]. Thus, customer
evaluations of service recovery measures are influenced not only by emotional and
social factors but also by their expectations of technical capabilities. Attribution
theory is also widely used in this field. Customers may attribute service failures to AI
systems, the company itself, or external circumstances. The determination of
responsibility is often influenced by the degree of autonomy of AI and its role in the
service process [20, 35]. Social response theory suggests that even though AI is
not a real human, customers still tend to interact with it according to social
interaction rules. Studies have found that when AI can provide an interactive
experience similar to human communication, customers often give more positive
evaluations of recovery services [ 5, 36].
From a technology perspective (see Table 1), existing research on AI-driven
service recovery primarily concentrates on two interrelated research foci:
Appearance and expression, and functional boundaries. These streams conceptualize
AI as a technological actor whose externally designed features and functional
capabilities shape service recovery processes and outcomes.
The first stream, “appearance and expression,” focuses on how the outward
form and communicative expression of AI systems influence recovery
evaluations. Within this stream, anthropomorphism – including gender, humanoid
versus non-humanoid design, and the presence of human-like cues – has been
widely examined as a core technological attribute that frames customer
interpretations of AI agents during service recovery encounters [ 20, 30, 33, 36–40].
In addition to visual design, researchers focus on language style, voice interaction,
humorous expressions, cute language, emojis, and communication tone. These
design features can affect customers’ perceived social presence, warmth, and
engagement [ 2, 3, 21, 31, 39, 42, 43, 45, 46].
The second type of research focuses on functional capabilities. Studies typically
explore factors such as autonomy, control, practicality, and technical limitations,
and analyze their impact on recovery effectiveness and customer acceptance
[5, 35, 47–50]. Many studies also compare AI recovery with manual recovery to
understand how different service recovery agents affect customer responses
[35, 47–49, 51]. Additionally, researchers have found that AI performs differently in Rethinking

attributional reasoning, social perception, and relational evaluation processes. By
shifting the analytical focus from technological design to psychological
interpretation, this perspective complements the technology-oriented view and
provides deeper insight into how customers cognitively and emotionally make sense

AI service recovery from a service perspectiveBuilding
on the customer perception perspective discussed in Section 4, which
emphasizes how users cognitively and emotionally interpret AI-driven service
recovery, this section shifts attention to the service perspective, focusing on how AI-
driven recovery is embedded, organized, and managed within service systems. From
a service perspective, AI is no longer merely a form of technology or an object of
customer evaluation. Instead, it acts as an active participant in the service process,
and its involvement affects recovery procedures, role division, and service delivery
methods.
Traditional research on service recovery has long indicated that the effectiveness
of recovery is jointly affected by process design, responsibility allocation, and
situational adaptability. The emergence of AI agents, such as chatbots and service
robots, has further complicated these issues. AI may partially or fully replace
frontline employees, alter the schedule for implementing recovery measures, and
introduce new automated and standardized models. Therefore, the outcome of AI

Research from the service perspective mainly draws on service management
theory, attribution theory, role-related theories, service process, and justice-based
theories. These theories focus on how recovery responsibilities are distributed
among enterprises, employees, and AI, whether AI is suitable for undertaking
specific service roles, and how process design affects customer evaluations.
Meanwhile, such studies also emphasize the importance of contexts, holding that the
effectiveness of AI-led service recovery varies across different service environments.
Overall, these studies regard AI service recovery as an issue of service design and
management rather than merely a technical problem. They focus on how service
structures, role arrangements, and situational conditions jointly influence the
outcomes of service recovery [ 5, 37, 60].

technology and customer perspectives, this perspective illustrates the operational
logic of AI-powered service recovery in real-world service scenarios.6.
ConclusionsThe
rapid diffusion of AI into service settings has fundamentally reshaped
how service failures are addressed and managed. As AI systems increasingly
participate in or lead service recovery processes, traditional assumptions regarding
human agency, emotional labor, and interpersonal interaction in recovery
encounters are being challenged. Against this backdrop, this study sets out to
synthesize and organize the fragmented body of research on AI-driven service
recovery, with the aim of clarifying its conceptual foundations, identifying dominant
research streams, and providing a structured framework for future inquiry. By
positioning AI-driven service recovery as a distinct and evolving phenomenon
within service management research, this work responds to the growing need for
theoretical coherence in a field characterized by rapid technological and
organizational change.
Building on this objective, Sections 2 through 5 collectively advance
a structured and integrative framework for understanding AI-driven service
recovery. Section 2 proposed an integrated AI technology–customer–service
framework, conceptualizing AI-driven recovery as a dynamic service process
jointly shaped by technological features, customer sense-making, and service
contextual conditions. Section 3 demonstrated that technology-oriented studies
conceptualize AI as a designed service actor whose appearance, expressive
behavior, and functional boundaries structure recovery interactions and outcomes.
Section 4 shifted the analytical focus to customers’ cognitive and affective sense-
making processes, highlighting attribution-based, perceptual–affective, and
relational–fairness mechanisms through which AI-led recovery is evaluated. AI

Future research directionsDespite
growing research on AI-driven service recovery, existing studies remain
fragmented across the technology perspective, customer perception perspective, and
service perspective. Although this study proposes an integrated framework linking
these three perspectives, empirical work has not sufficiently examined their
interdependencies. In particular, limited attention has been paid to combining the
three perspectives. Future research should adopt more integrative and multi-level
approaches to examine the dynamic interaction among technology design, customer
perception, and service context, rather than treating them as independent
explanatory domains.
A second gap concerns the static nature of existing research across the customer
perception perspective and the service perspective. Most studies rely on cross-
sectional or single-encounter designs, which fail to capture the processual nature of
AI-driven service recovery. However, the service perspective highlights that
recovery unfolds through interaction sequences, timing, and coordination between
AI and human agents. Future research should, therefore, adopt longitudinal or
process-oriented designs to examine how repeated AI-driven service recovery
experiences shape customer trust, attribution stability, and relationship norms over
time. In addition, relational constructs emphasized in the service perspective – such
as communal versus exchange relationship norms and AI relationship framing –
should be further examined as dynamic rather than static mechanisms across
recovery episodes.
In addition, future work may benefit from integrating ethical and governance
considerations – such as transparency, accountability, and customer autonomy –
into the study of AI-driven service recovery, thereby extending current
frameworks beyond effectiveness toward broader questions of legitimacy and
sustainability.
A final direction concerns the theoretical boundaries underlying the
technology perspective, customer perception perspective, and service
perspective. Across these perspectives, AI is variously conceptualized as
a technological artifact, a perceptual object, and a service actor. However,
dominant theories, such as attribution theory and role-related perspectives,
remain grounded in human-centered assumptions. Future research should,
therefore, refine how responsibility, agency, and accountability are
conceptualized in AI-driven service recovery. In particular, there is a need to
