Abstract
Understanding how customers react to changes in service encounters during crisis situations, is
extremely valuable for companies. Guided by the four fundamental premises of customer
experience (Becker and Jaakkola, 2020) and drawing on the Touchpoints-Context-Qualities
(TCQ)-framework (De Keyser et al., 2020) and Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) Theory
(Mehrabian and Russell, 1974), this study aims to better understand (1) the customer experience
during a crisis, and (2) how outside versus within firm-controlled touchpoints, (3) contextual
factors (here, individual and firm/industry) and (4) a firm’s service adaptation (e.g., firms adapt
to regulatory measures) and service transformation (e.g., firms transform their business by
exploring new service delivery models) strategies shape customer experiences and coping
behaviors. Using social media data (here, tweets) in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic,
our qualitative and quantitative findings reveal that (1) customers’ affective responses are not
restricted to negative experience (here, fear, anger, sadness, and disgust), but also include
positive experiences (here, joy, surprise, acceptance, and anticipation), (2) the government as
third-party affects the customer experience, (3) service adaptions and service transformations
evoke different affective responses, and (4) both positive and negative experiences influence
customers’ coping behaviors, but positive experiences exert a greater influence on the inward
coping behaviors (here, active coping, planning, and  positive reinterpretation), while negative
experiences exert a greater influence on the outward coping behaviors (here, seeking social
support for instrumental reasons, and venting). Our findings have implications for managers
and public policy makers that want to manage the customer experience during crisis situations.
Keywords: Customer experience; Coping behaviors; Crisis; Service adaptation; Service
transformation.

1. Introduction
The customer experience (CX) is a key marketing concept and comprises a customer’s non-
deliberate, spontaneous responses and reactions to offering -related stimuli (Becker and
Jaakkola, 2020) within a specific context (De Keyser et al., 2020). Understanding and managing
the customer experience is a key priority for many firms (Caruelle et al., 2024; Zendesk, 2023).
This imperative is underscored by the findings of an executive survey conducted by Oxford
Economics in partnership with Adobe, revealing that eighty-five percent of the 1,500 executives
being surveyed, recognize that improving the customer experience is a top or significant
business priority (Adobe, 2023). Customer experience has commonly been conceptualized in
terms of the customer journey and touchpoints before, during, and after the purchase of goods
and services (Lemon and Verhoef, 2016). In developing a unified customer experience theory,
Becker and Jaakkola (2020, p. 637) proposed four foundational premises of customer
experience that are generalizable across settings and contexts, and together cover the “big
picture” of what customer experience is, what affects it, its key contingencies, and the role that
a firm can play in it.
However, in times of crises, interactions and structures change, and the meaning of the
customer experience is therefore also likely to change (Tronvoll and Edvardson, 2022, p. 539).
In business contexts, a crisis can be understood as an abnormal situation that poses
extraordinary challenges for a firm in managing its relationship with customers, with immediate
and often enduring impacts, not in the least for the customer experience (Tr onvoll and
Edvardson, 2022). For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, restaurants and hotels were
forced to close, essential shops could remain open but had to implement sanitary measures, and
the supply and demand of several products and services was severely impacted. Within
academic research, there is a clear call for a better understanding of customers during a crisis
(e.g., Sheth, 2020). Specifically, understanding how customers experience and respond to

 4
changes in the service encounter caused by mega-disruptions is being highlighted as being
extremely relevant (Berry et al. 2020; Verhoef et al. 2023). The purpose of this paper is to
deepen our understanding of customer experiences and how customers cope with changes in
the service encounter due to crisis situations and a firm’s reactions.
In doing so, we rely on the four fundamental premises of customer experience (Becker
and Jaakkola, 2020) and integrate the Touchpoints-Context-Qualities (TCQ)-framework (De
Keyser et al., 2020) and Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) Theory (Mehrabian and
Russell, 1974) to better understand (1) the customer experience (here, affective responses)
during a crisis, and (2) how outside (here, government) versus within firm -controlled
touchpoints, (3) contextual factors (here, individual and firm/industry) and (4) a firm’s service
adaptation (e.g., firms adapt to regulatory measures) and service transformation (e.g., firms
transform their business by exploring new service delivery models) strategies shape customer
experiences and coping behaviors (here, both problem-focused and emotion-focuses coping
behaviors). This paper uses publicly available customer data collected from social media (i.e.,
Twitter or now known as X1), and uses quantitative (e.g., advanced Natural Language
Processing (NLP) classification techniques) and qualitative (e.g., human-coding of text-data)
research methods to gain valuable insights from the textual data.
Guided by the four fundamental premises of customer experience (Becker and Jaakkola,
2020), this paper begins by integrating the literature on capturing the customer experience via
social media data (e.g., Zaki and Neely, 2019), the Psychoevolutionary Theory of Emotion
(PTE) introduced by Plutchik (1962, 1980) to capture the affective responses of the customer
experience, the Theory of Coping (Lazarus, 1993) to capture problem-focused and emotion-

2. Theoretical Foundations and Conceptual Framework
A crisis could have negative consequences for companies, if not managed appropriately
(Coombs, 2007), while also offering opportunities if exploited and managed wisely (Bernstein,
1996). Some organizations that perform well during the crisis may gain new customers, but
other firms appear destined to fail (Pedersen et al., 2020). As a result, knowing how to react in
the most optimal way in such situations is of importance for both managers and public policy
makers. Gaining insights into how customers experience (here, emotions as affective responses)
and respond to changes (here, customer coping behaviors) in the service encounter during a
crisis (here, the COVID-19 pandemic crisis), is therefore extremely valuable for companies.
 The four fundamental premises of customer experience proposed by Becker and
Jaakkola (2020), along with the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) Theory proposed by
Mehrabian and Russell (1974) in the field of psychology, and the Touchpoints-Context-
Qualities (TCQ) framework from the service marketing literature (De Keyser et al., 2020), form
the theoretical underpinnings for investigating the relationships between the variables of
interest in our research (See Figure 1).

In parallel, the Touchpoints-Context-Qualities (TCQ) framework, introduced by De
Keyser et al. (2020) for a more comprehensive understanding of customer experience, asserts
that the customer experience is shaped by touchpoints, qualities, and context. Specifically,
touchpoints are defined as points of interaction between the customer and the company,
qualities are attributes that reflect the nature of customer responses and reactions to these
interactions with the company, and context is defined as the situationally available resources
(De Keyser et al., 2020).
Hence, in alignment with the fundamental premises of customer experience delineated
by Becker and Jaakkola (2020), the S-O-R Theory and the TCQ framework are selected as they
nicely complement each other (See Figure 1). More precisely, in the S-O-R model, stimuli and
organism align with touchpoints and qualities in the TCQ framework, respectively. However,
the TCQ framework explicitly considers the context in which the customer experience is
embedded, a factor overlooked in the S-O-R model. On the other hand, the S-O-R model
includes the behavioral response(s) of the underlying mechanism or organism (in this case,
customer experience), a consideration not addressed in the TCQ framework.
2.1. The Customer Experience (P1, Organism & Qualities) during Crisis Situations
As their first fundamental premise (P1: “What is customer experience?”) Becker and Jaakkola
(2020, p. 637) define customer experience (CX) as “as non-deliberate, spontaneous responses
and reactions to particular stimuli”. In doing so, they adopt a multidimensional view by
considering affective, cognitive, physical, sensorial, and social responses to stimuli (e.g.,
Lemon and Verhoef, 2016). Hence, the customer experience reflects the qualities component

 8
of the TCQ framework and resonates with the individual’s emotional state (organism) of the S-
O-R model.
Given that customers frequently express their responses and reactions online, and that
this kind of data is not only publicly accessible but also recognized as valuable source for
capturing the customer experience (Zaki and Neely, 2019), this study employs text-mining
techniques to delve into the emotions of customers (i.e., affective response), as suggested by
Zaki and Neely (2019). Specifically, we rely on Twitter data to capture different emotions via
sentiment analysis (e.g., Sailunaz and Alhajj, 2019). During periods of crisis, such as the
COVID-19 pandemic, companies typically have fewer resources (e.g., reduced marketing
budgets and time) to gather data (e.g., survey data) for understanding the customer experience.
Therefore, utilizing publicly available data sources like Twitter offers a pragmatic alternative
for gaining insights into the customer experience amidst such challenges.
In line with prior experience research in the COVID-19 context, we focus on different
specific emotions (e.g., Huang et al., 2020), and include both positive and negative emotions to
measure the customer experience. In doing so, we adopt a positivist approach (See Becker and
Jaakkola (2020), p.636) and rely on the Psychoevolutionary Theory of Emotion (PTE)
introduced by Plutchik (1962, 1980) to capture the eight basic emotions: joy, surprise,
acceptance, and anticipation as positive-valenced experiential dimensions, and fear, anger,
sadness, and disgust as negative-valenced experiential dimensions.
Note that affective responses such as the experience of different emotions is core to the
experiential marketing logic for more than three decades (Zha et al., 2023). As a result, and in
line with past customer experience research (Caruelle et al., 2024; McColl-Kennedy et al. 2017;
Verhulst et al., 2020), our study mainly focuses on affective responses to stimuli. Nevertheless,
understanding the affective dimension is particularly important, as especially emotions (i.e.,
affective responses to stimuli) have been found to shape customer attitudes and behaviors

 9
(Bagozzi et al., 1999; Caruelle et al., 2024) and to play a crucial role during all stages and
touchpoints of a customer experience (e.g., Puccinelli et al., 2009;Verhulst et al., 2020), not in
the least for healthcare-related experiences (e.g., McColl-Kennedy et al., 2017) which resonates
with crisis situations like the COVID-19 pandemic.
2.2. What affects the Customer Experience (P2, Stimuli & Touchpoints) during Crisis
Situations?
As their second fundamental premise (P2: “What affects the customer experience?”) Becker
and Jaakkola (2020) underline the need to examine both within and outside firm-controlled
stimuli. In doing so, they reveal that the majority of past research has neglected the outside
firm-controlled stimuli, which is problematic since “the customer experience stimuli reside
within and outside firm-controlled touchpoints” (Becker and Jaakkola, 2020; p.639). Stimuli or
environmental cues refer to the touchpoints that are defined as points of interaction between the
customer and the company or other actors.
In the context of crisis situations such as the COVID-19 pandemic, external forces or
actors may play a bigger role in the eyes of the customers. Specifically, when different firms
and their strategic actions are examined (i.e., within firm-controlled touchpoints) and linked to
the customer experience, it is likely that in some cases these customers also blame the
government (rather than the focal firm), which could (partially) impact their reactions towards
the focal firm. In other words, it is crucial to disentangle the impact of the focal firm (i.e., within
firm-controlled stimuli) and the influence of the government as a third party (i.e., outside firm-
controlled stimuli) in order to better understand the customer experience – which is further
supported by the third-party stream in the customer experience literature (e.g., De Pourcq et al.
2021; Kranzbühler et al. 2019).
2.3. Contextual Factors (P3, Context) during Crisis Situations

 10
As their third fundamental premise (P3: “What are key contingencies for customer
experience?”) Becker and Jaakkola (2020; p. 640) posit that “customer experience is subjective
and context-specific”. When studying the customer experience, it is crucial to account for the
context in which customers are embedded (De Keyser et al., 2020).
In line with prior research on the customer experience (e.g., Li and Larivière, 2023), this
study accounts for both the situational and the individual context (See Table 2). For the
situational context, we control for firm characteristics (here, product classification and
channel). Specifically, we rely on the popular product classification presented in the marketing
textbook by Kotler et al. (2015), and for the purpose of this study, distinguish between
convenience goods/services versus other types of goods/services (Kotler et al., 2015). In
addition, we code whether a firm is mainly operating via online versus offline channels. For the
individual context, we make use of user identification data that is available from the Twitter
platform (here, the number of followers and the number of tweets posted in the past).
2.4. The Role of Firms in Shaping the Customer Experience (P4, Stimuli & Touchpoints)
during Crisis Situations
As their fourth and final fundamental premise (P4: “What role can firms play in the customer
experience”) Becker and Jaakkola (2020; p. 641) underline the role that firms can play in
shaping the customer experience by “monitoring, designing and managing a range of stimuli to
which their customers may respond”.
In this study, we examine the role of the firm (i.e., firm-controlled stimuli) via the
interactions that customers have with service providers during the COVID-19 crisis. These
interactions are shaped by the strategies that firms have implemented as a response to the crisis.
Specifically, according to Berry et al. (2020), companies can react to a crisis in two ways:
namely by implementing service adaptations or service transformations. Service adaptions are

 11
defined as a firm’s efforts to comply with regulatory measures that are often mandated by the
local government and unlikely to persist the post-crises era (Berry et al., 2020).
In the context of COVID-19, a service adaptation is what a firm does to improve safety
(e.g., facemasks, disinfecting surfaces, and social distancing). Service transformations are
defined as a firm’s efforts to transform their business in a more innovative way, such that these
changes represent additional benefits to customers that are likely to be superior to what existed
before the crisis, such that they are more likely to persist the post-crisis era (Berry et al., 2020).
In the context of COVID-19, a service transformation improves safety but does so in an
innovative, appealing way while offering additional benefits that are inherently better than
what existed previously (e.g., the introduction of online grocery shopping, telehealth
appointments with doctors). Therefore, service transformations offer “safety plus” options that
many customers may desire for the indefinite future (Berry et al., 2020). Hence, customers’
touchpoints with the service providers are affected by the chosen service strategy during the
pandemic.
It's worth mentioning that the concepts of service adaptation and service transformation
were introduced in an editorial piece authored by Berry et al. (2020).  Nevertheless, these
concepts resonate with the notions of adaptation and transformation in Resilience Thinking
Theory (Folke et al., 2010) within the field of social ecology (Holling, 1973; Leixnering and
Hollerer, 2022). In this literature stream, resilience is defined as the capacity of a system to
absorb disturbance and reorganize while undergoing change to still retain essentially the same
function, structure, identity, and feedback (Walker et al., 2004). The focus of resilience is on
the dynamics of the system when it is disturbed far from its modal state. Social ecologists
encapsulate adaptation and transformation as different aspects of change under the umbrella
term resilience thinking (Folke et al., 2010) in which adaptation is associated with incremental

 12
adjustment and reorganization, while transformation is associated with the notion of deep, more
radical, and thus potentially disruptive change (Leixnering and Hollerer, 2022).
2.5. Customer Coping Behaviors (Responses) during Crisis Situations
Coping behaviors represent the behavioral consequences (responses) to the customers’
emotional state (i.e., organism / qualities) following the interactions in the service encounter
(stimuli / touchpoints). During a service encounter, which essentially can be seen as a process
during which customers interact with the firm, customers are exposed to multiple stimuli that
elicit emotional responses and lead to various behaviors (Bagozzi et al., 1999; Bitner,
1992; Caruelle et al., 2024; Tombs and McColl–Kennedy, 2003).
In this study, customers’ behavioral responses are examined by means of customer
coping behaviors, which is in line with past research in the context of COVID-19 that found
that experiences (albeit service provider experiences in contrast to customer experiences)
culminated into coping behaviors (Huang et al., 2020). In doing so, we rely on the Theory of
Coping (Lazarus, 1993) that states that individuals can use two mechanisms to deal with events:
emotion-focused coping behaviors and problem-focused coping behaviors. Problem-focused
coping behaviors consist of actions taken by customers in response to their interaction and
experience with a firm, whereas emotion-focused coping behaviors consist of the expression of
certain emotions resulting from an interaction and can be either negative or positive.
In line with the seminal work of Carver et al. (1989), this study explores different types
of both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping behaviors, as different types can be
triggered in different ways following an experience. Indeed, in their study on students' and
employees' professional IT usage, Liang et al. (2019) conceptually classified emotion-focused
coping behaviors into two categories (inward and outward coping) and empirically discovered
that these coping behaviors were triggered in distinct ways. This underscores the importance of
not consolidating different types of coping behaviors into aggregated categories. Building on

 13
this reasoning, our study also categorizes coping behaviors  (both problem-focused and
emotion-focused coping behaviors) into inward and outward categories. Specifically, for the
purpose of our research, outward coping behaviors are defined as coping behaviors that are
directed towards others, whereas inward coping behaviors are defined as coping behaviors
directed towards the self.
By combining problem-focused versus emotion-focused coping behaviors, and outward
versus inward coping behaviors, andmaking use of the seminal work of Carver et al. (1989),
we obtain four categories of coping behaviors (See Figure 1): inward problem-focused coping
behaviors (e.g., active coping and planning), outward problem-focused coping behaviors (e.g.,
seeking social support for instrumental reasons), inward emotion-focused coping behaviors
(e.g., positive reinterpretation), and outward emotion-focused coping behaviors (e.g., venting).
When theorizing the relationship between customer experience and coping behaviors, it
is worth mentioning and detailing on the research by Huang et al. (2020), as these authors
examined the impact of negative emotions on problem-focused and emotion-focused coping
behaviors of nurses and college nursing students during COVID-19. While this paper holds
merit and relevance for our research, these authors, however, focused on service providers
instead of customers as the unit of their analysis. Additionally, their study did not examine the
changing touchpoints in the service encounter in response to a crisis. Moreover, their
investigation only considered negative emotions, overlooking the potential insights offered by
positive emotions. They also did not explore various types of inward and outward coping
behaviors, as suggested by Liang et al. (2019), nor specific problem -focused or emotion-
focused coping behaviors as suggested by Carver et al. (1989) and Xin et al. (2022).
Furthermore, their focus was confined to a single setting, making it difficult to control for
contextual variations tied to firm or industry characteristics.

2.6. Current Knowledge of Customer Responses in the Context of the COVID -19
Pandemic
This paper is designed to better understand customer experiences and coping behaviors in the
context of crisis situations, and in doing so, we consider the COVID-19 pandemic as a research
context. To gain a better understanding of current knowledge and how research contributes to
it, Table 1 and Table 2 summarize the relevant literature to date with regard customer responses
in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. In these tables, we use customer reactions as an
overarching term to designate any type of feeling or behavior (e.g., customer satisfaction,
buying behavior) that is induced within a customer. Specifically, in Table 1, the literature is
summarized in terms of the study goal, the methodology, and its key findings. Table 2 further
details on the empirical studies and indicates the differences with our study in terms of study
type, data collection method, the customer reaction metrics under investigation, and whether
the research accounted for the impact of adaptive versus transformative firm strategies, firm
characteristics, individual characteristics, and third-party influences (e.g., government).
From Table 1, it is clear that many past studies have proposed conceptual frameworks
without empirically confirming them. These conceptual studies discuss different aspects of
customer responses during the COVID-19 pandemic using a variety of theoretical frameworks.
For example, Karpen and Conduit (2020) use three different paradigmatic lenses to discuss
customer engagement, whilst Kirk and Rifkin (2020) use the environmentally-imposed
constraints point of view to describe the customer journey during the pandemic. Moreover,
whereas this body of research consists of valuable groundwork, it further calls for a better
understanding of customers during crisis situations such as the COVID-19 pandemic by means
of empirical studies.
Since the pandemic has not only led to service mega-disruptions (Kabadayi et al., 2020)
but equally disrupted consumers’ desires and needs (Klaus and Manthiou, 2020) and behavior

 15
(Sheth, 2020), the current body of research highlights the need for further research on the
customer experience and customer behavior during the pandemic. In addition, several authors,
such as Berry et al. (2020) and Kim (2020), have discussed how companies could or should
react to the pandemic, but empirical evidence is still lacking on how these strategic actions
affect customers.
Recent studies have indicated the importance of taking a customer perspective and
investigating the impact on consumers’ satisfaction and well -being (Henkel et al., 2020;
Pantano et al., 2020). Some authors have taken an empirical approach on studying customer
responses in the context of COVID-19 (See Table 2). For example, Prasetyo et al. (2021)
investigate the factors affecting customer satisfaction in online food delivery services during
COVID-19 using structural equation modeling. Barnes et al. (2020) investigate how firms can
drive customer delight during the pandemic by applying a critical incident technique to Google
reviews.
Even though existing research has investigated distinct aspects of a customer response
(e.g., customer satisfaction (Brandtner et al., 2021; Prasetyo et al., 2021), customer engagement
(Rather, 2021), purchase behavior (Addo et al., 2020)), there is no current literature on the
impact of the pandemic on the customer experience  and subsequent customer coping
mechanisms in the context of a crisis situation such as COVID-19. This is unfortunate, since
recent research has demonstrated that COVID-19 affected the experience of service providers
(working in the healthcare setting), which was found to further impact both their problem -
focused and emotion-focused coping behaviors in their work context (Huang et al., 2020).
Despite its importance, the impact of the customer experience on customers’ coping
mechanisms, and how these are influenced by the firm’s strategies (here, adaption versus
transformation) is yet unknown (See Table 2). Specifically, there are no studies that investigate
the coping mechanisms that customers adopt to deal with the changes in their interaction with

 16
service providers because of the pandemic. Furthermore, the impact of service transformations
versus service adaptations on customers has not yet been researched, despite conceptual calls
(Berry et al., 2020) and conceptual links to the Resilience Thinking literature (Holling, 1973;
Folke et al., 2010).
As is clear from our literature review, most studies do not take the actions undertaken
by companies into account, whereas other studies have either focused on service adaptations
(e.g., Brandtner et al. (2021), focuses on measures resulting from regulatory actions), or service
transformations (e.g., Prasetyo et al. (2021) study online food delivery services) in isolation. A
notable exception, however, is the study by Barnes et al. (2020) who, via the critical incident
technique, identified various drivers of customer delight during COVID-19, and reported both
adaptive (e.g., safety procedures) and transformative (e.g., online ordering and contact-free
delivery) strategies.  However, the relative importance of both strategies on customer reactions
was not further examined, nor compared in their research. In addition, their sample focused on
drivers of customer delight only, thereby ignoring the impact of adaptive versus transformative
strategies for less satisfactory (e.g., negative,  neutral, or positive in contrast to delight)
customer experiences and service encounters.
Additionally, most of the empirical studies to date have ignored the industry context,
which is surprising given that different types of industries were affected in different ways by
the pandemic (e.g., some industries were forced to close, whereas other industries co uld
continue serving their customers), which in turn guided their strategic actions, and impacted
the customer experience. Finally, these studies also ignored the impact of the government,
which here acts in the role of a third party and links to the outside firm-controlled stimuli
(Becker and Jaakkola, 2020).

3. Methodology
Our research design regarding how to measure the customer experience builds upon past
research on social media listening. Social media listening or monitoring is defined as an
approach for companies to understand consumer needs and demands, and to a larger extent the
overall trends in the interest group (Zachlod et al., 2022). Social media listening has proven to
yield important insights for companies regarding brand sentiment (Schweidel and Moe, 2014)
and idea generation in innovation (Muninger et al., 2019).
To perform social media listening, several analytical decisions have to be made such as
(1) acquisition and tracking of the data and (2) preparation and analysis of the data (Agnihotri
et al., 2023). The first step determines the source of the data, the collection design, and method
(Boegershausen et al., 2022). In our case, user-generated content from Twitter has proven to be
one of the major sources of information during a crisis (Janssens et al., 202 4). Moreover,
Twitter had a convenient and user-friendly API from which data could easily be scraped in high
volumes with specific hashtags (Boegershausen et al., 2022). The second step decides on the
different analysis techniques to get insights from the data. Since our study uses textual data to
get insight into the customer experience, we choose a mixed methods set-up in which text
mining and natural language processing techniques are used to extract customer experience
tweets, and content analysis is used to gain a deeper understanding in the coping strategies. For
an elaborate overview of social media listening and best practices in gaining insight from social
media data, we refer the reader to respectively Zachlod et al. (2022) and Boegershausen et al.
(2022).
3.1. Data Collection
For this research, we make use of real-time COVID-19 Twitter data. Tweets related to the
COVID-19 crisis were collected on a daily basis between the 28th of January 2020 and the 2nd
of November 2022 via the Twitter API, using the following hashtags: #CoronaOutbreak,

 23
#Coronavirus, #COVID19, #COVID-19, #COVID2019, #SARSCoV2. We made use of a
specific server environment, optimized to collect a maximum number of tweets per day to make
sure the collected tweets are as representative as possible. The collected data contains the text
of a user’s tweet (i.e., textual information about a COVID-19 event and the customer’s feedback
on it), the date the tweet was posted, as well as additional metadata (i .e., tweet and user
information).
 Considering the potential differences between countries in terms of the impact and the
course of the COVID-19 crisis (e.g., due to distinct governmental regulations), and the
importance of correctly interpreting and labeling the tweets in the next steps of this study, we
decide to focus on Belgian tweets only. As the geolocation in the metadata of a tweet is missing
for more than 98% of the Twitter data, we use the location specified in the user information to
extract the set of tweets posted in Belgium. This data collection results in 51,827 Belgian
tweets, of which 47% are in English, 31% in French, and the remaining 22% are in Dutch. Our
dataset did not contain any German tweets.
3.2. Sample Selection
The data collection, discussed above, resulted in a vast number of tweets related to the COVID-
19 pandemic. These tweets discuss a variety of aspects of the crisis, varying from daily updates
on the number of COVID-19 cases to individuals expressing their fears and frustrations. In this
study, we are specifically interested in tweets addressing customer experiences and subsequent
coping behaviors in service encounters. Hence, the goal is to extract tweets describing customer
experiences from the mass of tweets, which cannot be done manually. Therefore, we use a semi-
supervised natural language processing set-up, which is commonly adopted in marketing
science (Lee et al., 2018).
To classify the tweets on COVID-19 into tweets discussing customer experiences and
other tweets, we use a multilingual BERT classification model. BERT (Bidirectional Encoder

 24
Representations from Transformers) is composed of several transformer encoders (Devlin et al.
2018). The main innovation of BERT is attributed to its bidirectional nature and the multi-head
attention mechanism. In contrast to traditional deep learning models, which read the text input
sequentially (i.e., either from left to right or from right to left), BERT is able to read the input
in both directions at once. This bidirectional characteristic allows the model to learn all the
surroundings of the word (left and right) to better understand the context and, hence, generate
better results (Devlin et al., 2018).
The multi-head attention mechanism enables BERT to focus on different characteristics
of a word and this allows BERT to capture the different meanings of a word depending on the
context (Devlin et al., 2018). Hence, in contrast to traditional language models, BERT comes
up with dynamic word representations depending on the context. For example, the word
‘content’ could mean ‘happy’ or ‘composition’ depending on the context, which can be easily
detected by a BERT model. Both Alantari et al. (2022) and Pamuksuz et al. (2021) identify
BERT as one of the best performing machine learning methods for analyzing (social media)
text in a marketing context.
Specifically, in this study, we employ a bert-base-multilingual-cased model which can
be retrieved via the Hugging Face library ( https://huggingface.co/google-bert/bert-base-
multilingual-cased) and use the simpletransformers library
(https://github.com/ThilinaRajapakse/simpletransformers) to finetune a binary text
classification model.   We train and test our BERT model on a subsample of 4,000 tweets,
which have been labeled as discussing a customer experience (i.e., 1) or not (i.e., 0). From these
4000 tweets only 51 tweets are identified as a customer experience, this equals 1.28% of the
tweets. Before feeding the tweets to our BERT model, we apply several preprocessing steps:
the text is set to lower cases, trailing white spaces are removed, sequence padding is applied to
very short texts, and the text is tokenized using the specific bert-base-multilingual-cased

 25
tokenizer. No other preprocessing steps are applied (e.g., stemming or stop word removal) to
make sure that the full context of the sentence was captured. For our pre-trained BERT model,
we use the bert-base-multilingual-uncased model which is trained on 102 languages using
Wikipedia data.
To fit this BERT model to our sample, we fine-tune all layers of the pre-trained model
with the learning rate set to 0.0004, the batch size to 8 and the number of epochs to 1. Note that
to fine-tune all layers the authors recommend 2 to 4 epochs (Devlin et al. 2018), however, on
small samples 1 epoch is often enough since additional complexity (i.e., more than 2 epoch)
would lead to overfitting (Komatsuzaki, 2019).  Since we are working in a high class-imbalance
setting, we choose the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUC) to evaluate the
general performance of our model, since the AUC gives an equal weight to both classes. The
AUC can be interpreted as the probability that a random positive observation (i.e., a tweet
describing a customer experience) gets a higher score than a random negative observation (i.e.,
a tweet not describing a customer experience). An AUC of 0.5 indicates that the classification
model does not perform any better than a random model, whereas values closer to 1 indicate a
better classification performance (Schetgen et al., 2021).
With an AUC of 0.8925 in our sample, we conclude that our BERT model is extremely
suited for the classification task at hand. Specifically, this means that the probability that a
random tweet describing a customer experience gets a higher score than a random tweet that
does not include a customer experience is equal to 0.8925. In other words, the added value of
our classification model over a random model is 0.3925.
Since we have a low number of positive cases, it is important to take the high imbalance
setting into account and therefore our model focuses on the learning of identifying the relevant
positive cases (i.e., precision) (Zinovyeva, Härdle and Lessmann, 2020). In this study, this
implies that the tweets that are classified as a customer experience by our model, are indeed a

 26
true customer experience. Therefore, we set the classification threshold as such that the
precision of our classification model is optimized. This led to a threshold of 0.9918 for our
classification model, with a precision of 0.9091. This means that 91% of the tweets that are
classified as a customer experience by our model, are indeed a customer experience.
Considering the trade-off that exists between the precision and recall, and the fact that we
choose to set the threshold to optimize the precision of our classification model, this caused a
decrease in recall. For the same threshold, the recall equals 0.3922.

We also experiment with lowering the classification threshold to 0.98 which results in
a drop in precision to 0.7957. In other words, 79.57% of the tweets that are classified as a
customer experience effectively contain a customer experience. This clearly shows that
lowering the threshold would lead to more false positives (i.e., more non-customer experience
tweets included in the sample) which is not desireable for the human coding part.
Finally, the accuracy of our BERT model, which indicates the percentage of correctly
classified tweets, equals 0.9818. By applying the trained BERT model on the full dataset of
51,827 Belgian tweets, we are able to extract all the tweets describing a customer experience.
This results in a final sample of 279 Belgian tweets discussing a customer experience, which
will be used in the next steps of this study.
Note that this sample size is considerably smaller than related studies in Table 2 which
can be attributed to the following differences. First, most studies in Table 3 use an online
platform that is designed to gather customer feedback on their product. For example, Robertson
et al. (2021) use online consumer reviews for Zoom on Google Play to understand the customer
experience. Since online consumer reviewers contain multidimensional information about the
customer experience (Wang, Du, and Wang, 2023), almost all observations can be considered
as customer experience messages. Second, other studies (Meena and Kumar, 2022) limit their
analysis to one specific industry and companies that were heavily impacted by the pandemic.

 27
Finally, most studies do not focus on the customer experience itself but related metrics like
customer engagement (Kleer and Kunz, 2023), customer satisfaction (Song et al., 2022) or
booking behavior (Filieri et al., 2023)  that are more readily available. The goal of our research
is broader and aims to investigate customer experience across multiple industries and
products/services within the wider context of the COVID-19 pandemic.
3.3. Operationalization and Description of Variables
In what follows, we discuss the operationalization of the different variables included in our
proposed conceptual framework (See Figure 1).
Customer Experiences (Affective Responses). Drawing inspiration from social media research,
we focus on affective responses, and extract both positive and negative emotions from the
textual data (e.g., tweets) to measure the customer experience (e.g., Sailunaz and Alhajj, 2019;
Zaki and Neely, 2019).
Following the methodological approach by Barnes et al. (2020), we manually coded the
presence versus absence of each the eight basic emotions in every customer tweet, thereby
relying on the Psychoevolutionary Theory of Emotion (PTE)  and the eight basic emotions as
identified by Plutchik (1962, 1980). Specifically, we coded the presence versus absence of four
positive-valenced emotions (i.e., joy, surprise, acceptance, and anticipation) and four negative-
valenced emotions (i.e., fear, anger, sadness, and disgust). Note that customer emotions can be
extracted via both machine-coding and human-coding, but a hybrid machine -human
intelligence approach has been proven to be the preferred methodological approach (Filieri et
al., 2022).
Specifically, based on empirical evidence, Filieri et al. (2022) conclude that the
efficiency of machine-learning algorithms (like using BERT) in combination with the human
coding of emotions is preferred, which is also the methodological approach taken in this paper.

 28
Specifically, these authors show (i) the poor interpretative capacity of machine intelligence, in
contrast to human coding which is needed to code the exact meaning of the emotion/feeling or
emotional states in the customer review, and (ii) conclude that although machine learning
techniques have become more advanced, they cannot yet match the human capacity to interpret
consumer emotional experiences (Filieri et al., 2022).
In this regard, it is also relevant to note that in our study, one customer review can
contain references to both the focal firm and the government, and these respective references
can be opposite in the expressed emotions (e.g., positive customer experience towards the firm,
but blaming the government); such that a manual coding and interpretation is needed to
disentangle this2. As noted by Becker and Jaakkola (2020), not being able to disentangle and
account for within and outside firm-controlled stimuli is problematic for understanding the
customer experience, and both stimuli can be present in the same tweet. Therefore, this study
explicitly accounts for the third-party effect in our modeling (See Figure 1, and Equation 1
below in the Methodology Section).
Furthermore, consistent with the approach outlined by Filieri et al. (2022), we conducted
an ad-hoc analysis involving the machine coding of each tweet to also extract the eight basic
emotions by employing the NRC emotional affect lexicon (Mohammad and Turney, 2013), and
subsequently compared the results with our manually coded variables.  The NRC Word-
Emotion Association Lexicon, in short EmoLex, extract the eight basic emotions (anger, fear,
anticipation, trust, surprise, sadness, joy, and disgust) based on a list of 14,182 associated words
in English (Mohammad and Turney, 2013).
EmoLex is often employed on social media data since it has the best performance in
emotion recognition on short texts like tweets and is easy to reproduce (Mingione et al., 2020;

 2 We thank the anonymous reviewer for drawing our attention to this point.

 29
Mohammad et al., 2015). Other methods like VADER (Hutto and Gilbert, 2014)  are not
possible in this case since it only outputs a valence score between -1 and 1 and can be discretiezd
to negative, neutral, and positive sentiment.  Our  comparison unveiled that a (marginally)
significant, but weak correlation existed between the human -coded and machine-coded
(EmoLex) variables solely for the emotions of joy, anticipation, fear, and disgust. Conversely,
no such significant correlations were observed for the remaining emotions of surprise,
acceptance, anger, and sadness. This finding underscores the irreplaceable nature of human-
coded emotion-coding over machine-based approaches, thus highlighting the preference for
human-coding in capturing the different emotions (i.e.,  the affective responses of the customer
experience construct).
To maximize validity, the two coders independently reviewed and manually coded the
data (Barnes et al., 2020). Next, results were combined, and any coding inconsistencies were
discussed and solved through discussion with the complete research team (Cole, 2023). To
assess reliability, both the inter-rater agreement (IRA) and inter-rater reliability (IRR) are
calculated (LeBreton and Senter, 2008).
Inter-rater agreement (IRA) refers to the absolute consensus in scores by coders (Bliese,
2000) and ranges from 0 to 1 (i.e., 0% to 100% of agreement in agreeing on how to classify a
target). Guidelines for interpreting IRA classify an IRA of 0 to 0.30 as a lack of agreement,
whereas an IRA of 0.31 to 0.50, an IRA of 0.51 to 0.70, an IRA of 0.71 to 0.90, and an IRA of
0.91 to 1 are respectively classified as a weak, moderate, strong, and very strong inter-rater
agreement (LeBreton and Senter, 2008); in which values of 0.70 have been used as the
traditional cut-off point denoting high versus low IRA (Brown and Hauenstein, 2005; Lance et
al., 2006; LeBreton et al., 2003).
Inter-rater reliability (IRR) refers to the relative consistency in rating provided by coders
(Bliese, 2000), and is captured via Cohen’s kappa (Cohen, 1960), that, similar to a correlation

 30
coefficient, ranges from -1 to 1. Guidelines for interpreting IRR classify Cohen’s kappa lower
than 0 as a poor inter-rater reliability, whereas a kappa value of 0 to .20, a kappa value of 0.21
to 0.40, a kappa value of 0.41 to 0.60, a kappa value of 0.61 to 0.80, and a kappa value of 0.81
to 1 are respectively classified as a slight, fair, moderate, substantial and almost perfect levels
of inter-rater reliability (Landis and Koch, 1977); in which values of 0.40 have been used as the
traditional cut-off point denoting high versus low IRR (Cicchetti and Sparrow, 1981; Fleiss,
1981; Landis and Koch, 1977; Regier et al., 2013).

Role of the Firm (Service Strategies) Customer interactions with providers of services and
goods are affected by the strategy that these providers have adopted in response to the COVID-
19 pandemic. According to Berry et al. (2020), companies can either implement service
adaptations or service transformations. Service adaptations are often the result of governmental
regulations and are merely aimed at improving safety (e.g., face masks, mandatory disinfection)

stay after the COVID-19 pandemic (such as keeping the plexiglasses, keeping detergent for
disinfection available to consumers, etc.). On the other hand, service transformations offer
additional benefits as well and could, therefore, continue to exist when the crisis is over (e.g.,
online shopping, online classes) (Berry et al., 2020).

absence of service adaptations and/or service transformations, thereby relying on the definitions

described by customers in their tweets. It may be that a company has taken certain actions but
that the customer did not talk about it online or simply did not notice it.

3.4. Empirical Approach for Testing the Conceptual Framework
To simultaneously examine all relationships of our conceptual framework (See Figure 1), we
use a mediation approach (Zhao et al., 2010) with Bayesian estimation (Yuan and MacKinnon,
2009) in line with previous research (e.g., Larivière et al., 2016). We estimate the Bayesian

 39
models using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques. As Yuan and MacKinnon
(2009) note, compared with conventional frequentist mediation analysis, the Bayesian approach
does not impose restrictive normality assumptions on sampling distributions of estimates,
making statistical inference straightforward and exact. In addition, Bayesian approaches are
preferred (and perform well) over conventional modeling approaching in case of smaller sample
sizes (which is also the case in this paper) due to the MCMC iteration process and the many
convergence metrics that need to be assessed before the posterior means of the final modeling
can be interpreted (Yuan and MacKinnon, 2009).
Following Gelman and Rubin (1992), we run three independent MCMC chains with
different starting points and 10,000 iterations each, by which the first half is considered as the
“burn-in” phase and the remaining half is used to estimate the posterior distribution for the
parameters. To assess the convergence of the MCMC algorithm, we inspect the Gelman-Rubin
convergence statistic R, autocorrelation plots, and trace plots of the residual variance for the
parameter estimates. Specifically, given the last 15,000  iterations (used to estimate the
parameters), the largest value of the Gelman-Rubin convergence statistic R ranges between
1.001 and 1.008 (note that Yuan and MacKinnon (2009)  have suggested that a value of R close
to 1 [the highest cut-off being 1.2] is an indication of reasonable convergence). Hence, this
investigation provides evidence of the MCMC algorithm’s convergence.
As suggested by Iacobucci (2008) and Yuan and MacKinnon (2009), the following three
equations are jointly estimated in order to test our proposed conceptual model:
(1) CXdi = b0d +b1dServiceAdaptationi +b2dServiceTransformationi +b3gdThirdPartygi
+b4cdControlci + e1di
(2) ProblemCopingpi = b4p+b5pd CXdi +b6pServiceAdaptationi
+b7pServiceTransformationi+ e2pi

(3) EmotionCopingei = b8e+b9ed CXdi +b10eServiceAdaptationi

+b7nServiceTransformationi+ e2ni
in which ServiceAdaptationi and ServiceTransformationi denote the reported firm strategies that
each customer i (with i = 1 to 279) experienced while interacting with the service provider

4. Findings
Table 6 and Table 7 respectively represent the findings of our benchmark model (i.e., Model 1)
and main model (i.e., Model 2). Our findings (See Panel A of Table 6, and Panel A of Table 7)
reveal that both service adaptations and service transformations exert a favorable effect on the
customer experience as both firm strategies are likely to simultaneously enhance the positive-
valenced emotions and lower the negative-valenced emotions. Interestingly, some notable
differences are observed. First, different types of positive emotions are evoked by both firm
strategies. Specifically, service adaptations lead to more anticipation, whereas service
transformations translate into more joy and more surprise. Second, both firm strategies lower
the negative-valenced emotions of disgust, but this favorable effect is found to be more
pronounced for service transformations compared to service adaptations (i.e., significant at
0.01; Clogg et al., 1995).
In addition, we observe significant third-party effects. Specifically, when customers also
referred to the government in a negative way, more anger was experienced. In contrast, when
customers referred to the government in a neutral or positive way, they reported and
experienced more joy. Finally, in line with the customer experience literature, we find evidence
for the significant influence of contextual factors. Specifically, bricks (in contrast to online
channels) were characterized by less sadness during the COVID-19 pandemic, whereas

 42
customers with more followers on their social media account (here, Twitter) reported and
experienced less anger.
Our research also unveils the influence of customer experience on subsequent coping
behaviors. Specifically, Panel B of Table 6 differentiates between aggregated problem-focused
and emotion-focused coping behaviors. Our results indicate that joy and surprise reduce
problem-focused coping behaviors, whereas anticipation enhances them. In addition, when the
negative emotions of anger, sadness and disgust are experienced, customers are also less likely
to engage in problem-focused coping behaviors. In contrast, the impact of the custo mer
experience via both positive-valenced and negative-valenced emotions on the emotion-coping
behaviors is found to be different, since the experience of joy, surprise, anticipation, fear, anger,
sadness, and disgust all enhance the emotion-coping behaviors. In other words, when more
emotions (both positive and negative) are experienced, customers feel less the need, and hence
less engage in problem-coping behaviors, while the opposite holds for their subsequent
engagement in emotion-coping behaviors.

When considering specific problem-focused and emotion-focused coping behaviors

First, with regard to the problem-focused coping behaviors, we observe that the positive

disgust exert a stronger negative influence on customers’ likelihood to seek social support for
instrumental reasons. In other words, for the problem-focused coping behaviors, our findings
indicate that, overall, the positive emotions (here, especially anticipation and acceptance) are
more likely to stimulate active coping and planning, whereas these same positive emotions, in
contrast, lower customers’ likelihood of seeking social support for instrumental reasons. In
addition, the impact of negative emotions on customers’ likelihood of seeking social support
for instrumental reasons is also more pronounced.
Second, with regard to the emotion-focused coping behaviors, our findings reveal that
the positive emotion of joy, surprise, and anticipation translates into more positive
reinterpretations, whereas, in contrast, the negative emotion of anger, sadness, and disgust
translates into more venting behavior. In line with findings observed for the specific problem-
focused coping behaviors, our results indicate that inward coping behaviors directed to the self
(here, active coping, planning, and positive reinterpretation) are more likely to be induced by
the positive emotions of joy, surprise, acceptance and anticipation, whereas the outward coping
behaviors directed to others (here, seeking social support for instrumental reasons and venting)
are more likely to be induced by the negative emotions of anger, sadness and disgust. With
regard to the latter finding, these negative emotions lower the seeking of social support for
instrumental reasons, whereas they stimulate venting.

on both emotion-focused coping behaviors (i.e., positive reinterpretation and venting), while
they reduce the seeking of social support for instrumental reasons. In contrast, the impact of
service adaptions on coping behaviors, was only found to be evoked via the mediation of the
positive-valenced and negative-valenced emotions, with the exception of seeking of social
support for instrumental reasons, for which also a direct and significant effect of service
adaptions was found.
5. Discussion
5.1. Theoretical Contributions and Implications
In this study, we rely on the four fundamental premises (P1 to P4) proposed by Becker and
Jaakkola (2020). Together, these premises cover the “big picture” of what customer experience
is (P1), what affects it (P2), what its key contingencies are (P3), and what role the firm can play
in it (P4) (Becker and Jaakkola, 2020, p 637). In addressing these four premises, our study
draws upon the TCQ framework (De Keyser et al. 2020) and the S-O-R Theory (Mehrabian
and Russell, 1974) to examine the interrelationships among the variables within our proposed
conceptual framework (See Figure 1). Guided by these premises/questions, this study deepens
our understanding of customer experiences and subsequent coping behaviors during crisis
situations such as the COVID-10 pandemic. Specifically, through both our qualitative and
quantitative analyses, we gain valuable insights into these four premises by:
(1) better understanding the customer experience (here, affective responses) during crisis
situations, encompassing different positive-valenced and negative-valence emotions,
(2) evaluating the differential impact of stimuli from third-party entities (here, government)
compared to those of the focal firm,
(3) investigating the influence of contextual factors such as individual, and firm/industry
characteristics, and

 49
(4) assessing the impact of various firm strategies, particularly those related to service
adaptations and service transformations on shaping the customer experience and eliciting
different types of problem-focused and emotion-focused coping behaviors.
As a result, this study makes several important contributions to the literature.
First, our study demonstrates the importance of considering different types of positive-
valenced and negative-valenced emotions. According to Lemon and Verhoef (2016, p. 86),
“extreme crisis can have a strong, negative and enduring effect on the customer experience”. In
our study, however, we rely on the basic emotions adopted from the Psychoevolutionary Theory
of Emotion (PTE) introduced by Plutchik (1962, 1980) and discover that during crises – which
may initially seem more closely linked to negative experiences and, therefore, prompting a
reliance on metrics for measuring these negative experiences – it is nonetheless crucial to also
capture positive emotions. This is because positive experiences can still be encountered even
in challenging circumstances. In our research, we find that positive emotions of joy, surprise
and anticipation are experienced and evoked by firm strategies such as service adaptations and
service transformations. Hence, our research contributes to past crisis research that merely
focused on negative experiences (e.g., Huang et al., 2020) or positive experiences (e.g., Barnes
et al., 2020) in isolation, thereby ignoring a more holistic understanding of customers’ emotions
in line with the Psychoevolutionary Theory of Emotion (Plutchik,1962, 1980).
Second, our study also echoes prior calls to better capture different types of customer
coping behaviors (Liang et al., 2019). In doing so, we relied on the seminal work of Carver et
al. (1989) that proposed different types of both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping
behaviors. Interestingly, our findings reveal different types of coping behaviors which we could
classify as being directed towards the self (i.e., inward coping behaviors) versus directed
towards others (i.e., outward coping behaviors), thereby following the terminology as suggested
by Liang et al. (2019). This classification helps us to better interpret our findings as we can

 50
derive from our data that the inward coping behaviors directed to the self (here, active coping,
planning, and positive reinterpretation) are more likely to be induced by the positive emotions
of joy, surprise, acceptance and anticipation, whereas the outward coping behaviors directed to
others (here, seeking social support for instrumental reasons and venting) are more likely to be
induced by the negative emotions of fear, anger, sadness and disgust. Hence, our research
contributes to the current understanding of how an individual’s experience influences problem-
focused and emotional-focused coping behaviors, thereby considering different inward and
outward coping behaviors as suggested by past literature (Carver et al., 1989; Liang et al. ,
2019).
Third, this study responds to the call by Becker and Jaakkola (2020) to include outside
firm-controlled touchpoints, and to examine how these affect the customer experience. In doing
so, this study examines the role of the government as third-party influence. We observe that not
only negative touchpoints with the government result in experiencing more anger, but also that
positive touchpoints with third parties (here, government) exist during crisis situations, which
significantly elevate the experience of joy. Hence, our research contributes to the customer
experience literature by deepening our understanding of how customer experiences reside
within and outside firm-controlled touchpoints (Becker and Jaakkola, 2020).
 Fourth, considering firm strategies during a crisis, this study demonstrates that
customers do discuss distinct service adaptation and service transformation strategies they
experienced in their social media channels (here, Twitter). Our research further shows that both
firm strategies (i.e., in contrast to no such strategies) exert a favorable impact on the customer
experience, but in a different way and to a different extent. Specifically, positive emotions of
joy and surprise are evoked by service trans formations, whereas positive emotions of
anticipation are evoked by service adaptations. In the context of negative experiences, both
service adaptations and service transformations contribute to a reduction in the experience of

 51
disgust. Notably, the impact is nearly twice as pronounced for service transformations when
compared to service adaptations.
In sum, it is crucial to discriminate between both firm strategies, as their underlying
theoretical mechanisms (here, affective responses as measured by the different emotions
captured from textual data) as organism (i.e., mediator in the modeling) to better understand
the customer response (here, customers’ coping behaviors) substantially differ. In doing so, our
study offers the first empirical investigation of comparing both service adaptation and service
transformation strategies during a crisis such as COVID-19 and its influence on the customer
experience and customers’ behavioral responses, as called for by Berry et al. (2020).

5.2. Implications for Managers and Public Policy Makers
Gaining insights into how customers cope with and experience changes in the service encounter
during a crisis, can be extremely valuable for both managers and public policy makers, as it
enables them to react in the most optimal way. Based on the findings of this research, we can
formulate different valuable take-aways for managers and public policy makers.
First, this study shows the value of making use of social media platforms, such as
Twitter, to better understand how organizations can respond to crisis situations. For managers,
using social media data can enable them to uncover whether customers notice their actions that
were implemented in response to the crisis, and how customers experienced these efforts. In
addition, managers can source ideas from competitors and even organizations operating in other
industries. In our research, a variety of service transformations were uncovered beyond the
introduction of online channels, and these include pick up services, remote services, moving
from offline to online payment in offline service delivery, better documentation about the
service, etc. These service transformations that can be adopted by many organizations, and
maintained in post-crisis times, as these actions foster the customer experience.

 52
Second, with regards to policy makers, the goal of relying on social media data can be
twofold. On the one hand, they may use it to track whether companies follow the regulations
imposed by the government. On the other hand, they can use it to better understand for instance
the value of digital solutions linked to service transformations, and subsequently adopt these
solutions within their own governmental entities. Additionally, they can further promote (e.g.,
informing, educating and subsidizing) these solutions to businesses and non-profit
organizations in case they have not yet adopted them.
Third, this research shows that service adaptations and service transformations,
implemented by companies as a response to a crisis, can also have a favorable impact on the
customer, as exemplified by the experience of positive sentiments such as joy and surprise and
positive coping behaviors such as positive reinterpretation in our data. These findings should
motivate companies to further implement adaptations and transformations. Specifically, we find
service transformations to have the biggest impact on customers, which further highlights the
value of sourcing and seizing service transformation opportunities that can be implemented by
organizations.
Fourth, while this study delves into a particular crisis situation, we believe that many of
its insights may extend to other crisis types. Specifically, when customers experience changes
in the service encounter due to a crisis, it is likely that their experiences (e.g., affective
responses) may not be confined solely to negative-valenced experiences only, but also include
positive experiences (P1: Becker and Jaakkola, 2020). In addition, it is crucial that third-party
influences are accounted for as they can induce both negative and positive experiences and
further elicit customer behaviors (P2: Becker and Jaakkola, 2020). Finally, companies can
shape the customer experience through adaptive and/or transformative responses (in line with
adaptive and transformative resilience thinking), in which the latter firm strategy is expected to
exert a bigger influence (P4: Becker and Jaakkola, 2020).

 53

5.3. Limitations and Future Research Directions
This study contributes to a better understanding of customer experiences and coping behaviors
during crisis situations and the role of a firms’ service adaptation versus service transformation.
Nevertheless, we note some limitations and avenues for future research.
 First, this study made use of publicly available social media data and a multi-method
approach for analyzing this data. In future research, other types of data could be examined and
gathered via different data collection methods ranging from conventional methods such as
surveys and in-depth interviews to more advanced methods such as chatbot interviews (Sidaoui
et al., 2020). This could enable researchers to gain further insights into other, potentially
relevant, concepts and variables, such as delving in other customer experience dimensions than
affective responses (Lemon and Verhoef, 2016), like customer demographics, customer traits
(e.g., resistance to change), and past customer behavior.
 Second, this study collects data based on hashtags about COVID-19 to ensure that the
tweets, and hence the customer experiences that we wanted to investigate, are related to the
COVID crisis, as the aim of our research is to examine how customer experiences are affected
in a crisis context. However, this restriction to hashtags could also explain the low sample size
of our final selected tweets (n=279) since not all users who talk about a specific customer
experience use COVID-related hashtags. It could perfectly be that users only mention the
company itself (i.e., @CompanyX) or talk about the experience without any hashtag or
mention.
Future research could therefore opt for a mixed data collection strategy in which they
search for hasthtags, general keywords and mentions to specific companies. Whereas  this could
look like better strategy at first, this process is most effective when working with one specific
industry which automatically limits the number of companies. For example, Meena and Kumar
(2023) studied the customer experience for online food services in India and thereby focused

 54
on only four companies. Since these companies were heavily impact by the COVID-19
pandemic, a lot of tweets were related to the customer experience. In our case, the aim was to
study the impact on customer experience across multiple industries and in that case, it would
be challenging to define a fixed list of industries and companies upfront of the data collection
thereby limiting the scope.
 Third, whereas this study investigates customer experiences and coping behavior, and
the role of the firm in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, it could be interesting to explore
these relationships for other crisis situations. It could be worthwhile to determine whether the
same effects can be found during crises or disasters that are inherently different from the
COVID-19 crisis. To answer this question, we believe that more research on customer
responses in times of crisis or disasters is necessary, and it could, therefore, be an interesting
avenue for future research.
