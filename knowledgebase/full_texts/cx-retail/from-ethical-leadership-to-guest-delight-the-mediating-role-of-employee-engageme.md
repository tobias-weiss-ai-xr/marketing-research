Abstract
The hospitality industry increasingly recognises that memorable
customer experiences are shaped not only by service delivery but
also by the leadership practices and engagement of employees
who interact with guests. This study investigates the relationships
among ethical leadership, employee engagement, and customer
experience in the hospitality sector, with particular focus on the
mediating role of employee engagement. A quantitative, cross-
sectional research design was employed, and data were collected
through a structured questionnaire from respondents associated
with hospitality organisations. The data were analysed using
Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM)
through SmartPLS. The measurement model was evaluated for
reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity, while
the structural model was assessed through path analysis and
bootstrapping. The findings indicate that ethical leadership
significantly enhances employee engagement, while employee
engagement significantly contributes to customer experience.
Ethical leadership also demonstrated a significant direct
relationship with customer experience. Furthermore, employee
engagement significantly mediated the relationship between
ethical leadership and customer experience, indicating partial
mediation. The findings highlight the interconnected roles of
leadership practices, employee engagement, and customer
experience in hospitality organisations. The study contributes to
hospitality and leadership literature by linking an
organisational-level leadership factor with an employee-level
mechanism and a customer-level outcome. Practically, the
findings emphasise the importance of ethical leadership,
employee engagement, and supportive employee-management
practices in creating positive and memorable customer
experiences.
Keywords: Ethical Leadership; Employee Engagement;
Customer Experience; Hospitality; Guest Delight; SmartPLS.

Ethical leadership is the expression of normatively appropriate behaviour in personal actions and interpersonal
relationships, as well as the communication, reinforcement, and decision-making of such behaviour (Brown
et al., 2005). Ethical leaders are typically described as fair, honest, and transparent, accountable, and
concerned with employees. These are especially important in the hospitality industry where staff members are
expected to deal with customers' needs, service issues, emotional situations, and situations that demand
discretion and problem-solving. Clear ethical guidelines and a fair approach by leaders can help to foster a
culture where staff know what is expected of them and feel supported and trusted.
Ethical leadership is particularly relevant as it is frontline hospitality staff who are directly at the
organisation/customer interface. In the hospitality industry, it has been found that ethical leadership has an
impact on employee and service outcomes. Schwepker and Dimitriou (2021) showed that ethical leadership
could be linked to the quality of an employee's performance when the employee is in direct contact with the
customer, and Kim and Brymer (2011) showed that ethical leadership could be linked to an employee's attitude

behaviours that are directly related to service delivery. Employee engagement is one of the key ways in which

employee engagement is a positive, fulfilling psychological state at work that is marked by vigour, dedication
and absorption. When employees are engaged, they put more energy into their jobs, both physically,
cognitively and emotionally. This is especially relevant in the hospitality industry where staff are required to
deliver ongoing, individualised and responsive service. Social Exchange Theory (SET) offers a theoretical
framework to understand the link between ethical leadership and employee engagement. Blau (2017)
suggested that people react to positive treatment reciprocally. If hospitality workers feel their leaders are fair,
trustworthy, supportive and ethical, they might respond with increased commitment and involvement in their
work. Another explanation is given by Social Learning Theory. Bandura and Walters (1977) suggested that
people imitate behaviours that they observe in significant role models. Ethical leaders who are honest, fair,
responsible and respectful can then serve as role models for employees, in keeping with the ethical leadership
outlined by Brown et al. (2005).
This relationship is supported by research that is specific to the hospital. Earlier studies have revealed
a positive relationship between ethical leadership and work engagement and other employee outcomes in the
hospitality industry (Aldulaimi et al., 2025). Recent studies have also found positive links between ethical
leadership and employee engagement, with employee-related factors like job satisfaction playing a role in this
link (Topal et al., 2026). The results of this study confirm that ethical leadership is a key organisational pillar
of employee engagement. Employee engagement also has a significant impact on customer experience.

experience. The service produced and consumed is often at the same time, which means that the behaviour,
communication, responsiveness and willingness of the employees are part of the customers' perception of the
service. Employees who are engaged are more likely to exhibit positive behaviours such as energy, dedication,

2. Literature Review
2.1 Ethical Leadership in Hospitality
Ethical leadership is a significant field of organisational research because leadership behaviour
influences the perception of organisational values, interpersonal relations and acceptable behaviour in the
workplace. Brown et al. (2005) defined ethical leadership as the expression of ethical conduct in personal
conduct and interpersonal relationships and the communication, reinforcement, and decision-making that
encourages ethical conduct among followers. Ethical leaders are usually viewed as being fair, honest, having
integrity, trustworthy, accountable and caring for employees. Brown and Treviño (2006) also noted that
ethical leadership is a unique leadership construct that can impact employee attitudes and behaviours through
social learning and ethical role modelling. Ethical leadership is especially important in the hospitality industry
as staff members often have to deal directly with customers and make decisions in a short time frame, under
emotional pressure, in response to complaints and with uncertainty about service.
Ethical leaders who set a good example and are fair can give employees guidance on how to behave
and psychological support. Hoang et al. (2023) conducted a systematic review of the literature on ethical
leadership in tourism and hospitality and identified three key theoretical frameworks: social exchange theory,
social learning theory, and conservation of resources theory, which have been used to examine the role of
ethical leadership in the tourism and hospitality industry. The empirical studies have confirmed the
significance of ethical leadership in hospitality. Kim and Brymer (2011) reported that in the lodging industry,
ethical leadership was related to employee outcomes like job satisfaction and organisational commitment.
Additionally, Yang et al. (2023) concluded that ethical leadership had positive effects on team service
performance and individual service performance, with the mediating role of ethical climate and ethical self-
efficacy, respectively, among hotel employees. The results of this study show that ethical leadership can create
an environment conducive to positive employee attitudes and effective service delivery.

quality of service provided, with service quality being closely tied to the psychological investment employees
make in their work. According to Schaufeli et al. (2002), work engagement is a positive and fulfilling
psychological state, which is defined by three components: vigour, dedication and absorption. Engaged

2.3 Employee Engagement and Customer Experience
Customer experience is now an important concept in the hospitality industry because customers judge
the organisation based on functional, emotional, cognitive, sensory and social interactions. Lemon and
Verhoef (2016) defined customer experience as a multi-dimensional phenomenon that occurs over time in the
customer journey, and not just at one point. In the hospitality industry, this could be from booking a room to
arriving, check-in, room, meal, employee interaction, problem solving, and leaving. Employees are thus a part
of the customer experience. Employees' service behaviours can impact customer experience, which can be
affected by employee engagement. Employees who are engaged tend to be more energetic, attentive,
enthusiastic and persistent in their work. These attributes can impact customers' service experiences. This
relationship is especially significant in the hospitality industry, where the service production and consumption
process often take place at the same time, resulting in the employee's behaviour becoming part of the service
experience.
Kim and Brymer (2011) showed that employee outcomes related to leadership were related to
behavioural outcomes and organisational performance in lodging organisations. Research on hospitality has
also shown that employee engagement and service orientation can help to enhance customer service. Other
evidence comes from a study of 30,783 TripAdvisor reviews of 100 hotels in 2026, which revealed that
employee attributes such as emotional resonance, functional mastery, proactivity in solving problems,
personal connection and flexibility were significant factors in creating memorable hotel experiences. This
study did not directly focus on employee engagement but showed that employee-related characteristics are
important in the customers' evaluation of hospitality experiences. Happy staff can then be more likely to
deliver attentive, responsive and personalised services. Vigour can aid in energetic service delivery,
dedication can enhance commitment to guest needs, and absorption can foster attentiveness during customer
interactions.
H2: Employee engagement has a significant positive effect on customer experience.

2.5 Mediating Role of Employee Engagement
Employee engagement could be a key pathway in which ethical leadership manifests in customer
experience. According to Social Exchange Theory, when employees are treated fairly and ethically by their
leaders, they are more likely to put in extra effort and commitment into their work (Blau, 2017). This
investment can then impact customer behaviours. The research on hospitality is in line with the mediation
logic. Wang et al. (2024) identified the mediation between ethical leadership and work engagement through
employee ambidexterity. Aldulaimi et al. (2025) showed that employee work engagement is a mediator
between ethical leadership and employee loyalty. Topal et al. (2026) also discovered that job satisfaction and
perceived HR practices accounted for the relationship between ethical leadership and employee engagement.

outcomes, few studies have focused on employee engagement as a specific mediator between ethical
leadership and customer experience. In a similar vein, Hoang et al. (2023) pointed out the need for additional
studies linking ethical leadership to other hospitality outcomes. The current study aims to fill this void by
exploring the relationship at three levels: organisational level (ethical leadership), employee level (employee
engagement) and guest level (customer experience). Engaged employees can then help to foster more positive
customer interactions, and ethical leadership can help to foster fairness, trust, and ethical guidance.
H4: Employee engagement significantly mediates the relationship between ethical leadership and
customer experience.
2.6 Theoretical Synthesis and Research Gap
There is a logical and consistent theoretical basis in the literature to explore the relationship between
ethical leadership, employee engagement, and customer experience in the hospitality industry. Social
Exchange Theory accounts for the fact that employees might respond to ethical treatment with increased
engagement (Blau, 2017), and Social Learning Theory accounts for the fact that employees might learn
appropriate attitudes and behaviours by observing ethical leaders (Bandura & Walters, 1977; Brown et al.,
2005). Previous research has shown that there are relationships between ethical leadership and employee
outcomes such as job satisfaction, organisational commitment, engagement, and service performance (Kim &
Brymer, 2011; Wang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023; Aldulaimi et al., 2025; Topal et al., 2026). Hospitality
customer experience research also shows that employee behaviour is a critical factor in influencing guests'
perceptions. However, there is limited literature available on organisational, employee and customer levels.
Past research often investigates ethical leadership and its impact on employee attitudes, creativity,
commitment, loyalty or service performance, and customer experience research typically looks at the service
encounter and employee behaviour. The mediating role of employee engagement between ethical leadership
and customer experience is relatively under researched. To fill this gap, the present study empirically tests the
direct and indirect relationships between ethical leadership, employee engagement, and customer experience
in the hospitality industry.

latent variables and multiple relationships (Hair et al., 2019). The analysis involved measurement and
structural model assessment. The measurement model was evaluated for indicator reliability, internal
consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity using Cronbach's alpha, composite reliability,
indicator loadings, AVE, and HTMT (Henseler et al., 2015). After establishing satisfactory measurement
properties, the structural model was assessed using path coefficients. The bootstrapping procedure was used
to evaluate the importance of hypothesised relationships. Final hypothesis testing (Hair et al., 2022) can be
conducted with a large number of bootstrap subsamples, e.g., 10,000. Four hypotheses were tested: The effect
of ethical leadership on employee engagement, the effect of employee engagement on customer experience,
the direct effect of ethical leadership on customer experience, and the mediating effect of employee
engagement between ethical leadership and customer experience. The respondents were informed about the
purpose of the research before filling the questionnaire, and the participation was voluntary. There was no
requirement for the respondents to give personally identifying information, and the information collected was
kept confidential. The data were only used for academic research. Participants were also told that they did not
have to participate and could drop out of the program without penalty.

6. Conclusion and Recommendations
6.1 Conclusion
The purpose of this study is to investigate the relationships between Ethical Leadership (EL),
Employee Engagement (EE) and Customer Experience (CE) in the hospitality industry, focusing on the
mediating role of employee engagement. The study employed a quantitative cross-sectional design and PLS-
SEM using the SmartPLS software, which was used to empirically test four hypothesised relationships. The
results confirmed that ethical leadership had a positive and significant impact on employee engagement.
Ethical leadership, defined as having five dimensions (fairness, integrity, transparency, responsibility, and
concern for employees), had a significant relationship with employee engagement. Leaders showing ethical
behaviour consistently can set behavioural expectations and serve as positive role models for employees, thus
supporting the social-learning approach to ethical leadership of Brown et al. (2005). The findings also
confirmed that there is a statistically significant positive relationship between employee engagement and
customer experience. Higher employee engagement leads to higher energy, dedication, attention and
responsiveness when interacting with guests. The involvement of employees is a significant element in
customer perceptions of their service experiences, as the service is largely reliant on the employee-customer
interactions that take place. This is in line with the customer-experience point of view of Lemon and Verhoef
(2016), who suggest that relationships are important throughout the entire customer journey.
In addition, the analysis results showed that there is a significant direct effect between ethical
leadership and customer experience. Customer experience was found to be connected to ethical leadership, in
addition to employee engagement. Ethical leaders set expectations for behaviour, values within the
organisation and service standards, which can impact the workplace and customer environment in which
employees provide their hospitality service. Importantly, the mediation analysis validated the significant
mediation of employee engagement between ethical leadership and customer experience. With a large indirect
effect, along with the existing direct effect being significant, there is partial mediation. Therefore, the
relationship between ethical leadership and customer experience was found to be both direct and indirect via

6.2 Recommendations
Strengthen ethical leadership: Develop managers' skills in fairness, integrity, transparency,
accountability, and ethical decision-making.
Enhance employee engagement: Provide recognition, feedback, professional development, supportive
working conditions, and opportunities for employee participation.
Connect leadership with customer experience: Integrate ethical leadership, employee engagement, and
customer-experience management into leadership training.
Recognise customer-oriented behaviour: Reward employees who demonstrate professionalism,
responsiveness, ethical conduct, and effective customer care.

employee level of engagement and customer level of experience in the hospitality industry.
6.3 Limitations and Future Research
A cross-sectional design does not allow conclusions to be drawn regarding change over time.
Longitudinal study designs can be used in future studies. Self-report and common-method bias also might
exist in questionnaire-based data, and a combination of employee, customer, and supervisor data could be
used in future studies. Other mediators or moderators such as job satisfaction, organisational commitment,
psychological safety, service climate, perceived organisational support and customer-oriented behaviour
could be explored in future research. The findings might also be amplified through comparative research of
hotels, restaurants, resorts and other cultures. The study finds that ethical leadership is significantly correlated
with employee engagement and customer experience, and that employee engagement is a significant
association between the two constructs of ethical leadership and customer experience. These results affirm
the need to incorporate ethical leadership and employee involvement into the strategies of the hospitality
sector to enhance customer experience.
