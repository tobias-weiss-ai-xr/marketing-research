Abstract
Community pharmacies operate at the intersection of healthcare delivery and retail competition, making both service
quality and price potentially important in shaping customer loyalty. This study examined whether loyalty toward
community pharmacies in Saud i Arabia is more strongly related to perceived service quality or to perceived price
competitiveness. A cross -sectional electronic survey was conducted among adult community -pharmacy users, and 277
eligible responses from 302 distributed questionnaires wer e included in the analysis. Perceived service quality was
measured using the Arabic short form of the Perceived Service Quality Scale ( pSQS-A-SF). Confirmatory factor analysis
and structural equation modeling were then used to assess the measurement model and examine the proposed
relationships with loyalty outcomes.   The structural model showed good overall fit (CFI = 0.981, RMSEA = 0.049, SRMR =
0.036). Perceived service quality was positively associated with both patronage loyalty (β = 0.29, p = 0.015) and
prescription loyalty (β = 0.44, p = 0.001), with the stronger  relationship observed for prescription loyalty. In contrast,
perceived price competitiveness was not significantly associated with any of the loyalty outcomes examined. Private
medical insurance was also positively associated with patronage loyalty ( β = 0.18, p = 0.014).  The findings indicate that
customers’ experiences with pharmacy services may matter more for continued patronage than perceptions of relative
price advantage. In the Saudi community -pharmacy market, professional staff interactions, reliable medicine -related
support, and patient-centered service appear to provide a stronger basis for customer retention than price competition
alone. These results reinforce the strategic importance of service quality for community pharmacies seeking to build
sustained customer relationships in an increasingly competitive healthcare retail environment.

Keywords
Service quality; Customer loyalty; Price competitiveness; Customer retention; Community pharmacy; Saudi Arabia .

1. Introduction
Community pharmacies have traditionally been viewed as convenient points for obtaining medicines, but their role has
expanded considerably in recent years. In many settings, they now contribute to medication management, patient
counseling, preventive care, chronic-disease support, and referral, in addition to their dispensing function. This broader
role has shifted attention toward the quality of the patient experience and the way pharmacy services are delivered.
Current quality frameworks therefore place e mphasis on person -centered care, accessibility, safety, professional
competence, the physical service environment, and integration with the wider healthcare system (Donabedian, 1988;
Hindi et al., 2024; Kaneko et al., 2025).
How patients judge the quality of a community pharmacy is not determined by a single aspect of the encounter. Their
evaluations may reflect the pharmacist’s competence, the clarity of communication, responsiveness to questions, the
quality of the relationship with pharmacy staff, medicine-related support, convenience, and the physical environment in
which the service is provided. This multidimensional view is consistent with established service -quality research, which
links perceived service performance wit h satisfaction and subsequent behavioral intentions (Parasuraman et al., 1988;
Cronin & Taylor, 1992; Dagger et al., 2007). In practice, two pharmacies may dispense medicines correctly while still
creating very different experiences for their customers.
This distinction becomes particularly important when patients have several pharmacies from which to choose. Price is
clearly part of the value judgment consumers make (Zeithaml, 1988), but price advantage alone may not be enough to
maintain a long -term relationship with a pharmacy. Evidence from pharmacy settings suggests that trust, satisfaction,
interpersonal interaction, and the overall service experience can influence whether customers return to the same
provider (Castaldo et al., 2016; Gül et al., 2023 ). Carter et al. (2023) and Albarq et el. (2026) likewise reported stronger
relationships between perceived service quality and pharmacy patronage or loyalty intentions than between perceived
price competitiveness and these outcomes. This raises an important managerial question: in c ommunity pharmacy, is
loyalty built primarily through better prices, or through the experience customers receive when they use the service?

Saudi Arabia provides a useful setting in which to address this question. Changes in the Saudi healthcare system have
increased the involvement of private community pharmacies in prescription dispensing and other patient-facing services.
One of the most vi sible examples is Wasfaty , the national electronic prescribing service that connects government
healthcare providers with participating community pharmacies. The program forms part of wider efforts to improve
access and efficiency through digitalization and outsourcing (Alshammari  et al., 2023). As more prescriptions are
dispensed in community settings, patients interact with these pharmacies more frequently, making the quality of those
encounters increasingly relevant to both healthcare delivery and pharmacy competition (Alzahrani et al., 2024a; Rasheed
et al., 2024).
The size of the Saudi community-pharmacy market also makes service differentiation more important. National statistics
indicate a large network of private pharmacies and a substantial pharmacy workforce (General Authority for Statistics,
2024). Greater acc ess benefits patients, but it also means that customers often have several alternatives available to
them. Location and medicine availability remain important, yet these factors may not be sufficient to retain customers
when competing pharmacies are readily accessible.
Evidence from Saudi Arabia generally shows positive patient experiences with pharmacy services, but it also points to
areas where service can still be improved. In a national study of 66,541 Wasfaty users, more than 70% of respondents
reported satisfaction with the services evaluated, although medication availability and communication remained areas of
concern (Alzahrani et al., 2024a). Another national analysis involving more than 91,000 respondents found that users of
community pharmacies and Wasfaty  had lower odds of satisfaction than users of primary -healthcare pharmacies in
relation to medication availability, prescription explanation, and waiting time (Alzahrani et al., 2024b). Regional studies
have reported a similar pattern: overall satisfaction  is often high, but problems remain in areas such as medicine
availability, waiting time, privacy, and consultation facilities (AlGhadeer et al., 2023; Kandasamy et al., 2026; Alharby et
al., 2026).
Satisfaction, however, is not the same as loyalty. A patient can be satisfied with a single pharmacy visit without necessarily
choosing the same pharmacy the next time a prescription needs to be filled. Similarly, a positive service experience does
not automatically mean that the patient will concentrate future purchases in one pharmacy or avoid competitors. This
distinction matters because patient-experience measures describe what happened during the service encounter, whereas
loyalty concerns what custome rs do, or intend to do, afterward (Hindi et al., 2024; Kaneko et al., 2025). For pharmacy
managers, this makes the link between service quality and continued patronage particularly important.
Research in community pharmacy has increasingly examined service quality using instruments designed specifically for
this setting. The original Perceived Service Quality Scale was developed and validated across six domains: health and
medicines advice, rel ationship quality, technical quality, environmental quality, non -prescription service, and health
outcomes (Mirzaei et al., 2019; Grew et al., 2019; Suleiman et al., 2025). A shorter version was later developed to reduce
respondent burden while preserving the underlying construct (Carter et al., 2022). More recently, the Arabic short form,
pSQS-A-SF, was culturally adapted and evaluated in Saudi Arabia. In that work, perceived service quality was associated
with patronage and prescription loyalty, whereas perceived price competitiveness did not significantly predict those
outcomes (Abanmy et al., 2026).
Despite this growing body of work, several issues remain insufficiently examined in the Saudi community -pharmacy
context. Much of the existing literature has concentrated on satisfaction with specific service attributes rather than on
whether an overall pe rception of service quality is reflected in different forms of loyalty. In addition, the relative
importance of service quality and price competitiveness has received limited attention in a healthcare retail environment
where insurance coverage and prescri ption reimbursement may reduce the influence of price on pharmacy choice. A
further question is whether service quality is related in the same way to repeated patronage, concentration of prescription
purchases, and continued use of competing pharmacies. Th ese outcomes are related, but they are not interchangeable,
and each has different implications for pharmacy retention strategies.
The present study addresses these issues by examining the relative associations of perceived service quality and perceived
price competitiveness with three loyalty -related outcomes: patronage loyalty, prescription loyalty, and patronage
disloyalty. Structural equation modeling is used in an independent sample of Saudi community -pharmacy users to move
beyond instrument validation and focus instead on the role of perceived service quality in explaining retention -related
behavior. The study therefore examines whether a stronger service experience provides a more meaningful basis for
customer loyalty than perceived price advantage in the Saudi community -pharmacy market.
1.1 Study Hypotheses
Based on the conceptual framework and previous community -pharmacy research, six directional hypotheses were
formulated:
H1: Perceived service quality is positively associated with patronage loyalty.
H2: Perceived service quality is positively associated with prescription loyalty.
H3: Perceived service quality is negatively associated with patronage disloyalty.

2. Methods

2.1 Study Design and Setting
A cross -sectional questionnaire -based study was carried out among adults who used community pharmacies in Saudi
Arabia. The study focused on how patients perceived the quality of services provided by community pharmacies and
whether these perceptions were related to their loyalty toward a particular pharmacy.
Data were obtained through a self-administered electronic questionnaire. An online format was used because it allowed
the survey to reach participants from different geographical areas and pharmacy settings while ensuring that the same
questions and instructions were presented to all respondents.
Participation was limited to individuals who had recent experience obtaining prescription medicines from a community
pharmacy. This requirement was important because respondents needed sufficient firsthand experience with pharmacy
services and staff intera ctions to provide a meaningful evaluation. To maintain a clear point of reference, participants
were asked to think about one specific community pharmacy from which they had obtained prescription medication and
to answer the subsequent questions with that pharmacy in mind.
The study was conducted within the Saudi community -pharmacy sector, which includes both chain and independently
operated pharmacies. These pharmacies provide prescription and non -prescription medicines and increasingly offer a
wider range of patient -oriented services. Their growing involvement in the national healthcare system, particularly
through electronic prescribing initiatives such as Wasfaty, makes the setting appropriate for examining whether patients’
evaluations of service quality are related to continued preference for and loyalty toward the pharmacy they use.
2.2 Participants and Eligibility Criteria
The target population consisted of adult community -pharmacy users living in Saudi Arabia. Participants were eligible if
they were 18 years of age or older, currently resided in Saudi Arabia, and had recently obtained prescription medication
from a communit y pharmacy. To ensure that respondents could provide an informed evaluation of their pharmacy
experience, they were also required to have personally visited and used a community pharmacy within the previous six
months.

Each participant was asked to identify one community pharmacy that they had used for prescription medication and to
answer the service-quality and loyalty questions with reference to that same pharmacy. Keeping the reference pharmacy
constant helped ensure  that the reported service experience and subsequent loyalty responses related to the same
provider.
Individuals were excluded if they were younger than 18 years, had not personally obtained prescription medication from
a community pharmacy during the specified period, or were unable to identify a specific pharmacy experience on which
to base their respon ses. Submitted questionnaires were also excluded when they were substantially incomplete,
contained inconsistent eligibility information, or showed evidence of duplicate participation.
Participation was voluntary. Before entering the main questionnaire, respondents were given a brief explanation of the
study and were asked to confirm both their eligibility and their consent to participate. No personally identifying
information was required.
2.3 Instrument and Measurement
Perceived service quality was assessed using the Arabic short form of the Perceived Service Quality Scale for community
pharmacies (pSQS-A-SF). This instrument was derived from the original theory -based pSQS and its short form and was
subsequently adapted for Arabic -speaking community-pharmacy users in Saudi Arabia (Mirzaei et al., 2019; Grew et al.,
2019; Carter et al., 2022; Abanmy et al., 2026). It was selected because it was developed specifically for com munity-
pharmacy service assessment rather than adapted from a general retail -service scale.
The previously validated Arabic version was used without additional translation or linguistic modification. Respondents
completed the scale with reference to the same pharmacy identified earlier in the questionnaire. This helped ensure that
their evaluatio ns of service quality and their loyalty -related responses were grounded in one consistent pharmacy
experience.
The pSQS-A-SF covers several aspects of the community -pharmacy experience, including relationship quality, technical
quality, environmental quality, non -prescription service, health and medicines advice, and perceived health outcomes.
These dimensions provide a broader view of service quality than dispensing accuracy alone and reflect both interpersonal
and service-delivery aspects of the pharmacy encounter.
Because the scale was being used in a new sample and within a broader explanatory model, its psychometric performance
was re -evaluated before interpretation of the structural relationships. Internal consistency was examined using
Cronbach’s alpha and compo site reliability. Convergent validity was assessed using standardized factor loadings and
average variance extracted (AVE), while discriminant validity was examined using inter -construct correlations. These
procedures are consistent with established recomm endations for evaluating latent constructs (Fornell & Larcker, 1981;
Hair et al., 2017; El-Den et al., 2020).
Other constructs included in the model were measured using previously validated multi -item scales drawn from the
service-quality, consumer-behavior, and community-pharmacy literature. Where small wording changes were necessary,
they were made only to ensur e that each item referred clearly to the focal community pharmacy while preserving the
original conceptual meaning.
2.4 Study Measures and Constructs
Three main perceptual constructs were examined: perceived service quality (pSQ), perceived price competitiveness (pPC),
and perceived service loyalty (PSL). The measures were based on the Arabic short form of the Perceived Service Quality
Scale for community pharmacies, which had previously been translated, culturally adapted, and evaluated in Saudi Arabia
(Abanmy et al., 2026).
Perceived service quality (pSQ)  reflected participants’ overall evaluation of the service provided by the focal pharmacy.
It covered aspects of the experience such as interaction with pharmacy staff, professional competence, support with
medicine-related needs, and the service environme nt. Respondents evaluated these items with reference to the same
pharmacy throughout the questionnaire.
Perceived price competitiveness (pPC)  captured whether respondents believed that the prices offered by the focal
pharmacy were competitive compared with available alternatives. The construct was included because patients’
pharmacy choices may reflect both the quality of the service received a nd the economic value they associate with the
provider.
Perceived service loyalty (PSL) represented participants’ intention to maintain their relationship with the focal pharmacy.
It reflected preference for that pharmacy, intention to continue using it for medicine -related needs, and the extent to
which respondents regarded it as their regular pharmacy.
In addition to these perceptual measures, indicators of actual pharmacy -use behavior were included to provide a more
direct picture of patronage patterns. These measures made it possible to examine whether more favorable evaluations
of service quality and price competitiveness were associated with greater concentration of pharmacy use and stronger
loyalty toward the same provider.

All perceptual items were rated using a five-point Likert-type scale consistent with the validated instrument. Higher scores
represented more favorable service -quality evaluations, stronger perceptions of price competitiveness, and greater
loyalty.
Before the structural model was examined, the reliability and validity of the constructs were assessed in the present
sample. Indicator reliability, internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity were evaluated to
establish whether the measurement model was sufficiently robust for subsequent hypothesis testing.
2.5 Data Collection Procedure
Data were collected through a self -administered electronic questionnaire distributed to eligible community -pharmacy
users in Saudi Arabia. The online approach was chosen to make participation possible across different locations and to
maintain a consistent survey format for all respondents.
The first page of the questionnaire explained the purpose of the study, the eligibility requirements, and the voluntary
nature of participation. Respondents were required to confirm that they met the inclusion criteria and to provide informed
consent before proceeding. Screening questions were placed before the main survey items to reduce the likelihood of
responses from ineligible participants.
Eligible participants were then asked to identify a specific community pharmacy from which they had obtained
prescription medication. All subsequent questions relating to service quality, price competitiveness, loyalty, and
pharmacy-use behavior referred t o that same pharmacy. This procedure helped reduce ambiguity and ensured that the
different constructs were evaluated in relation to a common service experience.
The questionnaire was distributed through WhatsApp, email, social -media platforms, and institutional networks using a
non-probability convenience sampling approach. Rather than relying on a single distribution channel, the survey was
circulated through several networks and participant groups to broaden participation and reduce excessive concentration
within one social or professional circle.
Data collection took place from March 2026 to June 2026. Participation was anonymous, and no information that directly
identified respondents was collected. Before analysis, submissions were reviewed and records were excluded if they failed
the eligibility screening, were substantially incomplete, or appeared to be duplicate or otherwise invalid responses.
To reduce response bias, participants were reminded that there were no correct or incorrect answers and were
encouraged to respond according to their own experience with the pharmacy they had selected. They were also free to
discontinue participation at any point before submitting the questionnaire.
2.6 Ethical Considerations
The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the
Deanship of Scientific Research Ethical Committee at King Faisal University (project number: KFU264975). Participation
was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained before respondents proceeded to the main questionnaire.
2.7 Statistical Analysis
Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 and Mplus  version 8.5. SPSS was used to summarize participant
characteristics, pharmacy -use patterns, and questionnaire responses. Responses recorded as “I do not know” were
treated as missing values.
The measurement model was examined first using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Standardized factor loadings,
composite reliability, average variance extracted, inter -construct correlations, and global model -fit indices were
considered before moving to the structural analysis. Missing observations in the latent -variable analyses were handled
through the estimation procedure available in Mplus.
Structural equation modeling was subsequently used to examine the relationships among perceived service quality,
perceived price competitiveness, and the loyalty -related outcomes. Standardized coefficients, p values, and explained
variance were reported fo r the estimated relationships. Demographic and pharmacy -use characteristics were also
examined as potential covariates, and statistically relevant covariate paths were retained in the final structural model.
Model fit was assessed using several indices rather than relying on a single criterion. These included the chi-square statistic
(χ²), comparative fit index (CFI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) with 90% confidence intervals, and
standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). The indices were interpreted collectively because their performance may
vary according to model complexity, degrees of freedom, estimator, sample size, and indicator characteristics (Schreiber,
2017; Shi et al., 2022; Goretzko et al., 2024; Groskurth et al., 2024). Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

evaluated. The strongest result was observed for health and medicines advice (HMA), with approximately 86% of

3.5 Confirmatory Factor Analysis of Perceived Service Quality, Price Competitiveness, and Loyalty Intentions
The initial CFA model included the pSQS -A-SF, perceived price competitiveness (pPC), and loyalty intentions. Overall
model fit was not satisfactory (χ² = 181.4, df  = 41, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.944, SRMR = 0.058, RMSEA = 0.124; 90% CI: 0.106 –
0.143). Although the CFI and SRMR were relatively acceptable, the RMSEA remained high. Inspection of the modification
indices also suggested that the model was not fitting the data a s well as expected. The main source of concern involved
the Health & Medicines Advice (HMA) item, which showed substantial overlap with Relationship Quality (RQ) and Health
Outcomes (HO).
For descriptive reporting, all six original pSQS -A-SF items were retained. However, HMA was removed from the latent
perceived service quality construct for the subsequent CFA and SEM analyses. After this adjustment, model fit improved
noticeably (χ² = 103.7, df  = 32, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.970, SRMR = 0.042, RMSEA = 0.102; 90% CI: 0.082 –0.123). The RMSEA
was still above commonly cited reference values, but the other fit indices were satisfactory. No further modifications were
made because there was no sufficiently clear theoretical basis for additional changes.
The revised measurement model showed acceptable reliability but only partial support for convergent validity. All
standardized factor loadings were above 0.50, and composite reliability values exceeded 0.70. For perceived service
quality, the AVE was 0.46,  which was slightly below the commonly used 0.50 criterion. Cronbach’s alpha, however,
remained satisfactory at 0.81. Taken together, these results indicate that the construct had adequate internal consistency,
although its convergent validity should be interpreted with some caution (Table 5).
The relationships among the constructs were also examined. Perceived price competitiveness was moderately correlated
with pSQS-A-SF (r = 0.642, p < 0.001) and with loyalty intentions (r = 0.621, p < 0.001). A much stronger relationship was
found between pSQS-A-SF and loyalty intentions (r = 0.918, p < 0.001). This high correlation raises a clear concern about
discriminant validity, as the two constructs may not be sufficiently distinct empirically in this sample.
The constructs were nevertheless retained as separate concepts because they represent different stages of the customer
response. Perceived service quality concerns how customers evaluate their experience with the pharmacy, whereas
loyalty intention concern s what they intend to do following that experience. Even so, the magnitude of the observed
correlation indicates considerable overlap, and this finding should not be overlooked when interpreting the structural
results.
Because of this high overlap, an additional CFA was estimated using only pSQS -A-SF and pPC. This more restricted model
showed acceptable fit (χ² = 30.8, df = 13, p = 0.004, CFI = 0.982, SRMR = 0.033, RMSEA = 0.079; 90% CI: 0.043 –0.115).
Overall, the CFA results support the reliability of the revised pSQS -A-SF measurement model, but they also indicate that
the distinction between perceived service quality and loyalty intentions should be examined again in an independent
sample.

3.6 Structural Path Model Predicting Patronage Behaviors
The structural model showed a good overall fit to the data (χ² = 58.1, df = 38, CFI = 0.981, RMSEA = 0.049, 90% CI: 0.022 –
0.073, SRMR = 0.036). These indices indicated that the specified model provided an acceptable representation of the
relationships among service quality, price competitiveness, and the loyalty-related outcomes.
Perceived service quality was the more important predictor of pharmacy loyalty. Higher pSQS-A-SF scores were associated
with greater patronage loyalty (β = 0.29, p = 0.015) and, even more strongly, with prescription loyalty (β = 0.44, p = 0.001).
H1 and H2 were therefore supported. In practical terms, respondents who evaluated the pharmacy more positively were
more likely to return to the same pharmacy and to obtain their prescription medicines there.
The relationship between perceived service quality and patronage disloyalty was much weaker and did not reach
statistical significance (β = 0.10, p = 0.412). H3 was therefore not supported. This suggests that a positive service
experience may encourage cus tomers to return to the same pharmacy without necessarily preventing them from also
using other pharmacies when needed.
Perceived price competitiveness, by comparison, was not significantly related to any of the three behavioral outcomes.
The estimated paths to patronage loyalty (β = −0.05, p = 0.604), prescription loyalty (β = −0.11, p = 0.401), and patronage
disloyalty (β = −0.18, p = 0.168) were all nonsignificant. H4, H5, and H6 were therefore not supported. The pattern across
these results indicates that, in this sample, customers’ evaluations of service quality were more closely related to loyalty
than their perceptions of relative price advantage.

4. Discussion
The main result of this study is that perceived service quality showed a clearer and more consistent relationship with
pharmacy loyalty than perceived price competitiveness. Service quality was positively associated with both patronage
loyalty and prescription loyalty, whereas price competitiveness was not significantly related to any of the three behavioral
outcomes examined. In the Saudi community-pharmacy setting, this pattern suggests that the way customers experience
the service may matter more for ret ention than whether they view the pharmacy as offering better prices than its
competitors.
This finding adds to previous community -pharmacy research in several respects. First, loyalty was not treated as a single
overall concept. The study distinguished between repeated patronage, concentration of prescription use, and the
tendency to use competing pharmacies. Service quality showed its strongest relationship with prescription loyalty, which
may indicate that patients place particular value on continuity when obtaining medicines. Second, service quality and
price competitiveness were examined together, allowing a direct comparison between two possible bases of competitive
advantage. The lack of significant price effects suggests that price alone may have limited power to retain customers in a

healthcare retail environment where professional competence, communication, reliability, and medicine-related support
are also important parts of the service experience.
Recent evidence from Saudi Arabia and the wider region supports this interpretation. In Aseer, satisfaction with pharmacy
services was associated with communication, medicine availability, ease of using Wasfaty, and waiting time (Kandasamy
et al., 2026). I n Hail, patients reported favorable views of pharmacist -patient relationships, although privacy and
consultation facilities remained areas of concern (Alharby et al., 2026). Similar findings have been reported in the United
Arab Emirates, where pharmacist attentiveness, adequate time for medication advice, accessibility, and waiting time were
important elements of patient satisfaction (Kharaba et al., 2026). These findings suggest that loyalty in pharmacy settings
is unlikely to depend on a single service attribute. Rather, it appears to develop from a combination of interpersonal,
operational, and environmental experiences.
The Saudi context is particularly important when considering these findings. Wasfaty has moved a substantial amount of
prescription dispensing from government facilities into participating community pharmacies, increasing the frequency
with which patients interact with these providers. National data from 66,541 Wasfaty  users showed generally high
satisfaction, but also identified medicine availability and communication with pharmacy staff as areas needing
improvement (Alzahrani et al., 2024a). Another national study involving 91,317 respondents found lower satisfaction
among community -pharmacy and Wasfaty  users than among primary -healthcare pharmacy users for medication
availability, prescription explanation, and waiting time (Alzahrani et al., 2024b). Earlier evidence from Al-Ahsa also showed
high overall satisfaction alongside weaker ratings for medicine  availability (AlGhadeer et al., 2023), while national
experience research has pointed to continuing concerns about access and private counseling (Rasheed et al., 2024).
The broader clinical role now being taken on by community pharmacies makes these service -quality issues even more
relevant. Research in Saudi Arabia shows that patient -centered pharmacy services are becoming more available, but
awareness and use of some advanced services remain uneven (Almanasef, 2023; Alotaibi et al., 2024; Khayyat et al., 2024).
At the same time, pharmacy services are increasingly moving beyond the traditional face -to-face encounter. Recent
research on e-pharmacy services in Saudi Arabia has shown that convenience, accessibility, and customer experience are
also becoming important in digital settings (Bahamdan & Almanasef , 2024). This supports a broader view of community
pharmacies as service organizations in which physical, interpersonal, clinical, and digital experiences together shape how
customers evaluate value.
The positive association between private medical insurance and patronage loyalty also deserves attention, although it
should not be interpreted too strongly. Insurance may influence pharmacy choice through reimbursement rules,
contracted pharmacy networks, out-of-pocket costs, or repeated use of a limited number of approved providers. Because
this study used a cross -sectional design, it cannot determine whether insurance itself creates loyalty or whether insured
patients simply follow medication -access pathways that make repeated use of the same pharmacy more likely. Future
research should examine insurance together with factors such as prescription source, medicine availability, out-of-pocket
expenditure, and network restrictions.
The measurement results also raise an important issue. The strong correlation between perceived service quality and
loyalty intentions is understandable from a theoretical perspective because customers who evaluate a pharmacy
positively may also be more wi lling to return. However, a correlation of this magnitude raises a genuine concern about
discriminant validity. The two constructs were retained because they represent different ideas: service quality refers to
the customer’s evaluation of the pharmacy exp erience, whereas loyalty intention reflects a future behavioral response.
Even so, the high level of empirical overlap observed in this sample means that the distinction between them should be
interpreted carefully and examined again in other samples. Simi lar concerns were reported in the Saudi validation of the
pSQS-A-SF (Abanmy et al., 2026).
The removal of the HMA item should also be viewed cautiously. Its exclusion from the latent service -quality construct
reflects the way the item behaved in this particular sample and model, not evidence that health and medicines advice is
unimportant to community-pharmacy quality. In fact, medicine -related support remains central to the professional role
of pharmacists. Future studies should therefore test whether the same measurement pattern is reproduced in
independent samples and should consider alternati ve HMA indicators as well as the full multidimensional pSQS (Fornell
& Larcker, 1981; El-Den et al., 2020).
Although the structural model showed good overall fit, the proportion of variance explained in the behavioral loyalty
outcomes was modest: 10.1% for patronage loyalty, 15.0% for prescription loyalty, and 3.6% for patronage disloyalty. This
is not unexpecte d. Pharmacy choice is likely to be influenced by many factors in addition to service quality and price,
including location, medicine availability, waiting time, opening hours, continuity with pharmacy staff, privacy, digital
functionality, brand reputation , and perceived value. International research has identified trust and satisfaction as
important drivers of pharmacy loyalty (Castaldo et al., 2016), while more recent evidence from chain pharmacies has
highlighted the role of brand, price, facilities, convenience, and staff knowledge (Huong et al., 2025). The modest R² values

therefore suggest that service quality is one important part of pharmacy loyalty, but not a complete explanation of
customer choice.
Several limitations need to be considered. The use of convenience sampling may have reduced the representativeness of
the sample and could have favored respondents who were more digitally connected or more engaged with pharmacy
services. The cross -sectional design also prevents conclusions about temporal or causal relationships. In addition,
patronage behaviors were self-reported and may therefore be affected by recall error or social-desirability bias. Regional
and pharmacy -chain differences may not have b een fully represented, even though previous research suggests that
community-pharmacy experiences vary across different Saudi settings (El -Kholy et al., 2022; Alotaibi et al., 2024;
Kandasamy et al., 2026; Alharby et al., 2026). Finally, modifying the measurement model within the same sample may
capitalize on sample-specific characteristics. Independent replication is therefore important, and model-fit indices should
be interpreted in relation to the model and data rather than treated as universal pass-or-fail rules (Schreiber, 2017; Shi et
al., 2022; Goretzko et al., 2024; Groskurth et al., 2024).
Future research could address these limitations by using probability -based or multi-region sampling where feasible and
by testing the measurement and structural models in independent samples. Longitudinal studies would also be useful in
determining whether  improvements in perceived service quality are followed by sustained patronage, greater
prescription concentration, or less switching between pharmacies. Future models could include additional variables such
as medicine availability, waiting time, accessib ility, privacy, pharmacist continuity, digital service quality, and perceived
value. Linking patient-reported experiences with objective refill or transaction data would provide an even stronger basis
for understanding actual loyalty behavior and could help reduce common-method bias.
Overall, the findings indicate that service quality is more closely associated with loyalty -related behavior than perceived
price competitiveness in the Saudi community -pharmacy market. This does not mean that price is irrelevant, but it
suggests that customers may be more likely to remain with a pharmacy when they experience reliable medicine -related
support, competent and responsive staff, clear communication, accessible service, and a patient -centered environment.
For pharmacies seeking to strengthen long-term patronage, improving the service experience may therefore offer a more
durable strategy than relying mainly on price competition.’

5. Conclusion
This study shows that perceived service quality was more closely linked to pharmacy loyalty than perceived price
competitiveness in the Saudi community -pharmacy market. The strongest relationship was observed for prescription
loyalty, indicating that professional, dependable, and patient-centered service may play an important role in encouraging
customers to continue obtaining their medicines from the same pharmacy. By contrast, perceived price competitiveness
was not significantly related to the loyalty ou tcomes examined. Taken together, the findings indicate that community
pharmacies may build stronger and more sustainable customer relationships by focusing on service quality rather than
depending mainly on price competition.
