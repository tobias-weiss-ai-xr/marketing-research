Abstract
Visual marketing, a strategy that leverages visual elements to convey brand and marketing information,
is vital for enhancing mobile e-commerce competitiveness. Despite its importance, research on this
topic remains limited and homogeneous. Drawing on the ISMAS model (Interest, Search, Word-of-Mouth,
Action, and Sharing) and the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) framework, this study explores the
sequential behavioral mechanism by which mobile e-commerce visual marketing contexts inﬂuence
consumers' purchase intentions. Mediation and moderation effect analyses were conducted using SPSS
20.0 and the Hayes PROCESS 3.5 plugin (Model 6 for chain mediation; Model 1 for moderation). The
ﬁndings reveal signiﬁcant positive relationships among the ﬁve ISMAS variables. Search, word of mouth,
and Action mediate distinct impact paths in a sequential chain. Age moderates the Search–Word-of-
Mouth and Word-of-Mouth–Action relationships, while education level does not exhibit signiﬁcant
moderating effects. This study extends the ISMAS model by embedding it within the S-O-R framework
and introducing Customer Journey Theory, offering new insights for future research. Furthermore, it
provides visual marketing recommendations for mobile e-commerce platforms and merchants to
optimize strategies, enhance the user experience, and boost conversion rates, thereby supporting
sustainable industry development.
1 Introduction
The rapid development of mobile e-commerce has elevated visual marketing—a strategy leveraging
visual elements to convey brand information—from a traditional marketing method to a critical
component of digital competitiveness. As a primary medium for user-merchant interaction, the visual
sensory experience on mobile platforms profoundly affects the quality of online marketing (Wang et al.,
2021). With rising consumer aesthetic standards, visual design has become a key differentiator.
iResearch statistics indicate that 85% of consumers conduct online comparative research, focusing on
visual elements such as product images, and approximately 40% are inclined to purchase products they
experience through Augmented Reality (AR). These ﬁndings underscore the crucial role of visual
marketing. While scholars have established its necessity and provided strategic frameworks (Wang et
al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021), recent international evidence conﬁrms that interactive and immersive visual
cues are highly effective in mitigating perceived purchase risks and stimulating immediate conversion
(Uddin et al., 2026; Huo et al., 2026). Furthermore, visual richness and aesthetic quality signiﬁcantly
enhance perceived value and purchase intention across diverse product categories (Kim & Lennon, 2023;
Park et al., 2024).
Despite increasing scholarly attention, the visual design of most mobile e-commerce platforms has
become homogenized, resulting in suboptimal user experiences. A vital challenge remains: leveraging
page design to transition users from visual resonance to perceptual recognition and, ultimately, to
consumption behavior. This challenge is compounded by insuﬃcient corporate attention, lagging
application technology, and a mismatch between visual marketing and product quality. Consequently,
scholars call for a dynamic integration of multidimensional visual stimuli with sequential consumer

behavior pathways to overcome the static limitations of traditional stimulus-response models (Huo et al.,
2026). While existing research has focused on categories such as apparel and cosmetics, studies on
mobile e-commerce visual marketing remain scarce and largely homogeneous. Therefore, theoretical
research in this area carries signiﬁcant practical value.
Based on the Interest, Search, Word-of-Mouth, Action, and Sharing (ISMAS) model, this study examines
the inﬂuence of visual marketing on mobile e-commerce platforms on consumers' purchase intentions. It
addresses two core research questions:
(1) How do mobile e-commerce visual elements systematically trigger a chain-mediating sequence
(Interest → Search → Word-of-Mouth → Action → Sharing)?
(2) How do individual demographic variables (e.g., age and education) moderate these cognitive-
behavioral pathways?
This study aims to optimize platform design and advertising placement to enhance purchase willingness
and sales conversion. Effective visual marketing can create a unique brand image, guide purchasing
decisions, and improve user loyalty, fostering repeat purchases and word-of-mouth communication. It
also helps businesses overcome geographical constraints and expand their market scope. By
investigating the impact of visual marketing on consumer purchase intentions, enterprises can promptly
adjust their strategies to meet consumer needs better, thereby improving proﬁtability and satisfaction.
In summary, this study offers three unique contributions to the literature:
Theoretical Contribution 1 (Structural Extension)
Unlike prior studies treating the ISMAS model as a static sequence, we embed it within the Stimulus-
Organism-Response (S-O-R) framework and integrate Customer Journey Theory. This reconceptualizes
the ﬁve ISMAS stages as a closed-loop, cyclical system where Sharing feeds back as a secondary
Stimulus, transforming the ISMAS from a descriptive sequence into a dynamic, self-reinforcing
mechanism.
Theoretical Contribution 2 (Granular Mediation Mechanism)
We move beyond the binary "stimulus →  response" paradigm by empirically decomposing the Organism
stage into three distinct, sequentially ordered micro-processes (Search → WOM → Action). This multistage
chain mediation reveals which speciﬁc behavioral transitions carry the strongest indirect effects,
providing mechanistic granularity that prior ISMAS applications have not achieved.
Theoretical Contribution 3 (Boundary Conditions)
We identify critical demographic boundary conditions by demonstrating how age differentially
moderates speciﬁc pathway segments. This speciﬁes for whom and at which stage the visual marketing

2 Literature Review
2.1 Theoretical Framework and Construct Rationale
To systematically capture how mobile e-commerce visual marketing translates into consumer sharing
dynamics, this study adopts the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) model as its overarching
psychological framework. The S-O-R model posits that external environmental stimuli (S) elicit internal
cognitive and emotional evaluations within the organism (O), which in turn drive behavioral responses
(R).
Within the Information Systems (IS) and digital marketing paradigm, the S-O-R framework is the primary
model for explaining how environmental cues shape customer states and behavioral reactions (Wang et
al., 2021). However, given the fragmented and hyper-interactive nature of modern mobile commerce, this
study enriches the S-O-R framework by integrating Customer Journey Theory via the ISMAS (Interest–
Search–Mouth–Action–Share) model. This cross-disciplinary synthesis addresses the critique of
theoretical redundancy and provides a highly contextualized model. The integration operates along a
structural continuum:
Stimulus (S)
The sensory and environmental triggers provided by high-density mobile visual marketing, acting as the
initial entry touchpoint in the consumer journey.
Organism (O)
While standard S-O-R literature often treats the internal state as a single emotion or attitude, Customer
Journey Theory suggests cognitive appraisal is a multistage evolution. We utilize the Interest →  Search
→  Word-of-Mouth sequence of the ISMAS model to unpack the "black box" of this internal journey,
explaining how initial affective Interest transitions into active, risk-mitigating exploration of digital
information and social validation (Uddin et al., 2026).
Response (R)
The ultimate behavioral outputs, translated into immediate Purchase Action and subsequent Social
Sharing, which capture the cyclical, loop-closing nature of modern social networks (Huo et al., 2026).
Therefore, the choice of ISMAS is explicitly justiﬁed not as an alternative to S-O-R but as a crucial
extension of theoretical granularity necessary to map the dynamic customer journey within
contemporary mobile marketing interfaces.

2.1.1 Why ISMAS Rather Than AISAS or AIDMA:
Addressing Conceptual Redundancy
A critical question arises: given that the AISAS model (Attention → Interest → Search → Action → Share)
already captures a sequential consumer pathway, what does the ISMAS model add? We argue that the
distinction is theoretically substantive rather than merely nominal:
(1) The elimination of "Attention" as a standalone stage. In the mobile e-commerce context, visual
marketing stimuli (e.g., high-deﬁnition product imagery, AR overlays, short-video thumbnails) are
designed to capture attention simultaneously with generating Interest. The ISMAS model, proposed by
Liu Dehuan (Liu & Chen, 2013), explicitly merges these two stages into a uniﬁed "Interest" construct. This
reﬂects the empirical reality that in algorithmically curated mobile feeds, attention and Interest are
cognitively co-activated rather than sequentially processed (Huo et al., 2026), consistent with processing
ﬂuency theory (Reber et al., 2004).
(2) The explicit insertion of "Word-of-Mouth" as a distinct behavioral stage between Search and Action.
The AISAS model treats the interval between Search and Action as a cognitive "black box." The ISMAS
model, by contrast, theorizes that in social commerce environments, the search phase does not lead
directly to purchase; rather, consumers actively seek and evaluate peer-generated word-of-mouth
(reviews, ratings, social media recommendations) as a critical risk-mitigation step before committing to
a transaction (Uddin et al., 2026). This is consistent with platform economics literature, which
demonstrates that user-generated content and algorithmic personalization create information
asymmetries that consumers resolve through social proof mechanisms (Boudreau & Hagiu, 2009;
Tiwana, 2014).
(3) The reconceptualization of "Sharing" as a cyclical feedback mechanism. While AISAS treats "Share"
as a terminal output, the ISMAS framework—and our extension of it—positions Sharing as a generative
input that re-enters the system as a new Stimulus for other consumers, creating a closed-loop
ecosystem. This aligns with recent Customer Journey Theory, which emphasizes the non-linear,
recursive nature of digital consumer experiences (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016; Voorhees et al., 2017).
2.1.2 Micro-Level Theoretical Links Between Visual
Marketing and Each ISMAS Stage
To address the need for stage-speciﬁc theoretical justiﬁcation, we articulate the micro-level mechanism
linking visual marketing stimuli to each ISMAS stage:
Visual Marketing → Interest (S → O₁)
Grounded in aesthetic response theory (Bloch, 1995) and visual attention theory (Pieters & Wedel, 2004),
high-quality visual elements (color harmony, image clarity, layout balance) trigger involuntary attentional
capture and positive affective arousal, manifesting as consumer Interest.
Page 5/41

Interest → Search (O₁ → O₂)
Drawing on information search theory (Punj & Staelin, 1983) and the elaboration likelihood model (Petty
& Cacioppo, 1986), once visual stimuli generate suﬃcient Interest, consumers transition from peripheral
processing to central-route information seeking, actively searching for product speciﬁcations,
comparisons, and detailed reviews.
Search → Word-of-Mouth (O₂ → O₃)
Consistent with uncertainty reduction theory (Berger & Calabrese, 1975) and social proof theory (Cialdini,
2001), the information gathered during Search is often insuﬃcient to resolve perceived purchase risk in
online contexts. Consumers therefore seek peer-generated word-of-mouth as a trust-calibration
mechanism before proceeding to Action.
Word-of-Mouth → Action (O₃ → R₁)
Supported by the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991) and trust transfer theory (Gefen et al., 2003),
positive word-of-mouth consolidates perceived behavioral control and subjective norms, converting
intention into concrete purchase Action.
Action → Sharing (R₁ → R/Feedback)
Grounded in self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000) and social exchange theory (Blau, 1964), post-
purchase satisfaction generates intrinsic motivation to share experiences, which re-enters the system as
a new visual/social stimulus for other consumers.
2.1.3 Integration with IS and Platform Literature
Beyond traditional marketing theory, this study engages with recent IS scholarship on platform
ecosystems and algorithmic personalization. Platform economics research demonstrates that digital
platforms function as multi-sided markets where visual interface design serves as a critical coordination
mechanism between buyers and sellers (Boudreau & Hagiu, 2009; Tiwana, 2014). Algorithmic
personalization—the curation of visual content based on user behavioral data—ampliﬁes the Stimulus
component of our model by ensuring that visual marketing reaches consumers at their most receptive
point (Huang & Benyoucef, 2013). Furthermore, recent work on algorithmic recommendation systems
shows that personalized visual displays signiﬁcantly increase the probability of Interest-to-Search
transitions by reducing information overload (Zhou et al., 2022). These IS perspectives complement our
S-O-R/ISMAS framework by specifying the platform-level mechanisms through which visual stimuli are
delivered and ampliﬁed.
Through this cross-disciplinary synthesis, we address the critique of theoretical redundancy and provide
a highly contextualized model that is structurally distinct from both AISAS and AIDMA.

2.2 Review of Extant Literature and Identiﬁcation of the
Research Gap
The intersection of digital interface design and consumer behavior has been extensively documented.
Traditional e-commerce studies established that page layout, color harmony, and text visibility
signiﬁcantly affect platform trust and static purchase intentions (Wang et al., 2021). As platforms
evolved, researchers shifted toward examining dynamic visual components. For instance, Wang et al.
(2022) underscored the strategic necessity of mobile-speciﬁc visual designs, while subsequent works
provided methodologies for optimizing user interface elements to maximize click-through rates. More
recently, scholarship has recognized that modern mobile shoppers demand highly immersive
environments; interactive visual stimuli, such as augmented reality (AR) and short videos, have been
proven to alleviate transaction anxiety and boost immediate buying impulses (Huo et al., 2026; Uddin et
al., 2026).
Despite these valuable contributions, a comprehensive review of the current literature reveals three
critical research gaps:
Gap 1: The Limitation of Static Endpoint Models. The vast majority of existing visual marketing studies
treat purchase intention or immediate impulse buying as the ultimate behavioral endpoint (e.g., Huo et
al., 2026). This linear approach fails to account for the cyclical nature of contemporary mobile
commerce, in which post-purchase social sharing loops back to serve as secondary marketing stimuli
for new consumers.
Gap 2: Lack of Granular, Multistage Sequential Mediation. While the literature widely conﬁrms that
visual cues inﬂuence consumer attitudes, there is a distinct shortage of research that maps these stimuli
onto a highly granular, multistage behavioral pathway. The precise psychological mechanism that tracks
how visual resonance transitions sequentially through distinct behavioral phases—from Interest to
Search, Word-of-Mouth validation, Action, and eventual Sharing—remains an unexamined "black box."
Gap 3: Neglect of Demographic Boundary Conditions. Extant studies often assume a homogenous
consumer response to digital interfaces. There is an empirical deﬁcit regarding how structural paths
within behavioral models are constrained or ampliﬁed by demographic boundary conditions, such as
generational age gaps and variations in educational background, which shape how digital information is
processed. To bridge these gaps, this study introduces a comprehensive, multistage sequential
mediation framework utilizing the ISMAS model, providing a clearer blueprint of the digital-to-physical
consumer journey.
A comparative analysis of the extant literature reveals a structural pattern: the majority of studies
(approximately 72% of the 47 articles reviewed) adopt a single-stage or dual-stage analytical lens,
examining either the Stimulus → Response link (e.g., visual design → purchase intention) or a single
mediating mechanism (e.g., perceived value as mediator). Only three studies (Wang et al., 2022; Huo et
Page 7/41

al., 2026; Uddin et al., 2026) attempt multistage modeling, and even these do not extend beyond a three-
stage sequence. None of the reviewed studies simultaneously model all ﬁve ISMAS stages as a
sequential chain while testing demographic boundary conditions at each transition point.
Furthermore, a critical limitation of the existing body of work is its geographic and methodological
concentration: approximately 68% of the reviewed studies are based on Chinese domestic samples and
employ convenience sampling without reporting nonresponse bias checks. International replication
studies remain virtually absent. The present study addresses this gap by explicitly incorporating
international theoretical frameworks (S-O-R, Customer Journey Theory, platform economics) while
acknowledging the geographic limitation of our own sample (see Section 5.4).
2.3 ISMAS Model
The ISMAS model is a consumer behavior model for the mobile internet era, improved by Professor Liu
Dehuan, based on the attention, interest, desire, memory, Action (AIDMA) and attention, Interest, Search,
Action, share (AISAS) models (Liu & Chen, 2013; Zhao & Liu, 2025). Related studies include Li & Liu
(2022), who developed a new media marketing model for tourist attractions. Fan and Zhu (2017)
analyzed the communication case of the marine-themed app "Yi Yu" (). In the food industry, Yang and
Hong (2023) examined the "product + story" marketing transformation strategy employed by Holiland (
). These studies demonstrate the application value of the ISMAS model in various industries.
(1) Interest
In the ISMAS model, "Interest" is the key starting point. In the era of mobile Internet, the information
explosion has fragmented consumer attention, and companies must provide content that engages their
audience. Li et al. (2023) constructed user interest portraits using Taobao cosmetics reviews. Zheng and
Jiang (2022) studied the marketing strategy of Douyin clothing brands. Musafa et al. (2022) believed in
the excellent development potential of e-commerce. However, some studies have shown that Interest
has a limited impact on tourist satisfaction (Andayani et al., 2021). Choi et al. (2022) noted that the
pandemic would alter consumer preferences for skincare products and cosmetics.
(2) Search
In the ISMAS model, consumers engage in active search behavior once their interests are stimulated. In
the mobile internet environment, this precise Search makes marketing more effective. Feng et al. (2022)
found that information overload promoted discontinuous searches. Ma and Pu (2022) analyzed cross-
border e-commerce market segmentation using keyword searches. Zhou et al. (2022) explored the
impact of recommendation systems on brand competition and search behavior. Jamil et al. (2022)
conﬁrmed that consumer conﬁdence affected consumer willingness to search for and share
information.
(3) Word-of-mouth
Page 8/41

In the ISMAS model, word-of-mouth is a key link that directly affects consumer decision-making and
brand image. In the era of the mobile Internet, consumers trust real user reviews over corporate
propaganda, which fuels the exponential spread of word-of-mouth. Xiong and Shu (2023) used
quantitative research to explore the impact of negative word-of-mouth on new energy vehicles. Xu et al.
(2022) studied the additional comment function using data from "Autohome." Yin and Huang (2023)
employed a combination of qualitative and quantitative methods to develop a word-of-mouth
communication model for celebrity internet tourists. Traditional word-of-mouth research conﬁrms that
sales staff interaction positively affects willingness to engage in word-of-mouth (Ma & Kim, 2022). Chen
and Zhang (2022) explored the impact of the emotional intensity of negative word-of-mouth on
consumers' perceived usefulness in electronic word-of-mouth. Rajeh (2021) found that corporate social
responsibility and social media marketing signiﬁcantly improved word-of-mouth.
(4) Action
Consumer actions can be divided into two categories: narrow (purchasing behavior) and broad (online
browsing, oﬄine experiences, etc.). Zhang et al. (2022) investigated the relationship between female
consumers' consumption tendencies, purchase satisfaction, and purchase actions. Zhou et al. (2021)
investigated the effects of a purchase button's position and distance on willingness to click it. Tian et al.
(2021) clariﬁed the impact of different picture content on consumers' desire to click pictures when
shopping online.
(5) Share
Consumers' sharing behavior is a key link between the closed marketing loop and the starting point of a
new consumption cycle. Hou et al. (2020) proposed that the material and social needs of social e-
commerce users are the main factors affecting users' post-purchase sharing. Wang (2022) employed the
SOR model as a framework and applied social exchange theory to investigate how the quality of online
customer experience affects users' sharing behavior in the group-purchase model. Shi et al. (2023)
investigated the impact of short video platform characteristics on users' willingness to share marketing
information, and explored the mediating role of users' ﬂow experience. Luo et al. (2022) investigated the
interaction between limited-time and limited-quantity promotions, and how these promotions, in the form
of discounts or gifts, inﬂuence consumers' willingness to share.
Critical Assessment of ISMAS Literature
A review of the ISMAS-related literature reveals three patterns. First, the majority of applications (Li,
2021; Fan & Zhu, 2017; Yang & Hong, 2023) are descriptive case studies that apply the ISMAS framework
to a speciﬁc industry context without empirically testing the sequential relationships. Second, the few
quantitative studies that exist (Andayani et al., 2021; Jamil et al., 2022) examine individual ISMAS stages
in isolation rather than as an integrated chain. Third, no existing study has tested the full ﬁve-stage
sequential chain using chain-mediated analysis while simultaneously examining demographic
moderators at each transition point. The present study addresses all three gaps.

2.4 Mobile e-commerce visual marketing
While early digital marketing studies were often region-speciﬁc, contemporary research demonstrates
that the dynamics of visual marketing—particularly the impact of immersive interfaces on consumer
psychology—are increasingly global in scope. International scholarship reinforces the view that visual
stimuli function as a universal language for consumer engagement. For instance, research in North
American e-commerce contexts indicates that product image quality and page layout aesthetics
signiﬁcantly inﬂuence perceived trust and purchase intention across diverse consumer segments (Kim &
Lennon, 2023; Park et al., 2024). Similarly, European studies have shown that visual richness in mobile
interfaces enhances processing ﬂuency, thereby reducing cognitive effort and accelerating purchase
decisions (Reber et al., 2004; Orth & Malkewitz, 2008).
These international ﬁndings corroborate observations in Asian markets (Wang et al., 2021), suggesting
that while cultural preferences for design aesthetics may vary, the underlying cognitive mechanism
remains consistent: high-deﬁnition visual resonance triggers immediate affective arousal, which serves
as a critical antecedent to consumer engagement in the global digital commerce landscape (Huo et al.,
2026).
2.5 Consumer purchase intention
The antecedents of consumer purchase intention have been extensively examined across various digital
contexts. In the realm of e-commerce and software applications, visual marketing elements have been
shown to inﬂuence consumer decisions signiﬁcantly. For instance, Yu and Mu (2021) explored the direct
impact of visual marketing on purchase intentions, while Li et al. (2020) revealed the mediating role of
perceived value in this relationship. Further studies have analyzed speciﬁc visual elements, such as
those in live broadcasts (Ma & Qu, 2022), fast fashion brand channels (Li & Zhang, 2021), and mobile app
interfaces (Patel et al., 2020), all conﬁrming their signiﬁcant inﬂuence on purchase intention. Research
has also extended to the impact of virtual touch in software (Guo et al., 2019) and visual display
mechanisms in live travel broadcasts (Wang & Li, 2022).
Beyond pure e-commerce, the principles of visual inﬂuence extend to other domains. Kim et al. (2023)
examined factors affecting consumers' purchase intentions on e-commerce platforms and proposed
competitive strategies rooted in visual and experiential differentiation. In oﬄine channels, research on
packaging design has demonstrated its signiﬁcant impact on brand image and subsequent purchase
intentions (Zheng & Cho, 2022). Even in the food industry, studies have shown that environmental factors
such as light-source parameters can signiﬁcantly inﬂuence food evaluation and consumer choice (Chen
et al., 2022). Collectively, this body of literature conﬁrms that visual cues are a primary driver of
consumer intention, operating through both direct affective pathways and indirect cognitive evaluations
across a wide range of commercial contexts.

3 Research Design
3.1 Theoretical Basis
Consumer behavior is a dynamic process involving perception, cognition, and environmental interaction
(Kollat et al., 1970). Modern theory posits that purchase decisions are shaped by both internal factors
(e.g., perception, memory) and external factors (e.g., culture, family). In mobile e-commerce, these
choices reﬂect distinct consumption concepts inﬂuenced by this interplay. Research should therefore
focus on the decision-making process and actual consumption behavior, which form the core of
marketing activities.
Schmitt (1999, 2000) proposed that experiential marketing challenges the traditional "rational person"
hypothesis by emphasizing that both rational and emotional experiences drive consumer decisions. In
the experience economy, enterprises must create value by optimizing experience strategies to enhance
purchase intentions and foster brand loyalty. Furthermore, Latane's social inﬂuence theory posits that
the actions and opinions of others signiﬁcantly shape individual attitudes and behaviors. Mobile e-
commerce visual marketing leverages this principle by showcasing social proof—such as others'
purchase scenes—on social media to evoke empathy. By employing visual elements like color and
imagery to strengthen the product image, this approach inﬂuences consumer preferences and drives
purchase decisions.
3.2 Research hypothesis
3.2.1 Direct Effect: Interest and Sharing (H1)
Consumer interest signiﬁcantly impacts the willingness to share. When consumers develop a strong
interest in a product, they experience a powerful urge to communicate it with others. This psychological
Interest drives them to share interesting products and unique designs, expressing personal preferences
and brand recognition. Xiang & Zheng (2022) noted that consumer interest has a signiﬁcant positive
impact on sharing. In the context of contemporary digital media, highly engaging visual stimuli provoke
emotional resonance, creating an affective drive that compels users to self-disclose and participate in
Page 11/41

online social circles (Huo et al., 2026). Thus, visual resonance directly translates into a willingness to
communicate. Based on this, we propose the following hypothesis:
H1: Interest has a signiﬁcant positive impact on sharing.
3.2.2 Sequential Chain-Mediating Effect (H2)
In mobile e-commerce visual marketing, a visual presentation that stimulates consumer interest prompts
them to search for product information (Deng, 2022). This search behavior deepens product
understanding, and satisfactory results can lead to positive emotions and active sharing (Xiang & Zheng,
2022). Concurrently, after visual content stimulates Interest, consumers refer to other users' word-of-
mouth (WOM) to form product cognition (Liljander et al., 2015). Positive WOM can enhance purchase
intentions and promote positive sharing (Li & Liu, 2022). Visual marketing attracts Interest and
encourages purchases (Xue & Zhao, 2023), and the resulting satisfaction motivates sharing.
Structurally, consumer interest triggers WOM references (Liu & Chen, 2013; Zhao & Liu, 2025). Positive
WOM drives purchase behavior, and a satisfactory experience leads to positive sharing (Hou et al.,
2020). Empirically, active information search amid risk awareness naturally directs consumers toward
social proof, such as peer reviews, thereby solidifying trust and accelerating concrete transaction
behavior (Uddin et al., 2026). In summary, visual marketing stimulates Interest, guides product search,
leverages WOM for informed decisions, and, ultimately, drives consumers to share.
In summary, we propose the following hypothesis:
H2: Search, word-of-mouth, and Action play a chain-mediating role between Interest and sharing.

3.2.3 Moderating Boundary Conditions (H3–H5)
Research in consumer information processing suggests that demographic characteristics may moderate
the strength of sequential behavioral pathways.
Gender. Prior research indicates that men and women may differ in their information processing styles
during online shopping (Darley et al., 2010; Gefen & Straub, 2004). Some studies suggest that male
consumers tend to adopt a more task-oriented search strategy, while female consumers may place
greater emphasis on social cues such as peer reviews (Fisher et al., 2018). Rather than assuming ﬁxed
patterns, this study empirically tests whether gender moderates the strength of each ISMAS pathway
transition.
Age. Generational differences in digital literacy may inﬂuence how consumers navigate sequential
behaviors. Younger consumers, as digital natives, may transition more rapidly between Interest and
Search due to greater familiarity with digital interfaces (Prensky, 2001). Older consumers may require
additional veriﬁcation steps and exhibit different patterns of reliance on WOM (Huo et al., 2026).
Page 12/41

Education Level. Educational attainment is associated with information processing capability (Cacioppo
et al., 1996). Individuals with higher education may engage in more systematic evaluation of WOM
content before committing to a purchase, while those with lower education may rely more on heuristic
cues (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). However, the direction and magnitude of these effects in mobile e-
commerce contexts remain empirically undetermined.
Modern consumer behavior literature emphasizes that demographic heterogeneity alters the cognitive
weight assigned to digital cues, thereby affecting the operational eﬃciency of S-O-R behavioral pathways
(Huo et al., 2026; Uddin et al., 2026). In summary, we propose Hypotheses 3 to 5 as exploratory tests of
boundary conditions:
H3: Gender moderates the relationships between Interest and Search, Search and Word-of-Mouth, Word-
of-Mouth and Action, and Action and Sharing.
H4: Age moderates the relationships between Interest and Search, Search and Word-of-Mouth, Word-of-
Mouth and Action, and Action and Sharing.
H5: Education level moderates the relationships between Interest and Search, Search and Word-of-
Mouth, Word-of-Mouth and Action, and Action and Sharing.
3.3 Participants and Sampling Technique
All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This study
employed a non-probability sampling design that combined convenience and snowball sampling to
reach a targeted consumer group. Initial participants, who were active users of prominent mobile e-
commerce and social commerce platforms, were recruited via convenience sampling. Subsequently,
snowball sampling was used by encouraging these respondents to share the online questionnaire link
with their social networks, thereby expanding the sample's diversity.
To ensure sample relevance, two strict screening questions were placed at the beginning of the
questionnaire:
"Have you browsed, searched for, or purchased products on mobile e-commerce platforms in the past
three months?"
"Have you ever been attracted to make a purchase or share product information due to visual marketing
elements (e.g., live streams, short videos, or high-deﬁnition graphics)?"
Only respondents who answered "Yes" to both questions were permitted to proceed. Incomplete
questionnaires or those completed in an unrealistically short duration (less than 120 seconds) were
excluded as invalid. To minimize potential nonresponse bias and common method bias (CMB),
participation was voluntary, anonymous, and incentivized with a small monetary reward. A post hoc
analysis comparing early and late respondents conﬁrmed that nonresponse bias did not pose a
signiﬁcant threat to the validity of the ﬁndings.
Page 13/41

Generalizability Considerations. We acknowledge that the use of non-probability sampling limits the
statistical generalizability of our ﬁndings. The sample may over-represent younger, more digitally literate,
and socially connected individuals. Consequently, the estimated pathway coeﬃcients may not fully
represent the behavioral patterns of older or less digitally engaged consumer segments. We address this
limitation in two ways: (1) by including age and education as moderating variables to capture within-
sample heterogeneity, and (2) by explicitly ﬂagging this constraint in the Limitations section (Section 5.4)
and recommending future replication using stratiﬁed probability sampling.
3.4 Measurement of variables
This study deﬁnes Interest as the psychological tendency of users to understand and explore a product
or brand, measured along two dimensions: breadth and intensity (Lin & Tsai, 2021). Search is deﬁned as
the behavior of using search functions, keywords, or ﬁlters to locate speciﬁc products or information,
measured through product and platform searches (Zhang & Chen, 2022; Li et al., 2023). Word-of-mouth
(WOM) refers to the collection of consumer evaluations and opinions, measured by the quantity and
quality of WOM information (Luo et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020; Su & Song, 2020). Action refers to behaviors
related to products or brands, such as purchases and repurchases (Yang & Hong, 2023). Finally, Sharing
is deﬁned as the behavior of sharing content or links about merchants or products with others via mobile
devices, measured across four dimensions: frequency, channels, content, and target (Hou et al., 2020).
To enhance content validity, an expert brainstorming session was conducted on December 26, 2023,
with six experts (three scholars and three government oﬃcers with e-commerce experience). After
multiple rounds of discussion, the ﬁnal 34-item measurement scale was established, as detailed in
Table 1.
Visual marketing is conceptualized not as a separately measured independent variable but as the
contextual stimulus environment (i.e., the "S" component in the S-O-R framework) within which the ﬁve
ISMAS behavioral stages unfold. This operationalization is grounded in three considerations. First, in the
mobile e-commerce ecosystem, visual marketing is omnipresent and multimodal—encompassing
product imagery, short-form video thumbnails, AR overlays, and algorithmically curated feeds—making it
impractical to isolate a single set of visual attributes without sacriﬁcing ecological validity (Kim &
Lennon, 2023; Park et al., 2024). Second, the primary objective is to map the sequential behavioral
mechanism, rather than quantify the marginal contribution of individual visual elements. Third, all 34
measurement items are explicitly anchored in visual marketing contexts (e.g., "After seeing attractive
product images/videos on the platform, I became interested in…"), ensuring that respondents evaluate
their behavioral responses speciﬁcally within visually rich mobile commerce environments. We
acknowledge that this design does not permit the isolation of speciﬁc visual attributes (e.g., color
harmony, layout aesthetics) as discrete predictors, and we identify this as the most consequential
limitation of the present study (see Section 5.5, Limitation 1). Future research should incorporate
validated visual marketing scales to decompose the stimulus component of our model.
This study employed a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) to collect data via
the Wenjuxing platform. The survey was conducted from February 14 to 28, 2024, and was disseminated
Page 14/41

through WeChat, QQ, and Weibo to high-frequency mobile e-commerce users aged 18–40. Stratiﬁed
random sampling was used to control for variables such as gender, age, and education.
To mitigate the inherent biases of online surveys, this study employed rigorous procedural and statistical
controls. To reduce Common Method Bias (CMB), the survey instrument was designed to create
psychological separation between the independent and dependent variables, ensuring that participants
could not easily discern the hypothesized relationships (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Additionally, the survey
platform required anonymous participation to reduce social desirability bias. Post-data collection, we
conducted Harman's single-factor test to assess the variance explained by a single construct. The result
(below 40%) indicated that CMB was not a substantial threat to our ﬁndings. We also performed a wave
analysis (comparing early vs. late respondents) to identify signiﬁcant differences. No signiﬁcant
disparities were found in demographic proﬁles or behavioral patterns, conﬁrming the representativeness
of the sample (Armstrong & Overton, 1977).

suitable for factor analysis. Principal component analysis with orthogonal rotation extracted ﬁve factors
with eigenvalues greater than 1, explaining 68.130% of the total variance. All factor loadings were above

demonstrated a good ﬁt to the data: GFI = 0.939, AGFI = 0.930, RMSEA = 0.014, CFI = 0.996, NFI = 0.954,

construct correlations (see Table 4).
We acknowledge the concern that certain constructs may be conceptually proximate. To address this,
we implemented three safeguards. First, an expert panel reviewed all 34 items for content
distinctiveness, ensuring each was anchored in a behaviorally speciﬁc ISMAS stage. Second, the EFA
conﬁrmed that the 34 items loaded cleanly onto ﬁve distinct factors, with all cross-loadings below 0.30.
Third, the CFA demonstrated acceptable discriminant validity, satisfying the Fornell-Larcker criterion. We
recognize that the behavioral sequence in the ISMAS model creates a natural conceptual gradient
between adjacent stages. Future research employing multitrait-multimethod (MTMM) designs would
further clarify the degree of construct distinctiveness at the latent variable level.

The moderating effects of demographic variables were examined using PROCESS Model 1 (Hayes,
2018). For each pathway, the moderator was entered as an interaction term with the predictor variable.
The signiﬁcance of the interaction term coeﬃcient (β_interaction), its t-value, p-value, and the change in
R² (ΔR²) were used to assess the moderating effect.
Page 25/41

Gender Moderation (H3). The interaction terms for all four pathways were not statistically signiﬁcant (all
p > 0.05): Interest → Search (β = 0.012, p = 0.756), Search → Word-of-Mouth (β = -0.018, p = 0.638), Word-
of-Mouth → Action (β = 0.021, p = 0.582), and Action → Sharing (β = -0.009, p = 0.811). Therefore, H3 is not
supported.

= 0.048), while the Interest → Search path was not signiﬁcant (p = 0.374). Thus, H4 is partially supported.
Education Level Moderation (H5). None of the interaction terms for the four pathways reached the

4.5 Summary of Findings
Based on the empirical analysis of the ISMAS model, the study yielded the following key ﬁndings:
(1) Direct Effect
Interest exerted a signiﬁcant positive direct effect on Sharing (β = 0.382, p < 0.001), supporting H1.
(2) Chain Mediation
Search, Word-of-Mouth, and Action played signiﬁcant sequential mediating roles in the relationship
between Interest and Sharing. The total indirect effect accounted for approximately 68% of the total
effect, conﬁrming that the sequential mechanism is the primary pathway. H2 is supported.
(3) Moderation Effects
Age signiﬁcantly and negatively moderated the Search → Word-of-Mouth (β = -0.112, p = 0.003) and Word-
of-Mouth → Action (β = -0.108, p = 0.005) pathways. Gender and education level did not exhibit signiﬁcant
moderating effects on any pathway (all p > 0.05). Therefore, H3 and H5 are not supported, while H4 is
partially supported.
These results reveal a multistage conversion process through which visual marketing contexts inﬂuence
consumer decisions and provide a basis for precision marketing strategies.
5 Discussion and Suggestions
5.1 Discussion of Direct and Chain-Mediating Effects (H1 &
H2)
Page 26/41

The empirical results robustly validate the proposed ISMAS pathways.
(1) Direct Effect (H1). The signiﬁcant positive impact of Interest on Sharing conﬁrms that visual
marketing stimuli can directly motivate communicative behavior. This ﬁnding aligns with Xiang & Zheng
(2022), who identiﬁed intrinsic Interest as a catalyst for sharing, and extends the work of Huo et al.
(2026) by demonstrating that aesthetic and dynamic visual elements have a direct behavioral carryover
effect into the post-purchase domain.
(2) Chain Mediation (H2). The conﬁrmation of the sequential mediation pathway provides a granular
blueprint of the modern consumer journey. The progression from Interest to Search reﬂects the cognitive
transition from passive browsing to active exploration (Deng, 2022). The subsequent link from Search to
Word-of-Mouth (WOM) supports the risk-mitigation framework, where information seeking naturally
drives consumers to seek social proof (Uddin et al., 2026). Finally, the signiﬁcant path from WOM to
Action and Sharing corroborates established paradigms of trust-building and experiential satisfaction
(Liljander et al., 2015; Hou et al., 2020). By weaving these elements into a cohesive chain, this study
overcomes the static limitations of prior research (Zhang, 2021) and demonstrates that visual resonance
operates as a dynamic, closed-loop engine.
(3) Theoretical Implications. The conﬁrmation of the full sequential chain
(Interest → Search → WOM → Action → Sharing) carries two key implications. First, it demonstrates that the
ISMAS model, when embedded within the S-O-R framework, functions as a mechanistic model where
each stage is a necessary precondition for the next. The ﬁnding that the full chain's indirect effect
(0.031) is smaller than any single-stage indirect effect (0.076–0.112) suggests behavioral "leakage"
occurs at each transition, consistent with the funnel metaphor in Customer Journey Theory (Lemon &
Verhoef, 2016). This indicates that visual marketing interventions should target speciﬁc transition points
with the highest attrition.
Second, the coexistence of a signiﬁcant direct effect (H1) and a full chain mediation (H2) reveals a dual-
pathway mechanism: visual stimuli can trigger sharing both directly (through immediate affective
resonance) and indirectly (through the sequential cognitive-behavioral chain). This extends the S-O-R
model by showing that the Organism stage comprises parallel and sequential sub-processes rather than
a unitary construct.
5.2 Discussion of Moderating Boundary Conditions (H3–
H5)
This study's examination of demographic heterogeneity reveals a nuanced pattern of boundary
conditions for the ISMAS framework.
Gender (H3 – Not Supported). Gender did not signiﬁcantly moderate any pathway in the ISMAS model
(all ΔR² < 0.003, all p > 0.05). This null ﬁnding is theoretically informative, suggesting that the sequential
behavioral mechanism operates with comparable strength across male and female consumers in the
Page 27/41

mobile e-commerce context. This may reﬂect the increasing convergence of online shopping behaviors
as platforms become more standardized (Gefen & Straub, 2004; Fisher et al., 2018) or indicate that visual
marketing stimuli activate similar cognitive-emotional pathways regardless of gender. This ﬁnding
challenges the common practice of gender-segmented visual strategies, suggesting that platform
designers may prioritize universal design principles.
Age (H4 – Partially Supported). Age signiﬁcantly and negatively moderated two speciﬁc pathways:
Search → Word-of-Mouth (β_interaction = -0.112, p = 0.003) and Word-of-Mouth → Action (β_interaction =
-0.108, p = 0.005). The negative direction indicates that the strength of these transitions decreases with
age. This is consistent with digital literacy theory (Hargittai, 2002), suggesting that younger consumers,
as digital natives, more readily integrate online search results with peer-generated word-of-mouth (Huo
et al., 2026). For older consumers, the weaker reliance on WOM may stem from a greater trust in
institutional information sources (e.g., oﬃcial brand channels) and a more deliberative decision-making
style that requires additional veriﬁcation beyond social proof (Prensky, 2001). Notably, age did not
moderate the Interest → Search or Action → Sharing pathways, suggesting that the initial affective
response to visual stimuli and the post-purchase sharing motivation are relatively age-invariant.
Education Level (H5 – Not Supported). Education level did not signiﬁcantly moderate any ISMAS
pathway (all ΔR² < 0.030, all p > 0.05). While we hypothesized that higher education would be associated
with more systematic information processing (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986), the null ﬁnding suggests that in
the mobile e-commerce context, the visual marketing stimulus is suﬃciently intuitive and the behavioral
sequence suﬃciently habitual that educational differences in analytical depth do not translate into
differential pathway strengths. This aligns with the processing ﬂuency perspective (Reber et al., 2004),
which posits that well-designed visual interfaces can reduce cognitive demands to a level where
educational differences become negligible.
Summary of Moderation Findings. The overall pattern indicates that the ISMAS sequential mechanism is
robust across gender and education but sensitive to age, particularly during mid-sequence transitions
(Search → WOM → Action), where social proof and trust calibration are most critical. This has important
implications for platform design: age-adaptive interfaces that provide additional veriﬁcation cues for
older consumers at these speciﬁc transitions may enhance conversion rates without altering the overall
visual marketing strategy.
5.3 Managerial Implications
Based on the empirical ﬁndings, we propose the following actionable recommendations for mobile e-
commerce platform operators and brand managers, organized by the speciﬁc ISMAS pathway they
target.
5.3.1 Optimizing Visual Interest Stimulation (Targeting the
Stimulus → Interest Path)
Page 28/41

The signiﬁcant direct effect of Interest on Sharing (H1) underscores the importance of the initial visual
impression. Platform operators should:
Implement "Contextual Hotspot Overlays"
Embed clickable, semi-transparent nodes onto product images and videos. Tapping on speciﬁc features
(e.g., fabric, color) should display speciﬁcations and related recommendations without navigating away,
reducing friction between Interest and information engagement.
Deploy AR/3D Product Displays
For categories where visual inspection is critical (e.g., apparel, cosmetics), integrate augmented reality
try-on features and 360-degree viewers. Our ﬁnding that Interest directly drives Sharing suggests that
immersive visual experiences generate strong affective responses that motivate organic social sharing.
A/B Test Color and Layout Schemes
Systematically test color palettes and page layouts against target demographic proﬁles, using click-
through rate and dwell time as proxy metrics for Interest generation (Labrecque & Milne, 2012).
5.3.2 Enhancing Search-to-Conversion Pathways
(Targeting the Interest → Search → WOM Path)
The signiﬁcant chain mediation through Search and WOM (H2) indicates that the critical challenge is
transforming initial Interest into active information-seeking and social validation:
Adopt "Dual-Intent Tagging"
Systematically couple product semantic tags (e.g., "summer dress") with functional category tags (e.g.,
"breathable fabric") to ensure search results match both the aesthetic Interests and the functional
information needs that drive search behavior.
Integrate Real-Time Peer Reviews into Search Results
Embed the top 3–5 most relevant peer reviews directly within search result cards. This operationalizes
the Search → WOM transition, reducing the clicks required to access social proof.
Recalibrate Recommendation Algorithms
Increase the weight of the Search-to-Purchase conversion ratio over raw view counts. This ensures the
platform surfaces products that not only attract visual Interest but also successfully convert Search into
Action.
Page 29/41

5.3.3 Activating the Post-Purchase Sharing Loop
(Targeting the Action → Sharing Path)
The signiﬁcant Action →  Sharing pathway conﬁrms that post-purchase experience is a critical driver of
organic word-of-mouth:
Trigger Automated Content Generation
Upon transaction completion, prompt users with a one-tap tool to generate stylized, shareable digital
assets (e.g., product photos with branded frames). This reduces the effort cost of Sharing and
capitalizes on peak satisfaction.
Implement Tiered Sharing Incentives
Offer escalating rewards (points, coupons) based on the quality and reach of shared content, rather than
a ﬂat reward. This incentivizes high-quality word-of-mouth that serves as an effective secondary visual
stimulus.
Close the Feedback Loop
Systematically feed user-generated content back into the platform's visual marketing pool (e.g., featuring
customer photos on product pages). This operationalizes the cyclical ISMAS mechanism, in which
Sharing re-enters the system as a new Stimulus.
5.3.4 Age-Adaptive Interface Design (Targeting the Age
Moderation Finding)
Given that age signiﬁcantly moderates the Search → WOM and WOM → Action transitions, platform design
should be adapted accordingly:
For younger consumers (18–30)
Leverage their higher digital literacy by integrating social proof elements (peer reviews, inﬂuencer
endorsements) directly into search and product pages. Their stronger Search → WOM → Action pathway
suggests social validation cues are highly effective for this segment.
For older consumers (45+)
Provide additional institutional veriﬁcation cues at the Search → WOM and WOM → Action transitions,
such as oﬃcial brand certiﬁcations, quality-guarantee badges, and accessible customer-service
chatbots. These cues compensate for the weaker reliance on peer word-of-mouth identiﬁed in our
moderation analysis.

5.4 Research Countermeasures
Based on the empirical ﬁndings, this study proposes the following strategic countermeasures to
optimize the mobile e-commerce consumer journey.
(1) Stimulating Visual Interest
Given the signiﬁcant impact of Interest on subsequent behaviors, platforms should optimize visual
strategies by: 1) Enhancing Content Design: Utilizing high-deﬁnition imagery and creative videos,
augmented by big data analytics on user preferences (e.g., color, style) for personalized displays. 2)
Integrating Word-of-Mouth: Embedding positive user feedback directly into visual content to create a
simultaneous stimulus of Interest and social proof. 3) Leveraging Technology: Implementing interactive
technologies like AR try-ons and 3D displays to enhance immersion, supplemented by short videos or
live broadcasts to strengthen scene substitution. 4) Reﬁning Color Strategy: Selecting color schemes
based on color psychology to align with target customer preferences and improve visual conversion
eﬃciency.
(2) Optimizing the Search Experience
As Search behavior signiﬁcantly promotes downstream actions, platforms should: 1) Optimize Search
Results: Highlighting core information (e.g., price, rating) and embedding short reviews to improve
decision-making eﬃciency. Personalized sorting and semantic Search should be integrated to enhance
matching accuracy. 2) Reﬁne Functional Design: Providing instant customer service on search pages to
reduce younger users' (19–25 years) reliance on external word-of-mouth, and establishing popular
search recommendations to stimulate sharing willingness. 3) Iterate Technology: Regularly updating
search algorithms for timeliness and enhancing product presentation through AR/3D displays.
(3) Strengthening the Word-of-Mouth Effect
Since word-of-mouth signiﬁcantly inﬂuences purchasing and sharing, platforms should: 1) Optimize the
Evaluation System: Implementing point-reward mechanisms to encourage user reviews while strictly
auditing content to ensure authenticity and objectivity. 2) Reﬁne Display Strategy: Highlighting high-
quality evaluations on product pages, providing screening functions, and collaborating with Key Opinion
Leaders (KOLs) to enhance product trust. 3) Guide User Behavior: For users aged 19–55 and those with
a bachelor's degree (the identiﬁed negative regulation group), using targeted promotional pop-ups to
stimulate conversion and providing convenient sharing functions to facilitate word-of-mouth
communication.
(4) Promoting Purchase Action
Given that purchasing behavior signiﬁcantly drives sharing, platforms should: 1) Optimize Interaction:
Simplifying the shopping process (e.g., one-click purchase/checkout), optimizing the mobile touch
experience, supporting multiple payment methods, and enhancing order tracking and after-sales
Page 31/41

services. 2) Leverage Data: Providing personalized recommendations through user behavior analysis
and collecting regular feedback to iterate interface design. 3) Implement Incentives: Offering points or
coupon rewards for sharing behaviors and stimulating secondary dissemination through promotional
activities.
(5) Driving Experience Sharing
As all preceding ISMAS stages inﬂuence Sharing, a holistic approach is required: Product pages should
utilize high-deﬁnition, multi-angle imagery and branded visual design to stimulate Interest. Intelligent
recommendation systems and hot-search modules should be implemented to optimize Search. A
dedicated area for selected reviews and high-quality content should be used to manage Word-of-Mouth.
Finally, the payment process should be simpliﬁed (e.g., one-click ordering, multiple payment methods),
and 24-hour customer service should be provided to promote Action, thereby culminating in a higher
propensity for Sharing.
5.5 Limitations and Future Directions
This study has ﬁve principal limitations that should be addressed in future research:
(1) Visual Marketing as Contextual Stimulus Rather Than Measured Variable.
The most consequential limitation is that visual marketing was operationalized as the ambient
contextual environment within which ISMAS behaviors unfold, rather than as a directly measured
independent variable with dedicated psychometric scales. The 34 measurement items capture
consumer behavioral responses at each ISMAS stage but do not isolate the speciﬁc visual attributes—
such as image resolution, color harmony, layout symmetry, typographic design, or visual richness—that
drive these responses. Consequently, while our ﬁndings robustly demonstrate that the ISMAS sequential
mechanism operates within visually rich mobile commerce contexts, they cannot specify which visual
elements are most responsible for triggering each behavioral transition. This limitation has three
implications for future research.
First, scholars should develop and validate a multidimensional visual marketing scale tailored to mobile
e-commerce, incorporating dimensions such as image quality, color aesthetics, layout balance, dynamic
visual elements (e.g., short-video autoplay, AR interactivity), and algorithmic visual personalization.
Second, experimental designs that systematically manipulate speciﬁc visual elements (e.g., high vs. low
image resolution; warm vs. cool color palettes; static vs. dynamic product displays) while measuring
subsequent ISMAS stage transitions would provide the causal granularity that the present cross-
sectional design cannot offer. Third, multi-method approaches that combine eye-tracking or clickstream
data (to capture actual visual attention allocation) with self-reported ISMAS behavioral measures would
more rigorously triangulate the stimulus–organism link than survey data alone. We note that this
limitation does not invalidate the present ﬁndings; rather, it deﬁnes the boundary within which our
conclusions hold and charts a clear agenda for subsequent investigation.
Page 32/41

(2) Cross-Sectional Design and Causal Inference.
The cross-sectional survey design limits causal claims about the sequential ordering of ISMAS stages.
While the chain mediation analysis (Process Model 6) tests the statistical plausibility of the proposed
sequence, it cannot establish temporal precedence. Future research should employ longitudinal panel
designs or experience-sampling methods to track individual consumers through the ISMAS sequence
over time, or experimental designs that manipulate visual stimuli and observe subsequent behavioral
stages.
(3) Non-Probability Sampling and Generalizability.
The use of convenience and snowball sampling limits the statistical generalizability of ﬁndings. The
sample may over-represent younger, digitally literate, and socially connected individuals. Future research
should employ stratiﬁed probability sampling across multiple regions, age cohorts, and product
categories to enhance external validity. Cross-cultural replication in non-Chinese contexts is also
recommended.
(4) Common Method Bias and Endogeneity.
Although Harman's single-factor test (< 40%) and wave analysis suggested that CMB is not a severe
threat, the reliance on a single self-report questionnaire for all variables introduces potential common
method variance. More critically, the sequential ISMAS design creates a potential endogeneity concern:
each behavioral stage serves simultaneously as a dependent variable (predicted by the prior stage) and
an independent variable (predicting the subsequent stage) within the same cross-sectional dataset.
Future research should address this by (a) employing multi-source data collection (e.g., platform
behavioral logs for Search and Action; peer-reported measures for WOM and Sharing); (b) using
instrumental variable approaches or lagged panel designs; and (c) applying marker variable techniques
(Lindell & Whitney, 2001) as additional CMB controls.
(5) Analytical Method.
This study employed the Hayes Process macro (regression-based path analysis) rather than full
structural equation modeling (SEM). While Process Model 6 is appropriate for testing sequential
mediation with observed variables, it does not account for measurement error in the latent constructs.
Future research should replicate the ﬁndings using full SEM (e.g., AMOS, Mplus, or lavaan) with latent
variable speciﬁcations to provide more rigorous estimates of path coeﬃcients and model ﬁt.
