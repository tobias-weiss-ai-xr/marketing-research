Abstract: the article develops a customer-journey approach to managing omnichannel customer
experience in apparel retail. It argues that competitive advantage in clothing retailing rests less on the
number of channels a firm operates than on its ability to deliver a single, coherent experience across
them. Drawing on customer -experience research, the study maps the apparel customer journey and
proposes a typology of «experience gaps» — the seams at which the journey breaks. The fitting
moment is identified as the cri tical phygital touchpoint that determines conversion and returns. On
this basis, the study formulates a journey -based management framework linking gaps to managerial
levers, tools, and expected outcomes. To operationalize decision -making, a multi -criteria decision-
analysis (MCDA) model based on the Weighted Sum Model is developed to rank the experience gaps
by impact and implementation cost, supported by a Kendall’s concordance test framework for expert
validation. Finally, an action -priority matrix and str ategic roadmap are proposed to guide phased
managerial implementation. The contribution provides a practical diagnostic instrument for retailers
and a structural foundation for empirical testing.

Introduction. Over the past decade retailing has moved from a multichannel logic, in which
physical stores, websites and mobile applications operated as separate silos, towards an omnichannel
logic, in which channels and touchpoints are managed as a single, integrated s ystem. In the
omnichannel model the boundaries between channels blur and the customer perceives the retailer as
one brand rather than a set of disconnected outlets [15]. Shoppers now browse on a smartphone,
examine a garment in a store, check availability online and complete the purchase wherever it is most
convenient, expecting the retailer to recognise them and preserve the context of the interaction at
every step [2].
In this environment the basis of competition has shifted. Price and assortment remain necessary,
but they are increasingly easy to match; what is far harder to replicate is a consistently good
experience across the whole journey [6]. Customer experience — the customer’s cognitive,
emotional, sensorial, social and behavioural response to the retailer across all touchpoints — has
therefore become the central object of retail management [9].
Apparel retailing is a particularly demanding and revealing setting for this challenge. Clothing is
a high-involvement, hedonic and identity-expressive purchase: garments must be seen, touched and,
above all, tried on; sizing is inconsistent between brands; and fashion assortments are large, seasonal
and quickly refreshed. As a result the apparel journey is unusually non -linear and touch -intensive,
and it produces the highest product -return rates in retailing [10]. The promise of a seamless
omnichannel experience is therefore both more valuable in clothing than almost anywhere else and
considerably harder to keep.
The practical problem is that many apparel retailers invest heavily in new channels and
technologies yet still deliver a fragmented experience. Customers repeatedly encounter "seams" —

the purchase, and each erodes the very seamlessness that omnichannel retailing promises. The core
managerial task is thus not to add channels but to remove the seams between them.
The aim of this article is to develop a customer -journey approach to managing omnichannel
customer experience in apparel retail. Three objectives follow from this aim: first, to map the apparel
customer journey across its stages and the touchpoints that compose it; second, to propose a typology
of the experience gaps that break omnichannel seamlessness in clothing retailing, with the fitting
moment identified as the critical phygital touchpoint; and third, to formulate a management
framework that links each  experience gap to a concrete managerial lever, an enabling tool and an
expected experience outcome.
Research material and methods . This study is conceptual and methodological, combining a
qualitative synthesis of omnichannel retail literature with quantitative decision modeling. General
scientific methods (analysis, synthesis, abstraction) were applied to literature data to build a stage-
by-stage apparel customer journey map and a typology of cross -channel experience gaps. The
Weighted Sum Model (WSM) with min–max normalization was used to prioritize gap closure based
on weighted Impact and Cost criteria. Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) with a χ2 significance
test was integrated to validate expert panel agreement.This methodology yields an actionable strategic
roadmap (Action-Priority Matrix) pairing identified seams with specific managerial levers and KPIs.
Literature analysis. The conceptual foundation of the study rests on three streams of research:
omnichannel retailing, customer experience, and the design of the customer journey. Omnichannel
retailing is commonly defined as the synergetic management of the numerous available channels and
customer touchpoints in such a way that the customer experience across channels, and the
performance over channels, is optimised [15]. The defining feature of this model, in contrast to
multichannel retailing, is int egration: channels are no longer independent routes to purchase but
interdependent components of one system, and the customer moves among them within a single
interaction rather than choosing one and staying in it.
Customer experience is a multidimensional construct that unfolds as a journey across a series of
touchpoints [9]. Journeys designed for consistency across touchpoints are perceived as more effective
and strengthen loyalty [8], and a "seam" is precisely a l ocal failure of the integration quality that
Sousa and Voss [13] distinguished within multichannel service. The experience must also be
authentic and recognisably one brand as the customer moves between the physical and the digital
[11]. Our earlier work conceptualised omnichannel customer service at a general level [7]; the present
study addresses the apparel-specific journey and its characteristic failure points.
Distinctive features of the customer journey in apparel retail. Several features make the
apparel journey distinct and concentrate its experiential risk at particular points. The first and most
important is fit and sizing uncertainty. A garment’s value depends on how it fits and feels on the
body, which cannot be assessed fully at a distance. Sizing is notoriously inconsistent between brands
and even styles, so a known size is unreliable. Physical or simulated try -on is therefore a structural
necessity, and its absence online is the main reason clothing generates the hi ghest return rates in
retailing [10].
The second feature is the hedonic and identity -expressive nature of the purchase. Clothing is
bought not only for function but to express self -image, signal belonging and produce emotional and
sensory pleasure. The touch of a fabric, the look of a garment in a mirror and the social reaction of
companions are integral to the experience, which means that the sensorial and social dimensions of
customer experience emphasised by Lemon and Verhoef [9] are especially pronounced in fashion.
The fourth feature is assortment complexity. A single apparel style multiplies into a large matrix
of sizes and colours, and the assortment as a whole turns over rapidly with seasons and fashion cycles.
This makes accurate, real -time inventory visibility a cross stores and warehouse both difficult and
decisive: an availability promise the retailer cannot keep at the exact size and colour the customer
wants is worse than none. Finally, the top of the apparel funnel is heavily social: inspiration and

A typology of experience gaps that break omnichannel seamlessness. An experience gap, or
seam, can be defined as a discontinuity at the boundary between two touchpoints or channels at which
the customer is unable to carry information, identity or value forward and must therefore stop, repeat
an action, or abandon the journey. In the vocabulary of Sousa and Voss [13], a seam is a local failure
of integration quality; in that of Kuehnl et al. [8], it is a breach of the consistency that makes a journey
effective. Naming and classifying these seams is the practical heart of jo urney-based management,

Several of these gaps deserve comment because they are specific to, or especially damaging in,
clothing. The assortment and inventory gap is acute in apparel because the relevant unit of availability
is not the style but the exact size-and-colour variant; a system that shows the style as "in stock" while
the customer’s size is unavailable creates a promise that fails at the worst possible moment. The
identity gap bites at the fitting-and-purchase stage, where an assistant who cannot see the customer’s
online wish list or sizes cannot help as expected. The returns gap, finally, is disproportionately
important in fashion because returns are so frequent: a difficult return colours the whole relationship
and suppresses future purchases, whereas an easy, any -channel return turns an unavoidable friction
into a demonstration of brand reliability [11].
Two further characteristics of these seams matter for management. First, the gap the customer
experiences and the cause that produces it usually sit in different places: an "out of stock in your size"
seam felt in the fitting room originates in a fragmente d inventory system in the back office, and an
impersonal greeting felt at the counter originates in customer data that is not shared across channels.
Second, seams compound along the journey. A single customer may meet a content gap while
comparing, an inv entory gap while searching for a size, and a returns gap after purchase; each is
survivable alone, but in sequence they accumulate into an impression of a retailer that "does not work
together", which is far more corrosive to loyalty than any single failure.
The fitting moment as the critical phygital touchpoint. Across the apparel journey one
touchpoint is decisive above the others: the moment at which the customer judges whether a garment
fits, looks and feels right. This is where purchase intention is confirmed or lost, and it is also the
origin of most returns.  Because it is precisely the point at which the physical and the digital must
meet, the fitting moment is the category’s central phygital touchpoint — a place where a well -
managed retailer converts a potential seam into a bridge between channels.

The human channel matters as much as the technological one. When a shop assistant, through a
clienteling application, can see the customer’s online profile, wish list and past sizes, the fitting room
becomes a point of continuity rather than a fresh start, and the assistant can a ct as the visible
embodiment of the integrated journey. At the upper, experiential end, immersive phygital formats
that blend physical space with digital interaction have been shown to deepen engagement by building
rapport and social connection during the in-store encounter [12]. Yet technology at the fitting moment
succeeds only when it serves the experience: gadgets that undermine the sensory pleasure of trying
on clothing can weaken rather than strengthen the brand [11].  Managed well, the fitting moment
ceases to be the journey’s most dangerous seam and becomes the clearest expression of what
omnichannel apparel retailing can offer.
A first frontier extends the fitting room into the customer’s own home through photo -based
virtual avatars. Layered on top of the avatar, artificial-intelligence size engines learn from the brand’s
size grid and from its accumulated purchase -and-return history to predict, for each product, the size
the particular shopper is most likely to keep, and to express that prediction as a fit -confidence score
with an explicit recommendation to size up or down [1, 5]. Because size -related uncertainty is the
single largest cause of returns in clothing, moving this judgement to the pre -purchase stage attacks
the fitting gap at its root: a decision otherwise made only after delivery — and frequently mis-made
— is brought forward, with growing accuracy, to the moment before the parcel is dispatched.
A second frontier upgrades the physical cubicle itself into an RFID -enabled smart mirror. Each
garment carries an RFID tag, so the mirror automatically recognises the items a shopper brings in
and, without any manual input, displays the sizes and colours c urrently in stock, flags what is
available in the adjoining store or the wider network and — driven by a real -time recommendation
algorithm — proposes complementary pieces and alternative styles. From the mirror the shopper can
request another size to be b rought to the room or shipped home, so that the classic cause of an
abandoned fitting, the missing variant, is resolved on the spot. Together the home avatar and the in -
store smart mirror turn the fitting moment into a continuous, data -driven touchpoint re aching the
customer before and during the visit. The deployment of RFID smart mirrors and photo -avatars
demands very large capital expenditure, which for most Ukrainian retailers, amid war and economic
instability, is a difficult undertaking.
This progress also carries an environmental dimension. Because fit uncertainty drives over -
ordering, returns are a major and often hidden source of the sector’s carbon footprint: a full -process
assessment of apparel sales finds that return flows can accoun t for more than 30% of e -commerce
emissions and, under extreme scenarios, raise them by well over a hundred per cent [14]. Every
avatar-based or AI -predicted fit that prevents a wrong -size purchase therefore cuts both cost and
emissions, aligning customer experience, profitability and sustainability.
A journey-based framework for managing omnichannel customer experience. The journey
map and the typology of gaps translate into a management approach that proceeds in five steps. First,
the retailer maps the journey from the customer’s perspective, as in Table 1, rather than from the
perspective of its separate channel teams. Second, it instruments the touchpoints and measures
experience and, in particular, consistency across them, using the kind of journey -effectiveness
measures proposed by Kuehnl et al. [8]. Third, it locates the specific seams where consistency breaks,
using the typology in Table 2 as a diagnostic checklist. Fourth, it applies a targeted lever to each
identified gap. Fifth, it governs the whole through cross-functional ownership of the journey, so that
responsibility for the customer’s experience is not fragm ented along the same channel lines that
produce the seams in the first place.

Because managers face real budget and resource constraints, the seams cannot all be closed at
once and must be sequenced; deciding which to close first calls for an objective prioritisation
mechanism rather than intuition, which the following multi-criteria model provides.

The fifth step, governance, deserves emphasis because it is where journey -based management
most often fails. Retailers are typically organised by channel, each with its own budget and targets,
so when the online unit and the store network are rewarded sepa rately, neither has an incentive to
hand the customer smoothly to the other. Closing seams durably therefore requires assigning
ownership of the end -to-end journey to a role or team that sits above the channels, together with
shared metrics — journey-level satisfaction, cross -channel conversion, return rates and repeat
purchase — that make the whole experience, rather than the performance of any single channel, the
measure of success.
Conclusions. Managing omnichannel customer experience in apparel retail requires moving
beyond the mere expansion of sales channels toward the deliberate removal of cross -channel seams.
This study establishes a customer -journey management framework specifically tailor ed to the high-
involvement, touch-intensive nature of clothing retailing.
The paper maps the multi -stage apparel journey and identifies six structural experience gaps
(seams) across data, operational, and category -specific dimensions. Crucially, the fitting moment is
conceptualized as the pivotal phygital touchpoint where custom er hesitation, conversion, and return
risks intersect.
To resolve the practical dilemma of resource constraints, the study develops a multi -criteria
decision-analysis (MCDA) model based on the Weighted Sum Model. By balancing conversion,
return-reduction, and lifetime value against CAPEX, IT complexity, and op erational resistance, the
