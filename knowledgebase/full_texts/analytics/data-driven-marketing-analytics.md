# Data-Driven Marketing Analytics

DATA‑DRIVEN MARKETING ANALYTICS
Data‑Driven Marketing Analytics: Research Methods and Analytical Tools bridges the gap
between traditional marketing research and modern big data analytics, offering a step‑by‑step
guide to transforming data into actionable insights.
Readers will gain practical skills to design and manage research projects, predict customer
behavior, and uncover hidden patterns in purchasing data. This book delivers these benefits
through hands‑on exercises, shareable datasets, and visual workflows, ensuring accessibility
for users without coding experience. Key features include real‑world case studies, interactive
examples, and tools for segmentation, forecasting, and strategic decision‑making.
This book is tailored for postgraduate students, researchers, and marketing professionals
seeking to master data‑driven decision‑making. It is ideal for advanced courses in Marketing
Analytics, Management Information Systems, and Business Statistics.
This book is supported by a comprehensive online repository containing over 100 ready‑
to‑use files, including datasets, Excel solutions, SPSS files, and Orange workflows.
Selçuk Burak Haşıloğlu is Professor of Management Information Systems at Pamukkale
University, Turkiye. He specializes in marketing information systems, with over 30 years of
research experience. He has authored 10+ books and 100+ papers and led 20+ projects, focusing
on marketing analytics, data science, decision sciences, and digital marketing. As the Founding
Chair of the Management Information Systems Department, he also provides consultancy to the
private sector on big data analytics and information systems management.

Selçuk Burak Haşıloğlu is a scholar specializing in marketing information systems, with a focus
on research, data analytics, business intelligence, decision support systems, data science, and
data mining. He has been engaged in academic work since 1993. He holds a BSc in Mathemat‑
ics, a MBA in Business Administration, and a MSc in Computer Engineering. He completed his
PhD in 2006 in the Department of Business Administration, Gazi University.
His master’s and doctoral theses focused on e‑commerce and marketing information systems,
and are considered among the earliest studies in these fields in Turkey. In 2009, he completed
his postdoctoral research at the Henley Business School, Informatics Research Center, where
he conducted research for one year. During this period, he was awarded the Science Award by
the Undersecretariat of Foreign Trade of the Republic of Turkey. To date, he has received six
scholarships and awards.
Professor Haşıloğlu has authored more than ten books in the fields of digital marketing,
management, and marketing information systems, and has produced over 100 scientific publica‑
tions. He has participated in more than 20 research projects, including five large‑scale projects
funded by national institutions such as TÜBİTAK and various ministries. He was promoted to
full  professor in 2017.
His primary research interests include marketing research, marketing analytics, data science
and data mining, decision analysis, decision sciences, and digital marketing.
He has served as the president of the Turkish Marketing and Marketing Research Associa‑
tion and the Turkish Chess Clubs Federation and has actively contributed to numerous social
responsibility projects. In addition to his academic work, he provides consultancy services to
the  private sector in areas such as business analytics, marketing research, business intelligence,
and information systems management, as well as the mathematical economics and dynamics of
blockchain‑based gaming ecosystems.
Professor Haşıloğlu serves in the Department of Management Information Systems at
Pamukkale University, which he founded.
ABOUT THE AUTHOR

MEET YAKUTIYE
This book is the product of the past 30 years. It embodies an analytical mindset shaped and
refined through marketing science. It also reflects a life spanning more than 30 years with my
beloved wife, Dilek—a life during which this “book” was conceived, nurtured, matured, and
ultimately brought to fruition. To call this a coincidence would be no different than believing
that storks bring babies. Yes, I did not bring this work into the world alone; it is a child we share.
It was born under the title Marketing Research and Analytics (in Turkish), and through
patience and careful craftsmanship, it has evolved into what it is today. My dear daughters,
Melda and Nilsu, have always shown understanding and support for the time I devoted to it.
For this reason, I could not bring myself to refer to something that has become a member of our
family simply as a “book.” So I gave it a name: Yakutiye.
Gazan’s inquisitive nature constantly drove him toward discovery. Mahmud Ghazan Khan,
who lived seven centuries before us, left a profound mark on history as one of the most influen‑
tial rulers of the Ilkhanid state. He valued all living beings—so much so that he would even care
for birds that went hungry in winter. He undertook major initiatives to improve the economy,
trade, and financial systems of his state. Yet perhaps his greatest contributions to humanity were
his investments in education and science.
Across the lands under his rule, he encouraged and sustained the construction of  libraries,
madrasas, observatories, and other institutions of learning and research. During his reign, stud‑
ies and education flourished in fields such as religion, astronomy, mathematics, medicine, and
chemistry. These institutions were not only functional but also remarkable works of art. I believe
that both the legacy of Seljuk civilization and Sultan Mahmud Ghazan’s appreciation for art
played a significant role in the creation of these unique structures.
One such masterpiece is the Yakutiye Madrasa, built in 1310 in Erzurum, whose inscription
bears the names of both figures. Architecturally, it reflects the artistic style of the Seljuk tradi ‑
tion. For centuries, scientific studies were conducted under its roof, and thousands of students
were educated there.
In the early chapters of Yakutiye, you will gain foundational knowledge in marketing
research. It is also where you will first encounter data analytics. By the end of the third chapter,
you will complete your first Excel‑based application. Almost every concept is presented through
a data‑driven perspective.

Meet Yakutiye xxiii
As you progress, you will engage step by step with data analytics applications related to mar‑
keting research. You will learn to overcome the intimidating aspects of surveys and understand
how to minimize errors through practical exercises. Key topics such as internal consistency anal‑
ysis, response style measurement, sample size determination, normal distribution,  hypothesis
testing, and fundamental statistical analyses—essential elements of marketing research—will
be taught and reinforced through data analytics approaches.
In the later chapters, Yakutiye will guide you into the realm of marketing analytics. In the
final six chapters, you will work extensively with Orange Data Mining. Each chapter offers
distinctive and valuable insights. You will begin with pricing analytics, followed by demand
forecasting. Techniques related to distance measures and market segmentation will then be
introduced. Perceptual mapping and positioning analytics will take your understanding to the
next level. Your progress will accelerate further as you explore machine learning applications
in marketing analytics. You will then work on association rules and market basket analysis.
Finally, the journey concludes with decision‑making, multi‑criteria decision techniques, and
decision trees.
Topics that may initially seem complex will become more accessible through your famili ‑
arity with Excel, while the visual, intuitive environment of Orange will further simplify and
enhance your learning experience.
As a final note, I have authored more than 100 works to date. Yet none has excited me as
much as Yakutiye. As you read, you will see that Yakutiye is not merely a textbook. It embod‑
ies years of accumulated knowledge, enriched with data‑driven insights. It contains answers to
many of the questions that occupy the minds of marketing researchers. In fact, it offers some‑
thing for everyone—and all of it is grounded in data analytics.
It is my sincere hope that Yakutiye, which tells the story of Data‑Driven Marketing Analyt‑
ics: Research Methods and Analytical Tools, will serve humanity for many years to come—just
as the Yakutiye Madrasa, built in memory of Mahmud Ghazan and his wife Bulugan under the
coordination of Hoca Yakut, has done throughout history.

BEFORE YOU BEGIN
Software and hardware requirements
All applications in this book are explained using Microsoft Excel. I use Excel for Microsoft 365,
and all workbooks in this book were prepared with this version. You will need at least Excel
2007 or a newer version.
You will perform your first Excel‑based application in Chapter 3. The workbook used in
this chapter contains one year of sales transaction data and has a size of approximately 40 MB.
Some Excel‑based analytical procedures may take time to process, and certain computations can

A MARKETING INSIGHT STORY: HOW DADAJJ TURNED
DATA INTO DECISIONS
Dadajj is a healthy food company built on an ambitious idea: delivering natural, farm‑based
products directly from soil to consumers’ tables. Working closely with local farmers, the
company sources organic vegetables, grains, legumes, and minimally processed food prod‑
ucts, and sells them through its own e‑commerce platform. From production to logistics,
Dadajj positions itself as a transparent and trustworthy bridge between sustainable agricul‑
ture and modern digital consumers.
As the company grew, Dadajj’s management team noticed a familiar but troubling pat‑
tern. Website traffic was increasing steadily, social media campaigns were attracting atten‑
tion, and many visitors were adding products to their shopping carts. However, a significant
portion of these sessions ended without a completed purchase. For a brand built on trust,
quality, and long‑term customer relationships, this behavior raised an important question:
why do customers who are clearly interested in healthy products stop just before complet‑
ing the purchase?
Understanding the problem before touching the data
Rather than jumping directly into numbers and dashboards, Dadajj followed a research‑driven
approach. The marketing team began with focus group interviews involving existing cus‑
tomers and first‑time visitors. These sessions revealed several qualitative insights. Some par‑
ticipants expressed hesitation about product prices compared to conventional food. Others
mentioned uncertainty about freshness, delivery conditions, or whether the brand truly
matched their expectations. A few participants admitted that they often added products to
the cart “just to check” and postponed the decision.

2 Data‑Driven Marketing Analytics
These discussions helped the team understand the language of the customer. More
importantly, they shaped the way the problem was framed. Cart abandonment was not
merely a technical issue—it reflected hesitation, trust, perceived value, and decision timing.
Building on the focus group findings, Dadajj designed a structured online survey. The
survey aimed to quantify the qualitative themes uncovered earlier. Questions focused
on purchase motivation, perceived price fairness, trust in organic labels, previous online
shopping experience, and sensitivity to social media recommendations. The results helped
the team narrow down a manageable set of variables that were both meaningful and
measurable.
At this stage, Dadajj had moved from intuition to research‑based variable selection. The
company now knew which attributes mattered—but not yet how they interacted in real pur‑
chase decisions.
From research data to behavioral patterns
The final step was to integrate these insights with large‑scale behavioral data collected from
the e‑commerce platform. Session‑level data included whether the user was logged in, pre‑
vious purchase history, traffic source (such as Instagram or direct visit), product price level,
session duration, and ultimately whether the purchase was completed after adding items to
the cart.
This dataset represented far more than survey responses or focus group opinions. It cap‑
tured actual decision behavior at scale. However, data alone still did not answer the mana‑
gerial question. The challenge was to transform this data into a form that could support
concrete decisions.
Decision trees as a bridge between analytics and action
To uncover decision logic embedded in customer behavior, Dadajj’s analytics team turned
to decision trees. Unlike many statistical models that focus on averages or abstract relation‑
ships, decision trees offered something managers immediately appreciated: clear, interpret‑
able decision paths.
The resulting tree revealed a small number of dominant patterns. For example, when a
customer was logged in, had previously purchased from Dadajj, and arrived via a direct visit
or search engine, purchase completion rates were very high—even for relatively expensive
products. In contrast, first‑time visitors coming from social media platforms such as Insta‑
gram were much more likely to abandon their carts, especially when prices were perceived
as high and no prior relationship of trust existed.
These findings transformed scattered observations into a coherent marketing insight. The
issue was not simply price or traffic source in isolation, but the combination of trust, familiar‑
ity, and decision context.
From insight to strategic decision
Armed with this insight, Dadajj redesigned several elements of its digital strategy. Returning
customers were offered a faster checkout experience and personalized recommendations.

First‑time visitors arriving from social media were presented with farmer stories, certification
details, and reassurance messages earlier in the journey. For high‑priced products, transpar‑
ent explanations about sourcing and production costs were emphasized.
The results were visible within a short period. Cart abandonment decreased, conversion
rates improved, and marketing decisions became more focused and confident. Managers no
longer asked, “Why are customers leaving?” but instead asked, “Which decision path is this
customer on, and how should we respond?”
Why this story matters
Dadajj’s experience highlights a core principle of marketing analytics: insight emerges at the
intersection of research, data, and decision‑making. Focus groups helped define the prob‑
lem, surveys refined the variables, and large‑scale data analysis revealed actionable decision
rules. Decision trees played a central role—not merely as a predictive tool, but as a transpar‑
ent and intuitive decision‑support system.
What are marketing insights?
In everyday business language, marketing insight is a term that is used frequently—sometimes
too frequently. Reports, dashboards, and presentations often claim to deliver “insights,” yet
many of them merely summarize data. In practice, not every analysis produces an insight, and
not every insight requires complex analytics. What distinguishes a true marketing insight is its
ability to change the way a decision‑maker thinks and acts.
At its core, a marketing insight is a meaningful understanding of consumers, markets, or
competitive conditions that leads to a clear implication for action. It helps managers move from
observation to interpretation and from interpretation to decision. Simply knowing what hap‑
pened is rarely enough; managers need to understand why it happened and what it means for
the next step.
From data to insight: a practical perspective
To better grasp the idea of marketing insights, it is useful to think in terms of a simple progres‑
sion: data → information → knowledge → insight. Although these concepts are closely related,
they serve different purposes in managerial decision‑making.
Data are the raw materials of analysis. A list of transactions, website clicks, survey responses,
or customer ratings is data. On their own, these numbers and categories say very little. They are
necessary, but not sufficient.
When data are organized and summarized, they become information. Monthly sales reports,
conversion rates, average satisfaction scores or frequency tables help managers see what is
happening. Information answers descriptive questions, such as how much, how often, or which
option performs better.
Knowledge emerges when information is analyzed and patterns begin to appear. For exam ‑
ple, identifying that customers who arrive via social media abandon their shopping carts more
frequently than returning users represents knowledge. At this stage, relationships and regulari ‑
ties are visible, but their managerial meaning is still open to interpretation.

Insight is the final and most valuable step. An insight connects knowledge with context, experi‑
ence, and purpose. It explains why a pattern matters and how it should influence a decision. Real‑
izing that social‑media‑driven visitors abandon carts more often is knowledge; understanding that
these visitors may require trust‑building signals or simplified checkout processes is an insight. In
other words, insight answers the question every manager eventually asks: “So what?”
Why marketing insights matter for managers
Managers make decisions under pressure, with limited time and imperfect information. They
are rarely overwhelmed by a lack of data; they are overwhelmed by too much data and too lit ‑
tle clarity. Marketing insights play a critical role by reducing complexity and highlighting what
truly deserves attention.
A well‑formulated insight does not aim to describe everything. Instead, it focuses on what is
decision‑relevant. It directs attention toward key drivers, trade‑offs, and consequences. This is
why marketing insights are inherently action‑oriented. If an analysis does not influence a deci ‑
sion, it may be informative—but it is not insightful.
In marketing practice, insights support decisions across a wide range of areas, including
customer segmentation, pricing, promotion design, channel selection, and customer experience
management. They help decision‑makers prioritize alternatives, evaluate risks, and justify stra‑
tegic choices.
Marketing research, analytics, and insights: working together
Marketing insights do not appear spontaneously. They are the outcome of a structured process in
which marketing research and marketing analytics play complementary roles.
Marketing research is primarily concerned with asking the right questions. It defines the
problem, clarifies objectives, and determines which data should be collected. Marketing analyt‑
ics focuses on extracting patterns and relationships from those data using statistical and compu‑
tational techniques.
Marketing insights emerge when the results of research and analytics are interpreted within
a decision context. Analytics may reveal patterns, but insight arises when those patterns are
translated into managerial meaning. For this reason, even advanced analytical models do not
automatically generate insights; they require interpretation.
This distinction is especially important in the data‑driven era. As analytical tools become
more powerful, there is a risk of confusing model outputs with insights. In reality, insight is not
produced by algorithms alone—it is produced at the intersection of data, analysis, and human
judgment.
Marketing insights in a data‑driven era
Digitalization has dramatically expanded the scope and scale of data available to marketers.
Transaction logs, clickstream data, social media interactions, and customer‑level histories offer
unprecedented opportunities for analysis. At the same time, they increase the complexity of
decision‑making.
In this environment, marketing insights are increasingly dynamic rather than static. They
evolve as new data arrive and as market conditions change. Predictive models and machine

Marketing research as the insight engine
Marketing insights do not emerge spontaneously from data, nor are they the result of isolated
analytical techniques. Insights are produced through a structured and purposeful process in
which marketing research plays the role of a central engine. Just as an engine transforms raw
fuel into usable power, marketing research transforms data into meaningful insights that can
guide managerial decision‑making.
At its core, marketing research provides the systematic foundation required to move from
observation to understanding. Customer opinions, behaviors, preferences, and experiences may
initially appear as fragmented signals. Without a research framework, these signals remain unor‑
ganized, anecdotal, and often misleading. Marketing research brings discipline to this complex‑
ity by defining research objectives, selecting appropriate methods, and ensuring that data are
collected and analyzed in a way that supports valid and reliable insights.
From an insight perspective, marketing research does not merely answer predefined ques‑
tions such as “What do customers prefer?” or “How satisfied are they?” More importantly, it
helps uncover why customers behave in certain ways, how different factors interact, and what
these patterns imply for future actions. In this sense, research is not an end in itself but a mecha‑
nism for insight generation. The value of research lies not in the volume of data collected, but in
its ability to reduce uncertainty and support better decisions.
In modern marketing environments, this insight engine operates alongside analytical tools
and data‑driven techniques. Surveys, focus groups, experiments, and observational studies pro‑
vide structured inputs, while analytics and modeling techniques enhance the depth and speed
of insight extraction. However, regardless of technological sophistication, the role of marketing
research remains fundamental: it defines what should be measured, how it should be measured,
and how findings should be interpreted within a managerial context.
Viewing marketing research as an insight engine also clarifies its strategic importance.
Insights generated through research shape product development, pricing decisions, communica‑
tion strategies, channel design, and customer relationship management. They serve as the bridge
between data and decision‑support systems, ensuring that analytical outputs are grounded in real
market understanding rather than abstract numbers.

Why marketing problems do not have ready-made answers
Let’s remember something. Throughout our education we have taken many courses in mar‑
keting and management. The content of these courses usually presents concepts in the form
of lists: types, functions, policies, strategies, and similar categories.
Almost every topic follows the same structure. First the theory is introduced, and then
the different types are explained. For example, when psychological pricing is discussed, the
theory is presented first and then various types such as fractional pricing or quantity discount
pricing are introduced.
However, in most of these courses, we are never told which of these alternatives is the best.
This is actually natural. Nevertheless, many of us have asked the same question:
“Which one should we use? Fractional pricing, quantity discount pricing, or both? Which
one is the most correct?”
Unfortunately, there is no universal answer.
The best possible answer usually starts with “it depends.” In other words, the answer
changes according to conditions.
T oday, however, even condition‑based answers are often insufficient. Competitive envi‑
ronments are constantly changing, and every business and every market has its own unique
characteristics. Theoretical solutions therefore tend to be general, not specific.
For this reason, do not expect Yakutiye—the book you are currently reading—to provide
ready‑made answers to marketing or management problems.
In fact, no theory book can clearly explain exactly what businesses should do to solve
every marketing problem.
Think about it for a moment. Imagine a theory book that claims to explain how companies
in the copper wire industry should design their marketing strategies. Would firms in that
industry become successful simply by following what is written in that book?
Of course not.
Because such a situation would ignore one of the most fundamental principles of business:
competition. All competitors would read the same book and apply the same strategies. Yet
the essence of strategy and competition is to stay one step ahead of everyone else.
Unlike medicine or pharmacology, business sciences do not offer ready‑made treatments
for every problem.

Definition
The marketing research process begins with a need. A need requires definition. Definition can
occur in two ways. The first is to identify and define a non‑existent opportunity or problem.
The marketing researcher takes action in line with the need before the existence of the problem
is felt. He/she is prepared for possible opportunities or problems. The second is research con‑
ducted to solve an existing or identified problem. Research covering the solution of problems
related to marketing components such as pricing and distribution channels is included in this
group. Both situations require the determination of the purpose of the research to be conducted.
A problem or purpose that is not correctly determined is like a building without a foundation.
After the definitions and determination of the purpose of the research are completed, the design
stage is started (Chapter 3).
Design
At this stage, the researcher determines how to solve the defined problem or reach the goal.
Accordingly, he/she first plans his/her design and then decides on his/her method. The design of
the research is divided into two as exploratory and summative design. Exploratory design takes
place in two ways: quantitative and qualitative discovery. Secondary data sources, pilot study,
case analysis, etc., are examples of exploratory research. Similarly, summative design takes
place in two ways: descriptive and causal research. Cross‑sectional and longitudinal designs are
examples of descriptive research; experimental designs are examples of causal research. You
need to be patient a little longer for the details (Chapter 4).
Data
The next stage after the design of the research is the stage that includes processes such as
population, sample, and data collection. Since collecting data from the entire population will
bring great costs and may cause an increase in systematic error, the sample is used. The most
important expectation for researchers is that the sample has a high representative power of the
