Abstract: This study explores the role of Knowledge Management (KM) in marketing-driven innovation through a case study of
an Indonesian interior design firm’s viral marketing campaign. While the campaign successfully increased brand awareness, it
also revealed KM c hallenges, particularly in how knowledge was acquired, stored, shared, and applied throughout the
innovation process. The lack of a formal KM strategy led to disconnected collaboration, knowledge silos, and potential
knowledge loss, hindering the firm’s scalability of future marketing endeavors. An  adopting a qualitative case study design, this
study includes semi-structured interviews and internal documentation analysis for evaluating KM practices. SECI Model is used
to analyze how marketing knowledge is created, converted, and embedded, whereas the People, Process, and Technology (PPT)
model determines major enablers and inhibitors to the implementation of KM. Furthermore, NVivo 15 qualitative coding and
sentiment analysis of social media commentary was e mployed to analyze public opinion and the effect of KM on campaign
success. Evidence suggests that current knowledge -sharing processes are largely ad hoc and dependent on individual know -
how, creating difficulty in replicating future marketing strategies. In an effort to eliminate these challenges, this research
advocates for a formal KM plan built on the SECI Model through the implementation of centralized knowledge databases,
documented proces ses, and virtual collaboration software.  The proposed framework  emphasizes standardized processes for
capturing and transferring marketing insights, enhancing employee competencies, leveraging data -driven marketing analytics,
and embedding KM principles into the organizational culture.
Keywords: Knowledge Management, Marketing Innovation, SECI Model, Viral Marketing.
I. INTRODUCTION
The architecture and interior design market in Indonesia is experiencing steady growth, driven by urban expansion, rising
disposable incomes, and increasing consumer interest in well-designed living and workspaces. The global interior design market,
valued at USD 134.22 billion in 2023, is projected to grow at a 4.1% CAGR through 2030 (Grand View Research, 2023). In
Indonesia, the home décor segment alone is expected to generate USD 1.03 billion by 2025, with a 3.46% annual growth rate
(Statista, 2024). The creative economy, contributing 7.4% of GDP in 2020 (Kemenparekraf RI, 2024), has played a crucial role
in advancing the industry, particularly as post -pandemic consumer behavior has shifted toward improving home aesthetics
(CNBC Indonesia, 2022).

An Indonesian interior design firm established in 2007 has grown into a one-stop solution for space-related needs, offering
interior design, construction, and furniture craftsmanship. With dedication to quality, accuracy, and productivity, the company
has finished hundreds of residential and commercial projects, catering to a variety of clients all over Indonesia. As competi tion

One such campaign was a viral campaign and exhibition with the help of a popular social media influencer to educate the
potential market with interior design. The campaign, revolving around building a fully equipped home inside a busy shopping
mall, gained much publicity because of its aggressive timeline and innovative idea. Throughout a two-week period of exhibition,
the installation attracted over 11,000 attendees, creating high social media engagement and solidifying the brand ’s reputation as
an innovative market leader in the interior design sector. The campaign not only raised awareness of the brand but also acted as
a strategic move to inform consumers about the influence of professional interior design, establishing a benchmark for innovative
marketing strategies in the industry.

The viral marketing campaign ’s success proved the company ’s capability to create mass engagement through strategic
influencer partnerships and innovative exhibition ideas.  However, despite these achievements, the company lacked a structured
Knowledge Management (KM) strategy, relying instead on informal knowledge exchanges and individual expertise. Interviews
with key stakeholders revealed that the absence of systematic documentation and structured knowledge flows led to inefficiencies
in planning and execution. Misalignment among teams, delays in leadership structure implementation, and challenges in internal
communication resulted in operational difficulties and knowledge silos, which increased the risk of burnout and reduced the
potential for scaling future marketing innovations (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995).

The study highlights the potential knowledge loss if the company continues to depend on undocumented expertise and
individual leadership. Polanyi (1996) notes that tacit knowledge, when not externalized, becomes a liability, particularly when
key employees leave the organization. While the company’s current approach to marketing innovation has yielded positive
results, the absence of a formal KM structure makes it difficult to sustain and rep licate success in evolving market conditions.
As Davenport and Prusak (1998) argue, companies with strong KM practices are better positioned to retain institutional expertise,
enhance adaptability, and drive continuous innovation. The implementation of a s tructured KM strategy, including knowledge
repositories, standardized documentation, and cross -functional collaboration mechanisms, would improve knowledge retention
and operational efficiency.

The company operates in a highly competitive industry where maintaining a balance between creativity and operational
efficiency is crucial for sustained success. As marketing initiatives expand and collaborative efforts grow, ensuring consistency,
scalability, and replicability in campaign execution becomes increasingly important. The following gap analysis evaluates the
current state of knowledge management and outlines a structured approach to bridge existing gaps, enabling a more effective and
sustainable innovation process.

II. LITERATURE REVIEW
This study is structured using three levels of theoretical abstraction: Grand Theory, Middle -Range Theory, and Applied
Theory (Merton, 1968). The SECI Model (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995) serves as the Grand Theory, providing a framework for
understanding knowledge creation, sharing, and application. The Stage-Gate® framework (Cooper, 1990; Tidd & Bessant, 2018)
functions as a Middle -Range Theory, offering a structured process for transforming knowledge into innovative marketing
strategies. At the Applied Theory  level, the STEPPS framework (Berger, 2013) explains the mechanisms of viral marketing,
particularly in influencer-driven campaigns. These theories collectively illustrate how Knowledge Management (KM) facilitates
marketing innovation and contributes to brand awareness.

A) Knowledge management and SECI Model
KM is the key to facilitating continuous learning and innovation, making sure past campaigns ’ insights are consistently
captured and utilized (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). The SECI Model describes how knowledge flows between tacit and explicit
knowledge by means of socialization, externalization, combination, and internalization. Still, for successful KM, a common Ba—
a vibrant space for knowledge sharing —is needed (Nonaka et al., 2020). Lacking formal KM, firms are prone to knowledge
fragmentation, which will make it challenging to scale and duplicate successful marketing innovations. Senge (1990) ins isted
that a learning organization flourishes by promoting continuous sharing of knowledge between teams, while Tjakraatmadja and
Kristinawati (2017) pointed out that KM needs to be strategically aligned with business goals to deliver long -term success.

Empirical evidence also validates the contribution of KM to marketing innovation. Research has shown that effective KM
practices boost product and process innovation, making firms responsive to changing markets (Edeh et al., 2022; Al -Dmour et
al., 2020). A bdi and Senin (2015) established that formalized KM systems enhance the transfer of knowledge, speeding up
organizational learning and innovation. In addition, Antunes & Pinheiro (2020) highlighted that process -based KM improves
collaboration and effectiveness, minimizing knowledge silos. With these findings, creative industry companies need to transcend
casual knowledge sharing and implement systematic KM systems to maintain innovation and competitiveness in the market.

B) Marketing Innovation Process
A formal innovation process, like the Stage -Gate® approach, guarantees that innovative ideas are rigorously assessed,
refined, and executed (Cooper, 1990; Bröring, 2005). The approach consists of discrete stages: ideation, scoping, business ca se
development, testing, and launch, with decision gates guaranteeing strategic fit. Tidd & Bessant (2018) highlight that structured
innovation is especially applicable in marketing, where iterative feedback and adjustment are essential. Leadership is also
important si nce stewardship -based leaders promote trust, align organizations, and maximize radical innovation results
(Domínguez-Escrig et al., 2019; Nabi et al., 2023). Utilizing KM -driven innovation processes in marketing enables companies
to make repeatability, scalability, and efficiency possible.

C) Virality and Brand Awareness
Viral marketing uses online media to extend brand reach by leveraging peer -to-peer content sharing (Berger, 2013). The
STEPPS model describes why some content is more contagious than others, focusing on elements such as Social Currency,
Triggers, Emotion, Public Visibility, Practical Value, and Stories. Empirical studies indicate that emotions, especially joy and
surprise, are responsible for content virality (Satrio et al., 2020; Çakirkaya & Afşar, 2024). McKinsey & Company ’s Consumer
Decision Journey (2009) also emphasizes the impact of viral marketing on customer engagement, defining brand awareness and
purchase decisions. This is consistent with Aaker’s (2023) approach to branding, in which storytelling and emotional attachment
increase brand credibility and customer loyalty.

Influencer advertising has developed into a vital force behind viral campaigns (Kim & Kim, 2023). Chen & Ren (2024)
contend that authenticity through influencers plays a key role in influencing consumers’ engagement and strengthening electronic
word-of-mouth (eWOM). Literature continues to point out that influencers are knowledge middlemen, influencing brand
perceptions and passing on information between companies and consumers (Ingrassia et al., 2020). Yet, partnerships come with
risks, such as misaligned goals, reputational risk, and governance problems (Tirmizi et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2023). Successful
influencer partnerships call for strong brand and influencer identity alignment (Chang & Wu, 2024) and strong governance
mechanisms to drive strategic co herence (Chesbrough, 2003). As influencer marketing evolves further, brands have to balance
creativity, authenticity, and risk management to achieve maximum impact.

The model is structured into three elements: Input, Process, and Output, mirroring the movement of knowledge and

III. METHODOLOGY
This research employs the qualitative case study method to inquire into the capacity of Knowledge Management (KM) to
facilitate marketing innovation. The case study strategy is especially helpful in the research of intricate context -dependent
processes whereby a thorough observation of knowledge construction, dissemination, and utilization becomes possible (Yin,
2018). To ensure study reliability, principles of action research (Reason & Bradbury, 2008) are built into the methodology, with
planning, acting, observing, and reflecting in circular iterative cycles being the focus. The study is crafted to pick up on internal
knowledge flows as well as external market reactions, and it will guarantee an inclusive view of the effects of KM on marketing
innovation. For confidentiality reasons, all identifiers of the company and the campaign are anonymized and instead target
generalizable industry insights.

A multi -method data collection approach is utilized, integrating semi -structured interviews, document analysis, and
sentiment analysis. Primary data is collected from interviews with principal stakeholders, obtaining views on KM practices,
campaign implementation, and collaboration dynamics (Sugiyono, 2019). Secondary data comprise campaign documents, media

IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A) The Role of Knowledge Management in Driving Innovation in a Viral Marketing Campaign
Knowledge Management (KM) is essential in catalyzing innovation, allowing companies to capture, share, and utilize
knowledge on a systematic basis (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). Through this research, the role of KM in a large -scale campaign
is explored as it converted a grand idea into a successful initiative. Utilizing the SECI Model (Nonaka & Toyama, 2003), the
research demonstrates how tacit and explicit knowledge are engaged at varying stages of the campaign. NVivo qualitative coding
analysis also uncovered trends in knowledge-sharing, decision-making, and implementation, indicating that although KM was a
prime facilitator, communication gaps, unwritten procedures, and dependence on leadership -based knowledge flows were
constraints in scalability.

The SECI model gives a systematic framework for comprehending how knowledge is generated, distributed, and used in
an organization. In the context of this campaign, each phase—Socialization, Externalization, Combination, and Internalization—
played a distinct role in shaping the innovation process. The figure below summarizes the strengths and areas for improvement
at each stage based on the research’s findings, which highlights how knowledge was transferred and where gaps in
documentation, communication, and institutionalization were identified.

The socialization stage of KM was at the heart of the campaign's conceptualization process, where casual conversation s
among the decision -makers and outside partners resulted in the creation of the fundamental idea. These unstructured
conversations, though successful in generating innovation, were not properly documented, so it was hard to retain and repeat
ideas for future projects. Word cloud analysis of interviews brought out prominent words associated with leadership, marketing,
and implementation, underlining the prime role of informal discussions in the campaign's direction. Yet, without a systematic
knowledge-sharing process, the ideas from these discussions were not rigorously captured.

In externalization, tacit knowledge was converted into tangible strategies, such as branding, influencer activity, and
content creation plans. Although this stage formatted creative ideas into an executable framework, communication dysfunction
among teams and external collaborators persisted as a prevailing issue. Word tree analysis across "communication" showed that
communication problems like conflicting deadlines, uncertainty in expectations, and coordination mismatches hindered
implementation. Interview  responses suggested dependency on casual , informal communication resulted in inconsistencies,
echoing the importance of centralized documentation facilities and more assertive project management tools to advance
coordination.

The integration phase was key to merging marketing strategies, operational processes, and design execution. Although
leadership effectively led knowledge-sharing initiatives, matrix coding questions revealed that the majority of strategic decisions
were at the top levels. This top -down flow of knowledge ensured effectiveness but also created bottlenecks, where mid -level
staff had limited access to key insights. The absence of a systematic, decentralized KM structure ensured that knowledge was
stored in leadership rather than being equally dispersed among teams, ensuring that innovation was more a function of individual
skill than organizational knowledge.

During the internalization stage, the emphasis moved towards integrating knowledge into organizational procedures. Yet,
the interview analysis indicated that learning after projects was not systematized, with the majority of lessons being tacit in
people. Staff used individual experience and makeshift memory instead of formal debriefing or documentation. This absence of
formal learning mechanisms heightened the danger of knowledge loss as a result of employee turnover. Creating post-campaign
reviews, internal training sessions, and formalized KM repositories would mitigate this problem by making key takeaways
systematically recorded and available for future projects.

The results also identify a lack of alignment between strategic intent and action, much as a result of communication and
knowledge-sharing inefficiencies among various teams. Leadership dominated KM facilitation, but their overinvolvement in
problem-solving and decision -making created knowledge silos, with the insights being hoarded by a select few and not
disseminated throughout the organization. In addition, technology was not effectively leveraged as a KM enabler, and teams used
unstructured channels lik e chat tools and oral directions, which added to misalignment. Leveraging digital collaboration
platforms, real -time monitoring systems, and formal knowledge -sharing tools would improve efficiency and scalability
considerably.

Overall, although KM facilitated the campaign's success, the research outlines areas of improvement, including
documentation, decentralized sharing, and digital integration. In order to continue and expand on future marketing innovation s,
organizations nee d to shift away from a leader -reliant KM model and towards a more formalized, technology -based one.
Institutionalizing KM best practices will allow companies to improve collaboration, enhance efficiency in execution, and crea te
long-term organizational learning so that innovation efforts are not only successful but also repeatable and scalable.

B) Key Components of KM for Innovation: People, Process, and Technology
The people's role in Knowledge Management (KM) was central to spearheading the innovation process throughout the
campaign. Top-level leadership dominated knowledge creation and dissemination, with most decision -making among principal
players in operations and marketing. Although the top -down flow of knowledge facilitated strategic alignment, it also created
knowledge diffusion bottlenecks that restricted access to important insights by operational teams. The use of informal modes of
communication also added to knowledge transfer gaps, resulting in inefficiencies in implementation and improvisation.

While the leadership was involved in KM, operational and mid-level teams had very few references to formal KM

Although integration of knowledge was high during implementation, post-project learning was comparatively weak. The

The success of the campaign was heavily dependent on how Knowledge Management (KM) supported innovation,
virality, and brand popularity. With organized internal knowledge-sharing and external partnerships, the campaign was run cost-
effectively. Nevertheless, whereas KM organized internal coordination, its support for forecasting public opinion and external
attitudes was not as formalized. NVivo -coded interviews, social media sentiment analysis, and word cloud frequency analysis
combined to offer insights on how KM affected the marketing influence of the campaign.

A strengths and challenges analysis also highlights the strengths and weaknesses of KM in this campaign. On the positive

anticipated criticisms.

The results indicate that although KM had a huge impact in facilitating innovation and cooperation, it has the potential to
improve its strategic implementation through the use of external audience input. Organizations would be advised in the future to
incorporate formal sentiment analysis within KM systems to better anticipate issues of concern in their audiences and better tailor
marketing campaigns as a result. In this way, future projects will be able to draw on internal and external expertise to deve lop
more universal and acceptable marketing innovations.

D) Strengthening KM for Future Marketing Innovation
The implications of the campaign analysis show drastic knowledge gaps in the generation, sharing, and storage of
knowledge in the innovation process of the company. The campaign was effective in applying tacit knowledge exchange through
ad hoc collaboratio n, yet the lack of formal documentation and standardized processes challenges the long-term retention and
reproducibility of the knowledge. Real -time decision -making and leader -to-leader dissemination of knowledge introduced
operational stress with difficulty in identifying and reusing key learnings for subsequent marketing innovations.

A treemap of interview responses indicated that the majority of conversations were about execution, defining success, and
viral marketing strategy, with comparatively little focus on formal documentation of knowledge. This indicates that although
innovation was eff ectively carried out, it was not formally documented, making it more difficult to hone and replicate similar
tactics in future campaigns. Moreover, an independent analysis of challenges identified operational bottlenecks, crisis
management problems, and mental fatigue, suggesting that the pace of the campaign put pressure on teams to make impromptu
decisions without defined guidelines.

One of the most significant issues was the limited use of the SECI model's Combination and Internalization stages. While
Socialization and Externalization were present during the idea generation and implementation phases, the knowledge was not

uniformly converted into overt frameworks or merged into formalized learning mechanisms. This resulted in dependence on
individuals and external partners, which developed knowledge silos. Without proper documentation or a centralized KM store,
the organization stands to lose valuable insights that can be leveraged to develop future marketing strategies.

In order to make it sustainable, the firm needs to reinforce KM through formal documentation of knowledge, the inclusion
of structured workflows, and the adoption of digital collaboration tools. An organized KM structure would enable future
campaigns to learn from previous achievements more effectively and with more strategic precision. This includes not only

A systematic Knowledge Management (KM) process is necessary so that future marketing campaigns can mimic and
build on previous successful initiatives. This involves keeping KM aligned with business objectives, determining key knowledge
areas, and further i mplementing the SECI Model application to facilitate a more systematic process of knowledge creation,
sharing, and storing. The main goals of the company are to enhance brand recognition, customer engagement, and brand

workflows that span the entire campaign process from ideation through execution and post -campaign analysis. The company
also needs to prioritize knowledge transfer mechan isms to avoid dependence on individual experience and allow insights to
remain available across teams.

Another essential area is crisis management and the agility of implementation, which was the defining factor in the success
of the campaign. While agility enabled the team to respond rapidly, the lack of preformulated contingency plans and formal
decision-making structures resulted in operational inefficiencies. Having a knowledge repository of previous problems and
solutions would enable future teams to develop a better-formalised method for managing uncertainties.

E) Knowledge Management Initiatives to Improve Existing System
To strengthen KM in future marketing innovations, the company can adopt a structured approach based on the SECI
Model. This would ensure that tacit and explicit knowledge are systematically captured, shared, and applied to support
innovation. The following initiatives align with each phase of the SECI Model:

1. Socialization (Tacit to Tacit Knowledge) – “The Creative Roundtable”
The campaign’s initial concept emerged through informal discussions, highlighting the importance of open knowledge
exchange. However, insights were not consistently documented, making it difficult to refine or revisit early ideas. To
address this, the company can introduce The Creative Roundtable, a structured but informal brainstorming platform where
designers, marketers, and external collaborators engage in regular discussions to generate and refine creative concepts.
2. Externalization (Tacit to Explicit Knowledge) – “The House Files”
As the campaign evolved, ideas had to be translated into structured marketing strategies, but much of this process was not
systematically documented.  In order t o bridge this gap, The House Files initiative would serve as a centralized digital
repository where team members could store and access strategic documents, execution plans, and influencer engagement
insights. This would preserve institutional knowledge and ensure that valuable learning remain s available for future
campaigns.
3. Combination (Explicit to Explicit Knowledge) – “The KM Playbook”
The execution phase required integrating multiple knowledge streams, from design and marketing to influencer
collaboration and branding. However, the reliance on individual expertise led to inconsistencies in knowledge transfer.
The LD Playbook would serve  as a comprehensive guide, consolidating best practices, workflows, and case studies to
ensure consistency in future marketing innovations.
4. Internalization (Explicit to Tacit Knowledge) – “Legacy Sessions”
The most valuable lessons from the campaign were learned through firsthand experiences, yet much of this knowledge
remained undocumented. To institutionalize knowledge transfer, the company can introduce LD Legacy Sessions, where
senior team members share real-world experiences and problem -solving insights with new employees. These sessions
would ensure that critical learnings are passed down, preventing knowledge loss as key personnel transition in and out of
projects.

One of the underlying issues concerning KM is the limited use of digital instruments for capturing and distributing codified
explicit knowledge. The firm has the potential to increase knowledge accessibility and improve real-time transfer of information
if collaborative KM platforms, analytic tools with artificial intelligence, and automated knowledge extraction systems are
deployed. These were not possible before because teams lacked access to structured information repositories and automated
documentation systems, so insights could only be shared verbally.

V. CONCLUSION
The research discovers that Knowledge Management (KM) is key to making marketing innovation possible by making
knowledge creation, sharing, and utilization possible. Informal means of knowledge sharing, including leadership -initiated
collaboration and exte rnal collaboration, were able to effectively assist in campaign ideation and execution, but a lack of
systematic KM frameworks restricted scalability and effectiveness. The dependence on tacit knowledge sharing posed challenges
in knowledge retention since  important ideas were not always documented for future application. For greater marketing
innovation, a more formal KM strategy is required, combining systematic documentation, internal learning sessions, and digita l
tools to make knowledge accessible acro ss teams. If the company puts these mechanisms in place, future marketing efforts can
be replicated and improved upon more effectively, facilitating long-term business growth.

The findings also indicate that KM significantly influenced the campaign’s ability to achieve virality, particularly through
socialization and external collaboration. Influencer engagement and real-time audience interactions played a key role in shaping
public perception and amplifying campaign reach. However, a lack of structured KM processes to analyze audience sentiment
and refine messaging strategies resulted in missed opportunities to anticipate public concerns. Integrating predictive sentim ent
analysis, real -time feedback mechanisms, and systematic audience research into KM frameworks would enhance campaign
adaptability and responsiveness.

From the research, it is also implied that KM impacted brand awareness by shaping how the campaign’s core message
was communicated and retained within the organization. While the campaign successfully positioned the company as an
innovative market player, the absence of formal post -campaign documentation limited long -term brand -building efforts.
Knowledge was largely retained within leadership, making it difficult to institutionalize learning across teams for continuou s
improvement. Establishing structured knowledge repositories, internal branding guidelines, and cross -functional knowledge -
sharing initiatives would ensure that branding strategies evolve based on past experiences. Thus, by embedding KM within
branding efforts, the company can develop future marketing strategies that can be more consistent, scalable, and aligned with
long-term business positioning.

In order to strengthen Knowledge Management (KM) and ensure long-term sustainability in innovation, a more structured
approach is needed. The company should establish a dedicated KM team or appoint a Knowledge Management Officer (KMO)
to oversee knowledge documentation, sharing, and application across teams. This role would ensure that valuable insights from
marketing campaigns are systematically captured, preventing reliance on informal knowledge -sharing. Additionally, integrating
KM into the company’s ERP system would provide a centralized knowledge repository, allowing teams to seamlessly document
and retrieve campaign strategies, decision-making processes, and key learnings for future use.

Beyond infrastructure, structured knowledge -sharing sessions and digital repositories are essential to foster a learning
culture. Post-campaign debriefs, mentorship programs and cross-team collaboration forums should be implemented to formalize
knowledge exchange. Creating an internal digital knowledge hub, such as an intranet or Notion -based system, would ensure
accessibility to case studies, marketing playbooks, and best practices. This would prevent knowledge silos and ensure that critical
insights remai n accessible even as teams evolve. Additionally, standardized marketing innovation documentation, including
templates and checklists for decision -making, challenges, and success factors, would improve scalability and consistency in
campaign execution.

Developing structured training programs on KM and innovation management is also crucial. Employees should be trained
in KM methodologies, such as the SECI Model, project documentation techniques, and digital knowledge-sharing tools. Ongoing
training and refresher sessions would reinforce best practices, ensuring KM becomes a natural part of everyday work. Moreover,
leveraging data analytics and sentiment analysis for real -time audience insights would enhance KM effectiveness. By
systematically tracking audience reactions and campaign performance, the company can refine future marketing strategies based
on tangible insights rather than assumptions.

Finally, stronger collaboration between HR, marketing, and innovation teams is necessary to institutionalize KM across
the organization. HR should integrate KM practices into employee development programs, leadership training, and performance
evaluation, e nsuring that knowledge -sharing becomes an ingrained part of the company culture. Recognizing and rewarding
employees for contributing to the organization’s knowledge base would encourage continuous learning and innovation.
Therefore, through integrating KM  into broader business strategies, the company can ensure sustained competitive advantage
through structured, data-driven, and collaborative innovation processes.
