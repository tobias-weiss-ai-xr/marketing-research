Introduction
Sustainability has moved from the margins of
corporate communication to the core of competition. Firms
increasingly promise ethical sourcing, low-carbon operations,
community value creation, and responsible employment
practices, and these promises are now embedded in
positioning statements, employer branding, investor
narratives, and customer -facing communications. At the
same time, strategic marketing itself has become more
analytical. Contemporary marketing planning depends on
market sensing, segmentation, targeting, resource allocation,
and performance control systems that are expected to justify
decisions with evidence rather than intuition [1 -3]. This dual
transition creates an important managerial challenge.

Abstract
This paper develops a governance-based explanation of how strategic marketing planning and marketing analytics can
make sustainability positioning more credible when corporate purpose is legally embedded in the firm’s governing
architecture. The study addresses  a cross -disciplinary gap: marketing analytics research explains how firms sense
markets, allocate resources, and monitor performance, while stakeholder governance scholarship explains why mission
protection matters, yet neither tradition adequately specifies how legal purpose commitments become measurable brand-
trust mechanisms. Using a theory -building, secondary qualitative design, the paper reinterprets published interview -
based evidence on 20 certified B Corporation leaders in Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand together with official B Lab
legal requirement documents. The analysis applies abductive thematic synthesis to connect constitutional purpose
clauses and stakeholder clauses with strategic planning routines, signal design, and outcome measurement. The findings
identify six purpose -enabled roles—Establishing, Applying, Developing, Announcing, Demonstrating, and Sharing —
through which legally embedded purpose moves from governance text to market performance. The paper further
develops a strategic mark eting analytics architecture showing how these roles can be translated into measurable
indicators, including trust conversion, stakeholder -fit, mission -consistent acquisition, complaint quality, partner
alignment, and reputational resilience. The study con tributes by positioning governance design as a market -facing
capability rather than a background compliance issue, by integrating signaling theory with marketing control systems,
and by showing that legal anchoring strengthens sustainability claims when planning routines and analytics visibly align
with purpose. The paper concludes that sustainable brand trust is most durable when strategic planning, analytics, and
stakeholder governance operate as one coordinated system rather than as separate legal, marke ting, and reporting
functions.

Managers are under pressure to communicate m eaningful
sustainability commitments, but they are also under pressure
to prove that those commitments are operationally real,
strategically prioritized, and durable under financial strain.
In many organizations, those two demands are not yet
reconciled [4-6]. The credibility problem is especially acute in
sustainability markets. Stakeholder theory and purpose
scholarship suggest that firms can create value for customers,
employees, communities, and investors when they organize
around a broader concept of v alue creation [7, 8]. Yet
signaling theory warns that market actors discount signals
that are easy to imitate or cheap to reverse [9]. This tension is
visible in current debates about stakeholder governance.
Advocates of purpose -led business argue that fir ms need
mission commitments that survive leadership change, capital
raising, and short -term performance shocks. Critics argue
that stakeholder governance can become indeterminate,
managerial, and weakly accountable when firms claim to
serve everyone withou t specifying how those claims will
affect real decisions [10]. Marketing consequences follow
directly from this governance debate. If sustainability
commitments remain discretionary, stakeholders may view
them as soft claims, temporary campaigns, or reputa tional
theater. If, however, purpose is embedded in formal
governance instruments and repeatedly translated into
market behavior, the same commitments may become
stronger brand signals and more durable sources of trust [11-
15]. Recent developments in B Corporation governance make
this issue especially salient. B Lab’s legal requirement
framework requires certified B Corporations to adopt
governance provisions that move decision -making beyond
shareholder primacy and toward stakeholder governance
[16]. In Aot earoa New Zealand, for example, certified firms
must insert a purpose clause and a stakeholder clause into
their governing documents, requiring directors to consider
the long-term consequences of decisions, employee interests,
relationships with suppliers and customers, community and
environmental impact, reputational standards, shareholder
interests, and the organization’s overall positive impact on
society and the environment, without necessarily giving one
factor priority over another [17]. This architec ture is not
merely symbolic. It changes the formal structure within
which the firm plans, communicates, and defends strategic
decisions. Empirical evidence from certified B Corps in
Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand shows that such legally
embedded purpos e can shape how leaders select
stakeholders, resolve tensions, and signal identity to external
audiences [18]. What remains insufficiently explained is how
these governance commitments integrate with strategic
marketing planning and analytics. Marketing st rategy
research has long emphasized the importance of choices
about market offerings, target segments, value propositions,
and implementation systems [19]. Marketing analytics
research shows how firms convert customer, channel, and
performance data into decisions about acquisition, retention,
pricing, and resource allocation [20, 21].
But the dominant analytics conversation often
focuses on efficiency, personalization, conversion, and
productivity, while treating governance as exogenous.
Conversely, stakeho lder governance and corporate purpose
scholarship richly discusses legitimacy, mission protection,
and organizational identity, but tends to stop short of
specifying the planning dashboards, measurement logic, and
control routines that turn purpose into a monitorable
marketing system [22 -24, 28]. The result is a structural blind
spot: firms may have data -rich marketing systems but weak
purpose discipline, or strong purpose rhetoric but no
analytics architecture that shows whether the market actually
experiences that purpose as trustworthy [29]. This paper
addresses that blind spot by developing a theory -building
framework that connects strategic marketing planning,
marketing analytics, and legally embedded purpose.
Drawing on official B Lab legal materials a nd the qualitative
evidence reported by Klettner, Cetindamar, and Sainty  the
paper explains how legal purpose commitments can be
transformed into measurable trust mechanisms through six
purpose-enabled roles: Establishing, Applying, Developing,
Announcing, Demonstrating, and Sharing [30]. These roles
link internal governance arrangements to strategic planning
choices, signal credibility, stakeholder self -selection, and
sustainability outcomes. The study therefore asks four
research questions. RQ1: How does  legally embedded
purpose reshape strategic marketing planning? RQ2:
Through what routines does purpose governance influence
market-facing signaling and brand trust? RQ3: What role can
marketing analytics play in converting purpose commitments
into observa ble performance controls? RQ4: What
sustainability and relationship outcomes become more likely
when planning, analytics, and stakeholder governance are
aligned? The paper makes four contributions. First, it extends
strategic marketing theory by treating g overnance design as
an antecedent of market strategy execution rather than as a
background legal condition. Second, it extends signaling and
brand authenticity theory by showing why constitutional
purpose commitments can function as high -credibility
signals only when coupled with visible routines and analytics
[31-33]. Third, it contributes to stakeholder governance
scholarship by providing a process account of how formally
embedded purpose can reduce mission drift, improve
stakeholder-fit, and support disciplined decision-making [34-

outcome [37].

Literature Review
Strategic marketing planning as a governance -sensitive
process
Strategic marketing is concerned with how firms
choose markets, define value propositions, allocate scarce
resources, and build positions that are defendable over time
[38]. The field is not limited to promotional planning; it
encompasses decisions about w here to compete, how to
compete, and how to align organizational capabilities with
changing market structures. Varadarajan  frames strategic
marketing as a domain that joins market intelligence,
segmentation, targeting, positioning, and implementation
choices into a coherent logic of competitive action [39]. This
tradition is important for the current study because it treats
planning as a cross -functional problem rather than a
communication exercise. Sustainability promises become
strategically meaningful o nly when they affect resource
allocation, capability development, and criteria for selecting
customers, partners, and channels [40 -44]. Marketing
analytics deepens this logic by providing techniques for
converting data into planning and control systems. In  data-
rich environments, firms can monitor customer journeys,
predict churn, estimate conversion lift, evaluate campaign
effectiveness, and optimize channel investments with
increasingly fine -grained precision [45]. The promise of
marketing analytics is no t simply better reporting but better
strategic choice. Rust et al. argue that marketing productivity
depends on systems that connect marketing actions to firm -
level outcomes, making accountability and learning central to
performance [46]. However, the dominant analytics literature
has historically privileged economic efficiency, behavioral
prediction, and return -on-investment logic. Metrics such as
click-through, acquisition cost, retention, lifetime value, and
attribution quality are indispensable, but the y do not
necessarily reveal whether the firm’s value proposition is
institutionally credible, normatively coherent, or protected
against opportunistic reversal. This is a critical omission in
sustainability contexts where trust depends not only on what
firms say and measure, but also on what they are formally
committed to doing [47-50]. Accordingly, strategic marketing
planning should be understood as a multi -stage process
involving market sensing, objective formation, target
selection, positioning, impleme ntation, and control. The
question raised by purpose -led business is how legally
embedded commitments enter that process. In conventional
planning models, governance often appears only as a
boundary condition or approval layer. Yet in sustainability -
sensitive markets, governance can shape the very feasibility
of positioning choices. A firm that has constitutionally
committed itself to stakeholder consideration is not planning
from the same baseline as a firm that can abandon social
commitments at minimal cost. This suggests that governance
architecture belongs inside the planning model rather than
outside it [51 -55]. A second implication for strategic
marketing planning concerns control and learning.
Traditional annual plans often separate strategy formulati on
from performance review, with metrics appearing late in the
process as monitoring tools. Purpose-led planning requires a
different logic: the metrics must be anticipated during
strategy formulation because the credibility of the value
proposition depend s on what can later be shown. For
example, if a firm wishes to position itself as a responsible
employer or supplier partner, it should identify ex ante which
indicators will evidence that claim and which governance
processes will produce those indicators.  This moves
marketing planning closer to a design-for-verifiability model.
Rather than asking only whether the promise is appealing,
managers must ask whether the promise is governable,
measurable, and explainable if challenged by skeptical
stakeholders. S uch a perspective broadens the meaning of
marketing control from a post hoc performance check to an
ex ante discipline of claim architecture [56-60].
The analytics challenge becomes more pronounced
as firms adopt increasingly granular customer data systems .
Advanced analytics can optimize acquisition and
personalization, but they can also intensify skepticism when
stakeholders observe a mismatch between sophisticated
persuasion and weak moral substance. A brand that can
hyper-target sustainability -conscious consumers without
substantively changing governance may achieve short -term
conversion but increase long -term distrust once
inconsistencies become visible. Hence, analytics is not
inherently credibility enhancing. Its strategic value depends
on whether it is used to deepen institutional alignment or
merely to sharpen message efficiency. This distinction is
central to the current framework. Analytics should not be
treated as a neutral technical layer; it is part of the moral and
strategic infrastructure of m arket action. Firms that use
analytics to make purpose more observable strengthen trust,
whereas firms that use analytics only to market purpose more
aggressively may intensify perceptions of manipulation [61 -
65]. The strategic marketing implications are p articularly

strong in segmentation and targeting. Conventional
segmentation seeks groups that differ in needs,
responsiveness, or profitability. Purpose -led segmentation
adds a different question: which stakeholder groups are likely
to interpret and reward  the firm’s commitments, and which
groups may systematically undermine them? This does not
imply ideological exclusion for its own sake. Rather, it
recognizes that mission consistency is itself a strategic
resource. Firms whose purpose depends on responsib le
production, circularity, or social inclusion may need
customer and partner portfolios that reinforce those
commitments. Analytics can support such segmentation by
identifying the relationship between values -fit and
downstream outcomes such as retention,  complaint severity,
referral quality, or channel conflict. In this sense, purpose is
not only a moral guide but a segmentation variable [66-70]. A
similar logic applies to resource allocation. Marketing
budgets are often evaluated according to expected sa les lift,
but governance -backed strategy requires a broader
investment lens. Spending on supplier audits, employee
training, traceability systems, stakeholder advisory forums,
and complaint -resolution quality may not always show
immediate promotional retur ns, yet such spending can
increase the credibility of the brand promise and reduce long-
run reputation risk. The present framework therefore
suggests that some expenditures traditionally categorized as
operational or compliance costs should be partially
reinterpreted as trust -building marketing investments. This
does not mean that every internal process is a marketing
expense; it means that in sustainability -sensitive markets,
certain governance and assurance mechanisms directly
contribute to brand equity b ecause they protect the truth -
value of the market claim [71-75].

Sustainability marketing, skepticism, and credibility
Sustainability marketing seeks to align value
creation with environmental and social responsibility, but the
field has been repeatedly c hallenged by the problem of
greenwashing and broader credibility deficits [76 -78].
Stakeholders often face severe information asymmetry
regarding whether sustainability claims are substantive or
symbolic. Consumers may encounter carbon -neutral labels,
ethical sourcing narratives, or impact reporting, but remain
unable to evaluate the depth, durability, and enforceability of
those claims. Rahman and Nguyen -Viet argue that green
marketing effectiveness is shaped not only by message
content but by how consumer s interpret the sincerity and
consistency of those claims [79]. Riva et al. similarly show that
brand trust in sustainability contexts is filtered through
skepticism; positive associations must be sufficiently strong
and consistent to overcome suspicion [3 0]. This is where
communication strategy becomes intertwined with
governance credibility. Morsing and Schultz distinguish
between information, response, and involvement strategies in
corporate social responsibility communication, showing that
stakeholder r elationships deepen when communication
moves beyond one -way declaration [80]. Yet even
involvement-oriented communication can fail if stakeholders
suspect that the organization’s commitments are reversible.
Vredenburg et al. sharpen this point through the language of
“woke washing”: marketing communications that take a
moral stance but are not matched by practice can damage
rather than strengthen brand meaning [81]. The implication is
that credibility does not derive from message frequency or
moral vocabula ry alone; it derives from the perceived
congruence between organizational claims, decisions, and
structures. For strategic marketers, the lesson is clear.
Sustainability positioning cannot be treated as a layer added
after core business decisions have been  made. Instead, the
planning process itself must determine which promises are
strategically supportable, what evidence can validate them,
and what governance protections make them durable. In this
sense, credibility is not only a communication outcome; it is a
design property of the firm [82-84].

Corporate purpose and stakeholder governance
Corporate purpose provides a strategic and ethical
vocabulary for explaining why a firm exists beyond short -
term profit extraction. Freeman’s stakeholder approach  laid
the foundation for understanding the firm as a nexus of value
creation for multiple constituencies rather than a vehicle
serving shareholders alone[85]. Donaldson and Preston
subsequently clarified the descriptive, instrumental, and
normative foundations  of stakeholder theory, showing that
firms are embedded in reciprocal relationships that exceed
narrow contractual models [86]. More recent purpose
scholarship has sought to move from broad stakeholder
sensitivity to more concrete definitions of organizati onal
reason-for-being. Henderson and Van den Steen explain that
purpose can strengthen identity and reputation by aligning
internal and external expectations [87]. Mayer advances a
similarly expansive view, presenting purpose as profitable
problem solving that avoids harm to people and planet [89].
Although purpose has become a prominent managerial
concept, operationalization remains difficult. Bhattacharya et
al. show that clearly articulated corporate purpose can
influence employee sustainability behavior s, indicating that
purpose is not merely rhetorical but behaviorally
consequential [90]. Yet the same literature also suggests that
purpose requires organizational reinforcement to survive

trade-offs, ambiguity, and internal disagreement. Levillain
and Segrestin  argue that movement from primacy to purpose
commitment depends on institutional forms capable of
making purpose public and durable [91]. Without such forms,
purpose risks becoming a flexible narrative that can be
amplified when useful and softened when costly. This
challenge is central to marketing strategy. Purpose often
enters market discourse as identity, story, or promise. But
unless it is connected to decision rights, performance metrics,
and escalation rules, it remains vulnerable to the charg e of
symbolic management. For this reason, the present paper
treats purpose not as a slogan but as a governing principle
that should influence planning assumptions, acceptable
customer profiles, supplier selection, innovation priorities,
and the definition  of marketing success. In other words,
purpose must be made operational before it can become
strategically credible [92 -94]. Another important literature
stream concerns accountability and materiality.
Sustainability marketing often fails because firms
communicate across a wide range of issues without clarifying
which commitments are truly material to their strategy,
stakeholders, and governance system. Purpose governance
can improve materiality discipline by forcing management to
articulate which stakeholder outcomes are sufficiently central
to be embedded in constitutions, policies, and investment
logic. This matters for brand strategy because overly broad
sustainability narratives can dilute meaning and make
verification difficult. By contrast, a clearly bounded purpose
allows the firm to define a more coherent trust proposition.
Strategic marketing planning therefore benefits when
purpose is specific enough to guide market choice and broad
enough to support long -term legitimacy. The resulting
positioning is more defensible because it is anchored in
prioritization rather than moral over-claiming [95-99].
Stakeholder governance asks whether and how
directors and senior managers can legitimately consider the
interests of multiple stakeholders in directing the  firm. The
question is theoretically difficult because multi -stakeholder
commitments can generate conflicts over goals,
accountability, and decision criteria. Amis et al. describe the
need for a theory of stakeholder governance precisely
because existing governance models struggle to explain how
firms can organize around joint value creation [100]. Bridoux
and Stoelhorst  respond by showing that stakeholder
governance can  be understood as a mechanism for solving
collective action problems in value creation systems [101].
Their account is particularly useful for marketing because it
highlights how stakeholder cooperation depends on
coordination, fairness, and credible commi tment. Yet
skepticism remains influential. Bebchuk and Tallarita argue
that stakeholder governance can be illusory when managers
invoke broad stakeholder language without meaningful
accountability [102]. Keay’s work on enlightened shareholder
value  also d emonstrates that the legal system frequently
permits some stakeholder sensitivity while still retaining
shareholder-oriented interpretive logic [103]. Thus, from a
market perspective, not all stakeholder claims are equal.
Some are weakly binding and easily  abandoned; others are
institutionalized in ways that materially alter decision
environments. For branding and reputation, this difference
matters. Stakeholders form judgments not just about what the
firm values, but about whether those values are likely t o
govern behavior under pressure. Mission drift scholarship
reinforces this argument. Ebrahim, Battilana, and Mair  show
that hybrid organizations face acute governance challenges
because social and commercial goals can pull in different
directions [104]. Battilana and Dorado similarly demonstrate
the organizational difficulty of building hybrids that integrate
competing logics without fragmentation [105]. These insights
help explain why legally embedded purpose is strategically
interesting: it may reduce a mbiguity by making stakeholder
commitments part of the constitutional baseline from which
other decisions are made. Rather than hoping that leaders will
remember purpose during difficult trade -offs, the firm can
structure those trade-offs in advance.

Signaling, Authenticity, and Legally Embedded Purpose
Signaling theory provides the most direct bridge
between governance and market interpretation. A signal is
credible when it helps external audiences infer an
unobservable quality and when it is sufficiently  costly,
constraining, or difficult to imitate [106]. In branding
contexts, the unobservable quality may be commitment,
integrity, identity consistency, or future reliability. Many
sustainability signals are weak because they rely on voluntary
disclosure, selective storytelling, or campaign -based
messaging. These can be replicated by firms that do not
materially change their behavior. Governance-backed signals
are different. When a firm amends its constitution,
incorporates stakeholder obligations, or adopt s a legal form
that constrains directors, it incurs political, organizational,
and interpretive costs [10 -110]. Such changes may affect
capital discussions, board deliberations, acquisition logic,
and future strategic flexibility. From the standpoint of si gnal
theory, these costs matter because they raise the expected cost
of opportunism. If sustainability claims are anchored in
mechanisms that are not easy to reverse, stakeholders may
rationally assign them greater credibility. Brand authenticity
research clarifies how such signals become psychologically
meaningful. Morhart et al . conceptualize authenticity as a

multidimensional perception involving continuity,
credibility, integrity, and symbolism [111]. In sustainability
contexts, these dimensions are sha ped by whether the brand
is seen as acting in line with enduring commitments rather
than episodic activism or fashionable rhetoric. Keller’s brand
equity framework is also relevant: strong brand meaning
arises when consumers build favorable, strong, and un ique
associations [112]. Governance -backed purpose can help
create such associations because it strengthens the link
between what the brand says and what stakeholders expect
the organization to do in the future. The user’s prior work on
brand image is cons istent with this interpretation. Latif and
Latif et al . show that brand -related outcomes in service
environments depend on coherent value signaling and
relationship experience [113, 114].
Extending that logic to sustainability markets
suggests that brand t rust is not reducible to symbolic green
cues. It is better explained as an emergent judgment that the
firm’s promises are consistent with organizational identity,
experience, and decision behavior. Legally embedded
purpose can therefore be interpreted as a n authenticity
infrastructure. Business law is often treated as a compliance
constraint on marketing, but it can also operate as
infrastructure for marketing credibility. B Lab explicitly
frames the legal requirement for Certified B Corporations as
a way t o ensure that companies remain accountable to all
stakeholders and continue to practice stakeholder
governance even after leadership changes or capital events
[115]. The New Zealand legal requirement specifies not only
a purpose clause but a stakeholder co nsideration clause that
directs directors to weigh long -term consequences,
employees, supply -chain relationships, community and
environmental impacts, reputational standards, shareholder
interests, and overall positive impact [116]. These provisions
translate abstract stakeholder commitments into formal
decision rules. The strategic significance of this framework is
heightened in jurisdictions where benefit corporation statutes
are absent or limited. Under such conditions, constitutional
amendments and simi lar governing document changes can
function as private -ordering substitutes [117 -120]. The firm
effectively writes mission protection into its own operating
architecture. This helps explain why B Corps offer a valuable
empirical context for studying the re lationship between law
and marketing. They allow researchers to observe how
governance texts may influence stakeholder attraction,
selection, onboarding, collaboration, and advocacy in a real
organizational setting. From a planning perspective, legally
embedded purpose does at least three things. First, it narrows
the range of strategically acceptable actions by requiring
managers to consider stakeholder consequences. Second, it
widens the informational frame used in decision -making by
legitimizing non -financial criteria. Third, it increases the
signaling value of external communications because the
organization can point to formal commitments rather than
relying solely on aspirational messaging. These effects
suggest that law is not merely a backdrop to market strategy;
it is part of the architecture through which trust becomes
measurable [121-124].

Theoretical Gap and Propositions
The literature therefore points to an unresolved
interdisciplinary gap. Strategic marketing planning explains
how firms make an d implement market choices while
marketing analytics explains how those choices are
monitored and improved [125 -128]. Stakeholder governance
scholarship explains why mission protection and joint value
creation matter, and purpose scholarship explains why firms
may adopt broader value commitments [129 -135]. However,
little research has fully integrated these conversations into a
single process model showing how legal purpose
commitments affect planning assumptions, analytics
priorities, brand authenticity, a nd sustainability outcomes.
Existing research often stops at either moral justification or
empirical description [136]. To address this gap, the present
study advances four propositions for future testing.
Proposition 1: Sustainability positioning will be perceived as
more credible when purpose is embedded in formal
governance instruments than when it is expressed only
through voluntary policies or campaign messaging.
Proposition 2: The effect of legal purpose anchoring on brand
trust will be mediated by pl anning alignment, defined as
visible consistency between purpose, resource allocation,
target selection, and operational routines. Proposition 3: The
effect of planning alignment on trust will be amplified by
analytics visibility, defined as the firm’s abi lity to monitor
and communicate purpose -consistent indicators such as
stakeholder-fit, mission -consistent acquisition, partner
compliance, complaint quality, and reputational resilience.
Proposition 4: Stronger trust generated through governance -
backed planning and analytics will improve stakeholder self-
selection, reduce mission conflict, and strengthen
sustainability outcomes over time [137 -140]. There are also
boundary conditions that may weaken the governance -to-
trust mechanism. First, legal embedding m ay generate
credibility only when stakeholders can understand or access
its implications. If constitutional commitments exist but
remain invisible or unintelligible to external audiences, the
signaling advantage will be muted. Second, some sectors
involve such complex supply chains or opaque
intermediaries that even sincere purpose commitments may

internal capability gaps may experience implementation
strain: purpose can be legally embedded before  the
organization has the talent, systems, or partner network to

still be valuable, but it must be accompanied by transparent
communication about progress and constraints. These
boundary condi tions reinforce the paper’s emphasis on
gradual, analytics -supported signaling rather than grand
claims detached from implementation capacity [141-144].

Methodology
This study adopts a qualitative, theory -building
methodology based on secondary analysis of published
empirical material and official legal requirement documents.
The design is appropriate because the paper’s objective is not
to estimate causal effect size s but to specify an integrative
explanatory mechanism that existing literature has not yet
articulated. Consistent with qualitative theory-building logic,
the study reinterprets rich empirical evidence through a new
conceptual lens —in this case, the integr ation of strategic
marketing planning, marketing analytics, and legally
embedded purpose [145 -147]. The focal empirical source is
the published interview-based study by Klettner et al., which
examines stakeholder governance and corporate purpose in
certified B Corps operating in Australia and Aotearoa New
Zealand [148]. That study draws on interviews with 20 B Corp
leaders across multiple industries and organizational sizes,
making it especially useful for mechanism -focused
reinterpretation. The contextual legal framework was
supplemented with B Lab’s global legal requirement
materials  and the jurisdiction -specific New Zealand legal
requirement page [149, 150]. These official sources were used
not as independent empirical observations of firm behavior
but as normative and institutional texts clarifying the formal
governance expectations surrounding certification. The unit
of analysis in the present paper is not the individual manager
or organization, but the governance -to-marketing
mechanism. Accordingly, th e analytic task was to identify
evidence of how purpose commitments are translated into
decisions, routines, signals, and outcomes that are relevant to

marketing strategy. Source material was eligible for inclusion
if it met three criteria: first, it provided explicit evidence about
purpose or stakeholder governance in certified B Corps;
second, it described organizational processes rather than only
abstract principles; and third, it allowed interpretation of
those processes in terms of planning, signaling,  and
relationship outcomes. No additional interviews, surveys, or
experiments were conducted. Likewise, no interventions or
treatments were administered because the study is non -
clinical and interpretive in nature. The analytic procedure
followed an abduct ive thematic synthesis. In the first stage,
evidence reported by Klettner et al.  was read repeatedly to
isolate first -order descriptions of governance practice,
including purpose articulation, decision filters, stakeholder
selection, collaboration routines , external communication,
and ecosystem advocacy [151].
In the second stage, these descriptions were coded
into six role categories that captured recurring process
functions: Establishing, Applying, Developing, Announcing,
Demonstrating, and Sharing. In the third stage, each role was
mapped to strategic marketing planning stages, analytical
indicators, and expected trust outcomes. This stage was
guided by signaling theory, stakeholder governance theory
brand authenticity research and strategic marketing planning
logic [152 -158]. To strengthen rigor, the study used three
forms of triangulation. The first was source triangulation
across scholarly evidence and official governance texts [159 -
162]. The second was theoretical triangulation across
marketing strateg y, stakeholder governance, branding, and
qualitative theory -building literatures [163 -165]. The third
was construct triangulation: each theme was interpreted in
relation to at least one governance construct, one marketing -
planning construct, and one perfor mance or relationship
construct. This reduced the risk of treating isolated anecdotes
as general explanations. The study also sought disconfirming
interpretations. For example, practices that looked like
purpose communication were scrutinized to determine
whether they represented simple promotional activity or
governance-backed signaling. Likewise, practices that
reflected stakeholder dialogue were assessed for whether
they changed planning or execution, rather than remaining
symbolic consultation. Analytic al generalization rather than
statistical generalization is the intended outcome. The B Corp
setting is particularly useful because it makes purpose
commitments institutionally visible, but it also imposes
contextual limits. Certified firms may already be more
mission-oriented than typical firms, and the strength of their
commitments may be shaped by certification culture, founder
values, and sectoral conditions. For that reason, the
framework developed here should be interpreted as a
transferable theoretic al model rather than as a universal
empirical claim. It identifies the conditions under which
purpose governance is likely to influence trust, not the
magnitude of those effects in all markets. Ethically, the study
relied only on published materials and pu blic legal
documents. No new human -subject data were collected. The
analysis was conducted conservatively, avoiding the
attribution of motives or private organizational details not
contained in the source materials. Reproducibility was
enhanced through a s tructured extraction logic: identify
governance action, assign role, map to planning stage, infer
analytics implication, and specify market -facing
consequence. Table 2 summarizes this study protocol.

Decision Roles: Turning Legal Purpose into Planning Logic
The Establishing role conc erns strategic direction
and governance architecture. Here, purpose is used to define
what the firm is trying to achieve, which stakeholders matter,
what kinds of trade -offs are acceptable, and what standards
of conduct should guide planning. In a conventi onal
marketing-planning sequence, this role affects the
assumptions underlying market analysis and objective
setting. Instead of asking only which segments are most
profitable or which channels are cheapest, the firm also asks
which customers, suppliers, a nd growth opportunities are
congruent with its declared purpose. Establishing therefore
redefines the criteria by which opportunities are evaluated.
From a planning standpoint, establishing influences
segmentation, targeting, and positioning in at least four ways.
First, it clarifies the categories of needs the firm exists to solve,
which helps discipline product -market focus. Second, it
identifies stakeholders whose well -being is constitutive of
success rather than incidental to it. Third, it legitimizes l ong-
term criteria such as environmental impact, labor quality, and
relationship durability in strategic reviews. Fourth, it frames
reputation not as an output of communications alone but as a
governance objective. These elements are critical for
sustainable brand trust because they reduce the gap between
value proposition design and organizational identity. The
analytics implication of Establishing is the need for purpose -
sensitive planning dashboards. Traditional planning systems
frequently emphasize marke t share, conversion, acquisition
efficiency, and revenue growth. Purpose-governed firms still
require these metrics, but they also require indicators
showing whether growth is mission -consistent. Relevant
indicators include the percentage of revenue from p urpose-
aligned customer segments, the proportion of approved
suppliers meeting social or environmental criteria,
stakeholder risk exposure in major strategic initiatives, and
the degree to which campaign promises are backed by
operational evidence. In this  sense, Establishing creates the
measurement logic for later stages of the system. The
Applying role refers to the use of purpose as a decision filter
in day -to-day commercial choices. If Establishing answers
what the firm stands for, Applying answers how that stance
is used in concrete situations. The source material indicates
that purpose can shape decisions involving supplier
selection, client acceptance, recruitment, partnership design,
product continuation, and operational prioritization [166].
This is especially important for marketing because it affects
whether the value proposition is enacted consistently across
customer touchpoints and channel relationships. Applying
converts broad commitments into operational screens. A
purpose-led firm may decide not to work with certain clients,
may redesign procurement rules, may reject growth
opportunities that undermine stakeholder promises, or may
change onboarding processes to ensure alignment. These are
not minor administrative choices; they determine the li ved
content of the brand.
When the firm’s accepted customers, partners, and
products reflect purpose constraints, the brand becomes less
vulnerable to accusations of hypocrisy. Conversely, when
marketing campaigns promote sustainability while
commercial routines continue to reward misaligned accounts,
the organization sends contradictory signals that erode trust.
From an analytics perspective, Applying calls for metrics that
detect alignment at the decision level. Examples include the
share of new customers  screened for purpose -fit, supplier -
compliance pass rates, mission -consistent retention versus
revenue-maximizing retention, purpose -related exception
requests, and the frequency of escalated trade -offs between
commercial opportunity and stakeholder standa rds. These
indicators may initially appear outside traditional marketing
analytics, but they directly influence the credibility and
consistency of customer experience. Applying thus links
governance to the operational integrity of the market offer.
The Developing role concerns collaborative problem solving
with stakeholders. Rather than viewing stakeholder demands
as static constraints, purpose -governed firms often use
dialogue to redesign offerings, refine standards, and create
joint solutions. This role a ligns strongly with the
involvement-oriented logic described in CSR communication
scholarship but it goes further by embedding collaboration in
the production of value itself [167]. Customers, suppliers,
employees, and community actors can become participants in
shaping how purpose is delivered rather than mere audiences
to whom purpose is announced. Strategically, Developing
matters because sustainability problems are rarely solved
within firm boundaries. Supply -chain emissions, circularity,
labor conditions, accessibility, and community impact often

depend on coordination across multiple actors. The source

supportive stakeholder coalitions [168]. In ma rketing terms,
this can improve co -creation, deepen relational switching

collaboration. These metrics allow managers to monitor
whether purpose is generating collaborative value or merely
symbolic consultation.

Signaling Roles: Turning Governance into Credible Market
Signals
The Announcing role is the first explicitly market -
facing signaling role. Once purpose is legally embedded and
internally organized, firms communicate it through
certification, websites, reports, pitches, recruitment materials,
packaging, and public narrat ives. Announcing matters
because stakeholders cannot infer governance commitments
unless they are made visible. Yet its credibility depends on
what stands behind it. In the source setting, certified firms can
point not only to sustainability claims but to formal
governance commitments associated with certification and
constitutional change [169-172]. This raises the informational
value of the announcement. Announcing functions as a
screening mechanism. It attracts stakeholders who are
looking for credible p urpose signals and deters those who
prefer purely transactional relationships. For customers, it
can reduce perceived greenwashing risk. For employees, it
can strengthen employer -brand interpretation. For investors
and partners, it can clarify the type of organization they are
dealing with and the likely persistence of its commitments.
However, Announcing also contains risk. If the firm
accelerates public signaling faster than internal alignment, it
increases exposure to the kind of backlash identified in t he
woke-washing literature [173]. High-visibility purpose claims
without governance-backed execution can intensify scrutiny
and reputational vulnerability. The appropriate analytics for
Announcing include trust conversion rates from purpose -
centered campai gns, stakeholder awareness of legal or
certification commitments, message -believability scores,
sustainability skepticism reduction, inbound inquiries from
aligned stakeholders, and discrepancies between external
claims and internal capability audits. The most important
insight here is that purpose communication should be
evaluated not only for reach and engagement, but for the
quality of stakeholder belief it generates. The Demonstrating
role involves behavioral proof. Stakeholders observe purpose
not only in declarations but in how employees behave, how
trade-offs are explained, how complaints are handled, and
how consistent the organization remains over time.
Demonstrating is therefore the role in which authenticity
becomes experientially visible. Morhart  et al. emphasize that
authenticity perceptions depend on credibility and integrity,
both of which are strengthened when stakeholders encounter
purpose in repeated interactions [174]. In the B Corp context,
demonstration can occur through onboarding, trans parent
reasoning, partnership enforcement, and culture -building
routines that make purpose legible at close range [175].
This role is especially significant for strategic
marketing planning because it collapses the supposed divide
between “operations” and “brand.” Customer service scripts,
supplier enforcement, hiring decisions, and service recovery
procedures all become part of brand proof. Demonstrating
reduces the risk that the market will interpret sustainability
as campaign rhetoric. It also provides a  basis for relational
resilience: when stakeholders have repeatedly seen the firm
act in line with purpose, they may grant it more legitimacy
during inevitable mistakes or tensions. Sustainable brand
trust is therefore cumulative and behavioral, not merely
declarative. Analytics for Demonstrating should prioritize
experience-consistency measures, including complaint -
resolution fairness, values -based service recovery, employee
purpose comprehension, onboarding completion, partner
compliance follow -through, a nd mismatch incidents where
staff behavior contradicts declared purpose. These indicators
help managers identify whether the organization’s
“backstage” routines support or undermine its public
positioning. The Sharing role extends purpose beyond
bilateral firm–stakeholder relationships into ecosystem
influence. Purpose -governed firms may educate suppliers,
advocate for standards, mentor peer organizations, and
participate in broader movement -building. Sharing
transforms marketing from a narrow persuasive ac tivity into
a diffusion activity. The brand becomes associated with
category leadership, field -level advocacy, and the
normalization of more responsible market practices. This is
important because sustainability trust often depends on
whether the firm is perceived as opportunistically exploiting
a trend or genuinely trying to change how business is done.
Sharing can produce indirect but powerful market outcomes.
It can increase reputational legitimacy, attract mission -

compatible collaborators, and create sp illover effects that
strengthen the firm’s position within purpose -oriented

employees and partners that purpose is not confined to
campaigns or annual reports. In strategic planning terms,
Sharing widens the domain of competitive advantage: the
firm is no longer competing only on product attributes but on
field-shaping influence and trust leadership. Relevant metrics
include ecosystem partnership growth, educational content
uptake, policy or stand ards participation, referral rates from

Viewed together, the six roles form a cyclical
strategic marketing planning system. Establishing shapes
market sensing and objective formation. Applying sh apes
targeting, account selection, and partner governance.
Developing shapes innovation and relational co -creation.
Announcing shapes positioning and communication
strategy. Demonstrating shapes experience delivery and trust
reinforcement. Sharing shapes ecosystem influence and long-
term legitimacy. This cycle is dynamic rather than linear.
Signals from Announcing or Sharing can attract new
stakeholders whose expectations then force revisions to
Establishing or Applying. Likewise, analytics gathered
during Demonstrating may reveal inconsistencies that
require planning redesign. Figure 2 presents this cycle as an
analytics-enabled loop. Purpose is legally anchored first, but
it remains commercially potent only when it enters the firm’s
planning controls. Mark et sensing should therefore include
stakeholder skepticism, regulatory risk, and mission -fit
variables. Objective setting should include trust and
alignment goals alongside sales goals. Segmentation and
targeting should incorporate values fit, not merely e conomic
potential. Implementation should assign ownership for cross-
functional purpose controls. Review systems should assess
not only campaign outputs but also whether the
organization’s behavior increased or weakened stakeholder
confidence. In this way, analytics becomes the feedback
mechanism through which purpose governance is
continuously tested.

Outcome Pattern Across Stakeholder Markets
The outcome of the full mechanism is a distinctive
configuration of sustainable brand trust. First, legally
embedded purpose raises the perceived cost of opportunism.
Because the firm has adopted formal governance
commitments, stakeholders infer that reversal is more
difficult and therefore that promises are more credible [176 -
180]. Second, purpose -backed signaling improves
stakeholder s elf-selection. Customers, employees, investors,
and partners who value the firm’s commitments are more
likely to join and remain, while misaligned actors may opt out
earlier. This can reduce relational friction, complaint
intensity, and mission conflict. Third, planning and analytics
alignment increase message–behavior consistency. When the
firm can demonstrate that its targeting, supplier choices,
employee systems, and performance dashboards all reflect
purpose, authenticity becomes easier to perceive [181 -185].
These dynamics generate marketing outcomes across
multiple stakeholder markets. In customer markets,
governance-backed purpose can increase willingness to
initiate and maintain relationships, improve advocacy, and
reduce skepticism. In labor markets , it can strengthen
employer branding, retention, and cultural fit. In supply and
partnership markets, it can improve channel governance and
reduce downstream reputational risk. In capital markets, it
can attract mission -compatible investors and reduce the
probability of mission dilution following financing events.
These outcomes are not guaranteed; they depend on
coherence across all six roles. Weak internal routines paired
with strong public claims produce fragility. Strong internal
purpose with weak sign aling produces under -realized
market value. Sustainable brand trust emerges when
governance, planning, and analytics reinforce one another.
Finally, the framework opens a different way of thinking
about sustainability outcomes. Much research assesses
outcomes through environmental or social performance
alone, while marketing research often emphasizes attitudinal
or behavioral responses. The present study argues that
sustainable brand trust is a mediating capability connecting
these domains. When governance -backed purpose
strengthens trust, stakeholders become more willing to
cooperate with the firm, share information, tolerate short -
term friction, and participate in solution development. These
relational dynamics can, in turn, improve the firm’s capacity
to achieve substantive sustainability outcomes. Thus, trust is
not merely an image variable; it is a coordination resource
that helps the organization deliver the very impacts it claims
to value.

Discussion
The study contributes to strategic marketing
scholarship by repositioning governance architecture as a
planning variable. Marketing strategy has traditionally
emphasized market choice, customer value propositions, and
performance accountability [186 -188]. The present
framework adds a prior question: what governance
commitments make a chosen position believable and
durable? In sustainability -sensitive markets, this question is
foundational rather than peripheral. A firm cannot credibly
promise stakeholder value if its governing logic allows those
promises to be abandoned whenever short -term incentives
shift. Purpose governance therefore belongs within the
theoretical domain of strategic marketing, particularly when
the brand’s meaning depends on future reliability. The study
also extends signaling theory. Prior research shows that
costly or constraining signals help reduce information
asymmetry [189]. What this paper adds is a clearer account of
the specific costs associated with governance -backed
sustainability signals. The cost is not limited to formal
amendment or certification fees. It includes reduced freedom
to pursue misaligned revenue, heightened accountability in
stakeholder-facing explanations, and the need to maintain
planning routines consistent with purpose. These costs make
the signal more informative. They also clarify why purpose
claims without planning and analytics support can fail: the
signal may look morally ambitious but remain strategically
cheap. For brand and authenticity research, the pa per
suggests that authenticity should be treated as an
organizational accomplishment rather than a
communications style. Morhart et al. ’s dimensions of

authenticity become easier to interpret when placed within a
governance system. Continuity is supported by legal
durability [190]. Credibility is supported by verifiable
routines. Integrity is supported by decision filters and
enforcement. Symbolism is supported by the translation of
purpose into shared meaning. This perspective helps explain
why some sustai nability brands retain trust through
controversy while others collapse after one contradiction: the
former have created a deeper institutional basis for their
claims. The paper further contributes to stakeholder
governance scholarship by translating purpose commitments
into managerial control language. One reason stakeholder
governance has appeared vague is that scholars and
practitioners often stop at the level of principle [191 -193]. By
specifying roles, planning stages, and analytics measures, the
current study shows how stakeholder governance can
become more disciplined and observable. This does not
eliminate normative contestation, but it reduces
indeterminacy by requiring managers to articulate how
purpose affects segmentation, targeting, procurement,
onboarding, communication, and review. The framework
thus offers a practical answer to criticisms that stakeholder
governance lacks implementable criteria.
Managerially, the study implies that sustainability
marketing should be governed like a strategic ca pability
rather than run like a campaign portfolio. Firms seeking
brand trust should first define whether purpose has formal
organizational standing. Where legally feasible, embedding
purpose and stakeholder consideration in constitutions,
charters, or inv estment agreements can increase durability
and internal clarity [194 -197]. Second, marketing leaders
should collaborate with legal, operations, HR, and
procurement functions to build purpose -linked planning
controls. Third, analytics teams should expand da shboard
design beyond efficiency metrics to include credibility and
alignment indicators. Finally, executive review processes
should treat mismatches between purpose claims and
operating behavior as strategic risk signals, not only as
communication issues.  The discussion also has implications
for boards and top management teams. Many organizations
allocate sustainability messaging to marketing departments
and governance questions to legal or board committees. The
evidence synthesized here suggests that this  separation is
strategically inefficient. Boards influence what the firm can
credibly promise, while marketing teams influence how those
promises are translated into stakeholder meaning. If these
functions do not coordinate, the organization either over -
communicates what it cannot govern or under-communicates
what it genuinely does well. Senior leadership therefore
needs integrated review forums in which governance
decisions, strategic market choices, and analytics outputs are
considered together. Such inte gration helps ensure that
purpose is not lost in translation between constitutional
language and customer experience [198]. Several limitations
should be acknowledged. The study is theory -building and
based on secondary qualitative evidence; it does not estimate
causal effects or determine which analytics indicators matter
most in all industries. The B Corp setting may also
overrepresent mission-driven organizations whose founders
and cultures are already unusually committed to stakeholder
value. Moreover, legal purpose commitments may work
differently in publicly listed firms, heavily regulated
industries, or jurisdictions with stronger shareholder primacy
norms. The analytics architecture proposed here is therefore
conceptual rather than empirically valida ted. It should be
treated as a framework for research and managerial
experimentation, not as a finished universal scorecard [199,
200].

Conclusion
This paper set out to explain how strategic marketing
planning and marketing analytics can be integrated with
legally embedded corporate purpose to strengthen
sustainable brand trust. Building on stakeholder governance
theory, signaling theory, branding re search, and qualitative
evidence from certified B Corps, the study showed that
governance commitments become market -relevant through
six roles: Establishing, Applying, Developing, Announcing,
Demonstrating, and Sharing. These roles connect
constitutional purpose clauses and stakeholder obligations to
planning assumptions, implementation routines,
communication design, and outcome measurement. The
central argument is that sustainable brand trust is not
generated by communication intensity alone. It is produc ed
when purpose is made costly to reverse, operationally visible,
and analytically monitorable. Legally embedded purpose
increases the credibility of sustainability claims because it
raises the expected cost of opportunism and narrows the
distance between stated values and future behavior. Strategic
marketing planning translates that commitment into target
choices, resource allocation, and experience design.
Marketing analytics, in turn, provides the control system that
reveals whether these commitments are  perceived, enacted,
and trusted by stakeholders. The study therefore reframes
governance as a strategic marketing capability. In markets
shaped by skepticism, information asymmetry, and rising
accountability demands, firms require more than compelling
narratives. They require governance -backed narratives
supported by routines and evidence. When purpose
governance, planning, and analytics are aligned,

sustainability positioning becomes more than an aspiration:
it becomes a defendable and measurable basis of  brand trust

Recommendations
Future research should empirically test the
framework across settings. Experimental studies can compare
stakeholder responses to three forms of sustainability
signaling: claims only,  claims plus third -party certification,
and claims plus certification plus formal governance
commitments. Longitudinal studies can examine whether
legally embedded purpose improves resistance to trust
erosion during reputational shocks, economic downturns,  or
leadership transitions. Multi -respondent field studies can
assess whether the proposed analytics indicators predict
customer retention, employee advocacy, supplier
cooperation, and investor alignment. For managerial practice,
four recommendations follo w. First, firms should begin
sustainability positioning with governance diagnostics rather
than campaign ideation. Managers need to determine
whether promised stakeholder outcomes are supported by
constitutions, board mandates, incentive systems, and
escalation pathways. Second, firms should redesign strategic
marketing plans to incorporate purpose -fit into
segmentation, targeting, partner selection, and innovation
prioritization. Third, analytics dashboards should include
mission-consistency indicators such as values-fit conversion,
supplier-alignment rates, complaint -quality measures, and
trust resilience after incidents. Fourth, organizations should
communicate purpose progressively: public claims should
expand only as internal routines and evidence become strong
enough to support them. A final recommendation concerns
policy and institutional design. In jurisdictions where formal
benefit-corporation structures are absent or underused,
professional bodies, investors, and certification systems can
help firms adopt governance instruments that give purpose
greater durability. Such instruments are not substitutes for
responsible execution, but they create a stronger foundation
for it. The practical lesson is simple: firms that want trusted
sustainability brands should design for credibility upstream,
measure it continuously, and communicate it only at the
speed of verifiable implementation.
