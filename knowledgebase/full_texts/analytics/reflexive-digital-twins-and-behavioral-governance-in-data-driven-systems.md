Reflexive digital twins and behavioral governance in data-driven systems
Abstract
Digital twins are increasingly used to model and optimise complex systems, yet their
behavioural and institutional implications remain fragmented across disciplines. Here, we
develop Reflexive Digital Twin Marketing Theory (RDTMT), conceptualising marketing as a
socio-technical learning system that co-evolves with consumers, algorithms, and governance
arrangements through continuous feedback. Using an Empirics-First approach, we conduct a
PRISMA-ScR–guided scoping review across Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and Google
Scholar (2010–2025), identifying 742 records and synthesising 55 studies at the intersection of
healthcare digital twins, marketing analytics, and ethical AI governance. Co-occurrence
mapping (VOS) reveals four convergent clusters—continuous synchronisation, adaptive
intelligence, reflexive governance, and societal co-evolution—indicating that “twin-like”
systems reshape behaviour by structuring how people are represented, predicted, and acted
upon. Building on these regularities, RDTMT introduces three constructs—Digital Reflexivity,
Ethical Feedback Elasticity and the Value Coherence Index—to explain when predictive
personalisation aligns with stakeholder trust and social legitimacy. This framework reframes
marketing not as unidirectional influence but as reflexive governance in data-rich
environments, offering testable propositions for responsible behavioural prediction and
feedback-driven institutional design.

 Introduction:
The importance of reflexive intelligence systems, which couple data, algorithms, and human
intelligence, has emerged in learning, appraisal, and adaptation through recursive processes. In
marketing and health care, intelligence systems and networks have shifted from traditional
linear approaches to dynamic environments in which knowledge building and management
occur through shared human and algorithmic processes. One of the best examples of such
processes is the Digital Twin, a virtual replica created through engineering processes
specifically for the purpose of optimization. 1. The Digital Twin is a prime example of an
essential ingredient of “smart” health. It works as the “mirror of the patient’s system,”
providing a real-time algorithmic simulation of the patient. This mirror, developed in a
contemporary fashion, combines biological, behavioural, and environmental inputs to enable
simulation and prediction. The current approach would redefine the ‘patient’ as the “status of
treatment,” developed within a care paradigm in which humans, institutions, and intelligent
systems collaborate in the ‘co-production of care.’ 2 In such a paradigm, the patient would be
seen as an ‘active source of data’ in the process of ‘managing their digital identity’ through
feedback and consent.
Similarly, in the research ecosystem, the Empirics-first  (EF)3can be seen as a Digital Twin of
inquiry—a reflective, adaptive system that mirrors the methodological, ethical, and epistemic
aspects of research. Like a Digital Twin (DT) that recalibrates a physiological model with real-
time data, a Reflexive Evaluation Framework (REF) continuously adapts and improves by
modelling the changing research dynamics—tracking outputs, learning paths, contextual
relationships, and ethical considerations.4
These reframing shifts evaluation from a static, judgment-focused process to a dynamic
epistemic ecosystem that can sense, model, and enhance its own functions. The REF thus serves

as both a reflection and a guide for research: it displays performance metrics while also steering
ethical, methodological, and social responsiveness. 5. In this way, it aligns with the main
principles of the Digital Twin approach—such as simulation, feedback, transparency, and co-
evolution—helping research remain rigorous, context-aware, stakeholder-accountable, and
adaptable to its evolving complexities, as shown in Table 1.

Conceptual Foundations: The Digital Twin as a Reflexive Evaluation Framework (REF)
    From Static Assessment to Reflexive Intelligence

The 21st century signifies a pivotal change in how organizations, researchers, and
policymakers approach evaluation, learning, and adaptation. Historically, evaluation
frameworks served as linear, retrospective tools—used to measure research outcomes after
completion. They prioritized procedural aspects, including compliance, methodological rigor,
and performance metrics. 8 However, this traditional approach is becoming less effective in a
time marked by complex, data-rich, and constantly changing systems.
The rapid integration of data infrastructures, machine learning, and algorithmic reasoning has
led to what can be called reflexive intelligence systems—ecosystems that learn about
themselves while they act. In these environments, evaluation cannot remain external to the
system it assesses; it must become adaptive, participatory, and co-evolving.9. This gives rise to
what we might call a Reflexive Evaluation Framework (REF)—a dynamic, living system that
incorporates feedback loops, ethical reasoning, and real-time data synthesis to model and guide
the ongoing development of research.
Within this broader epistemic transformation, the Digital Twin (DT) —a real-time digital
mirror of a physical entity—offers a compelling conceptual and operational model for
reimagining evaluation as algorithmic reflexivity.
    The Digital Twin as a Model of Co-Evolution
Originally rooted in engineering, the Digital Twin serves as a high-precision, continually
updated simulation of a physical system. In healthcare, it depicts a patient through an
algorithmically driven identity that constantly learns from diverse data streams such as
genomics, sensor outputs, and medical records. 10.This ongoing synchronization between
biological data and computational models enables predictive analytics, adaptive treatments,
and dynamic optimization of health outcomes.

Philosophically, the Digital Twin extends beyond its technical origins. It reflects a relational
ontology—viewing identity, agency, and governance as co-created by humans, algorithms, and
institutions.11. Consequently, it shifts healthcare from a fragmented, physician-centered
approach to a cybernetic ecosystem of care, where feedback loops constantly influence
diagnosis, treatment, and trust.
This human–machine co-creation model offers a compelling analogy for future research
evaluation. Just as the Digital Twin forecasts patient health in real time, a Reflexive Evaluation
Framework can simulate and refine the health of a research ecosystem—monitoring its
methodological robustness, ethical standards, and social impact in a dynamic balance.
 Toward the Reflexive Evaluation Framework (REF)
Building on the Digital Twin concept, the REF redefines evaluation as a dynamic digital
ecosystem. It functions not as an external audit tool but as an internal intelligence that develops
alongside the research it assesses. 12. The REF reflects the three-dimensional structure of the
Digital Twin, consisting of:
1. Identity Construction — Establishing and continually updating the research system’s
“ontology” (its goals, stakeholders, and context).
2. Data Agency and Learning — Incorporating real-time data from various sources to enable
ongoing feedback and methodological adjustments.
3. Adaptive Governance and Ethics — Implementing flexible oversight mechanisms that adapt
to new ethical, social, and scientific issues.
These three components interact in a reflexive cycle of inquiry and adaptation. The REF
“learns” from the research system it monitors, much as a Digital Twin learns from the patient

it models. This method keeps evaluation both rigorous and responsive, maintaining a balance
between accountability and flexibility—a vital duality often missing in traditional frameworks.
The Reflexive Logic of the Digital Twin
From an Empirics-First (EF) perspective, the Digital Twin (DT) is more than a metaphor; it
serves as a shifting methodological lens that facilitates the assessment of real-world data. This
does not imply that the REF necessitates prior application of theoretical principles before
evaluation. Instead, within the DT's methodological framework, the Reflexive Evaluation
Framework (REF) advances through an inductive observation process. Four levels of
reflexivity—Continuous Synchronization, Simulation and Prediction, Transparency and
Explainability, and Co-Evolutionary Adaptation—guide this process by transforming
evaluation from a static analysis into a continuous, dynamic activity evolution.

Integrating Evaluation, Ethics, and Governance
Traditional evaluation frameworks often isolate methodology from morality—treating ethical
oversight as external regulation. The REF collapses this divide by embedding adaptive ethics
into its architecture. Borrowing from the Digital Twin’s adaptive governance  principles, it
establishes:
• Dynamic Consent and Stakeholder Inclusion:  Continuous negotiation of
participation and transparency in evaluation processes.
• Algorithmic Accountability: Systematic auditing of evaluative metrics, biases, and
interpretive assumptions.
• Equity-by-Design: Ensuring that inclusivity and representational diversity are built
into data collection and evaluative models from inception.
• Learning Ethics: Treating ethical oversight as iterative and reflexive—responsive to
new data, feedback, and context.
In this sense, the REF mirrors the ethical metabolism of the Digital Twin: a self-regulating
system that sustains legitimacy by continuously aligning its internal operations with evolving
moral and epistemic norms. Consistent with the Empirics-First (EF) paradigm, this metabolism
emerges inductively from real-world observation rather than theoretical prescription.
The REF as a Socio-Technical Ecosystem

The Digital Twin and REF both challenge the traditional divide between “subject” and
“system.” In the REF, the researcher, evaluator, and institution are all part of a shared feedback
ecosystem. This makes the framework socio-technical—combining algorithmic infrastructure
with an ethical community. 20 By incorporating data analytics, stakeholder participation, and
participatory governance, the REF functions simultaneously as:
1. A Cognitive Model — illustrating how research knowledge develops and validates.21
2. An Ethical System — integrating reflexive accountability into decisions.22
3. An Institutional Mechanism — aligning policies, incentives, and feedback loops across
different organizational levels.23
These interconnected layers turn evaluation into an ecosystem of co-production, where human
judgment and algorithmic reasoning work symbiotically.
Reflexivity as the Epistemic Core
The core concept of the REF is reflexivity—the ability of a system to reflect on its own learning
process. This sets adaptive evaluation apart from traditional oversight methods. It means that
knowledge is not just created but also continuously analyzed, leading to deeper insights into
how knowledge is organized, limited, and shaped by context. 24 In this perspective, evaluation
acts as a form of meta-intelligence: a persistent dialogue between objectivity and subjectivity,
technical accuracy and ethical considerations. Consequently, the REF functions as both an
analytical tool and a moral guide—ensuring that the development of knowledge remains open,
inclusive, and responsible to its wider human and institutional contexts.

Methodological Implications: Operationalizing the Reflexive Evaluation Framework
(REF)
  Methodology: Open-Ended Scoping Review and Conceptual Synthesis
This research employs an open-ended scoping review combined with conceptual synthesis to
implement the Reflexive Evaluation Framework (REF) in the realm of Digital Twin (DT)
research and marketing science. The goal is not to validate a specific theory but to explore how
AI-driven simulation, adaptive governance, and data agency transform healthcare ecosystems
and marketing research paradigms.
The scoping review is particularly suitable due to the innovative and interdisciplinary nature
of the Digital Twin concept. Instead of limiting the inquiry with hypothesis-driven inclusion
criteria, the review aims to encompass the full scope of knowledge across various fields—from
biomedical engineering and ethics to health informatics, marketing analytics, digital
transformation, and stakeholder co-creation.26
Following PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-
Analyses–Scoping Review) standards, the process emphasizes transparency, reproducibility,
and reflexivity, moving away from a theory-first approach. Instead, the literature serves as a
discovery space—a reflexive environment where empirical results and conceptual ideas
develop through iterative synthesis.
This methodology considers the Digital Twin not just as a technological tool but as a socially
embedded metaphor for learning systems, aligning with the REF’s epistemic aim of viewing
research as a dynamic, adaptive living system.
   Research Process: Breadth, Depth, and Reflexivity

The empirical scoping and synthesis process was built around three meta-principles, all aligned
with the Reflexive Evaluation Framework (REF). During the scoping review, the REF offered
a structured approach to organizing and interpreting the diverse literature emerging at the
intersection of Digital Twin (DT) systems, marketing analytics, and ethical AI governance. 27.
The initial DT–REF mapping created conceptual analogies that guided the coding and data
extraction, emphasizing co-evolution, transparency, and continuous learning. Building from
this, the analysis progressed iteratively as new patterns emerged within the corpus. Guided by
the principles of breadth, depth, and reflexivity, the review not only described the data but also
uncovered underlying constructs and epistemic tensions across different fields—reflecting an
Empirics-First (EF) approach in which theoretical insights emerge inductively from observed
empirical regularities rather than from predetermined frameworks. Table 3 demonstrates how
these principles were put into action to interpret interdisciplinary evidence and integrate

 • Feedback as Data: Evaluation metrics such as engagement rates and adoption indices

 • Participatory Evaluation Protocols: Stakeholders—patients, consumers, and partners—

    Implications for Marketing Research Practice
Applying this reflexive and twin-inspired methodology  transforms marketing research in
three major ways:
1. Transitioning from Rigid Design to Adaptive Inquiry, research evolves into a dynamic
co-learning process that continually updates hypotheses based on real-world data and
stakeholder feedback.
2.  Moving from Metrics to Meaning, evaluation shifts from purely performance
indicators like ROI and customer satisfaction to encompass ethical integrity, trust
development, and societal impact.
3. Evolving from Audience to Co-Producer, stakeholders are reconceptualized as active
epistemic agents who co-create meaning through participation and interaction with
algorithms.
This connects marketing to contemporary movements such as stakeholder capitalism,
responsible AI, and adaptive governance, thereby maintaining its relevance and credibility in
the digital age. The methodological framework presented here adopts the Reflexive Evaluation
Framework (REF) as both a research approach and an evaluative philosophy. By combining
comprehensive scoping reviews, in-depth conceptual synthesis, and reflexive learning, it
enables marketing and healthcare researchers to emulate the co-evolutionary intelligence of the
Digital Twin. In this process, methodology itself becomes an active agent in the development
of knowledge—constantly adapting to complexity, improving transparency, and promoting a
shared culture of ethical innovation across research, industry, and society.

 Discussion:
    Real-World Relevance: Marketing in the Age of Intelligent Representation
The findings highlight a significant transformation in how marketing systems impact human
welfare, stakeholder involvement, and social responsibility. Originally limited to engineering
and healthcare, the Digital Twin (DT)35, now serves as a model for capturing dynamic market
realities—continuously mirroring consumers, brands, and institutions through data-driven
interactions. In real-world marketing environments—from predictive analytics to experiential
retail—the DT approach enables simulated marketing scenarios, allowing strategy testing via
digital representations before implementation. This shifts marketing from mere descriptive
analysis to proactive co-creation, directly supporting human-centered welfare, ethical
personalization, and sustainability. Despite ongoing debates—ranging from utopian efficiency
to dystopian surveillance—integrating DT with Reflexive Evaluation Frameworks (REFs)
clarifies that DT systems can enhance societal accountability in marketing when embedded
within transparent, reflexive, and participatory evaluation. Consequently, marketing shifts from
merely promoting consumption to fostering responsible co-evolution among firms, markets,
and society.
    Literature Evolution: From Predictive Analytics to Reflexive Intelligence
The review shows that while current marketing literature is data-rich in analytics and customer
modelling, it lacks reflexivity and is divided into silos. Existing frameworks in AI marketing,
CRM, and digital transformation mainly focus on prediction and personalization, but they
rarely explore how these systems learn from their own processes. 36. By integrating DT’s
feedback-driven structure with marketing theory, this research broadens the field’s scope in
two significant ways:

1. Moving from Linear Causality to Reflexive Systems:
Traditional marketing views causality as unidirectional—the firm influencing the
consumer.37. The RDTM perspective introduces recursive causality, in which both
consumers and firms continuously adapt in real time through data feedback, creating
learning markets rather than static target markets.

2. Moving from Descriptive Models to Epistemic Simulations:
Marketing models transition from static predictors, such as regression or
segmentation,38 to dynamic epistemic twins—systems that simulate behavioural
responses, ethical considerations, and societal impacts before strategy deployment.

This shift aligns marketing more closely with fields such as cybernetics, systems theory,
and digital ethics, which see markets as living, reflexive systems rather than mere
mechanical exchanges.
   Research Process: Reflexivity as a Methodological Ethic
The study’s scoping review and conceptual synthesis employed an open-ended, agenda-free
approach, enabling theories to develop naturally through interdisciplinary collaboration. Led
by the REF, the process balanced scope, depth, and reflexivity, uncovering the dynamic
interaction between technology, ethics, and market systems.
•  Breadth: Including 742 records across healthcare, informatics, and marketing ensured
exposure to diverse epistemic traditions.
•  Depth: By interrogating latent meanings—e.g., who the “digital consumer” is and how
“value” is algorithmically constructed—the review uncovered deep parallels between
patient and consumer modelling.

• Reflexivity: The iterative coding and theory-building process mimicked the Digital
Twin’s simulation loop, continuously adjusting theoretical boundaries in response to
emerging insights.
This methodological reflexivity resulted in a research design that reflects on itself, embodying
the same logic as the phenomenon being examined.
   Research Outcomes: Discovering a Reflexive Regularity in Marketing Systems
The conceptual synthesis reveals an empirical pattern across marketing and healthcare digital
twin (DT) applications: systems that incorporate reflexive feedback loops tend to achieve
higher adaptive performance and gain greater stakeholder trust. In marketing, this concept is
embodied by predictive ethics—the capacity of systems not only to forecast consumer
preferences but also to foresee ethical and societal impacts. The RDTM theory, therefore,
emphasizes reflexivity as a key factor influencing performance, connecting economic and
social outcomes. Practical implications for marketing include:
• Integrating digital twin simulations into strategic decision-making to predict consumer
attitudes and societal reactions.
• Creating real-time dashboards that combine marketing KPIs with metrics for ethics,
sustainability, and inclusion.
• Using dynamic consent features that enable customers to co-manage their digital
representations. These developments broaden marketing’s role from solely predicting
behaviour to fostering participatory sensemaking, where value is co-created, validated, and
ethically guided.
    Robustness and Generalizability: Learning from Failure and Cross-Context
Convergence

The PRISMA-ScR synthesis shows that while DT applications vary across sectors, their
success consistently depends on reflexive governance mechanisms. Failed implementations
(e.g., overfitted predictive models or non-transparent algorithms) provide essential learning
opportunities—illustrating that robustness in marketing models arises not from control but
from adaptability.
The RDTM framework demonstrates generalizability across domains:
• In healthcare, DTs optimize clinical outcomes through adaptive modelling.39
• In marketing, they optimize consumer engagement through ethical learning.40
• In governance, they sustain institutional legitimacy through transparency and participatory
oversight.41
Thus, reflexivity itself emerges as a meta-principle of generalizability—a foundation for
transdisciplinary knowledge transfer and theory development.

Conclusion and Implications
    Integrative Summary
This research advances an Empirics-First (EF) approach to developing theories in marketing,
resulting in the Reflexive Digital Twin Marketing (RDTM) Theory. Using a PRISMA-ScR-
guided scoping review and VOS co-occurrence mapping of 742 studies from healthcare,
marketing analytics, and ethical governance, the study highlights the common logic of
simulation, feedback, and co-evolution that characterizes current data-driven marketing
systems. The findings indicate that marketing, similar to intelligent healthcare or adaptive
industrial systems, is shifting from predictive management to reflexive learning—where
feedback from markets, stakeholders, and technologies continuously influences strategy and
understanding.
By viewing marketing as a dynamic, data-enabled ecosystem, the RDTM framework redefines
the discipline’s epistemic base: marketing is not just about influencing behavior but also about
learning from the behaviors it shapes.42
The RDTM Theory offers a reflective framework for understanding contemporary marketing
systems, incorporating digital analytics, governance, and social accountability. This integrative
approach advances marketing thought and practice in various transformative ways.
1. Moving from Prediction to Reflexivity: Viewing marketing systems as self-learning entities
that adapt based on their own data, thereby closing the cause-and-effect loop.
2. Moving from Optimization to Accountability: Incorporating ethical considerations and
governance into marketing analytics, acknowledging that algorithms serve moral and
managerial roles.

3. Moving from Firm-Centric Control to Co-Evolution: Considering markets as collaborative
adaptive networks where firms, consumers, and institutions co-create value and meaning
through data feedback.
These advancements build on traditional marketing theory by making ethical learning and
system adaptation core to marketing intelligence.
They also demonstrate how an Empirics-First approach—focused on observation rather than
assumption—can yield conceptual frameworks that are both sound in theory and impactful in
practice.

   Practical Implications
The RDTM framework provides practical pathways for managers, policymakers, and
marketing system designers to responsibly leverage AI and data.
• Strategic Simulation: Marketers can utilize Digital Twin environments to experiment with
strategies prior to implementation, predicting both commercial and ethical results.

• Reflexive Dashboards: Combining ethical, environmental, and social indicators with
traditional KPIs fosters flexible, transparent decision-making.
• Dynamic Governance: Companies can establish participatory feedback mechanisms where
customers and stakeholders jointly influence data policies, fostering trust and legitimacy.
These approaches redefine marketing success, shifting focus from mere transactions to
adaptable value creation that balances profit with purpose.
For society, such reflexive marketing practices improve transparency, protect digital autonomy,
and promote the co-evolution of technology and trust.
   Limitations
While this study offers an integrated and reflective synthesis connecting Digital Twin (DT)
systems, marketing analytics, and ethical governance, several limitations should be recognized.
First, its empirical basis relies on secondary data from existing literature. Although PRISMA-
ScR and VOS analyses ensured comprehensive and transparent coverage, the lack of primary
empirical data restricts testing causal mechanisms within the emerging Reflexive Digital Twin
Marketing (RDTM) Theory. Second, the conceptual synthesis highlights theoretical
consistency across healthcare, marketing, and governance, but these fields differ in data
maturity and technological adoption. As a result, reflexive mechanisms such as adaptive
governance and feedback elasticity may function differently across industries, data
infrastructures, or regulatory frameworks. Third, the timeframe (2010–2025) reflects Digital
Twin's early adoption phase, meaning new developments in AI and algorithmic governance
beyond this period could alter or extend the proposed theory. Lastly, as an Empirics-First (EF)
approach, this study favours inductive, data-driven discovery over hypothesis testing. 43 This
strengthens contextual validity but may limit generalizability. Future EF research should

triangulate multiple datasets and methods—connecting conceptual insights with practical
measurement and longitudinal analysis.
Future Research Directions: The Empirics-First Pathway
Aligned with the Empirics-First philosophy, future research should prioritize real-world
phenomena that challenge current theories and create opportunities for inductive insights.
Three especially promising avenues are:
1. Empirical Discovery: Investigate unexplored marketing areas—such as consumer digital
twins, algorithmic empathy, and AI-driven loyalty programs—to uncover new behavioral
patterns.
2. Reflexive Experimentation: Use longitudinal and experimental methods to see how feedback
loops affect marketing adaptability and stakeholder trust.
3. Cross-Domain Generalization: Apply RDTM principles to fields like sustainability, policy
communication, and social innovation to evaluate their transdisciplinary relevance. By
anchoring theory in emerging empirical realities, marketing research can stay relevant in a
world shaped by intelligent, adaptable systems.
Closing Reflection: Toward Reflexive Marketing Science
This research reimagines the future of marketing as a reflexive science—an evolving discipline
that develops alongside the intelligent systems it creates and studies.
The Reflexive Digital Twin Marketing Theory presents both a framework and a perspective: a
model that demonstrates how marketing systems learn and adapt, and a mindset that
emphasizes responsibility, transparency, and co-evolution as key to gaining a competitive edge.
By embracing an Empirics-First approach, marketing scholars and practitioners can connect
data with meaning and insight while maintaining ethical standards. In today’s era of digital

reflexivity, marketing’s greatest potential lies not just in prediction, but in its capacity to
understand and respond to the impact of its own intelligence.
