Abstract
Optimizing social media marketing investments requires identifying the empirical drivers of online consumer engage -
ment. Grounded in uses and gratifications theory, this study models how consumer dispositions (opinion leadership and
trust) and goal orientations (information seeking and popularity) govern engagement behaviors. Using survey data ( N =
440) analyzed via structural equation modeling (SEM) and linear regression, we quantify the direct and indirect pathways
shaping brand interactions. The empirical results show that social media popularity is a critical mediator, linking foun -
dational consumer dispositions to active consumer engagement with brands using social media. While general trust and
opinion leadership traits establish the baseline propensity to interact, engagement converts most effectively when platforms
fulfill self-promotional goals. These findings contribute to marketing analytics by providing an actionable framework for
predictive audience segmentation and data-driven resource allocation. These findings also show how social media mar -
keting teams can optimize marketing budgets by designing digital touchpoints that support consumer self-expression and
community-building.

Americans stating they use YouTube daily, just one of many
online choices (Gottifried and Park 2025). Prior research

with brands online within increasingly automated digital

sumers want brands to interact with their audiences, partic -
ularly using video content (Keutelian 2026). Furthermore,
82% of consumers research products on social media, and
53% report having purchased on a social media platform
(Salminen 2026). However, advances in algorithmic recom-

understanding of the individual-consumer factors that drive
online consumer engagement. To achieve this, the current

Prior research regarding consumer engagement has

social media, thus making a valuable contribution to uses
and gratifications theory.
Firms heavily invest in social media analytics, with
global expenditures continuing to rise as brands adopt cloud
solutions and localized consumer insights to optimize return
on investment (Jagtap 2026). Knowing which consumers to
target is essential for effective strategic deployment. This
study examines consumer factors that drive online con -
sumer engagement. While previous research has considered
individual components of social media engagement in iso -
lation (Cheung et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2024), examining the
connections as designed in the following theoretical frame-
work contributes to our understanding of online consumer
engagement in an AI-mediated marketplace. It also provides
a more definitive approach to online social media market -

Literature review
Uses and gratifications theory
Uses and gratifications theory is based on the premise that
consumers seek to meet targeted needs by engaging with
specific media sources (Katz and Foulkes 1962). The moti-
vation for selecting specific media is of interest, as consum-
ers derive gratification from the experience (Wimmer and
Dominick 2000). Social media has afforded consumers a
greater opportunity to meet their needs and to become inter-
active participants (Tran et al. 2026). As a result, consumers
are motivated to purposely seek out, connect with, and uti -
lize media to meet their specific needs (Sharkasi and Agag
2026). As such, uses and gratifications theory posits that
consumers are active participants in media rather than pas -
sive recipients of media exposure (Dolan et al. 2016).
Prior research has used uses and gratifications theory
to examine consumer engagement (Whiting and Williams
2013). Although Le ( 2023) investigated compulsive con -
sumer behaviors, Leiner et al. ( 2018) addressed platform
mechanics, and Balabanis and Chatzopoulou ( 2025) exam-
ined visual content type, this study considers consumer dis-
positions and goals in a normal social media setting. In their
study on social media use, Lervik-Olsen et al. (2023) found
that consumers satisfy self-status-seeking and information-
seeking needs. Using social media, popularity needs are met
through online engagement (Chou and Lin 2023). Social
media use also meets information-seeking needs (Bozkurt
et al. 2026; Florenthal 2015). It is proposed in this study that
consumer engagement satisfies social media goals, namely

Social media dispositions can also be examined through
the lens of uses and gratifications theory. Consumer activity
is driven in part by particular dispositions, whereby con -
sumers evaluate whether the activity is fulfilling and decide
whether to continue it based on the results (Blumler 1979).
Social media trust is a disposition (Hollebeek and Macky
2019). Opinion leadership has also been considered in the
literature as a social media disposition motivated by the
need for social ties and recognition (Aleahmad et al. 2016;
Barta et al. 2023). However, opinion leadership and trust
are not as readily understood from a uses and gratifications
perspective.
Whiting and Williams (2013) and Florenthal (2015) iden-
tified consumer motives; Cheung et al. ( 2022) considered
the followers of social media influencers; and Chou and
Lin (2023) examined brand-initiated tactics from a uses and
gratifications perspective. Social media uses and gratifica -
tions frameworks developed by Bikowski ( 2023), Dolan et
al. (2016), and Hollebeek and Macky (2019) are purely con-
ceptual. Rather et al. ( 2024) and Özer and Uğurhan ( 2026)
also relied on uses and gratifications theory as an under -
pinning for virtual reality use and video involvement. This
study is distinctive in using uses and gratifications theory
to quantitatively test the link between dispositions, goals,
and consumer engagement on social media. Table 1 syn -
thesizes prior research on social media premised on uses

Consumer engagement
Definitions of engagement have used the terms customer,
consumer, and consumer–brand interchangeably. They are
distinguished from each other based on the subject, such
as the individual consumer (Florenthal 2019) or commu -
nity (Bikowski 2023) as well as the context of engagement,
such as online (Barreto and Ramalho 2019), e-commerce
(Leong et al. 2024), phygital (Mele and Russo-Spena 2021),
or social media (Cheung et al. 2022). Researchers have also
distinguished these terms based on user-generated (Mum -
malaneni et al. 2026) or brand-generated (Beckers et al.
2018). Prior studies have also used digital consumer engage-
ment (Krowinska and Dineva 2025), digital visual engage -
ment (Valentini et al. 2018), actor engagement (Brodie et
al. 2019), user engagement (Dang et al. 2026), and social
media engagement behaviors (Abid et al. 2023). Common
among the definitions is the idea that the construct repre -
sents dimensions beyond mere transactions between brands
and consumers. Table 2 presents a selection of definitions of
consumer engagement constructs.
Some definitions have highlighted a particular dimen -
sion, such as emotional engagement (Herrando et al. 2017)
or cognitive engagement (Schaarschmidt and Dose 2023).
Blut et al. ( 2023) expanded on levels of consumer engage -
ment by addressing the investment of various resources
in the brand. Hollebeek et al. ( 2023) also noted cognitive,
affective, and behavioral dimensions that mirror the stan -
dard dimensions used in many research studies (Hollebeek
et al. 2014). However, Obilo et al. ( 2020) show that only
the behavioral dimension matters because consumer activ -
ity generates revenue, and that it adequately represents the
consumer engagement construct. Drawing on the work of
Barger et al. ( 2016), Schaarschmidt and Dose ( 2023), and
Hollebeek et al. ( 2023), consumer engagement in social
media in this study is defined as a consumer’s cognitive,
affective, and behavioral connection to social media, as evi-
denced by the creation and contribution of digital content.
Antecedents to consumer engagement interest brands for
good reasons. Most (88%) online consumers trust online
reviews written by other consumers as much as personal
recommendations, and most (97%) read online reviews
before purchasing a product (Capital One Shopping 2026).
Therefore, consumers engage online with each other with or
without the influence of firms or their brand presence. The
question, then, is which type of consumer is most likely to
engage with firms online and influence others. Thus, a par -
ticular consumer profile may resonate more strongly with
co-creating online content that adds value for both consum-
ers and firms. This question is central to studies examining
antecedents to consumer engagement, focusing on con -
sumer factors represented as social media goals (i.e., social
media information seeking and social media popularity) and
social media dispositions (i.e., opinion leadership and social
media trust).
Social media goals
Research on social media goals draws on uses and grati -
fications theory (Blumler 1979). Consumers set expecta -
tions based on their needs and then use social media to meet
those needs, deciding whether to continue based on the
consequences (Tran et al. 2026). Social media participation
begins with needs, motivations, or goals. Several motiva -
tions for social media consumer engagement are apparent,
as described previously. Dimensions range from three core
motivations (Krowinska and Dineva 2025) to four motives
(Leckie et al. 2021) or nine goals (Leiner et al. 2018). As Le
(2021) notes, common motivators include information seek-
ing and socialization.

Social media popularity
Grounded in uses and gratifications and consumption val -

media popularity depends on the anticipated value of the
activity. Krowinska and Dineva ( 2025) provided an over -
view of motivations for social media use, noting the need
to investigate further the emotions associated with it. Posi -
tive emotions stem from social media activities, providing
entertainment (Umashankar et al. 2024), relaxation (Liu
and Huang 2025), fun (Barta et al. 2023), and socialization
(Balabanis and Chatzopoulou 2025).

social media popularity to reflect the current landscape of
AI-driven personalization, defining it as a consumer’s sub -
jective appraisal of their relative network reach, follower
accumulation, and relational appeal across social media
platforms. Social media information seeking aligns with the
utilitarian dimension of social media goals, whereas indi -
vidual-centric social media popularity captures the hedonic

Social media dispositions
Existing literature shows that several social media disposi -
tions act as antecedents to consumer engagement, with uses
and gratifications theory as an underpinning (Daniels and
Wu 2024; Trunfio and Rossi 2021). Opinion leadership is
of particular interest in this study, as prior studies have not
examined the elements researched as a single dimension.
Separately, social status, organic influencer, and social influ-
encer behaviors address online behavior without connect -
ing to the collective consumer benefits inherent in opinion
leadership, such as affirmation and a sense of belonging to a
community (Lyroni and Spais 2025; Noguti 2022).
This study also investigates social media trust as a con -
sumer disposition that leads to consumer engagement.
Evolving market conditions mean that concerns about secu-
rity and privacy require consumers to feel assured and com-
fortable with social media to engage online (Sharkasi and
Agag 2026). Ultimately, consumers are more likely to use
digital information sources they trust (Hochstein et al. 2025;
Otopah et al. 2024).
Prior research has shown that certain consumer character-
istics influence online consumer activities. However, little
research has examined the specific social media dispositions
suggested by Vander Schee et al. ( 2020) and Vander Schee
et al. ( 2025) that lead to consumer engagement, namely,
opinion leadership and social media trust. Moreover, inves-
tigating how those two social media dispositions influence
social media goals also merits further consideration. This
study seeks to fill this gap.
Opinion leadership
The stimulus-organism-response framework (Mehrabian
and Russell 1974) and uses and gratifications theory (Katz
and Foulkes 1962) provide theoretical underpinnings for
opinion leadership on social media. Opinion leaders tend
to have extroverted personalities (Leung et al. 2022). Butt
and Phillips (2008) described extroversion as a person’s ten-
dency to be sociable and to experience positive emotions.
Extroverts have a greater number of online connections
(Chang et al. 2023), tend to post more comments and photos
(Schoenmueller et al. 2025), update their social activities
(Song et al. 2023), disseminate information (Ismagilova et
al. 2021), and gain social capital or social currency with
others more often (Rynarzewska et al. 2025). Shumanov et
al. (2021) showed that extroversion leads to positive online
consumer engagement; however, Dharba et al. (2024) found
that extroversion does not necessarily positively influence
consumer engagement. Therefore, extroversion alone does
not capture the opinion leadership social media disposition
associated with consumer engagement.
Kumar et al. ( 2010) proposed a framework for captur -
ing total customer engagement value. Two dimensions of
the framework are customer-influencer value and customer-
knowledge value. Direct benefits for firms include increased
customer acquisition, retention, and share of wallet, as well
as a better understanding of customer preferences. More -
over, Kumar and Pansari ( 2016) showed that having brand
knowledge and sharing it with others helps firms by increas-
ing communication, facilitating feedback, and positively
influencing consumer engagement. Opinion leadership as a
social media disposition captures these two dimensions by
incorporating extroversion and information sharing.
In this study, opinion leadership is defined as the extent
to which a consumer influences others’ consumption deci -
sions through recognized brand expertise and sought-after
product advice. This definition draws on prior research
on consumer personality traits and consumer dispositions,
showing that opinion leaders on social media exhibit infor -
mation sharing, self-efficacy, and influencer behavior (Agu-
irre et al. 2023; Balabanis and Chatzopoulou 2025; Noguti
2022). This study examines how opinion leadership influ -
ences social media goals and consumer engagement, filling
a gap in the literature on the antecedents of social media
dispositions.
Social media trust
In the context of relationship marketing and grounded in
commitment-trust theory (Morgan and Hunt 1994), brands
aspire to build relationships by fostering commitment and
trust with customers. Uses and gratifications theory also pro-
vides context for consumer trust in using social media (Özer
and Uğurhan 2026). Extending consumer trust to social
media offers brands an opportunity to increase engagement
and profitability (Hayes et al. 2021). Trust is associated with
satisfaction: higher satisfaction with a brand is associated
with higher trust (Damberg et al. 2022). Furthermore, satis-
faction reduces uncertainty and anxiety while allowing for
vulnerability, thereby increasing trust (Rashidi-Sabet and
Bolton 2024).
To align with the current technological environment, this
study defines social media trust as a consumer’s tendency to
feel comfortable and secure when using social media, which
incorporates their reliance on algorithmic transparency. This
definition was adapted for social networks from Moorman
et al. (1993) who defined consumer trust as being “willing to
rely on an exchange partner in whom one has confidence” (p.
82). Consumers who trust automated platforms to keep their
information private and handle data transparently are more

Social media information seeking is positively associated
with consumer engagement and social media popularity.
Polimerou and Spais ( 2025) studied a telecommunications
company's social media page involving 353 survey respon -
dents. Their results showed that consumers initially engage
with a brand to seek information and socialize. Naqvi et
al. ( 2021) found similar results: information seeking led
to consumer engagement. Over time, consumers develop a
need for recognition to stay engaged with the brand. Only
their information-seeking goal changes behavior (Hochstein
et al. 2025) and keeps them engaged with the brand beyond
online entertainment and recognition activities (Eigenraam
et al. 2021). Moreover, consumers are motivated by timely
information, service responses, and product information
(Ozuem et al. 2025) to aid in decision-making (Otopah et al.
2024). Therefore, it is proposed that:
H1: Social media information seeking is positively associ -
ated with consumer engagement.

Social media goals: social media popularity
Social media popularity is positively associated with con -
sumer engagement. Consumers attract attention by creat -
ing and sharing brand stories to relate to other social media
users within custom-curated digital spaces (Hochstein et
al. 2025). In this way, the brand provides the content, the
user relates to the experience, and the social media plat -
form enables mass distribution. In their mixed-methods
study using both qualitative and quantitative approaches,
Umashankar et al. ( 2024) found that entertainment and
fun were among the social media goals in brand–consumer
interactions. Using branded content on Facebook, Krowin -
ska and Dineva ( 2025) showed that integration and social
interaction are dominant social media goals that lead to
consumer engagement behaviors, namely, contributing to
and creating brand-related content. Therefore, it is proposed
that:
H3: Social media popularity is positively associated with
consumer engagement.
Social media dispositions: opinion leadership
Opinion leadership is positively associated with consumer
engagement, social media information seeking, and social
media popularity. In their study on Instagram likes, Bala -
banis and Chatzopoulou ( 2025) found that opinion leader -
ship was positively associated with consumer engagement.
Opinion leadership also went beyond simply providing con-
tent, as survey respondents noted benefits such as express -
ing themselves freely as influencers and receiving feedback.
As established in the previous section, opinion leadership
requires having information to share, fostering social media
information seeking (Hochstein et al. 2025). Carney et al.
(2026) found similar results on TikTok regarding AI-gen -
erated content disclosures, where opinion leadership, inter -
personal utility, and reduced parasocial connection were
linked to consumer engagement. Self-expression in this
way provides a hedonic benefit through affirmation as an
opinion leader, fostering virtual consumer engagement (Pan
et al. 2025). As established in the previous section, positive
affirmation from opinion leadership reinforces the desire for
social media popularity (Mummalaneni et al. 2026). More-
over, Silver and Small ( 2023) found that the urge to be an
opinion leader and the tendency to self-enhance motivated
opinion leadership behavior. Therefore, it is posited that:
H4: Opinion leadership is positively associated with con -
sumer engagement.
H5: Opinion leadership is positively associated with social
media information seeking.
H6: Opinion leadership is positively associated with social
media popularity.
Social media dispositions: social media trust
Social media trust is positively associated with consumer
engagement, social media information seeking, and social
media experience. Consumers who feel connected to and
trust the social media community in general are more likely
to engage with brands online (Santos et al. 2022). Moreover,
the degree to which consumers trust other consumers, mar -
keters, and automated curation systems in an online brand
community also influences consumer engagement (Liu et al.
2018; Otopah et al. 2024). By extension, consumers who
feel secure, protected, and clear about how their data is used
online will engage in more information-seeking behavior on
social media. Beck et al. (2023) also found, in their study of
198 participants, that prior social media popularity enhances
social media trust and thus fosters continued social media
popularity. Therefore, it is posited that:
H7: Social media trust is positively associated with con -
sumer engagement.
H8: Social media trust is positively associated with social
media information seeking.
H9: Social media trust is positively associated with social
media popularity.
Method
Measurement instrument
We adapted the survey scales from the existing literature.
We adapted opinion leadership survey items from an opin -
ion leader status scale (Risselada et al. 2016). We adapted
survey items for social media trust from scales measuring
trust and social recommendations (Dahl et al. 2019; Turcotte
et al. 2015). We adapted items measuring the social media
information seeking construct from scales on information
interest by Chahal and Rani ( 2017) and information seek -
ing by Shao and Ross ( 2015). We measured social media
popularity using survey items adapted from a popularity
scale (Zywica and Danowski 2008). We adapted consumer

engagement measures from Muntinga et al. ( 2011) as rec -
ommended by Barger et al. (2016). Table 3 summarizes the
construct definitions used in this study.
Sample and data collection procedures
Survey items were measured on a 5-point Likert-type scale
ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The sur -
vey was administered to business faculty as a pretest to
assess clarity and face validity. An Institutional Review
Board (IRB) approved the study with students. A conve -
nience sample of college students enrolled at a Midwestern
public university then completed the revised survey. A total
of 440 surveys were completed out of 586 students, for a
75% response rate. Table 4 provides the respondent profile.
Measurement model
We used exploratory factor analysis with varimax rota -
tion to identify an initial set of dimensions, retaining fac -
tors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0. Five dimensions
emerged, explaining 70.31% of the variance. Cronbach's
alpha scores for all dimensions exceeded 0.80, meeting the
0.70 benchmark (Nunnally 1978) and supporting internal
validity. Confirmatory factor analysis tested the measure -
ment model fit. The Chi-Square Value/Degrees of Freedom
(CMIN/DF) was less than the suggested value of 3.0; the
overall Chi-square statistic was significant (χ 2 = 389.827,
DF = 302, CMIN/DF = 1.291, p < 0.001). However, other
goodness-of-fit measures suggest a satisfactory model.
The Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA)
(0.026) was less than the 0.08 cutoff suggested by Hair et al.
(2010). Finally, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) of 0.989
exceeded the suggested 0.95 cutoff (Hu and Bentler 2009).
All individual items loaded significantly ( p < 0.001), as
shown in Table 5.
All 27 standardized loadings met the minimum 0.5 guide-
line suggested by Cortina ( 1993), with 22 meeting the 0.7
preferred guideline. The Average Variance Extracted (A VE)
was 0.603, and each construct had an A VE > 0.512, meeting
the convergent validity criterion of 0.5 set by Fornell and
Larcker (1981). Table 6 presents the discriminant validity
assessment, in which construct values for Maximum Shared
Variance (MSV), Average Shared Variance (ASV), and A VE
were compared to verify that MSV < A VE and ASV < A VE
for each construct.
Discriminant validity was also established by ensuring
the square root of A VE exceeded all paired correlations
shown on the diagonal of the correlation matrix in Table 7.
Table 7 also shows the descriptive statistics and correlations
for the five constructs utilized in the measurement model.
Although triangulation was beyond the scope of this
study, common method bias was reduced by assuring
respondent anonymity, using specific and concise language
in the survey items, and deriving the scales to measure the
constructs from various sources (Podsakoff et al. 2003).
Common method bias was also addressed post hoc by using
Harman’s single-factor method and the Common Latent
Factor (CLF) method (MacKenzie and Podsakoff 2012). We
compared standardized regression weights for analyses run
with and without the CLF. They were determined not to be
significant; therefore, the CLF was not retained in the struc-
tured model (Yüksel 2017).

Direct effects
Two of the four direct effects on consumer engagement were
positive and significant. The direct paths from social media
information seeking (H1: β = 0.042; p > 0.050) and opinion
leadership (H4: β = 0.075; p > 0.050) to consumer engage -
ment were not significant. Therefore, H1 and H4 were not
supported. Social media popularity (H3: β = 0.286; p <
0.001) and social media trust (H: β = 0.188; p < 0.001) each
had positive, direct relationships with consumer engage -
ment, supporting H3 and H7 (Table 8).
Indirect effects
The five hypothesized indirect pathways to consumer
engagement were positive and significant. First, both indi -
rect paths from opinion leadership were supported. Opinion
leadership had a positive path to social media information
seeking (H5: β = 0.357; p < 0.001) and to social media pop-
ularity (H6: β = 0.488; p < 0.001), supporting H5 and H6.

supported. Social media trust had a positive path to social
media information seeking (H8: β = 0.331; p < 0.001) and
to social media popularity (H9: β = 0.167; p =0.004), sup-
porting H8 and H9.

Moderating influences
We added gender, social media usage, year in school, and
course enrolled in to the full structural path model to test
whether these variables meaningfully changed how con -
sumers engage with social media. The critical ratio values
did not differ significantly between the original model and
the model with moderating influences. Therefore, although
introducing these variables slightly changed the regres -
sion weights, they did not change the outcome of whether
to accept or reject the hypotheses regarding the direct and
indirect paths.
Discussion
This study's results make valuable theoretical contributions
to the marketing discipline and offer important practical
implications for businesses using social media marketing.
Social media use is common among consumers and busi -
nesses, with continued growth projected (Digital Applied
2026). Firms also continue to increase spending on social
media marketing compared to traditional media outlets
(Ascani and Ancillai 2026). Further increases are planned
as brands reach out to consumers where they congregate
online. Therefore, insights that help companies develop an
effective, data-driven social media marketing strategy offer
a competitive advantage and a stronger return on digital
marketing investment (Wang et al. 2024).
Social media is based on networks among consumers
and has extended to connections between consumers and
brands. Therefore, research regarding social media mar -
keting has focused on consumer engagement (Gligor et al.
2023). This study investigated the influence of social media
dispositions and goals on consumer engagement. The rela -
tionships between consumer engagement and its anteced -
ents were viewed through the lens of uses and gratifications
theory (Özer and Uğurhan 2026). This study adds to the lit-
erature by establishing hedonic and social motivations as
goals for online consumer engagement.
The model responds to the call for research on consumer
factor antecedents to consumer engagement by Vander
Schee et al. (2020) and Vander Schee et al. (2025), namely,
social media dispositions and social media goals. Previ -
ous studies have examined social media goals as a form
of consumer motivation for online engagement (Aguirre
et al. 2023; Blut et al. 2023). This study focused on social
media information seeking and social media popularity as
social media goals. Contrary to other studies (Balabanis and
Chatzopoulou 2025; Polimerou and Spais 2025), the results
showed that the direct path from social media informa -
tion seeking to consumer engagement was not significant.
Although this finding is surprising, information-seeking
behavior in AI-curated feeds may align more closely with
passive consumer engagement, manifested as content con -
sumption (Krowinska and Dineva 2025), rather than active

The results empirically demonstrate that social media
popularity positively influences consumer engagement. The
social media popularity goal addresses hedonic and social
motivations, in which consumers seek fulfillment of self-
status (Chou and Lin 2023), fun (Barta et al. 2023), and
socialization (Balabanis and Chatzopoulou 2025). In con -
temporary networks, AI personalization drives these expe -
riences by serving tailored, relevant content. Therefore,
consumers may engage online for enjoyment, but they may
also seek to enhance their standing within the social media
community. This dual motivation to connect with others
online is reflected in the social media popularity construct.
This finding contributes to the literature on uses and gratifi-
cations theory, as consumers are motivated by social media
goals and progress to consumer engagement as a result of
social media popularity (Basu et al. 2025).
The results also show that social media information seek-
ing and social media popularity fully mediate the relation -
ship between opinion leadership and consumer engagement.
Opinion leadership leading to social media information
seeking is grounded in uses and gratifications theory (Chen
et al. 2002) and self-enhancement (Aguirre et al. 2023). The
motivation to obtain and share helpful information with oth-
ers partially drives the disposition to be an opinion leader
(Silver and Small 2023). The more consumers find infor -
mation useful and derive satisfaction from sharing it, the
more likely they are to seek it on social media. This finding
builds on prior research suggesting that consumers may be
inclined to share information to gain recognition as an opin-
ion leader for general shopping knowledge and to provide
details about brands (Noguti 2022). Therefore, the connec-
tion to consumer engagement may reside only in the recog-
nition derived from the hedonic and social dimensions of
the social media experience. This is an important finding,
as opportunities to share information, coupled with pleasur-
able and recognition-based incentives, can maximize con -
sumer engagement (Fang et al. 2023).
The results show that social media information seeking
and social media popularity partially mediate the relation -
ship between social media trust and consumer engagement.

This finding builds on prior studies showing that consumers

social media trust and consumer engagement. Evolving
digital landscapes suggest that this trust depends heavily on
algorithmic transparency about how consumer data is used.
Moreover, this finding builds on prior research estab -

consumers (Petrescu and Krishen 2023), thereby fostering

reliability in online information (Leong et al. 2024). The
model also shows a positive direct path from social media
trust to consumer engagement. This finding adds to uses and
gratifications theory by suggesting that consumers who trust
social media may become more popular online, which in
turn reinforces their trust in social media (Özer and Uğurhan
2026).
Conclusions
Social media goals may change over time, moving beyond
the cognitive need for information to encompass the hedonic
and social dimensions of interacting with others through
highly personalized, AI-driven social media experiences.
Brands can highlight interactivity while addressing the
self-status aspect of social media popularity by incorporat -
ing automated badging or community standing as metrics-
driven mechanisms for consumers to display their level of
connectivity. Because a consumer’s online social profile
reflects their personal brand, marketers should promote
individuality by utilizing algorithmic personalization that
allows consumers to develop a unique social media identity
(Wallace and Buil 2023).
Social media dispositions may be more difficult for
brands to cultivate, as they are considered inherent con -
sumer factors (Gligor et al. 2023). However, they manifest
in social media goals, such as information seeking, and can
be activated to drive certain online behaviors, including
measurable consumer engagement. Social media informa -
tion seeking can be encouraged by providing timely, rele -
vant content that recommendation engines curate effectively
and that consumers can access easily.
Finally, to make consumers feel comfortable and secure
when using social media, brands can focus on establish -
ing credibility by addressing both positive and negative
reviews, highlighting authenticity through user-generated,
brand-related stories, and fostering trust by communicat -
ing data privacy protocols (Hochstein et al. 2025). Social
media trust develops over time through familiarity and
consistency. Therefore, brand strategies that emphasize
algorithmic transparency, data security, and reliability, such
as regular, interactive, and informative content, should be
considered.
Limitations and future research
As with all research, this study has limitations. Gen Z was
the intended target audience. Therefore, the survey was
administered only to college students, and the findings may
differ from those of other age cohorts. All survey respon -
dents were enrolled in a marketing course at the time of
survey completion and were therefore likely to be majoring
in marketing or a business-related field. The absence of rep-
resentation from other disciplines could bias the results. All
survey respondents were enrolled at a Midwestern public
university, and their social media experiences may vary by
residency.
This study examined consumer engagement through the
lens of uses and gratifications theory. Future research should
examine social media consumer engagement through other
theoretical perspectives. For example, prior research has
considered attachment theory (Vander Schee et al. 2025),
attribution theory (Carney et al. 2026), congruity theory
(Dang et al. 2026), service-dominant logic (Katsifaraki and
Theodosiou 2024), social capital theory (Lee et al. 2026),
social exchange theory (Kumar and Sharma 2026), and the
theory of close relationships (Chung et al. 2023). Continued
studies employing a variety of theoretical approaches will
broaden and deepen understanding of consumer engage -
ment with social media, making a valuable theoretical con -
tribution and providing further implications for marketing
practice.
Future research should also explore how branding strate-
gies influence consumer engagement. Rather than respond -
ing to consumer factors and goals, brands can take a more
active and intentional role in fostering consumer engage -
ment with positive branding consequences (Hollebeek et al.
2023). For example, the connection between social media
dispositions can be further explored with automated brand -
ing strategies, such as social media post characteristics (Liu
et al. 2024), advertising characteristics (Lee et al. 2026),
storytelling features (Odoom 2025), and generative AI dis -
closures (Carney et al. 2026).
The results show that social media popularity fully medi-
ates the relationships between opinion leadership, social
media information seeking, and consumer engagement. The
indirect paths involving social media information seeking,
a highly cognitive endeavor, were significant. Thus, social
media popularity as a goal also addresses the hedonic and
social dimensions of social media information sharing

tion now shapes these goals, future studies should examine
social media dispositions with specific cognitive, emotional,
