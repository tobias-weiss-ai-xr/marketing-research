Enterprise marketing management is increasingly shaped by an abundance of data
rather than by its scarcity. Customer contacts are distributed across websites,
marketplaces, social networks, mobile applications, call centres and physical points of
sale. Each channel produces indicators with different structures, update frequencies
and levels of reliability. Managers therefore face a practical problem: t he enterprise
may possess extensive information while still lacking a coherent basis for choosing a
market, revising a value proposition, allocating a communication budget or retaining
customers. The quality of marketing management depends on whether scatt ered data
can be converted into conclusions that are relevant to a specific decision.
The technological conditions for such work are expanding quickly. In 2025,
52.7% of EU enterprises with at least ten employees or self -employed persons used
paid cloud computing services, compared with 45.3% in 2023 [1]. Cloud infrastructure
improves access to shared databases and analytical software, yet access alone does not
ensure meaningful use. The OECD reports a similar pattern for artificial intelligence:
20.2% of firms in countries with available data used AI in 2025, up from 8.7% in 2023.
Adoption reached 52.0% among large firms and 17.4% among small firms [2]. The gap
reflects differences in finance and technology, but it also points to unequal analytical
competencies and management practices.
Research on marketing analytics supports this managerial interpretation. X.
Liang, G. Li, H. Zhang, E. Nolan, and F. Chen [3] establish a relationship between
marketing analytics, market agility and firm performance. M. A. Hossain, R. Agnihotri,
M. R. I. Rushan, M. S. Rahman, and S. F. Sumi [4] examine how marketing analytics
capability and AI adoption contribute to competitive advantage. S. Gupta, T. Justy, S.
Kamboj, A. Kumar, and E. Kristoffersen [5] explain the influence of big data on

marketing performance through the development and application of organisational
knowledge and capabilities. O. E. Olabode, N. Boso, M. Hultman, and C. N. Leonidou
[6] demonstrate that the contribution of big data analytics capability to market
performance depends on its integration with the enterprise’s business model and the
intensity of competition.  These findings direct attention away from the number of
digital tools purchased and toward the organisational ability to use evidence in market-
facing decisions.
For this reason, information and analytical support for marketing management
should be understood as an organised managerial activity that provides decision-
makers with relevant evidence about customers, competitors, market conditions and
the results of the enterprise's own actions. It includes data collection and verification,
the choice of analytical methods, interpretation of findings, communication of
conclusions to responsible managers and subsequent evaluation of implemented
decisions. This definition  is broader than a marketing information system. A system
may store and transmit data, whereas information and analytical support must explain
what the information means for a concrete marketing choice.
A useful distinction can be made among three components of this support. The
information component concerns the availability, provenance, consistency and
timeliness of data. The analytical component concerns the methods and competencies
used to identify pa tterns, explain deviations and estimate possible outcomes. The
managerial component determines whether the findings enter budgeting, segmentation,
positioning, product, pricing, distribution and communication decisions. Weakness in
any component reduces the value of the other two. Accurate data have limited practical
value when the enterprise lacks appropriate models or qualified interpretation. A
technically sound model also has little effect when its output arrives after a decision
has been made or is presented in a form that managers cannot use.
The connection between analytical work and marketing decisions can be specified
through the decision domains presented in Table 1. The table does not describe a
universal sequence. Enterprises may work with several domains simultaneously, return
to earlier  assumptions and change the depth of analysis according to the cost and
reversibility of the decision. Its purpose is to identify the evidence that each type of
decision requires and the errors that occur when managers substitute convenient
indicators for relevant ones (table 1).
The decision perspective changes the criteria used to assess information quality.
Completeness cannot mean the accumulation of every available indicator. It means that
the evidence covers the variables capable of changing the decision. Timeliness depends
on the period during which the finding remains useful. Accuracy concerns both
measurement and interpretation: a precisely calculated click -through rate may still be
misleading if it is treated as evidence of incremental sales. Comparability requires
common definitions, periods and attribution rules. The most demanding criterion is
actionability, because an analytical result should identify what managers can decide,
what uncertainty remains and which effect will be monitored after implementation.
Different analytical methods answer different managerial questions. Descriptive
measures reveal the current level and structure of performance. Diagnostic analysis

Prescriptive methods compare feasible actions subject to budget, capacity and risk
constraints. These methods are complementary, but a more complex model is not
automatically more useful. For a reversible operational decision, a simple cohort
comparison or controlled test may provide sufficient evidence. A strategic decision
involving entry into a new market requires broader data, alternative scenarios and
explicit assumptions.
The interpretation of analytical results deserves particular attention. Dashboards
often encourage managers to monitor what is easy to count: traffic, impressions,

reactions, leads or short -term conversions. Such indicators become dangerous when
they are detached from contribution margin, customer retention, cannibalisation and
the cost of the action. The same problem appears in AI -based recommendations. An
algorithm can rank customers by predicted response, but management must decide
whether the response is profitable, whether the proposed intervention is appropriate
and whether the data contain bias. Human judgement therefore remains part of
information and analytic al support, especially when objectives conflict or historical
data no longer represent current market conditions.
Organisational arrangements determine whether analytical findings influence
decisions. Marketing specialists understand customers and instruments but may lack
access to integrated data. Analysts can build models but may not know which variables
managers can control. IT units maintain infrastructure but do not define the commercial
significance of an indicator. Effective support requires agreed definitions, access
rights, responsibility for data quality and a shared formulation of the managerial
question before analysis begins. M. S. Rahman, M. A. Hossain, and F. A. M. Abdel
Fattah [7] identify holistic marketing decision -making as a mediating factor between
marketing analytics capability and competitive marketing performance. The finding
confirms that analytical capability produces value through management practice rather
than through technology in isolation.
The unequal adoption of AI by firm size also has direct implications for the design
of analytical support. Smaller enterprises rarely need to reproduce the infrastructure of
large corporations. They can begin with a limited set of decisions that have measurable
economic consequences, such as customer retention, campaign allocation or demand
forecasting. Data from accounting, sales, web analytics and customer communication
can be integrated at a scale appropriate to the enterprise. The priority is consistency of
definitions and regular review of decision results. This approach prevents scarce
resources from being absorbed by technically impressive projects with no clear
marketing application.
Two types of effectiveness should be assessed separately. Information and
analytical effectiveness concerns the reliability, relevance and speed of the evidence
produced. Managerial effectiveness concerns observable changes in decisions and
outcomes: a shorter response time, a more accurate allocation of expenditure, lower
acquisition cost, higher retention, improved margin or a rejected action whose expected
return did not justify its risk. The distinction is important because an analytical unit
may produc e high- quality reports that managers do not use, while a widely used
dashboard may still support poor decisions if its indicators are incorrectly defined.
Strategic and operational analysis should also remain connected. Strategic choices
define the markets, customers and sources of value that deserve attention. Operational
data test whether these assumptions hold in actual behaviour. If operational metrics are
optimised without strategic context, the enterprise may increase short-term conversions
by attracting low -value customers or weakening its positioning. If strategy is not
revised in light of current evidence, management may continue to allocate resource s
according to outdated assumptions. Information and analytical support should

therefore maintain stable decision criteria while allowing the evidence and
recommended actions to change as the market changes.
The study supports the conclusion that information and analytical support is a
managerial capability built around decisions, not a catalogue of software. Its
contribution lies in linking evidence requirements to specific areas of marketing
management and i n assessing analytical work by its effect on the quality of choice.
Enterprises gain value when they define the decision before selecting indicators,
combine analytical methods with market knowledge, document assumptions and
compare implemented actions with their economic results. The further spread of cloud
services and AI will expand the available instruments, but competitive benefits will
depend on disciplined interpretation, organisational cooperation and the capacity to
revise marketing actions when evidence contradicts established practice.
