This study examines public brand responses in the digital environment as a form of strategic
communication based on framing rather than as isolated reactive actions. Drawing on framing
theory and contemporary research in crisis and reputational communicati on, the article assumes
that the systematic use of stable response frames enables organizations to manage reputational
tension and maintain communicative subjectivity under conditions of continuous public visibility
on social media.   Method. The empirical material consists of anonymized public interactions
between a Ukrainian brand with international presence and its audiences on social media platforms,
including responses to comments from end consumers, professional communities, and B2B
stakeholders. Using a qualitative, inductive research design, the study applies framing analysis to
retrospectively collected practice-based data. Communicative stimuli were classified, and recurring
logics of public responses were reconstructed and conceptualized as strategic response frames. The
results identify several recurrent types of public inquiries that generate reputational tension, such
as misinformation, delegitimization attempts, emotional dissatisfaction, integrity- related doubts,
and demands for authenticity and transparency, and demonstrate a stable correspondence between
these inquiry types and dominant response frames. The findings indicate that public responses
function not only reactively but also as sense -making mechanisms that structure the interpretive
field around the brand. The study contributes to crisis and strategic communication theory by
extending event -driven reactive models toward a continuous framing perspective and offers
practical implications for developing internal response protocols and communication playbooks in
high-visibility and reputation-sensitive digital contexts.
Keywords: strategic communication, framing analysis, digital crisis communication, social me-
dia responses, reputational management

The digital communication environment has fundamentally transformed the interaction be-
tween organizations and audiences, making it continuous, public, and multi-layered. In social me-
dia, any brand message – whether a reply to a comment, a clarification, a rebuttal, or an explanation
– extends beyond an individual dialogue and becomes part of an open public space. Under these
conditions, organizational responses no longer function solely as customer service tools; they in-
creasingly serve as mechanisms for managing reputation and building trust in contexts of height-
ened informational tension.
Contemporary research on strategic and crisis communication on social media emphasizes
that the effectiveness of organizational responses depends not only on their timeliness or formal
correctness, but also on their ability to stabilize interpretations of events for a broader audience
and mitigate reputational risks (Larisu & Zulham , 2024; Başaran, 2024). A number of empirical
studies demonstrate that reactive or fragmented responses, constructed according to the logic of
individual customer support, fail to produce long- term trust-preserving effects in digital environ-
ments, where messages are easily replicated, decontextualized, and reappropriated by other actors
within the communication environment (Zhaldak & Kozii, 2024).
A distinctive feature of digital communication is the coexistence of diverse communicative
stimuli within a shared public space, including factual inquiries, emotionally charged complaints,
and destructive messages intended to discredit organizations or di sseminate misinformation.

Syntheses of recent research in digital crisis communication indicate that standardized response
templates do not account for this heterogeneity and may exacerbate reputational tension or lead to
a loss of organizational subjectivity (Abiodun & Nwabueze, 2025). Consequently, there is a grow-
ing need for structured response models that allow differentiation among types of communicative
challenges and enable the deliberate adjustment of response logic, tone, and content.
From a theoretical perspective, this issue aligns with framing theory, which posits that com-
munication does not merely transmit information but actively constructs interpretive frames that
determine which aspects of a situation become salient to audiences.  In digital communication,
brand responses function as frames that either stabilize public interpretation or, conversely, deepen
interpretive uncertainty. However, most existing studies focus on general principles of crisis re-
sponse, leaving operational framing models suitable for systematic application in everyday digital
interaction largely underexplored (Bielecka &  Feifei & Strzelecki, 2025).
The purpose of this article is to develop a framing model of organizational strategic response
on social media, conceptualizing it not as an isolated reaction but as a tool for managing reputa-
tional tension and trust. Specifically, the study aims to: (1) typologize communicative stimuli in
an organization’s digital interaction with audiences; (2) describe the behavioral principles under-
lying strategic response; (3) identify structural response frames and define their functions within
the public communicatio n environment; and (4) construct a generalized model linking zones of
reputational tension with communicative response frames.
This study contributes to the literature by conceptualizing organizational responses on social
media as elements of strategic communication with a framing structure that stabilizes public inter-
pretation. The proposed model also offers practical guidance for developing internal response pro-
tocols and communication playbooks in domains characterized by high public visibility and repu-
tational sensitivity.
Method
Research Design
The study adopts a qualitative, methodologically oriented design aimed at identifying and
conceptualizing recurring logics of strategic organizational responses in digital media. The meth-
odological foundation is inductive framing analysis, which is widely used in studies of crisis and
reputational communication on social media to reconstruct the meaning -making processes and
strategic structures underlying public responses (Coombs & Holladay, 2021; Abboodi  & Pileggi
& Bharathy, 2023; Abiodun & Nwabueze, 2025).
Unlike studies based on pre -constructed datasets, this research draw s on a retrospective ana-
lytical systematization of materials derived from professional practice in strategic communication.
This approach is consistent with practice-based research, in which analytical models emerge from
practical decision-making rather than being imposed ex ante (Cornelissen, 2020; Heide & Simons-
son, 2022).

Research Material
The study is based on a corpus of public digital interactions between an organization and its
audiences on social media platforms. These interactions emerged through routine communication
practice and include responses to user comments that varied in communicative intent and levels of
reputational tension.
For the purposes of this study, the material was retrospectively collected, systematized, and
anonymized. A structured list of examples used in the analysis is provided in Appendix A (de-
scription of the analytical material) and Appendix B (table of anonymi zed public interaction ex-
amples). These appendices provide illustrative evidence and support the transparency of the re-
search procedure.

Anonymization involved removing organization names, product identifiers, personal data, and
other elements that could directly identify a specific brand. This approach is consistent with ethical
recommendations for the use of practice-based cases in qualitative communication research (Heide
& Simonsson, 2022).
The case presented in this study was not selected as an isolated or arbitrary example. Rather,
the analytical model was developed through an iterative examination of multiple communication
cases derived from professional practice.
Throughout this process, recurring response logics were identified, compared, and refined
across different organizational contexts. The selected case represents an analytically illustrative
example in which these logics appear in a consistent and systemati c form, allowing their concep-
tual reconstruction.
This approach follows the principle of theoretical saturation in qualitative research, whereby
a case is selected for detailed analysis once the core analytical categories have demonstrated sta-
bility across observations.
In addition to the focal case, elements of the proposed model were observed in other commu-
nication contexts, including development sector communication and public sector interaction.
These contexts differ in stakeholder structure, institutional constraints, and reputational dynamics,
and therefore provide additional settings for assessing the broader applicability of the identified
response logics. This approach ensures that the identified response frames are grounded in recur-
ring communicative patterns rather than derived from a single empirical instance.

Analytical Procedure
The analytical procedure comprised three consecutive stages.
First, communicative stimuli to which the organization responded publicly were classified into
three broad categories: factual inquiries, emotionally charged complaints, and destructive or dis-
crediting messages. This initial classification provided the bas is for subsequent inductive coding,
through which more specific empirical categories of public inquiries were identified (see Table 1).
This categorization is consistent with contemporary approaches to digital crisis communication
analysis, in which the type of communicative stimulus determines the permissible boundaries and
logic of the response (Coombs & Holladay, 2021; Abboodi & Pileggi & Bharathy, 2023).
Second, a qualitative analysis of public responses examined the presence of explanation or
argumentation, articulation of organizational position, management of emotional tension, reliance
on facts or principles, and the strategic role of the response within the public communication en-
vironment. The analysis extended beyond linguistic characteristics and centered on the functional
role of responses as elements of strategic communication (Cornelissen, 2020).
Third, inductive framing analysis was used to generalize recurring response logics into ana-
lytical frames. The frames were constructed through comparative analysis of responses and by
correlating stimulus types with response characteristics. This inductive  approach facilitates the
identification of stable communication models that may serve as theoretical instruments for further
research or as practical response protocols (Abiodun & Nwabueze, 2025).

Analytical Categories
The analysis employed the following analytical categories: type of communicative stimulus,
response function, dominant response frame, response tone, and strategic role within the public
communication environment. Together, these categories enabled respons es to be examined not as
isolated utterances but as elements of an integrated system of strategic communication in the dig-
ital environment.

Results
Empirical Context of the Case Analysis
The results are based on the analysis of public digital interactions of a Ukrainian brand with
an international presence, operating in a highly competitive consumer market segment and main-
taining consistently high public visibility on social media. The cas e involves regular public inter-
action between the brand and diverse audience groups, including end consumers, professional
communities, and B2B partners.
A key feature of the case is the brand’s consistent practice of responding publicly to critical
inquiries rather than redirecting communication to private channels. As a result, a stable corpus of
publicly accessible responses has accumulated over time, pr oviding an empirically relevant basis
for examining structured reputational interaction in digital public discourse.
Another distinctive feature of the case is that the brand’s public responses demonstrate a stable
internal logic that does not vary depending on platform, wording of the inquiry, or emotional in-
tensity of the comment. This allowed the responses to be analyzed not as a set of reactions but as
a coherent system of authorial response frames that emerged through communication practice.

Reputational Effects of Framed Responses
The results indicate that systematic use of authorial response frames enables the brand to main-
tain its independent communicative position under conditions of sustained public tension. Public
responses not only mitigate individual reputational threats but also establish a reproducible logic
of reputational interaction that helps stabilize public interpretation of the brand.
Discussion
Relating Authorial Frames to Crisis and Strategic Communication Theory
The findings of this study demonstrate that public organizational responses in digital environ-
ments can be conceptualized as framing mechanisms selected according to the type of reputational
tension rather than as isolated reactions to individual comments.  The empirical analysis revealed
a consistent correspondence between different categories of public inquiries and distinct authorial
response frames, indicating that organizations rely on stable communicative logics when manag-
ing reputational challenges.
This relationship is particularly evident in responses to reputational attacks and misinfor-
mation. For example, when the organization was accused of operating in Russia, the response did
not engage in emotional rebuttal or defensive argumentation. Instead,  it restored the factual basis
of communication by clarifying the organizational position, presenting the relevant decision, and
referring to documentary evidence. This recurring communication logic was reconstructed as
the Reality → Decision → Resource frame, whose primary function is not to persuade the origi-
nal commenter but to stabilize interpretation for the wider observing audience.
These findings allow the authorial response frames to be situated within broader contemporary
theories of crisis and strategic communication. In classical models – particularly Situational Crisis
Communication Theory (SCCT) – the central focus is on aligning the type of crisis with an appro-
priate response strategy (Coombs & Holladay, 2010). Such approaches primarily conceptualize
public responses as reactions to discrete events that have already occurred.
In contrast, the authorial frames identified in this study are not tied to specific crisis events.
They operate as a continuous system of meaning- based responses that can be applied in crisis
contexts as well as in pre -crisis or routine reputational situations. This makes it possible to con-
ceptualize them not merely as tools of crisis suppression but as mechanisms for shaping public
interpretation in digital communication.
Accordingly, the proposed frames extend the logic of SCCT toward proactive reputational
subjectivity, in which organizational responses not only mitigate negative effects but also construct

Authorial Frames as a Response to the Limitations of Reactive Models
The empirical material demonstrates that most reputational interactions in digital communi-
cation are not triggered by discrete crises but by recurring communicative tensions. The analyzed
inquiries were predominantly associated with delegitimization, critical public evaluations, emo-
tional dissatisfaction, integrity doubts, and requests for authenticity rather than acute crisis events.
These patterns suggest that organizations increasingly operate under conditions of continuous rep-
utational exposure. This te ndency is illustrated by the  Q–A–Angle frame. Rather than rejecting
criticism that the brand was "mostly about marketing," the organization acknowledged the premise
of the question before explaining why communication itself constituted part of its strategic value
proposition. Instead of resolvi ng a single interaction, the response articulated the organization’s
philosophy and reinforced the brand's identity in public communication environment.
     Research on digital crisis communication emphasizes that social media have altered the nature
of crises by making them prolonged, fragmented, and continuously reproduced (Austin et al.,
2012; Eriksson & Olsson, 2016). The findings of the present study empirically support this obser-
vation. Rather than encountering isolated crises, the analyzed organization continuously managed
dispersed forms of reputational tension through recurring framing logics. Unlike reactive strategies
described in prior literature, techniques such as Reality → Decision → Resource, Myth → Fact
→ Expansion, and Q–A–Angle enabled the organization to maintain a stable communicative po-
sition without repeatedly shifting into defensive communication. This finding aligns with contem-
porary approaches to strategic communication that prioritize consistency and intelligibility of
meaning over speed of response (Cornelissen, 2020).

Framing Responses as a Form of Strategic Subjectivity
The findings indicate that the identified response frames perform a broader function than au-
dience appeasement or issue resolution. Across different categories of public inquiries, the ana-
lyzed responses consistently shaped public interpretation by defining which aspects of the interac-
tion were communicatively relevant. The response frames therefore functioned as mechanisms of

However, the present study extends this argument by demonstrating t hat organizations maintain
subjectivity not through avoiding criticism but through the consistent framing of public interpre-
tation.

relationship between distinct types of reputational tension and c orresponding framing logics. The
empirical analysis demonstrated that different categories of public inquiries –  including reputa-
tional attacks, delegitimization, critical public evaluations, emotional dissatisfaction, integrity
doubts, and requests for authenticity – were consistently associated with particular response

Preliminary Analytical Validation Across Communication Contexts
Although the empirical model was developed from the analysis of consumer communication,
its analytical applicability was subsequently examined across additional communication contexts
characterized by different stakeholder expectations and institutional constraints. This comparative
observation was not intended as formal validation but rather as an exploratory assessment of the
transferability of the proposed framing logic.
The comparison suggested that the structural relationship between reputational tension and
response framing remained stable despite substantial differences in communicative environments.
In development-sector communication, for example, response frames com parable to Reality →
Decision → Resource were primarily used to explain organizational decisions, demonstrate long-
term project sustainability, and reinforce institutional reliability. Rather than addressing isolated
criticism, these responses emphasized transparency in decision -making and strate gic continuity.
     A similar pattern was observed in public -sector communication, where responses were pro-
duced under conditions of heightened public scrutiny and political accountability. Here, response
frames analogous to Q–A–Angle and Anti-Justification enabled institutions to articulate their
position without adopting reactive or defensive communication strategies. Rather than responding
to individual accusations, these responses focused on preserving institutional legitimacy and main-
taining coherent public interpretation.
These observations suggest that the proposed model functions as a transferable system for
meaning-making rather than a collection of sector -specific communication techniques. While the
wording of responses inevitably varies across organizational settings, the underlying framing logic
appears to remain stable. This finding indicates that response framing should be understood as a
context-sensitive mechanism for shaping public interpretation through the strategic selection of
communicative logics, rather than as a fixed repertoire of standardized responses.

Limitations and Directions for Further Research
The study is based on a single analytically examined case, which limits the possibility of sta-
tistical generalization. However, the purpose of the research is theoretical model construction ra-
ther than empirical generalization.
The identified response frames should therefore be understood as applicable across different
communication contexts, while allowing for variation in their practical implementation depending
on industry specifics and institutional conditions.
Further research may expand the empirical base and test the model across multiple organiza-
tional settings.

Conclusion
This article proposes an empirically grounded approach to analyzing public brand responses
in the digital environment as a system of strategic framing rather than a collection of isolated
reactions. Based on a single in-depth case analysis, the study demonstrates that reputational com-
munication on social media can be described as a reproducible model of meaning- based response
that functions independently of a formally recognized crisis event.
The principal outcome of the research is the development of an analytical model linking re-
curring forms of reputational tension to corresponding response-framing logics derived inductively
from communication practice.  Unlike classical crisis strategies, which are primarily organized
around crisis type or organizational responsibility attribution, the proposed response frames oper-
ate through public interpretation management and enable the brand to maintain subjectivity under
conditions of continuous public pressure.
The theoretical contribution of the study lies in expanding the understanding of crisis and
strategic communication beyond reactive response logic. The authorial response frames illustrate
the possibility of shifting from situational communication decision s toward a stable architecture
of meaning that shapes audience expectations regarding the organization’s principles, boundaries,
and action logic.
The practical value of the study lies in demonstrating how these response frames can be used
as strategic thinking tools for communication teams.  Rather than serving as ready-made response
templates, they function as analytical models that promote consistency of public reactions across
different reputational contexts.
Overall, the findings confirm that systematic framing of public responses is a key contributor
to reputational resilience in digital environments and represents a promising direction for further
academic research in strategic communication.
