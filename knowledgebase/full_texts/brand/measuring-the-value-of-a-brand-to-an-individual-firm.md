Abstract
Traditionally marketers focus on brand equity metrics such as choice or purchase intention to assess the value of a brand
for	consumers.	Studies	of	firm	brand	value	typically	employ	a	cross-firm level of analysis  using data from a sample of
firms	to	show	that	brands	have	greater	financial	value	than	unbranded	or	weakly	branded	products.	However,	this	type
of analysis only establishes the value of brands in general. An important marketing problem is to measure the value of
a	brand	to	an	individual	firm.	This	calls	for	an	individual firm level of analysis . We propose a two-step method for this
type	of	analysis,	combining	a	consumer	choice	experiment	with	financial	modeling	to	compare	a	firm’s	cash	flow	with	the
brand to what it would be, counterfactually, without the brand. We provide an empirical proof-of-concept demonstration
of this measurement methodology. The goal is treating brand as an asset, not an expense.

The individual firm level of analysis
What	if	one’s	interest	was	primarily	in	the	value	of	a	brand
for	an	individual,	specific	firm,	irrespective	of	values	of
brands	for	some	set	of	firms?	To	address	this,	we	present
a framework for linking the value of a brand to consum -
ers	to	the	financial	result	for	the	individual	firm	offering	the
brand. Consistent with the marketing literature, this frame -
work uses a discrete choice experiment to estimate the value
of the brand to consumers. Taking into account the value to
consumers, it then develops techniques for estimating the
value	of	the	brand	to	the	firm	as	a	financial	asset.	In	this
context,	the	appropriate	measure	of	firm	brand	value,	or	for
that	matter	of	any	financial	asset,	is	the	contribution	of	the
brand	to	cash	flow.	From	a	financial	perspective,	the	value
of	a	firm	as	a	whole	equals	the	present	value	of	all	expected
future	discounted	cash	flows.	As	such,	a	brand’s	value	to	the
firm	is	logically	the	amount	of	its	contribution	to	the	firm’s
cash	flow	value.
More	specifically,	we	introduce	a	two-step	method.	Step
1: Following He and Calder (2020), the present research uses
a consumer choice experiment to determine consumer brand
value. Note that creating the branded versus unbranded con-
ditions experimentally obviates the need to control other
variables. Unlike with observational data, other variables
cannot	affect	the	experimental	comparison.	Step	2:	We	plug
these	consumer	choice	probabilities	into	a	financial	mod-
eling framework to simulate a rigorous counterfactual sce -
nario,	determining	what	the	firm’s	cash	flows	would	be	if
the focal brand were replaced by a weakly branded alterna -
tive.	The	difference	in	cash	flow	is	the	value	of	the	brand	to
firm.	We	demonstrate	this	two-step	process	using	an	empiri-
cal	study	of	a	coffee	brand.

perspective. Finance is the language of business. “Valua -
tion”	is	the	term	for	the	methods	used	by	finance	to	value
any business asset. Thus, the valuation of a brand implies the
use of some valuation method. Whereas consumer research
and marketing typically evaluate brand value using con -
sumer	brand	equity	metrics,	these	are	not	financial	metrics.
Firm brand valuations are provided for very large brands
by brand ranking companies such as Interbrand, BrandZ,
and Brand Finance. Typically, these valuations incorporate
a number of considerations such as market factors, how
much of a role brand plays in purchase decisions, and in
some cases consumer survey data (Salinas 2016). But they
often give discrepant valuations and have not been widely
adopted	by	firms	for	financial	decision	making	and	report-
ing (Sinclair 2016,) even though they receive media atten -
tion and have seen limited use in marketing research (e.g.,
Madden et al. 2006).
In	the	absence	of	better,	more	consistent	methods	firms
are presently led to treat brands as expenses (costs) rather
than as assets. In accounting terms an expense is an expen -
diture	that	is	used	by	a	business	to	generate	a	profit	in	a
period.	In	financial	terms,	an	asset	is	a	resource	controlled
by	a	firm	from	which	future	economic	benefits	are	expected
to	flow.	Logically,	a	brand	can	be	viewed	as	a	financial	asset
because brand equity is a resource largely controlled by the
firm	that	can	create	economic	returns	back	to	the	firm	over
future	periods.	However,	corporate	finance	and	accounting
rules (such as Financial Standards Accounting Board rules)
force companies to treat branding as a short-term expense
(lumped into selling, general and administrative expenses
(SG&A) on the income statement) rather than a long-term
financial	asset.	Companies	spend	millions	building	brands
but	have	no	standardized,	approved	way	to	put	that	asset’s
value on a balance sheet. or otherwise incorporate it into
decision making. In the United States brands can be treated
as assets only in the case of M&A activity or licensing
arrangements where a market price can be ascribed to a
brand.	But	even	in	this	case	the	brand	becomes	financially
“moribund” in that changes in brand valuation are restricted
(Sinclair and Keller 2017).
This	effort	to	further	the	development	of	individual	firm
level of analyses seeks to address this problem. This is not
to	say	that	cross-firm	analysis	is	not	valuable,	only	that	it	is
not directed at this problem. A key step in this development
lies in appreciating the counterfactual nature of the branded-
unbranded distinction.
Branded versus unbranded as a
counterfactual question
Our	approach	to	firm	brand	value	takes	the	form	of	an
experiment-enabled counterfactual comparison—compar -
ing a branded product to the same product, counterfactually,
without the brand.
This is a what if	question—what	if	the	firm	did	not	have
the brand. The question is the logical extension of a brand
being an idea in the mind of the consumer that is distinct
from the product (Oh et al. 2020). Brand equity can, in fact,
be	defined	as	the	differential	or	incremental	contribution
of the brand to the underlying product with no branding
(Borkovsky et al. 2017; Bronnenberg, Dubé, and Syverson
2022; Bronnenberg et al. 2015; Ferjani et al. 2009; Keller
1993, 1998, 2002; Srinivasan et al. 2005). Because it is a
counterfactual, the question obviously cannot be addressed
directly. A reasonable way of implementing it, however, is
to compare a branded product with an unbranded or weakly
branded product that is described as being otherwise similar.
Although a variety of consumer brand equity metrics
have been used in consumer research, we use choice behav-
ior	because	it	is	more	proximate	to	consequent	firm	brand
value.	By	definition	a	counterfactual	situation	is	one	that
cannot be observed factually. It can only be considered
or thought about. Because choice in this case is a choice
between an actual brand and the same product, counterfac -
tually, without the brand, a stated preference approach to
choice is called for (He and Calder 2020). With this approach
consumers are asked to state a preference for a branded or
an unbranded alternative in a discrete choice experiment.
Revealed versus stated preferences
There are two alternative approaches to consumer choice
behavior. One alternative is a revealed preference (in market
choice) approach, the other is to use stated preference (hypo-
thetical choice). As discussed, the counterfactual nature of
the branded-unbranded distinction is a major rationale for
our adaption of a stated preference approach. Other reasons
for using stated preference are discussed below. One issue
with stated preference is the possibility of hypothetical bias.
Although counterfactual choice is necessarily hypothetical,
the issue of hypothetical bias is discussed later in connec -
tion with future research.
Revealed preference based on actual market or pur -
chasing data provides a seemingly straightforward meth -
odological approach to measuring consumer brand value.
Unfortunately, the use of purchasing data is not as well
suited to present purposes as it might seem (He and Calder
2020). Studies such as the previously discussed Ailawadi et

al. ( 2003) research employ a cross-section comparison of
a large number of brands with their matching private label
products in the market. As noted earlier, however, actual
marketplace comparisons (observational studies) can be
subject to many uncontrolled variables, such as whether and
how the products are both available for comparison in the
first	place	(e.g.,	distribution	differences).	These	variables
can confound the branded-unbranded comparison. Since
the stated preference approach lends itself to experimental
treatment, the confounding issue does not arise.
With stated preference price can be treated experimen -
tally. With revealed preference market data, branded ver -
sus unbranded comparisons cannot be separated from price
without	difficult	to	implement	market	interventions.	Con-
sumers ordinarily can only choose between branded at one
price	and	unbranded	at	a	different	price.	Consequently,	price
can	mask	the	effect	of	brand.	Consumers	may	value	a	brand
but not choose it due to price.
It is also worth noting that our stated preference approach
is distinct from observational methods that infer a brand
effect	from	market	shares,	prices,	and	other	observed	mar-
ket variables. In those settings, the estimated brand term is
recovered as a residual demand shifter, and its interpretation
depends on the adequacy of the maintained controls, instru-
ments, and equilibrium assumptions. By contrast, our objec-
tive is to identify the incremental contribution of the focal
brand through an experimentally constructed counterfac -
tual in which brand and price are varied by design. For that
reason, our measure should be interpreted as a structurally
derived brand-evaluation metric, and not as only a substitute
for revealed-preference approaches.
Thus, with stated preference price can vary separately
from branded-unbranded. The experiment can include all
combinations of brand and price—the branded at its mar -
ket price, the comparison product at its price point, the
branded	at	the	comparison’s	price	point,	and	the	compari-
son product at the price point of the branded. This allows us
to	isolate	the	effect	of	price	from	that	of	the	brand,	whereas
simply comparing the branded product and the alternative
at	their	respective	market	prices	confounds	the	two	effects.
Although price must be taken into account in the branded-
unbranded comparison, other factors, if relevant in the same
way, could also be considered. But note that some variables,
such	as	level	of	advertising,	would	not	affect	choice	sepa-
rately from the branded-unbranded comparison. Depending
on the branded-unbranded comparison, price sensitivity
may of course vary, and price could even have a signaling
effect.
Also, we note that the logic for using stated prefer -
ence to evaluate consumer brand value is similar to that
of studies using conjoint measurement techniques (Green
and Srinivasan 1978, 1990; Green et al. 1981; Macias and
Rodriguez, 2018). Park and Srinivasin (1994), for example,
used a residual approach to measure preference for tooth -
paste and mouthwash brands. Their experiment used stated
preferences about brands and perceptual ratings of attri -
butes. They subtracted attribute ratings of objective attri -
butes (e.g., laboratory ratings) from the overall preference
measure to obtain the part of preference accounted for by
the brand. This residual brand equity was found to be cor -
related with subjective attribute ratings (e.g., clean feel -
ing). Beyond measuring these diagnostic sources of brand
equity, they also obtained a willingness-to-pay measure for
the brand and a comparison private label product, yielding
the	price	premium	for	the	brand	as	a	financial	metric.	Along
the same lines, Ferjani et al. ( 2009) focused on a conjoint
approach to derive measure of brand equity and related this
to a measure of willingness to pay for the branded product
versus	an	unbranded	one.	They	found	that	this	difference
was	related	to	firm	market	shares,	considered	as	an	indica-
tor	of	brand	profitability.	Neither	study	directly	treated	firm
brand	value	as	cash	flow.
Although conjoint techniques developed separately from
discrete choice experiments, they have evolved to use esti -
mation techniques such as logit (Hauser and Rao 2004;
Louviere et al. 2010, 2015). Our approach is thus to utilize
stated preference to measure consumer brand value using
a discrete choice model in a way that is consistent with the
literature. That said, our main purpose is to build on this
choice	model	to	obtain	a	measure	of	firm	brand	value.
We do this by combining a straightforward discrete
choice	model	with	financial	modeling	to	compare	a	firm’s
cash	flow	with	the	brand	to	what	it	would	be	counterfactu-
ally	without	the	brand.	Firm	brand	value	is	the	difference	in
cash	flow	resulting	from	the	value	of	branded	to	consumers
and the value of unbranded to consumers. Projected cash
flow	returns	are	an	accepted	standard	for	valuing	any	finan-
cial asset. Its use is endorsed by the Marketing Accountabil-
ity Standards Board (MASB, https://themasb.org) as part of
the development of internationally accepted standards for
evaluating brands (e. g. ISO 2019), with participation by
one	of	the	present	authors.	Next,	we	turn	to	the	specifics	of
the methodology used and report an empirical result to dem-
onstrate	how	to	use	it	to	measure	firm	brand	value.

coffee	versus	the	Unbranded	(ABC)	coffee	variable,	Price
was also a variable in the discrete choice experiment. A
large	cup	of	coffee	was	priced	at	either	Can$3	or	Can$6,
yielding four choices. Research participants responded by
ranking (by dragging) the four choices, presented in random
order, from most preferred to least preferred. Conceptually,
this	can	be	viewed	as	the	difference	in	preference	between
two	levels	of	branding	as	affected	by	two	levels	of	price.
Again, it is necessary to manipulate price in the discrete
choice experiment in order to fully describe the choice for
participants	and	to	estimate	the	effect	of	price.	It	is	possible

brand	might	need	to	be	specified	as	well	(e.	g.,	travel	time,
wait	time,	etc.)	However,	price	is	sufficient	for	purposes	of
this research and the product used.

Future research and limitations
The	methodology	developed	in	this	paper	could	be	refined	and
extended. Further work could of course investigate sensitivity
to modeling choices in measuring consumer brand value. And
our dataset is available for use in such work. More importantly,
two issues deserve attention here.
One is the issue of validation. This issue, however, is not
as simple as it might appear. There is no true or “benchmark”
value	to	compare	our	coffee	brand	value	to.	In	other	words,
there is no criterion variable with which to establish criterion
validity. We can of course compare our estimate of the value to
the	firm	of	the	coffee	brand	to	another	estimate	such	as	a	rev-
enue premium measure. We can compute a measure conceptu-
ally	equivalent	to	Ailawadi	et	al.’s	revenue	premium	measure.
The	revenue	premium	measure	computed	for	the	coffee	brand
value would equal CAN$535 million per year, compared to
our	estimate	of	CAN	$254	million	per	year.	But	a	difference
between the two measures would be expected. This is because
we measure the branded-unbranded counterfactual in cash
flow.	Ailawadi	et	al.	define	a	cost-adjusted	premium	but	do	not
compute this for lack of cost data. With our analysis, however,
we can compute a cost adjusted measure. Because it accounts
for	cost,	our	measure	is,	as	should	be	expected,	different	from	a
revenue premium measure.
To elaborate, Ailawadi et al. note that evaluating the
branded-unbranded counterfactual requires the underlying
demand function. However, they further note that the demand
function is generally unavailable. Ailawadi et al. recover
it from observational market data, as do more recent stud -
ies in the structural literature (e.g., Bronnenberg et al. 2015).
In contrast to this approach, we experimentally measured
the logit consumer choice and incorporated cost as follows.
Computed	with	our	experimentally	identified	counterfactual,

In sum, although Ailawadi et al. acknowledge the importance
of cost, the revenue premium measure is not cost adjusted. The
revenue premium measure provides a way of demonstrating
the	financial	relevance	of	brands,	but	it	is	flawed	as	a	measure

taken as a benchmark.
As this analysis points up, the only grounds for favor -
ing	one	estimate	of	financial	brand	value	to	the	firm	over	the
other lies in considering the merits of each, not in whether
one matches or validates the other. The same could be said for
other approaches. For instance, the royalty relief valuation, a
financial	accounting	approach,	is	based	on	finding	a	licensing
agreement for a brand that is similar to the brand being valued
(Paugam et al. 2016). The royalty fee for the licensed brand is
the	financial	value	that	would	be	forgone	by	the	firm	if	it	did
not have the brand. This of course depends on how comparable
the licensed brand is to brand being valued and on whether the
licensing	firm	plans	to	use	the	brand	in	the	same	way.	For	these
reasons, royalty relief estimates would not provide a reason-
able benchmark.
Again,	any	approach	to	firm	brand	value	should	be	consid-
ered	on	its	merits.	The	present	approach	provides	a	cash	flow
measure that goes beyond revenue premium measures. One
that requires additional inputs, but as demonstrated by our cof-
fee brand study these data inputs, which of course could be
subjected	to	refinements,	are	relatively	straightforward	in	not
requiring	extensive	or	difficult	to	obtain	data.
The second issue has to do with so-called hypothetical
bias—will consumers react to hypothetical brand choice situa-
tions	as	they	would	to	actual	market	situations?	Haghani	et	al.
(2021a, b) point up the need to specify the kind of bias that is
at issue with a particular discrete choice experiment rather than
merely assuming that it must match a natural setting. The kind
of	biases	of	specific	concern	in	the	present	choice	experiment
are what Haghani et al. refer to as the incentive consequenti-
ality of hypothetical responses and the lack of experience or
familiarity with choice alternatives.
To address these potential biases Haghani et al. recom -
mend employing mitigation strategies to correct for them. For
consequentiality, one strategy would be to develop scenarios,
or scripts, for the choice alternatives that emphasize thinking
about how the participant would choose in an actual choice set-
ting. For lack of experience, one strategy would be to give par-
ticipants time to think about using the choice alternatives and/
or using enhanced modes of presentation. In general mitigation
strategies alert participants to a particular bias and help them
treat hypothetical scenarios as real.
It should also be taken into account that counterfactual
thinking is in fact a ubiquitous feature of human information
processing (Byrne 2016; Roese 1994). Moreover, consumers
are especially able to engage in counterfactual thinking with
respect to brands (e.g., Krishnamurthy and Sivaraman 2002).
Thus, although mitigating hypothetical bias needs further
exploration, in our view the use of counterfactual thinking in
measuring	firm	brand	value	is	more	a	feature	than	a	bug.
Limitations. A key boundary condition of our demonstra-
tion study lies in its empirical scope, which relies on a single
product category, one focal brand, and a particular comparator
(“ABC	Coffee”).	Accordingly,	the	estimated	brand	effect	could
depend on a number of variables such as familiarity, market-
place legitimacy, or consumer distrust of the unbranded com-
parator rather than the value of the focal brand per se. While
our objective was to provide a proof-of-concept for translat-
ing	choice	probabilities	into	defined	corporate	cash	flows,	the
characteristics of this counterfactual comparison warrant fur-
ther consideration. Future research could systematically vary
factors such as comparator distrust.
Additionally,	a	limitation	of	our	financial	modeling	step	is
its reliance on unvalidated inputs rather than actual corporate
data. To demonstrate the mathematical tractability of the frame-
work, we used realistic market approximations for parameters
such as store count, marginal costs, segment distribution, and
profit-maximizing	behavior.	While	these	assumptions	were
necessary for our proof of concept, they may inherently con-
vey	an	unwarranted	impression	of	absolute	financial	precision.
The	highly	specific	value	generated	by	the	model	should	there-
fore be interpreted as a proof-of-concept for the process	of	firm
brand	valuation	rather	than	a	definitive	valuation	of	the	focal
asset.
Implications. Brands are part of a growing shift from
an economy dominated by tangible capital to one driven by
investments in intangible assets such as brands. Clearly the
stock market values companies at a large multiple of book val-
ues. Companies even trade at a multiple of replacement costs
for	their	tangible	assets	(Tobin’s	Q).	The	correlation	of	annual
changes in reported earnings with annual variations in stock
returns has decreased substantially over time. Brands as intan-
gible assets are obviously part of the reason for these departures.
Yet	information	about	financial	brand	values	for	companies	is

Measuring the value of a brand to an individual firm
not available. Lists of “top 100 brands” by ranking companies
do	not	begin	to	fill	this	information	gap	(Leit2022).	Investors
use SG&A expenditures, which are growing over time, but
this information only partially recognizes brand value (Banker
et al. 2019).	Capital	markets	may	be	efficient	with	respect	to
available information but not with unavailable information.
Although accounting scholars debate the wisdom of putting
brand values on the balance sheet (Lev 2008; Skinner 2008),
as Mizik and Nissim (2011) point out there are other report-
ing mechanisms that could make such information, if available,
accessible to investors. Further development of and use of the
individual	firm	level	of	analysis	of	firm	brand	value	could	have
broad relevance.
In this larger context, advances in the analysis of individ-
ual	firm	level	brand	value	could	enable	firms	to	track	yearly
changes	in	firm	brand	value	along	with	associated	changes	in
branding investments. As discussed, this would be a marked
departure from treating brands as an expense. If widely adopted,
this practice in turn could lead to improved studies using cross-
firm	analyses.	Given	that	firm	brand	value	is	an	organizational-
level	variable,	studies	could	investigate	the	relationship	of	firm
brand	value	to	any	number	of	firm	variables	such	as	product
innovation, employee satisfaction, and investor relations.
