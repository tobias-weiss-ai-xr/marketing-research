Abstract:  Regional labels are promoted as tools of rural identity and place-based development,
but their role in post -socialist peripheries remains underexplored. This article
examines the Borsod –Torna–Gömör regional label in Northeast Hungary and its
contribution to agrotourism. The study combines aggregated survey results covering
56 certified members with seven expert interviews and document analys is.
The findings show that the label strengthens territorial identity, cooperation and
quality signalling, while weak marketing, limited digital visibility and fragile
institutional support constrain its wider impact. The case shows that regional labels
can act as institutionalised instruments of place -based branding, but their resilience-
building capacity remains conditional.

1. Introduction
Rural regions on the geographical margins of Europe often struggle with persistent
disadvantages. These include demographic decline, the outflow of younger generations , and
limited local employment opportunities. The situation is particularly pressing in Central and

373/563

Eastern Europe (CEE), where many peripheral rural areas remain positioned at the edge of
national development strategies and European cohesion policy debates (Rodríguez-Pose, 2013).
One response to these challenges has been the creation of regional labels and place -based
branding initiatives. These instruments seek to reinforce territorial identity, valorise local
products and services, and build connections between agriculture, tourism and local
development (Kavaratzis, 2004; Anholt, 2010; Csurgó & Megyesi 2015). However, a regional label
should not be understood simply as a logo or a promotional device. In this article, it is treated as
an institutionalised form of place -based branding: a certification and quality -signalling
mechanism that links local products, services and territorial narratives under a shared symbolic
framework.
While many Western European examples of rural branding and local product labelling have been
examined in the literature, less is known about how such initiatives function in post-socialist rural
peripheries. In these contexts, branding initiatives often operate under more fragile institutional
conditions, with weaker marketing capac ity, project-based resources and less stable forms of
cooperation (Müller, 2021). As a result, regional labels may strengthen identity and local
cohesion, but still struggle to generate wider market visibility or durable development effects.
This paper exa mines the Borsod -Torna-Gömör regional label in Northeast Hungary. It asks: to
what extent can a regional label support agrotourism development in a peripheral rural region,
and under what institutional and organisational conditions can it move beyond symbo lic
recognition? To address this question, the study draws on aggregated survey results covering all
certified members of the label network, seven expert interviews and document analysis.
The contribution of the article is twofold. Empirically, it provides  evidence from a post -socialist
rural periphery where local products, tourism services and territorial identity are connected
through a regional certification system. Theoretically, it links the case to broader debates on rural
place-based branding, instit utional capacity, territorial capital and resilience (Gonda, Angler
& Csóka, 2021; Bock, 2016). The article argues that regional labels can function as institutional
infrastructures for rural development, but their impact depends on the organisational capa city,
knowledge transfer and coordination mechanisms that allow symbolic value to become
a practical development resource. In doing so, the article highlights both the promises and
the structural limits of branding initiatives in the Central and Eastern European periphery.

2.  Theoretical background
Territorial branding has become an important theme in discussions of rural and regional
development. Much of the early debate focused on cities, but the logic of branding has gradually
been applied to smaller regions and rural peripheries as well (Kavaratz is, 2004; Pike, 2009).
Scholars agree that place brands are more than visual logos or slogans. They are symbolic
frameworks that link identity, reputation and markets, and their meaning is continuously shaped
through interactions between internal and exter nal stakeholders (Kavaratzis & Hatch, 2013).
Anholt (2010) described place branding as “the management of place reputation”, stressing that

374/563

communities do not simply promote themselves but actively construct how they are perceived
in wider networks. Szondi  (2007) added that, especially in Central and Eastern Europe, where
identity narratives were disrupted by regime change, branding can be a way of reconstructing
local meaning.
In this article, place -based branding is understood as the broader process through which
territorial identity, reputation and local assets are mobilised for development purposes
(Kavaratzis, 2004; Anholt, 2010; Kavaratzis & Hatch, 2013). A regional label is treated as one
specific institutionalised form of this process: a certifi cation and quality -signalling mechanism
that connects local products, services and territorial narratives under a shared symbolic
framework (Kašková & Chromý, 2014; Jaďuďová et al., 2023). In this sense, the terms are not
used interchangeably. Place-based branding provides the conceptual frame, while regional labels
refer to the concrete organisational instruments through which branding is enacted in rural
development practice (Donner et al., 2017; de San Eugenio-Vela & Barniol-Carcasona, 2015).
In rural contexts, the stakes are often higher than in cities. Rural regions rarely compete for global
visibility, but they struggle with demographic decline, weak economies and limited institutional
capacities. Rural place branding has therefore been discussed not only as a promotional activity,
but also as a possible element of endogenous rural development. Domínguez García et al. (2013)
emphasise the importance of developing an “inner brand” based on shared local narratives and
territorial resources. Donner et al. (2017) further show that rural place branding can support
endogenous development when it is embedded in local food and tourism networks, stakeholder
cooperation and shared values. Similarly, de San Eugenio -Vela and Barniol -Carcasona (2015)
argue that rural  branding may contribute to local development by strengthening collective
territorial identity and revalorising endogenous resources. Here, branding may help to reframe
disadvantage into uniqueness: authenticity, tradition, landscapes and gastronomy can be turned
into assets. The Italian Slow Food movement illustrates how local gastronomy and traditional
food cultures can be mobilised through an internationally recognised movement while remaining
rooted in place -based food practices and the valorisation of local food systems (Mayda, 2005;
Fonte, 2008). Austria’s Vulcano ham producers combine farm visits, storytelling and events to
create a brand that integrates food, tourism and education (www.vulcano.at 3). In Hungary,
the Éltető Balaton label shows how local products can be woven into tourism routes around Lake
Balaton (Kovács & Nemes, 2019). These examples suggest that territorial labels are not only
marketing tools but also vehicles for identity, cooperation and local development.
Comparable examples from post-socialist Central and Eastern Europe also show that regional
labels can contribute to rural development and regional identity formation. In Czechia, regional
product labelling schemes have been analysed as part of region formation and regional
institutionalisation, linking certified products to territorial identity and the re -connection of
production and consumption (Kašková & Chromý, 2014). In Slovakia, regional product labelling
has been examined as a tool for supporting rural development, local prod ucers, traditional

products and regional tourism (Jaďuďová et al., 2023). These cases are particularly relevant for
post-socialist rural contexts, where branding initiatives often operate under weaker institutional
conditions and depend strongly on local coordination capacities.
Yet the question remains: can branding alone trigger development, or does it simply mirror
existing strengths? Recent work on rural place branding processes stresses that branding
outcomes depend on the type of place, the initiating  actors, the support base, the purpose of
branding and the target groups involved (Gulisova, 2021). Scholars therefore emphasise that
labels cannot work in isolation; they require supportive institutions and stakeholder
coordination. Healey (1998) describe d institutional capacity as the networks of trust, shared
knowledge and collaboration that enable collective action. Amin and Thrift (1994) coined
the term “institutional thickness” to capture the density of actors and relations needed for
development. Without such depth, brands risk being empty shells. Rodríguez-Pose (2013) made
the same point in a different register: institutions determine why some regions prosper while
others decline.
This insight has practical consequences. In Western Europe, many branding initiatives have been
supported by the EU’s LEADER programme, which provides funding and organisational
structures. Müller (2021) shows how LEADER in North Rhine -Westphalia not only financed
projects but also created new governance arenas where municip alities, producers and NGOs
worked together. Labels could thrive because there was a web of cooperation sustaining them.
In contrast, in weaker institutional contexts, labels often struggle to mobilise producers,
coordinate marketing or maintain credibility (Braun, 2012; Lucarelli & Berg, 2011). This problem
is especially relevant in peripheral rural regions, where project -based funding often creates
short-term opportunities without necessarily building durable organisational capacity.
Territorial capital i s particularly relevant for understanding the developmental role of regional
labels because it shifts attention from promotion to the assets that branding seeks to mobilise.
Camagni (2009) described territorial capital as the bundle of tangible and intangi ble resources
that shape the potential of a place, including natural resources, cultural heritage, infrastructure,
networks of trust and institutional arrangements. Capello (2014) further emphasised that such
assets are highly place-specific and cannot simply be transferred or replicated elsewhere. In rural
areas, this may include local food, craft traditions, landscapes, tourism services and forms of
cooperation. Regional labels can make selected elements of this territorial capital visible, certify
them and connect them to wider market and tourism narratives. Yet visibility is not the same as
mobilisation. Peripheral regions often underuse their assets because of weak human capital,
insufficient infrastructure, limited market access or lack of organisation al capacity (Bock, 2016),
while even rich local endowments may remain dormant without appropriate institutions
(Rodríguez-Pose, 2013). A wine region may have centuries -old traditions, but without
cooperative networks and marketing strategies these traditio ns do not necessarily become
competitive advantages. The mobilisation of territorial capital therefore depends on
coordination, institutional capacity and the ability to translate local distinctiveness into
recognisable offers for visitors, consumers and external partners.

Examples across Europe illustrate this. In France, regional brands such as “Vin de Bordeaux” have
long mobilised cultural and natural assets into globally recognised products. In Portugal, thematic
tourism routes integrate architecture, crafts and gastronomy to valorise local heritage (Novikova,
2021). In Hungary, the Vidék Minősége (Rural Quality) umbrella brand demonstrates how
coordinated efforts can connect small producers into a national network with shared quality
standards (Kovács & Nemes, 2019). These cases show that territorial capital can only be activated
through deliberate institutional action.
Resilience adds a further dimension to the analysis of regional labels because it shifts attention
from visibility and identity towards longer-term adaptive capacity. In rural development
research, resilience is commonly understood as the ability of a system to absorb shocks, adapt
to external pressures and reorganise under changing conditions. Rural studies usually distinguish
between engineering resilience, which refers to returning to a previous equilibrium, ecological
resilience, which focuses on absorbing disturbances without losing core functions, and
evolutionary resilience, which emphasises adaptation and transformation over time (Davoudi et
al., 2012; Christopherson et al., 2010). For the present study, the evolutionary interpretation is
the most relevant. Regional labels are not expected simply to restore earlier rural economies;
rather, their potential lies in their ability to sup port diversification, cooperation, knowledge
transfer, and new forms of value creation in regions affected by depopulation, economic
restructuring, and weak institutional capacity.
In post -socialist contexts, resilience has a double edge. Bock (2016) point s to persistent
vulnerabilities: outmigration, dependency on EU subsidies, and fragmentation of local
institutions. Swain (2013) observed how rural communities in Central and Eastern Europe often
rely on improvisation and “force -of-circumstance” entreprene urship rather than systematic
planning. At the same time, new alliances, NGOs and grassroots projects have emerged, showing
that rural actors can adapt creatively when opportunities arise (Bock, 2016). This duality is
important: post -socialist rural region s are not simply deficient versions of Western European
cases, but places where weak institutional capacity, project dependency and local improvisation
coexist.
This raises a critical question: can regional labels contribute to resilience, or do they simpl y
repackage existing weaknesses? The available evidence suggests a more qualified answer.
Regional labels can strengthen resilience -relevant capacities when they foster cooperation,
encourage knowledge transfer, protect local know-how and connect producers to wider markets.
However, they do so only when they operate not merely as visual identifiers, but as
institutionalised tools of collective action. Interreg Europe’s policy evidence on regional branding
shows that collective territorial brands can increase SME visibility and competitiveness by pooling
resources, signalling local origin and quality, and linking local producers with tourism and regional
development strategies (Interreg Europe, 2021). The Austrian GenussRegion, institutionalised
through the A MA GENUSS REGION quality seal, illustrates this mechanism by linking regional
origin, standardised quality and culinary identity across rural direct marketers, food
manufacturers and gastronomy businesses, with external quality controls

(www.genussregionen.at4). Roostika’s study (2019) of collective branding in the Batik Banyuripan
SME cluster points to a similar mechanism at the producer level: collective brands can help small
firms overcome the costs of individ ual branding, protect local names and products, establish
quality standards, share knowledge and organise common marketing efforts. In this sense,
regional labels may contribute to rural resilience by supporting diversification, cooperation,
quality governance, knowledge sharing and the continuity of local productive traditions.
Yet this contribution should not be treated as automatic. Recent evidence on Italian inner areas
shows that the relationship between place -based labels and economic resilience is mi xed.
Compagnucci, Faggian and Urso (2025) examine whether labels linked to agricultural quality,
landscape protection and cultural heritage helped municipalities mitigate the effects of the 2008
Great Recession. Their results suggest a mixed and partly adv erse relationship between place -
branding factors and resilience patterns, rather than a straightforward positive effect. This
suggests that labels may also reproduce existing inequalities when they are weakly connected to
local production systems, captured by stronger actors, or disconnected from broader territorial
strategies. Their resilience -building capacity , therefore, remains conditional on institutional
support, active participation by local actors, and the ability to turn symbolic recognition into
practical cooperation.
Taken together, the literature indicates that rural place branding, institutional capacity, territorial
capital and resilience are interdependent rather than separate. In the framework used here,
regional labels are understood as institutionalised instruments of place-based branding. Branding
and identity make local assets visible and provide symbolic cohesion. Institutional capacity builds
networks of trust and governance, while territorial capital represents the tangible and intangible
resources of the region. Resilience emerges from the interaction of these dimensions, reflecting
the adaptive capacity of peripheral rural areas. Figure 1 summarises this conceptual framework.

3.  Methodology
3.1 Research design
The study employed a mixed-methods case study design, combining quantitative and qualitative
approaches in order to capture both the structural characteristics of the label network and
the perceptions of local and institutional actors. Mixed -methods research is particularly useful
when numerical patterns need to be interpreted together with contextual and institutional
explanations (Bryman, 2016; Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018). This approach is also well suited to
peripheral rural regions, where official statistics are often scarce, fragmented or insufficient for
understanding local development processes.
The empirical focus of the research is the Borsod -Torna-Gömör regional label in Northeast
Hungary. The label is operated by the Borsod -Torna-Gömör Association, a LEADER Local Action
Group that coordinates local development activities and manages the certification system
connected to regional products and rural tourism services. At the time of the research, the label
network included 56 certified members across two categories: Kiváló Tájtermék (“Excellent
Regional Product”) and Príma Porta (“Open Farm”). This clearly bounded membershi p made it
possible to conduct a complete survey of the certified network. A fuller description of the region,
the Local Action Group and the label system is provided in Section 4.
A comprehensive survey of all certified members of the Borsod -Torna-Gömör re gional label
provided an aggregated quantitative overview of members' characteristics, development

379/563

intentions, marketing practices,  and cooperation patterns. Semi -structured expert interviews
offered qualitative depth on governance, institutional capacity and agrotourism -related
development opportunities. In addition, document analysis was carried out on the internal
regulations, design manual and strategic plans of the Borsod -Torna-Gömör Association.
The combination of these sources made it possible to tri angulate aggregated member survey
results with institutional and strategic perspectives. A detailed description of the region and
the label system is provided in the following case study context section.

3.2 Data collection
The quantitative component consisted of a structured questionnaire distributed to all 56 certified
producers and service providers listed in the database of the Borsod -Torna-Gömör Association.
The population covered two categories: 26 holders of the Kiváló Tájtermék (“Excellent Regional
Product”) label and 30 Príma Porta (“Open Farm”) tourism providers. All 56 questionnaires were
returned, yielding full coverage of the certified membership. Given the small and clearly defined
population, the survey can be treated as a complete enumeratio n of the label network rather
than as a sample survey. The responses were processed and used in aggregated form;
accordingly, the survey results are used for descriptive analysis and graphical presentation, not
for respondent -level statistical modelling, r elationship analysis or formal classification of
members.
The questionnaire contained 19 questions divided into thematic blocks (Table 1). Most questions
used closed formats, including categorical questions and Likert -type scales, while several items
allowed open comments in order to capture individual perspectives. The survey was conducted
between August and October 2024, in parallel with the preparation of the Local Development
Strategy. This timing supported participation because certified members were a lready being
contacted through the Local Action Group’s planning process, the survey topic was directly linked
to ongoing strategic discussions, and respondents could see the practical relevance of providing
information.
The qualitative component consisted of seven semi-structured interviews with key stakeholders
selected through purposive sampling. Purposive sampling was used because the aim was not
statistical representativeness, but the inclusion of actors with direct knowledge of the label,
agrotourism and local development processes (Patton, 2015). Participants included local mayors,
leaders of civil associations, representatives of national agrotourism organisations, and
experienced producers or service providers certified under the Borsod -Torna-Gömör label.
Interviews were carried out in October 2024, both online and face-to-face, and followed a flexible
interview guide. Each interview lasted 45−75 minutes.

The selection of interviewees focused on three types of expertise: actors with direct

actors personally involved in the production of regional products or the provision of tourism

3.3 Analytical framework
The integration of quantitative and qualitative data followed an interpretative mixed -methods
logic. First, descriptive analysis of the aggregated survey results provided a systematic overview
of development intentions, marketing use, cooperation patterns and perceived challenges
among certified members. Second, qualitative interview data were coded thematically to reveal
the institutional and governance conditions behind these patterns. Third, document analysis
helped contextualise the label system as an institutionalised form of place -based branding,
especially through its certification rules, design manual and strategic documents (Bowen, 2009).
The final synthesis connected the empirical findings to the conceptual dimensions introduced in
the theoretical framework: place -based branding, institutional capacity, territorial capital and
resilience. This approach enabled moving beyond a simple description of aggregated member
responses and interpreting how the regional label operates as a development instrument in
a peripheral rural context, without treating the survey material as a basis for responden t-level
modelling or formal typology-building.

3.4 Justification
This research design is appropriate for analysing regional labels in rural peripheries for three
reasons. First, the complete survey provides full -coverage aggregated evidence on the certified
membership, which is especially important in small rural networ ks where the population of
relevant actors is limited. Second, expert interviews provide insights into institutional dynamics,
governance constraints and cooperation problems that are often invisible in survey aggregates
or official statistics. Third, docu ment analysis enables an understanding of the formal rules and
strategic objectives of the label system.
By combining these sources, the methodology captures both the measurable aggregate
characteristics of the label network and the institutional conditions that shape its operation. This
is particularly important in post -socialist rural contexts, where development initiatives often
depend on fragile institutional capacities, project-based resources and the active involvement of
a small number of local actors (Jeney & Varga, 2016). The design therefore supports the article’s
aim: to analyse how a regional label functions as an institutionalised form of place -based
branding in a peripheral rural region.

4.  Case study context: the Borsod-Torna-Gömör region
The Borsod -Torna-Gömör (BTG) region lies in Northeast Hungary, directly along the Slovak –
Hungarian border. The area is linked to the historical county territories of Borsod, Torna and
Gömör, whose settlement and institutional relations were significantly res haped by the border
changes following the Treaty of Trianon in 1920. Today, the region covered by the Borsod-Torna-
Gömör Local Action Group includes 77 settlements across the Edelény, Kazincbarcika, Miskolc
and Putnok districts (Figure 2). Its settlement s tructure is highly fragmented and dominated by
small villages, many of them with fewer than 1,000 inhabitants.

Socio-economic indicators reflect long-term peripheralisation. The total population of the area is
around 85,000, but it has been steadily decreasing for three decades. Youth outmigration is
particularly severe: census data indicate that the proportion of residents under 30 has fall en
below 25%, while the share of people above 60 already exceeds one third. Unemployment rates
are consistently higher than the Hungarian average, and local incomes remain among the lowest
in the country (KSH, 2023). The collapse of coal mining and heavy i ndustry in the 1990s left
a legacy of weak employment opportunities and dependence on public works programmes.

Service provision is also uneven: many villages have limited access to healthcare, cultural
institutions or reliable public transport.
Despite these difficulties, the region retains significant territorial assets that can serve as a basis
for rural development. Natural resources include the karst landscapes of Aggtelek and Jósvafő,
including the UNESCO World Heritage cave system, as well as exten sive forests and river valleys
that support nature -based tourism. Cultural heritage is also rich: folk traditions, religious
monuments and historic mining towns provide potential for heritage tourism. Local gastronomy
– including honey, cheese, wine and traditional pastries – strengthens the region’s identity, while
handicraft producers maintain skills such as pottery and weaving. These resources form
the territorial capital upon which branding and tourism strategies can be built, although their
mobilisation depends on institutional coordination and market visibility.
Institutionally, the Borsod -Torna-Gömör Local Action Group was founded in 2007 within
the framework of the EU’s LEADER programme. Its objective was to design and implement
an integrated local development strategy by bringing together municipalities, civil associations,
local enterprises and community actors. In this sense, the Local Action Group created
an institutional platform for cooperation in a fragmented rural area. One of its flagship initiatives
was the creation of the Borsod -Torna-Gömör regional label, developed as both a quality
assurance tool and a place-based branding instrument. The label was designed to connect local
products and rural tourism services under a common territorial identity and visual framework.
The regional label is divided into two categories. The Kiváló Tájtermék (“Excellent Regional
Product”) certifies food and craft producers, such as winemakers, beekeepers and traditional
artisans. The Príma Porta (“Open Farm”) ca tegory targets rural tourism providers offering
accommodation, catering or experience -based services, including farm visits and local food -
related activities. Certification is governed by an Arculati Kézikönyv (Design Manual), which sets
quality criteria a nd regulates the use of the logo. In practice, certification involves application,
evaluation by the Local Action Group committee and continued compliance with the label’s
requirements. The certified membership includes food producers, craft producers, gue sthouse
owners, tourism providers and cultural organisations, linking agricultural production, local
services and rural tourism within a single territorial framework.
The significance of the Borsod -Torna-Gömör label therefore lies not only in its role as
a certification scheme, but also in its institutional reach. By integrating producers and tourism
providers, it offers a local mechanism through which territorial identity, quality signalling and
rural development ambitions can be connected. At the same time, it operates in a region marked
by demographic decline, fragmented settlements, weak market access and limited institutional
resources. This makes the case particularly suitable for examining how regional labels function
as institutionalised instruments of place-based branding in post-socialist rural peripheries.
For this article, the case is used to examine how the dimensions identified in the conceptual
framework – place-based branding, institutional capacity, territorial capital and resilience –
interact in a disadvantaged rural context. The Borsod -Torna-Gömör label mobilises local assets

and identity, but it also reveals the structural constraints that shape place-based development in
peripheral regions. Its value as a case therefore lies precisely in this tension: it is neither a simple
success story nor a failed branding initiative, but an example of how rural labels may create
symbolic and institutional foundations for development while remaining dependent on fragile
local capacities.

5.4 Synthesis: symbolic cohesion with limited development capacity
Taken together, the results show that the Borsod -Torna-Gömör regional lab el is stronger as
an internal identity and cooperation framework than as an externally visible market instrument.
It helps certified actors connect their products and services to a shared territorial narrative,
supports mutual recommendation and creates a basis for cooperation between local products
and rural tourism services. These are substantial achievements in a fragmented peripheral
region.
At the same time, the label’s external developmental capacity remains fragile. Weak marketing,
limited digital vi sibility, demographic pressures and unstable institutional resources restrict its
ability to generate broader market recognition or stronger tourism flows. The findings also
suggest variation in how members use the label: some actors appear to use it activ ely in
promotion and cooperation, while others rely on it mainly as a symbolic sign of belonging. Given
the aggregated nature of the survey results, this variation is treated here as an interpretative
observation rather than as a formal typology. This dist inction is important for the discussion:
the developmental value of a regional label depends not only on the existence of certification,
but also on the institutional capacities that enable members to use it collectively and
strategically.

6. Discussion
The findings from the Borsod -Torna-Gömör case illustrate both the promise and the fragility of
regional labelling initiatives in disadvantaged rural regions. The discussion interprets the results
through four connected themes: regional labels as institution alised instruments of place -based
branding; institutional capacity and the limits of project -based branding; territorial capital and
future development pathways; and fragile resilience in post-socialist rural peripheries.

6.1 Regional labels as institutionalised instruments of place-based branding
The results support the argument that regional labels should not be understood simply as logos
or promotional devices. In this case, the label functions as an institutionalised instrument of
place-based branding:  it connects products, services, territorial identity and quality signalling
under a shared framework. This corresponds to identity -based approaches to place branding,
which emphasise that place brands are shaped through the interaction of internal and ext ernal
stakeholders rather than through communication alone (Kavaratzis & Hatch, 2013).
The Borsod-Torna-Gömör label is especially strong in its internal function. It helps local actors
recognise themselves as part of a common territorial narrative and supp orts cooperation
between producers and tourism providers. This is consistent with rural place branding literature,
which shows that branding can contribute to endogenous development when it is embedded in
local networks, shared values and stakeholder coope ration (Domínguez García et al., 2013;
Donner et al., 2017; de San Eugenio-Vela & Barniol-Carcasona, 2015).

391/563

At the same time, the findings show the limits of symbolic cohesion. Internal identity does not
automatically translate into external recognition. The label is meaningful to members, but its
market visibility remains limited. This distinction is central to the article’s contribution: regional
labels may create the symbolic and relational foundations of place -based branding, but their
developmental im pact depends on whether these foundations are supported by sustained
organisational capacity and market-oriented coordination.

6.2 Institutional capacity and the limits of project-based branding
The case also confirms the importance of institutional capac ity. The Borsod -Torna-Gömör
Association coordinates the label and provides the organisational framework behind
certification, visual identity and local development activities. However, the interviews indicate
that the label’s operation is constrained by li mited staff capacity, project -based resources and
reliance on voluntary effort. This finding echoes the institutional capacity literature, which
stresses that collective development initiatives require networks of trust, shared knowledge and
durable coordination (Healey, 1998; Amin & Thrift, 1994).
The contrast with better -supported branding initiatives is instructive. In more visible regional
label systems, stronger institutional coordination, professional marketing and integration into
tourism routes help translate local identity into external recognition. Similar conclusions appear
in studies of LEADER -related branding and rural place branding processes, where support
structures and governance arrangements shape whether labels become development tools or
remain symbolic markers (Müller, 2021; Gulisova, 2021).
The Borsod -Torna-Gömör case therefore illustrates a common problem in peripheral rural
development: project -based initiatives can create important starting points, but they do not
automatically produce durable institutional capacity. The label exists, the network is bounded,
and members recognise its value. Yet without continuous coordination, joint marketing and
professional support, the label risks remaining stronger as a sign of belonging than as
a development instrument.

6.3 Territorial capital, digitalisation and future development pathways
The case can also be interpreted through the lens of territorial capital. The region possesses
significant assets: karst landscapes, folk traditions, local gastronomy, artisanal products and rural
tourism opportunities. The label makes these assets visible and connects them to a shared
territorial framework. However, the results show that territorial capital is not automatically
mobilised. The weaker use of digi tal channels, the absence of a strong product database and
the limited role of marketing in development plans all indicate that local assets require
organisational and communicative capacities in order to become development resources.
This is where future development pathways become important. Training, digitalisation and inter-
regional learning are not only practical recommendations; they are mechanisms through which
dormant or underused territorial capital can be activated. Digital tools could make certif ied

392/563

products and services more accessible to visitors and distributors. Training could strengthen
members’ ability to communicate the value of the label. Inter -regional learning could help
the network adapt practices from more established initiatives witho ut copying them
mechanically.
International and Hungarian examples support this interpretation. The Austrian GenussRegion
programme shows how regional brands can benefit from national -level coordination,
professional training and joint promotion (www.genus sregionen.at). The Italian Slow Food
movement illustrates how local gastronomy and traditional food cultures can be mobilised
through an internationally recognised movement while remaining rooted in place -based food
practices and the valorisation of local food systems (Mayda, 2005; Fonte, 2008). Hungarian
initiatives such as Éltető Balaton also show how local products can be linked to tourism routes
and territorial identity. Against these examples, the Borsod -Torna-Gömör label highlights
the specific constraints of a weaker institutional and market environment.

6.4 Fragile resilience in post-socialist rural peripheries
The case also sheds light on how resilience is shaped in post -socialist rural regions.
The demographic challenges observed here – outmigration, ageing and limited generational
renewal – are not unique to post -socialist Europe. Similar processes affect many rural areas
across Europe. What appears more distinctive is the combination of demographic pressure with
weaker institutional capacit y, dependence on project -based funding, fragmented local
coordination and reliance on a small number of motivated actors.
In this sense, the label contributes modestly to resilience. It creates a framework for identity,
cooperation, and knowledge exchange that may help local actors adapt to external pressures.
Yet its resilience-building capacity remains conditional. Limited digital skills, fragile institutional
resources and weak external visibility restrict its ability to absorb shocks or generate
transformative change. Rather than producing resilience automatically, the label provides
an institutional platform that can support adaptation if strengthened by training, coordination
and sustained support.
This pattern corresponds to observations from other po st-socialist rural contexts, where
communities often combine creativity and improvisation with institutional fragmentation and
dependency on external funding (Bock, 2016; Swain, 2013). The Borsod -Torna-Gömör case
therefore demonstrates both vulnerability and possibility. Regional labels can contribute to rural
resilience, but their impact depends on the interplay between local initiative and institutional
scaffolding. Resilience is present, but it is fragile.

7. Conclusion
This article examined the role of the Borsod -Torna-Gömör regional label in supporting
agrotourism within a disadvantaged Hungarian periphery. Using a mixed -methods case study
design that combined aggregated survey results covering all certified members wit h expert

393/563

interviews and document analysis, the study revealed both the strengths and limits of regional
labelling in a post-socialist rural context.
The first conclusion is that the label contributes to place-based branding by reinforcing territorial
identity, supporting quality signalling and encouraging cooperation among local producers and
tourism providers. Its strongest effect lies in internal cohesion: it helps local actors connect their
products and services to a shared regional narrative and creates  a framework for mutual
recommendation and cooperation.
The second conclusion is that these achievements are constrained by structural weaknesses
operating at two interrelated levels. At the macro level, demographic decline, youth
outmigration, peripheral location and weak market access limit the development environment
in which the label operates. At the micro and organisational level, limited marketing capacity,
uneven digital competences, weak coordination resources and dependence on project -based
support restrict the label’s ability to become a stronger development instrument. These two
levels are connected, but they imply different types of response: broader rural and regional policy
support is needed for the macro-level conditions, while organisational development, training and
joint marketing can address the micro-level constraints.
The third conclusion concerns future development pathways. Training, digitalisation, inter -
regional learning and generational renewal could strengthen the label’s role, but only if they are
supported institutionally. Individual development plans and local commitment are not enough
on their own. For the label to become a stronger rural development tool, symbolic recognition
must be combined with sustained coordination, profes sional communication and collective
market access.
Beyond the local context, the case provides lessons for European rural development. It confirms
that regional labels can act as institutionalised instruments of place -based branding rather than
mere promotional tools. At the same time, it shows that in post -socialist rural peripheries such
instruments remain fragile when they depend on project-based resources and a small number of
committed actors. Place -based policies therefore need to go beyond creating l ogos or
certification schemes: they must provide institutional scaffolding, facilitate knowledge transfer
and support the capacities required for collective branding.
In sum, the Borsod-Torna-Gömör regional label demonstrates both the potential and the fragility
of regional branding in peripheral Europe. It succeeds in building identity and cooperation  but
struggles to translate them into broader  recognition and economic outcomes. Its experience
suggests that the future of regional labels lies in their abil ity to combine symbolic value with
institutional strength, thereby becoming more effective tools of rural resilience and
development.
