Abstract

This paper explores the antecedents and behavioural consequences of Green Brand
Equity (GBE) in higher education, using the University of Aveiro as a case study.
While GBE has been extensively studied in commercial contexts, its application to
academic ins titutions remains limited. Based on a survey of 351 valid responses
from students, staff, and researchers, the study examines how Green Brand Image
(GBI), Green Satisfaction (GS), and Green Trust (GT) influence GBE, and how GBE
in turn affects Green Purcha se Intentions (GPI) and Green Word -of-Mouth
(GWOM). The results confirm all hypothesised relationships except for the path
from GBI to GBE, which was not statistically significant. GS and GT emerged as
significant drivers of GBE, suggesting that affective and experiential elements
outweigh image in shaping brand equity in academic contexts. GBE was also found
to significantly influence both behavioural outcomes. The study provides theoretical
contributions to green branding literature and practical insights  for sustainability-
oriented university strategies.

2 Literature Review & Hypotheses Development

Brand image plays an important role in the markets where it is difficult to differentiate
products or services based on tangible quality features (Mudambi et al., 1997). It can be defined
as the consumer’s mental image of a brand  linked to an offering (Cretu & Brodie, 2007). Chen
(2010) argues that it is important for companies, as it is crucial for product differentiation and for
communicating functional, symbolic, and experiential benefits.
The image of green brands is valuable  because it deepens environmental associations,
creates brand uniqueness, and strengthens brand impression (Li et al., 2019). Chen (2010) defined
GBE as “ a set of perceptions of a brand in a consumer’s mind that is linked to environmental
commitments and environmental concerns”. Recently, Choubey & Sharma (2024) presented GBI
as the perception and reputation of a brand regarding its dedication to environmentally friendly
practices and environmental sustainability.
Satisfaction has been widely used to measure the relationship between consumers and
businesses (Chen, 2010). Satisfaction is a delightful degree of post -consumption evaluation or a
pleasurable degree of consumption -related fulfilment (Oliver, 1997). Traditionally, customer
satisfaction is perceived as a determinant of long -term customer behaviour (Jannah, 2024). Only
satisfied customers will repeat their buying behaviour and bring long-term benefits to businesses;
therefore, enhancing customer satisfaction is a major priority  for most companies ( Onat &
Güneren, 2024; Ranaweera & Prabhu, 2003).
Chen (2010) defined GS as “a pleasurable level of consumption -related fulfilment that
satisfies a customer’s environmental desires, sustainable expectations and green needs”. Martinez
(2015) described GS as a pleasurable sensation stemming from the product’s ability to meet
consumers’ expectations in an environmentally safe and sustainable manner. Lee et al. (2023)
defend that GS refers to the pleasant evaluation of the performance of a green product or service.
When a firm invests effort in improving its brand image, it can not only avoid the trouble
of environmental protests or punishment but also enhance customer satisfaction with
environmental concerns, sustainable expectations, and green needs. Because brand image is an

Trust is the belief that the other party is reliable and dependable, and that they are not
manipulative and committed to their  promises (Luhmann, 1979). In marketing, trust is often
defined according to social psychology studies, which maintain that trust is based on the reliability
and goodwill of the other party (Ha, 2022).
GT refers to individuals’ “willingness to depend on a product or service based on the belief
or expectations resulting from its credibility, benevolence and ability about environmental
performance” (Chen, 2010). Nguyen et al. (2023), following Morgan & Hunt (1994) definition of
trust that refers to the degree of trustworthiness and honesty in the partner, defined GT as the
willingness to believe in the good that the brand has promised to do in terms of the ultimate
interests of customers, stakeholders, and sustainable development.
It has been regarded as the “ willingness of consumers to rely upon products which they
have confidence in arising from their sustainable performance ” by Martinez (2015). It can be
recognised by the customer when a brand keeps its promises regarding green performance (Bulsara
et al., 2014).
When consumers recall the brand and exhibit a favourable attitude toward it based on their
experience, they are more likely to trust a brand's green claim . (Tung, 2024). Brand image
positively influences consumer trust by diminishing perceived risk and increasing the probability
of purchase at the moment of the transaction (Chen, 2010). Also, previous studies found a positive
relationship between GBI and GT (Chen, 2010; Martınez, 2015; Bekk et al., 2016).
Thus, the following hypothesis is formulated:

H2: GBI is positively associated with GT.

GBE is an evolving concept that integrates the principles of sustainability and
environmental responsibility into the traditional framework of brand equity. The overall concept
of brand equity is rooted in the idea of using it as a parameter to determine its value through brand
extension or as a product or service (Farquhar, 1989).
Aaker (1991) defined brand equity as “a set of brand assets and liabilities linked to a brand,
its name and symbol that add to or subtract from the value provided by a product or service to a
firm and to the firm’s customers”. In addition, Keller (1993) posited that brand equity can create a
differential effect on consumer response to brand marketing.
Based on the above definitions, Chen (2010) defined GBE for the first time  as “a set of
brand assets and liabilities about green commitments and environmental concerns linked to a
brand, its name and symbol that add to or subtract from the value provided by a product or
service”. According to Ishaq (2021), GBE is a collection of brand assets and liabilities related to
environmental, social, and economic problems, as well as eco -friendly pledges linked to a brand
that either increases or decreases the value supplied by the company’s product or service.
For Hue & Oanh (2023), GBE is understood as a set of consumers’ perceptions, behaviours,
and effects related to green and environmental concerns associated with a brand, its name, and its
symbol. Choubey & Sharma (2024) defend that green brand equity is the power and intangible
value of a brand’s association with environmental sustainability.
It indicates a brand’s value to customers, notably in terms of its dedication to environmental
responsibility and eco -friendly practices (Chen, 2010; Ishaq, 2020). It focuses on the emotional
and symbolic value connected to the brand’s sustainability initiatives rather than just the functional
advantages of goods or services (Choubey & Sharma, 2024)
Consumers’ perceptions of a brand’s value are influenced by its green  image. Consumers
value brands that relate to sustainability and environmental responsibility and this positively
influences GBE (Choubey & Sharma, 2024)

3.1 Target Population and Sampling

The target population consisted of students, researchers, teaching staff, and administrative
staff at the University of Aveiro, a public higher education institution in Portugal. Given the
diversity of roles and levels of engagement with the institution, t his population was considered
appropriate for examining the formation of green brand equity within a university setting.
A non-probability convenience sampling strategy was used, which is commonly adopted
in exploratory studies within academic institutions (Ha, 2021). The sample size was not determined
through a prior power analysis; however,  a total of 351 complete and valid responses were
obtained, exceeding the minimum thresholds typically recommended for regression analysis with
multiple predictors (Hair et al., 2018).
All questionnaires included in the analysis were fully completed. Responses were screened
to exclude incomplete submissions and entries that failed to meet the inclusion criteria (e.g., not
being affiliated with the University of Aveiro). No responses were eliminated after this screening,
indicating a high level of data quality.

3.2 Data Collection Procedure

Data collection took place between 17 April and 7 May 2025 via an online survey
administered via FormsUA, a secure, customised platform developed by the University of Aveiro.
Participants were invited by email and through institutional networks.
A pilot test was conducted with six members of the target population (two students, two
teachers, one researcher, and one administrative staff member). Minor wording adjustments were
made based on the feedback received to enhance clarity, but no major changes were necessary.

university, and length of institutional affiliation), and the model constructs.

3.4 Data Analysis

Quantitative data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 30. The analysis
began with descriptive statistics to characterise the sample and proceeded with reliability testing
for all measurement scales. Cronbach’s alpha values for all constructs e xceeded 0.90, indicating
excellent internal consistency.
To test the research hypotheses, a series of linear and multiple regression models were
estimated. Each regression was preceded by verification of the underlying assumptions, including
linearity, independence of errors, homoscedasticity, and absence of multicollinearity.
Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Tolerance
statistics. In the model predicting GBE based on GBI, GS, and GT, all VIF values were below the
commonly accepted threshold of 10: GBI (VIF = 4.548), GS (VIF = 3,095), and GT (VIF = 5,641).
These results indicate that multicollinearity is not a concern in this model. Similar findings were
observed in the other regressions, where all VIF values were 1,000, confirming the independence
of predictors in those cases.
In addition, Durbin -Watson statistics were examined to assess the independence of
residuals. For all regression models, values ranged from 1,634 to 1,958, which fall within the
generally acceptable interval of 1,5 to 2,5, suggesting that residuals are unco rrelated and that the
assumption of independence is met.
Model diagnostics also included examination of residual statistics and standardised
residuals. Across all models, residuals appeared symmetrically distributed around zero, and no
extreme values indicative of  outliers were identified. Standardised residuals were within ±3.8 in
all cases, consistent with acceptable bounds for linear models.
Overall, the models demonstrated adequate fit and explanatory power. The model
predicting GS from GBI explained 58.7% of the variance (R² = 0.587), and the model predicting
GT from GBI explained 77.3% (R² = 0.773), both with high statistical significance. The multiple
regression model predicting GBE based on GBI, GS, and GT explained 39.8% of the variance (R²
= 0.398). Although GBI did not emerge as a statistically significant predictor of GBE in this model
(p = 0.063), GS and GT were both significant, confirming their role as antecedents of brand equity
in this context.

One possible explanation is that, in academic institutions, brand equity is not primarily
constructed through outward image, but rather through affective and experiential factors such as
trust and satisfaction. In this study, both GT (β = 0.314) and GS (β = 0.188) emerged as significant

in terms of sustainability, the effect of GBI on GBE may be neutralised.
Another potential explanation concerns the maturity of the stakeholder relationship. As
discussed by Ha (2021) and Delafrooz & Goli (2015), individuals who have been affiliated with
an institution for a longer period may develop a more critical view of its  sustainability claims. In
this study, exploratory analyses indicated that long -term affiliates reported lower levels of trust
and satisfaction, possibly due to accumulated exposure to inconsistencies or unmet expectations.
These findings highlight the nee d for authentic engagement rather than rel ying solely on image -

both GPI and GWOM. The effect of GBE on GPI (β = 0.517) aligns with findings by Hume (2010)
and Sharma (2023), confirming that a strong green brand can enhance stakehold ers’ intention to
remain affiliated with the institution. In this context, “purchase intention” can be interpreted as the
willingness to continue studying or working at the university, motivated by its environmental
positioning.
