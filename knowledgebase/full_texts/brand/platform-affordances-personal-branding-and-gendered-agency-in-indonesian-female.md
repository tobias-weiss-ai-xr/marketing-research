Abstract
Digital platforms have fundamentally restructured the conditions under which
musical artists construct public identities, negotiate market authority, and
navigate gendered constraints on creative labor. Yet the dynamics through which
female artists in non-Western, subcultural contexts achieve sustained platform
visibility remain underexamined in the extant literature. This study investigates
how Ramengvrl (Putri Estiani), one of Indonesia’s most commercially recognized
independent female rappers, constructs a coherent personal brand on Instagram
while simultaneously managing the gendered hierarchies of hip-hop culture and the
algorithmic logic of platform capitalism. Employing a qualitative interpretive single-
case design, the study integrates Peter Montoya and Vandehey’s [ 20] Eight Laws of
Personal Branding, Lasswell’s [18] Communication Model, and theories of algorithmic
governance [5] and platformization [ 21] as a coordinated analytical framework.
Data were collected through longitudinal digital ethnography of the @ramengvrl
Instagram account and semi-structured interviews with four purposively selected
informants. The analysis generates the theoretical proposition that, in Indonesia’s
conservative platform environment, Ramengvrl’s visibility rests on a mutually
constitutive relationship between technical specialization in rap, the strategic
commodification of a non-conformist persona, and a sustained visual signature that
maintains parasocial intimacy. The case further advances the proposition, warranting
future comparative testing, that technical mastery may function as a structural
prerequisite for gendered discursive agency in Global South hip-hop contexts, rather
than simply one brand attribute among many. These findings extend scholarship on
platform labor, personal branding, and female creative practice in Southeast Asian
digital cultural economies.

constructed, sustained, and commercially leveraged. Theorized as the “platformization

historically defined by hyper-masculine norms represents a particularly significant, and
comparatively underexamined, disruption.

2 Literature review
2.1 Platformization and the Indonesian music industry
The contemporary music industry is undergoing structural transformation through
“platformization” , the process by which digital platforms’ economic and infrastructural
logics increasingly govern how music is produced, distributed, monetized, and con -
sumed [21, 27]. In Indonesia and the broader ASEAN region, this process is mediated
by the cultural hegemony of Western pop music, requiring domestic artists to negoti -
ate for symbolic legitimacy on global and local terms simultaneously [ 13]. Social media
platforms such as Instagram have become primary sites of this negotiation: they enable
self-presentation, audience interaction, and cultural meaning-making while imposing
algorithmic filters that determine whose content achieves visibility [ 14]. For indepen -
dent artists operating outside major label structures, strategic management of platform
presence is no longer supplementary to artistic practice; it has become constitutive of it.
2.2 The independent hip-hop scene and DIY authenticity
Hip-hop culture has historically functioned as a dialectical force resisting the corpora -
tization of popular music. The independent, “do-it-yourself” tradition within the genre
enables artists to maintain creative and economic autonomy while constructing alter -
native fields of cultural production outside institutional gatekeeping [ 27]. Central to
hip-hop credibility is the norm of “realness” , the demand that an artist’s public persona

cohere with their actual social experience and artistic practice, which serves as the pri -
mary currency of subcultural legitimacy. Digital platforms have amplified both the reach
and the contradictions of this ethos: the same affordances that enable autonomy also
impose commercial logics that can erode the subcultural authenticity they appear to
celebrate.
2.3 Personal branding, micro-celebrity, and algorithmic visibility
Personal branding, the practice of strategically managing one’s public identity to achieve
professional and social positioning, has become central to creative labor in platform
economies [ 12]. Rooted in the neoliberal construction of the individual as a market-
facing entity, personal branding compels creative practitioners to differentiate their self-
expression to attract audiences, collaborators, and revenue streams [ 17]. In social media
contexts, this practice converges with “micro-celebrity”: the cultivation of an audience
through persona-driven digital communication characterized by parasocial intimacy
and sustained emotional engagement [16, 27].
An expanding body of work has examined how digital identities are constructed and
maintained on platforms such as Instagram. Elyamany [ 9] demonstrates how virtual
influencers construct layered digital identities through chronotopic visual semiotics
and strategically calibrated authenticity claims. Elyamany et al. [ 11] extend this analy -
sis to show how trans-disciplinary identity performance generates parasocial investment
among platform audiences regardless of the performer’s ontological status. Although
these studies focus on artificial rather than human performers, they illuminate the semi -
otic labor involved in building legible, trustworthy digital personas, labor that human
artists in subcultural contexts must undertake under the additional burden of genre-
specific credentialing demands. Elyamany [ 10] further reveals how micro-level semiotic
choices in Instagram posting, emoji use, translanguaging practices, function as resources
for constructing virtual authenticity, underscoring that digital identity construction
operates at every scale of communicative practice. Montoya and Vandehey’s [ 20] Eight
Laws of Personal Branding provide a structured evaluative framework for analyzing
these practices, applied here not as universal prescriptions but as context-sensitive heu -
ristics requiring critical mediation for subcultural, non-Western settings.
2.4 Hip-hop feminism, gendered agency, and identity construction
The construction of digital identity is particularly fraught for female artists operating
in hyper-masculine subcultures. Social networking platforms serve simultaneously as
spaces of self-expression and as arenas of surveillance, stereotyping, and gender polic -
ing [2]. Within hip-hop specifically, scholars have documented how female artists must
perform a double legitimation: establishing artistic competence within a genre that gate-
keeps skill along gendered lines, while simultaneously asserting empowered identities
that challenge the genre’s patriarchal structures [22, 24]. Pough’s [22] concept of “wreck” ,
the active demand for recognition in a public sphere that has historically denied women
voice, provides an analytical lens for understanding how female rappers assert presence
not despite but through the resistant possibilities of hip-hop culture. In Indonesian and
broader Global South contexts, these dynamics are further inflected by local religious
conservatism, patriarchal social structures, and the specific constraints of platform

capitalism, producing conditions in which female artists must work considerably harder
for the technical legitimacy that grants entry into subcultural discourse.
2.5 Digital authenticity and the research gap
Authenticity in digital culture is a contested and inherently performative concept. What
registers as “authentic” online is frequently a curated performance optimized for plat -
form reception rather than a transparent expression of a stable interior self [ 5, 8]. For
hip-hop artists specifically, this tension is acute: the genre’s foundational demand for
“realness” collides with the economic logic of platform visibility, which rewards emo -
tionally expressive, personality-driven, algorithmically optimized content that may or
may not align with subcultural norms. Despite a growing literature on personal brand -
ing, micro-celebrity, and hip-hop feminism individually, comparatively little scholar -
ship has examined how these domains intersect for female artists navigating the specific
constraints of Indonesia’s conservative, platform-dependent music scene. This study
addresses that gap through an interpretive single-case analysis designed to generate
theoretically grounded propositions about the interplay of technical legitimacy, platform
governance, and gendered agency in Global South digital cultural economies.
3 Theoretical framework
Three theoretical domains are synthesized here, selected for their complementary ana -
lytical purchase on the phenomenon under investigation.
Peter Montoya and Vandehey’s [ 20] Eight Laws of Personal Branding, specialization,
leadership, personality, distinctiveness, visibility, unity, persistence, and goodwill, serve
as the primary evaluative structure. This framework should be situated critically: the
Eight Laws were developed in a North American corporate context that assumes rela -
tively unconstrained individual agency, commodified self-presentation, and a market
environment receptive to assertive self-promotion. Applied to a subcultural, female,
Indonesian artist working within the constraints of platform capitalism and conservative
gender norms, these assumptions require significant mediation. In the present study, the
Eight Laws are treated as analytical heuristics rather than normative prescriptions, scaf -
folding that directs interpretive attention without predetermining its outcome. Where
empirical data complicate or depart from the framework’s expectations, these moments
of friction are treated as analytically productive findings, not anomalies to be suppressed.
Lasswell’s [ 18] Communication Model provides an integrative scaffold for situating
each of Montoya’s laws within a communicative transaction: Who (Ramengvrl as sender
and brand architect) Says What (brand messages, aesthetic choices, lyrical content)
In Which Channel (Instagram as the primary platform) To Whom (followers, indus -
try actors, collaborators) With What Effect (market authority, subcultural legitimacy,
parasocial community). Lasswell’s model has been critiqued for neglecting feedback
loops, power asymmetries, and the dialectical relationship between communicator and
audience. Applied here, it serves as a descriptive mapping device rather than a causal
model. It draws particular attention to the “Effect” dimension, which, in the context of
platformized creative labor, extends beyond persuasion into the production of a specific
social position: the female technical rapper as subcultural authority. This reframing is
analytically consequential because Lasswell’s original model does not anticipate it.

Algorithmic Governance and Platform Affordances [ 5, 21] constitute the structural
context within which branding operates. Following Nieborg and Poell [ 21], cultural
commodities on digital platforms are “contingent” , continuously reworked to meet the
changing monetization logics of platform operators. Cotter’s [ 5] concept of “playing
the visibility game” describes how content producers must internalize and reproduce
platform preferences, for episodic, emotionally resonant, high-engagement content, to
achieve algorithmic amplification. Together, these concepts situate Ramengvrl’s brand -
ing decisions not as purely individual performances but as structurally constrained
practices mediated by the technical architectures through which she must operate. The
synthesis of these three frameworks enables the study to analyze personal branding as
simultaneously a personal performance (Montoya), a communicative act (Lasswell), and
a structurally constrained practice (algorithmic governance).
4 Methodology
This study employs a qualitative interpretive methodology framed as a single-case
study. The case selection follows the logic that Yin [ 28], Creswell and Poth [ 6] identi-
fies as “revelatory”: the case provides access to a phenomenon not previously available
to systematic scholarly inquiry. Ramengvrl represents a theoretically significant case for
three specific and demonstrable reasons. First, her outlier status within the Indonesian
female rap scene is empirically documentable: at 234,000 Instagram followers in 2024,
she commands an audience more than three times the size of the next most-followed
artist among a defined comparison group of active Indonesian female rappers, indicat -
ing that her visibility reflects a deliberate strategic architecture rather than incidental
musical output (see Table 1). Second, she is one of very few Indonesian female rappers
to have achieved simultaneous subcultural credibility and mainstream commercial scale,
a combination that makes her case theoretically instructive because it is exceptional.
Third, her nine-year longitudinal trajectory provides temporal depth rarely available in
studies of digital cultural labor. The study’s goal is theoretical proposition-generation
rather than statistical generalization [ 28]; findings are offered as interpretive proposi -
tions warranting broader empirical testing.

The analytical approach was primarily deductive: Montoya’s Eight Laws served as the
evaluative framework against which empirical data were systematically assessed. This
deductive scaffolding was complemented by an inductive dimension, open coding was
conducted within each law’s categorical domain to identify context-specific manifesta -
tions that the framework did not anticipate. Initial codes (e.g., “technical performance
as gendered legitimation, ” “visual consistency as subcultural anchoring, ” “industry-build-
ing impulse”) were generated from the data and subsequently sorted against Montoya’s
categorical structure. One emergent theme, the transition from individual brand to cre -
ative infrastructure, resisted accommodation within any single Montoya category and is
reported separately as an inductive finding in the results. This hybrid approach is consis-
tent with the logic described by Braun and Clarke [ 4], in which theoretical frameworks
provide directional structure without foreclosing data-driven complexity.
Interpretive validity was supported through multi-source triangulation: the artist’s
stated strategic intent was cross-referenced with audience reception data (follower inter-
view) and independent expert critique (communication scholar interview). Visual eth -
nographic interpretations were validated against informant accounts during follow-up
verification sessions. A structured analytical journal was maintained throughout the
coding process to document interpretive reasoning and competing explanations, sup -
porting procedural traceability. Summaries of key interpretive findings were shared with
the artist informant prior to finalization to verify factual accuracy; theoretical interpre -
tations were retained as the researchers’ scholarly conclusions. All authors are affili -
ated with an Indonesian communication studies institution, situating them as insider
researchers with contextual familiarity with the subject’s cultural and industrial milieu.
Reflexive analytical memos were maintained throughout the project to surface poten -
tial interpretive biases, and emergent interpretations were subjected to systematic peer
scrutiny within the research team before finalization.
5 Results and analysis
The following sections present the analysis of Ramengvrl’s branding strategy organized
around Montoya’s Eight Laws, followed by one emergent inductive finding. Each sub -
section opens with a Lasswell communicative mapping that identifies the components
of the branding act and, more specifically, the communicative effect it produces, an
important distinction for moving from descriptive cataloguing toward theoretical analy -
sis. Ramengvrl (Putri Estiani) was born in Jakarta and began releasing music in 2016,
building her initial audience through SoundCloud before migrating to Instagram as her
primary platform. Her trajectory, from corporate employment to independent artist to
multi-award winner and production company founder, spans approximately nine years
and represents the longitudinal case examined here.

5.1 The law of specialization: technical legitimacy as a brand anchor
Lasswell Mapping , Who: Ramengvrl as technical rapper and content producer. Says
What: demonstrations of lyrical craft (rap covers, behind-the-scenes writing content).
In Which Channel: Instagram and, historically, SoundCloud. To Whom: hip-hop com -
munities and broader Indonesian digital audiences. Effect: the establishment of technical
competence as the foundational brand claim and, significantly, as a gendered legitima -
tion strategy.
In a saturated digital music market, specialization narrows the brand’s claim to a
domain of demonstrable mastery, reducing competitive ambiguity [ 25]. Ramengvrl
anchors her digital identity in technical rap proficiency, lyrical dexterity, compositional
craft, and generic knowledge, distinguishing herself from pop-adjacent artists who
incorporate hip-hop elements without a subcultural knowledge base. Evidence of this
specialization is visible in the consistent promotion of technical content: rap covers,
behind-the-scenes lyric-writing footage, and participation in competitive platforms such
as The Rap of China. Her account of how audiences found her work reflects the platform
logic she navigated:
“Waktu itu semua orang tahu gue juga banyak dari eh Instagram gitu. Kind of the
word of mouth-nya tuh dari Instagram. Kalau Soundcloud itu word of mouth juga
cuma lebih niche lagi. ” [“At that time, everyone knew me mostly through Instagram.
The word of mouth came from Instagram. With SoundCloud, it was also word of
mouth, but the audience was far more niche. “].
The dual-platform architecture is worth noting: SoundCloud served as the subcul -
tural credentialing space; Instagram served as the amplification channel. This reflects a
sophisticated understanding of how different platforms address different audience seg -
ments within a single brand project.
The Lasswell “Effect” dimension reveals something the framework alone does not
anticipate: specialization for Ramengvrl is not a brand strategy in a generically cor -
porate sense but a gendered survival strategy. In a genre where female competence is
routinely questioned within masculine subcultural hierarchies, technical mastery oper -
ates as a prerequisite for broader discursive participation. Expert Adinda Nindyachan -
dra observed: “I think she established branding to show that dia sangat hardworking… I
think we can definitely see that there is a persona behind the karya. ” [“…that she is very
hardworking… there is a persona behind the work. ”] This “persona behind the work” sig-
nals that specialization is an ongoing performance of professional rigor that simultane -
ously constitutes and legitimates the brand.
5.2 The law of leadership: the sociopolitical broker in the digital scene
Lasswell Mapping , Who: Ramengvrl as scene leader and industry builder. Says What:
collaborative initiatives, collective platforms for emerging talent, institutional infrastruc-
ture. In Which Channel: Instagram, live performance, company formation. To Whom:
Indonesian hip-hop artists, emerging talent, industry stakeholders. Effect: the consolida -
tion of leadership authority not through seniority alone but through active ecosystem
building, repositioning the individual brand as a collective resource.
Leadership in personal branding requires moving beyond individual visibility to
become a recognized authority who organizes and provides direction within one’s

field [19]. The 2024 “Team Tomodachi Indonesia Remix” project is the clearest mani -

“Gue jadi pengen bikin hal lain yang bisa ngebawa player hiphop lain juga naik…

This transition from individual performance to industry development is a defining
feature of brand leadership in Global South creative economies, where the absence of
robust institutional infrastructure requires successful artists to become de facto sector
builders. The Lasswell “Effect” here extends beyond brand prestige into ecosystem legiti-
macy: Ramengvrl’s authority is derived partly from her demonstrated capacity to make
others’ success possible.
5.3 The law of personality: relatability through strategic imperfection
Lasswell Mapping , Who: Ramengvrl as a “sassy, ” unapologetic persona. Says What:
unfiltered personal expression, humor, deliberately imperfect content. In Which Chan -
nel: Instagram posts, Stories, and real-time audience engagement. To Whom: Indone -
sian Gen Z followers seeking non-conformist representation. Effect: parasocial intimacy
grounded in perceived congruence between public persona and private character.
Montoya’s Law of Personality requires a brand to be grounded in an authentic char -
acter that includes rather than conceals imperfections [ 19]. Ramengvrl’s personality is
defined by a “sassy, ” opinionated, and unapologetic stance that departs markedly from
the stoic feminine ideals characterizing much of Indonesian mainstream media. Fol -
lower Stanley Mathew described her as “out of the box, unhinged, sassy, centil, smart-
mouthed, and sarcastic. ” This quality acts as a strategic asset in an attention economy
where over-curated personas generate audience skepticism [ 3]. Ramengvrl’s account of
her social media practice is worth examining closely:
“I’m not trying to be anybody else atau jadi fake juga di social media… mungkin
ada semacam filter em eh apa ya beberapa hal yang gue enggak masukin ke sosial
media gitu… but at least in public, I want it to look like an extension of me. ” [“…or to
be fake on social media… maybe there is some kind of filter, some things I don’t put
on social media… but at least in public, I want it to look like an extension of me. “].
The phrase “an extension of me” describes a practice of selective disclosure, neither
unmediated transparency nor pure performance. Expert Nindyachandra confirmed
the audience efficacy of this approach: “It doesn’t feel like dia kayak orang yang susah
digapai… kita tuh bisa jadi berteman gitu loh sama dia. And that’s the best personal

5.4 The law of distinctiveness: visual signature and ideological positioning
Lasswell Mapping, Who: Ramengvrl as a visually and ideologically distinct entity. Says
What: a stable visual code (short bob, oversized sunglasses) and a publicly sustained
hip-hop feminist stance. In Which Channel: Instagram content, live appearances, media
coverage. To Whom: digital audiences and industry professionals. Effect: immediate rec-
ognition that anchors audience loyalty and enables ideological differentiation in a satu -
rated field.
Distinctiveness requires that a brand express itself in a way that is perceptibly different
from competitors, ensuring memorability in a saturated marketplace [ 1]. Ramengvrl’s
distinctiveness operates on two mutually reinforcing registers: visual and ideological.
Her visual signature, the short bob haircut and oversized sunglasses, has been main -
tained consistently since her breakout period, functioning as a stable sensory anchor.
Stylist Joviana Henza described the negotiation of this visual identity: “I never met a cli -
ent that is so sure about her personal branding… her branding needs to be solid enough
for me to work on. ” Follower Stanley Mathew confirmed the anchoring effect: “His per -
sonal branding is really, really attached to that bob-haired girl and the glasses girl. ”
Ideologically, Ramengvrl’s distinctiveness rests on a publicly sustained hip-hop femi -
nist position: a commitment to challenging misogyny and conservative gender norms
within Indonesian entertainment. This stance is not performative decoration but the
substantive content through which Pough’s [ 22], Saputra and Simamora [ 26] concept
of “wreck” , the active demand for recognition in a public sphere structured to deny it,
is operationalized. The visual signature ensures immediate recognition; the ideological
positioning supplies the values-driven content that sustains long-term loyalty, particu -
larly among female and Gen Z audiences for whom visible women in subcultural author-
ity positions remain scarce. One structural tension merits acknowledgment: maintaining
a stable visual identity creates pressure against the algorithmic preference for content
novelty, a friction that this study’s data do not fully resolve and that warrants further
investigation.
5.5 The law of visibility: engineering a multi-modal presence
Lasswell Mapping, Who: Ramengvrl distributed across multiple platforms and commer -

Instagram, physical advertising, brand and musical collaborations. To Whom: existing
followers, algorithmically reached non-followers, mass-market audiences. Effect: multi-
demographic reach and mainstream legitimacy that reinforces rather than dilutes sub -
cultural positioning.
Visibility is the principle that a personal brand must be seen consistently to produce
results, “out of sight is out of mind” [ 25]. Ramengvrl achieves high visibility through a
multi-modal strategy integrating algorithmic content production, physical brand pres -
ence, and commercial partnership (see Table 2).

5.6 The law of unity: intentional curation and behavioral consistency
Lasswell Mapping , Who: Ramengvrl as a brand whose public and private selves are
deliberately aligned. Says What: selective personal disclosure structured around brand
coherence. In Which Channel: Instagram Stories, personal interactions, offline profes -
sional conduct. To Whom: followers who assess brand consistency across digital and
physical encounters. Effect: sustained consumer trust grounded in perceived congru -
ence between digital persona and actual character.
Unity requires that a brand’s public presentation reflect its private values [19]. For digi-
tal artists, this creates a specific management challenge: balancing professional curation
with audience expectations of transparency. Ramengvrl practices what might be called
intentional restraint, a deliberate decision to withhold sensitive personal material not
as deception but as care: “nobody needs to see that… the fans do not need to see that. ”
This restraint applies particularly to mental health content that might feel exploitative if
shared publicly.
Unity is sustained through behavioral consistency across offline and online encoun -
ters. Stylist Joviana Henza confirmed that the artist’s directness in professional settings is
continuous with her online tone. Stanley Mathew affirmed: “Yes, so much, sassy, smart-
mouthed, also sarcastic!” This cross-context consistency produces what Poursaeed et al.
[23] describe as consumer trust grounded in perceived congruence: the digital persona is
experienced as an extension of a real character rather than a fabricated construct.

5.8 The law of goodwill: advocacy and community-embedded branding
Lasswell Mapping , Who: Ramengvrl as public advocate and community builder. Says
What: feminist advocacy, industry support initiatives, institutional infrastructure. In
Which Channel: lyrics, social media, PT Going Noodles Cuan. To Whom: female fol -
lowers, emerging artists, broader industry stakeholders. Effect: deep audience loy -
alty grounded in shared values and the perception that the brand generates communal
benefit.
Goodwill holds that a brand is most durable when it generates positive value for its
broader community [ 19]. Ramengvrl operationalizes goodwill through two intersect -
ing channels: public advocacy for women’s empowerment and structural support for the
Indonesian hip-hop ecosystem. Her positioning as a hip-hop feminist, using lyrics and
social media to challenge misogyny and conservative gender norms, is perceived by her
audience as genuine self-expression rather than strategic performance, a distinction that
Pough [22] would recognize as the difference between wreck as posture and wreck as
practice. Expert Nindyachandra stated: “Personal branding can only go so far but when
it doesn’t actually generate impact then it’s just personal branding that doesn’t work. ”
The institutional embodiment of goodwill is PT Going Noodles Cuan, which provides
material support for others in the industry. By converting personal brand capital into
collective infrastructure, Ramengvrl transforms goodwill from sentiment into structure,
deepening audience affective investment. Supporting Ramengvrl becomes, for her fol -
lowers, a means of supporting the broader cultural project she represents.

5.9 Emergent finding: from individual brand to creative infrastructure
The eight laws of Montoya’s framework address the construction and maintenance of
an individual personal brand. One inductive theme that emerged from the data resists
full accommodation within any single law: the trajectory from individual artist to insti -
tutional infrastructure builder. This transition, from performing artist to founder of a
production company that actively enables other artists’ careers, cannot be fully captured
by persistence (Law 7) or goodwill (Law 8) alone. It represents a qualitatively distinct
phase in brand development that Montoya’s framework, conceived for corporate indi -
vidual branding, does not anticipate.
The data suggest this transition is driven by a recognition that individual brand capi -
tal, however significant, remains structurally precarious within platform economies that
may revise algorithmic priorities, alter content policies, or redirect user attention at any
moment [21]. PT Going Noodles Cuan is more than an extension of Ramengvrl’s brand;
it represents an attempt to construct an institutional alternative to platform dependency.
Her statement, “Gue mau ngebut buat ngerjain ini karena gue gak bisa afford hiphop
indo untuk stale lagi” [“I can’t afford Indonesian hip-hop stalling again”], registers not
only personal ambition but structural diagnosis: the ecosystem is fragile, and its fragility
demands an infrastructural response. This emergent finding connects to scholarship on
creative economy precarity [ 7] and enacts the collectivist ethics that hip-hop feminist
scholarship identifies as integral to authentic feminist practice in the genre [22].
6 Discussion
The findings of this single-case interpretive study generate four theoretical propositions
about the relationship between technical legitimacy, platform governance, and gendered
creative agency in Indonesian digital hip-hop. These propositions are offered not as
established findings but as theoretically grounded claims warranting broader compara -
tive testing.
6.1 Technical legitimacy as a structural prerequisite for gendered agency
The most consequential proposition this case generates concerns the relationship
between technical mastery and gendered discursive agency. The data suggest that for
Ramengvrl, technical competence in rap is not one brand attribute among many but a
structural prerequisite, a form of capital that must be accumulated before other claims
(feminist advocacy, creative leadership, commercial partnership) acquire subcultural
legitimacy. This resonates with Rose’s [ 24] analysis of the pleasures and dangers fac -
ing women in hip-hop, where technical skill creates a foothold in a space structured to
exclude female presence, and with Pough’s [ 22] framework of wreck, where entry into
public discourse depends on first demonstrating the right to be there. What this case
adds to that tradition is a platform-specific dimension: in algorithmically governed digi -
tal environments, the demonstration of technical skill is not merely a subcultural per -
formance but a content strategy. Displaying craft through covers, behind-the-scenes
footage, and competitive platform participation simultaneously satisfies genre creden -
tialing demands and produces high-engagement content that Instagram’s algorithms
preferentially amplify [ 5]. Technical mastery thus serves a double function, satisfying
subcultural gatekeeping norms while simultaneously meeting platform optimization
requirements. This double functionality suggests that hip-hop feminist scholarship,

developed primarily in North American broadcasting and live performance contexts,
requires theoretical extension to account for the specific affordances and constraints of
platform-mediated subcultural production in Global South settings.
6.2 Platformization, contingent cultural labor, and the global south supplement
Nieborg and Poell’s [21] concept of the “contingent cultural commodity” , artistic output
continuously reworked to meet platform monetization logics, describes Ramengvrl’s
Instagram strategy accurately at the level of content management: consistent posting,
Reels optimization, emotionally resonant persona content. However, the framework as
originally formulated assumes a relatively homogeneous platform capitalism operating
similarly across geographical contexts. Ramengvrl’s case reveals a dimension that Global
South artists must navigate which Nieborg and Poell do not theorize: the simultaneous
management of platform optimization and subcultural credentialing within a domestic
music economy that lacks the institutional buffers, major labels, established PR infra -
structure, critical media with national reach, that Western artists can partially rely upon.
The absence of these buffers means that personal brand capital must perform functions
in Indonesia’s hip-hop scene, industry development, ecosystem building, institutional
infrastructure, that in more developed music economies are distributed across multi -
ple specialized actors. This extends beyond a scaling problem into a structural condi -
tion producing qualitatively different forms of platform labor in Global South contexts.
The concept of the contingent cultural commodity requires supplementation with an
account of this structural burden if it is to apply adequately beyond the Euro-American
contexts in which it was developed.
6.2.1 The productive tension of misfit commodification
A significant secondary finding concerns the commodification of “misfit” identity
as market differentiation. Ramengvrl’s origin narrative, introvert, misfit, corporate
employee who rejected institutional conformity, functions as a brand myth. In the digital
economy, outsider identity is no longer a professional liability but a resource for brand
differentiation and audience identification [ 5]. For Indonesian youth navigating the ten -
sions between traditional values and global digital culture, this narrative provides a legi -
ble model demonstrating that non-conformity can be lived, sustained, and commercially
viable. This finding extends Cotter’s [ 5] analysis of authenticity as curated performance
by specifying the conditions under which a particular form of curated authenticity, mis -
fit identity as calculated positioning, generates durable brand equity in a socially con -
servative environment. The tension between authenticity and commodification is not
resolved by Ramengvrl’s strategy; it is productively maintained. This maintenance is
itself a communicative achievement: the perception of genuine non-conformity must be
continuously renewed to remain commercially credible. Elyamany [ 10] observes analo -
gous dynamics in virtual influencer contexts, where semiotic labor in micro-level con -
tent choices sustains audience perceptions of authentic self-expression even in avowedly
artificial performers. Human artists in subcultural contexts face a parallel labor of
authenticity maintenance, arguably more demanding, because real selfhood is legible to
audiences in ways that a virtual influencer’s constructed ontology is not.

6.3 Brand capital as ecosystem infrastructure
The emergent finding, the transition from individual brand to creative infrastructure,
generates this study’s most novel theoretical proposition: in Global South creative econo-
mies where formal industry infrastructure is underdeveloped, successful personal brand
capital does not merely accumulate; under specific conditions, it converts into ecosys -
tem infrastructure. The conditions visible in Ramengvrl’s case include sufficient audience
scale to command institutional attention, a genre community with identifiable collective
precarity, a personal value system aligned with collective rather than purely individual
advancement, and access to formal organizational mechanisms through which brand
capital can be institutionalized. This proposition requires testing across additional cases,
particularly among female artists in other Global South platform-dependent creative
sectors, before it can support broader claims. As a theoretical proposition, however, it
represents a meaningful extension of Montoya’s framework, which treats the individual
brand as its terminal analytical category. The most consequential dimension of brand
development in precarious creative economies may be precisely what lies beyond it: the
capacity to build institutional structures that can outlast platform dependency and gen -
erate collective sustainability.
6.4 Limitations
Several limitations merit explicit acknowledgment. As a single-case interpretive study, the
findings are theoretical propositions rather than established generalizations; they require
testing across broader comparative samples before stronger claims can be warranted. The
study’s reliance on a small informant set, particularly the inclusion of only one active fol-
lower, means that audience reception data are illustrative rather than representative. Mon-
toya’s framework, developed in a North American corporate context, carries assumptions
about individual agency and market logic that may not translate seamlessly into subcul -
tural, non-Western settings; this limitation was addressed analytically by treating the
laws as heuristics rather than prescriptions, but future research might develop alternative
frameworks more specifically grounded in Global South creative economies. Finally, the
fast-changing nature of platform algorithms means that branding strategies effective at the
time of data collection may require continuous adaptation.
7 Conclusion
This interpretive single-case study of Ramengvrl’s personal branding strategy on Insta -
gram reveals a multi-layered approach to identity construction in Indonesia’s platformi -
zed digital music economy. Through an analytical synthesis of Montoya’s Eight Laws,
Lasswell’s Communication Model, and theories of algorithmic governance, the study
proposes that Ramengvrl’s visibility is the outcome of a deliberate and theoretically legi -
ble strategy: technical specialization provides the foundational legitimacy on which sub -
sequent brand claims rest; adaptive persistence sustains brand coherence across shifting
platform affordances; and the institutionalization of goodwill through PT Going Noo -
dles Cuan converts individual brand capital into collective infrastructure.
The study generates four theoretical propositions warranting future empirical testing.
First, technical mastery may function as a structural prerequisite for gendered discursive
agency in Global South hip-hop contexts, not merely one brand attribute among many,
extending hip-hop feminist frameworks into platform-specific, non-Western settings.

Second, the concept of the “contingent cultural commodity” requires supplementation
with an account of the additional structural labor demanded of Global South artists who
must develop institutional infrastructure in the absence of developed music industry
support systems. Third, the productive maintenance of the tension between authentic -
ity and commodification, rather than its resolution, represents a communicative strategy
with specific dynamics in socially conservative digital environments. Fourth, personal
brand capital in precarious creative economies may, under specific conditions, convert
into ecosystem infrastructure, with implications for scholarship on independent cultural
labor and platform precarity.
Future research should pursue comparative case studies of female hip-hop artists
across Southeast Asian digital contexts, longitudinal analysis of brand capital sustain -
ability as platform algorithms evolve, and audience-level research that extends beyond
the single-informant limitation of this study’s design. As digital platforms continue
to restructure creative labor, the capacity to build a personal brand that is technically
grounded, algorithmically navigated, and institutionally extended may be among the
most consequential assets available to independent cultural producers in the Global
South, and among the least systematically theorized in the existing literature.
