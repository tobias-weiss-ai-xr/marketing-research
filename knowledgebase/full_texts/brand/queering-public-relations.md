Queering Public Relations
This book introduces a fresh, critical perspective that expands the conceptual
frameworks traditionally employed in PR research. It interrogates the field’s
embeddedness within capitalist, market-oriented systems, which often prioritize
profit, brand management, and market expansion. A queer lens examines how
PR practices serve market logics that commodify identities, turning diverse
experiences into marketable categories.
This book critiques the normative biases undergirding contemporary PR
scholarship and practices. By queering PR, the book highlights fractures in
how certain groups, identities, and lives are represented (or invisible) in media
and communication. It challenges the field to rethink logics of inclusivity,
representation, and advocacy, particularly in how non-dominant identities are
constructed and communicated in public spheres, serving as a counterpoint
to the corporate-driven, homogenized narratives that dominate PR.
Drawing on queer theory, this book will particularly resonate with
scholars involved in critical public relations and communication studies,
gender studies, queer theory, cultural and media studies, and ethical business
practices.
Erica Ciszek is Associate Professor at Moody College of Communication, The
University of Texas at Austin, USA. Her scholarship explores how people and
organizations navigate power, identity, and structural conditions that shape
voice, visibility, and erasure. Ciszek’s research examines what communication
does, for whom, under what conditions, and with what consequences. Using
theoretically and empirically rigorous methods, she studies marginalized and
hard-to-reach populations and considers how communication can promote
equity, inclusion, and social change.

Preface
In 2022, Lee Edwards described my engagement with queer theory and the
project of queering public relations as “a significant undertaking.” I am not
sure I could have prepared myself for the “laborious endeavor” this work
would become. At times, the scale of this book felt unwieldy – philosophi -
cally, politically, and personally. Each day I returned to the writing, I felt as
though I were digging my own grave within the university. To sit with queer
theory was to sit with the work of destabilizing normative power structures,
exposing the complexities and contradictions that institutions often seek to
conceal, and unearthing the inconvenient truths upon which academia, capi-
talism, and public relations itself so often rest.
This book is animated by difficult questions – questions that have too often
been avoided, deferred, or rendered unintelligible within the field. Queering
public relations, as Edwards (2022) reminds us, “invites acceptance of uncer-
tainty” and encourages us to treat the field “as a realm of possibilities.” In
that spirit, this book offers a realm of intellectual, political, and ontological
possibilities. It asks what becomes visible when we refuse the comfort of
disciplinary norms; what forms of recognition are made possible, and impos-
sible, through public relations; and what it might mean to think, write, and
practice otherwise.

Introduction
The Problem in Public Relations Scholarship
Public relations scholarship has long been organized around managerialism,
legitimacy, professionalism, and organizational effectiveness. Across many
of the field’s most influential frameworks  – including excellence theory,
systems theory, relationship management, dialogic theory, and rhetorical
approaches – public relations has been understood primarily as a strategic
organizational function: a practice through which organizations manage rela-
tionships, communicate with stakeholders, secure legitimacy, and respond to
social expectations. These traditions have generated important insights into
public relations as organizational communication, relationship building, rhe-
torical action, and institutional participation in civil society.
At the same time, they have helped establish the normative architecture
of the field. They have shaped what counts as legitimate public relations
theory, which actors matter, which methods are valued, and which forms of
communication are understood as professional, strategic, ethical, or effective.
Publics are often positioned as stakeholders, audiences, consumers, activists,
or communities whose significance is defined in relation to organizational
goals. Even when public relations is framed as ethical, dialogic, or socially
responsible, it is frequently evaluated through concepts that privilege stabil-
ity, consensus, transparency, and legitimacy.
This is not to suggest that public relations scholarship has remained static
or uncritical. Since the turn of the century, critical public relations scholar -
ship has drawn from feminism, critical race theory, cultural studies, postco -
lonial theory, and political economy to challenge the field’s organizational
and managerial assumptions. These interventions have shown that public
relations is not a neutral tool of communication management, but a cultural,
political, and ideological practice implicated in colonial governance, racial
capitalism, corporate legitimation, ecological harm, and the normalization
of dominant social orders.
Yet even as the field has become more attentive to power, it has not always
asked how public relations produces the categories through which power
becomes recognizable. How does public relations help determine which

This book responds to that problem. Rather than asking only how organi-

Queer theory offers an analytic lens for pursuing this question. This book
does not use queer theory primarily to study LGBTQ (lesbian, gay, bisex-

approaches queer theory as a critical practice for interrogating normativity:
how norms are produced, repeated, naturalized, and enforced; how certain
identities, relationships, bodies, and forms of life become intelligible; and
how others are rendered incoherent, excessive, deviant, impossible, or dis -

project of questioning the normal.
A queer approach to public relations therefore cannot be confined to
research that “is for, or about” queer subjects, to borrow gender scholar
Susanne Luhmann’s formulation. 1 Queering public relations means asking
how the field has participated in producing the boundaries of the profes-
sional, the ethical, the authentic, the responsible, the visible, and the credible.
It examines the systems through which normalcy is made, managed, and
defended.
This matters because public relations never merely reflects social norms;
it helps make and maintain them. Historically, both practice and scholarship
have been structured through white, heterosexual, cisgender, colonial, and
professional ideals. These ideals have shaped whose voices are heard, whose
bodies are rendered respectable, which publics are recognized as legitimate,
and whose claims are dismissed as outside the proper boundaries of strategic
communication. Campaigns in corporate social responsibility, crisis commu-
nication, advocacy, and institutional reputation have often normalized heter-
osexuality, binary gender, racial hierarchy, respectability, and organizational
authority while marginalizing other forms of life.
The intervention of this book is therefore not to add queer topics to public
relations scholarship, but to show that public relations itself is a site where
legitimacy, intelligibility, visibility, and disclosure are produced and negoti -
ated. By bringing queer theory to public relations, this book interrogates the
normative frameworks that shape both academic theory and professional
practice. It asks what public relations makes recognizable, what it renders
unintelligible, and what forms of life must be translated, professionalized,
disclosed, or disciplined to become visible within public life.

Put differently, this book argues that public relations is not only a strategic
communication practice, but it is also a normative practice that helps organ-
ize the terms through which public life becomes knowable. Queer theory
allows us to see this more clearly by shifting attention from inclusion to intel-
ligibility, from representation to norm production, and from organizational
legitimacy to the conditions under which legitimacy itself is granted.
Why Queer Theory Matters
Critical scholar Patrick Dilley writes that “the state of queer and queer the -
ory” is “as elusive to nail down as mercury,” something that may be con -
tained only temporarily before it “fill[s] – even overflow[s] – our container.”2
This elusiveness is central to queer theory’s intellectual and political force.
Queer theory resists stabilization into a single definition, method, or object
as it is not a unified theory in the conventional sense. Rather, it offers a criti-
cal orientation toward power, discourse, embodiment, and the production of
meaning.
The term queer has historically functioned as an identity marker for people
whose sexualities, genders, desires, and embodiments fall outside dominant
definitions of the normal. In this sense, queer names subjects marginalized
by normative assumptions about gender and sexuality. Yet queer has also
come to signify more than an inclusive category of identity. As a noun, queer
may name a person who identifies with a sexually or gender-marginalized
group, but the term often carries a broader political charge. It can signal not
simply identity, but a refusal of the norms that render certain lives deviant,
unintelligible, inferior, or disposable. Queer, in this political sense, marks an
affiliation grounded not only in shared experiences of marginalization but
also in resistance to the social structures that produce marginalization in the
first place.
Understood this way, queer is not defined merely by absence – not het -
erosexual, not cisgender – but also names the presence of alternative desires,
practices, identifications, relations, forms of belonging, and ways of know-
ing that emerge from positions outside dominant social arrangements. Queer
therefore unsettles the categories through which sexual and gender identities
are conventionally organized. Feminist scholar Annamarie Jagose argues,
queer questions dominant understandings of identity by deconstructing the
categories, oppositions, and assumptions that sustain them.3
This critical and political orientation also allows queer to function as a
verb. To queer something is to examine how norms are produced, repeated,
naturalized, and enforced. Queering a text, institution, discipline, or prac -
tice involves asking how certain identities become intelligible while others
are marginalized; how distinctions such as normal/deviant, insider/outsider,
professional/excessive, and legitimate/illegitimate are constructed; and how
those distinctions organize knowledge, authority, and social life. Education

This is why queer theory matters for public relations: it allows scholars
to approach the field itself as an object of critique. To queer public relations
is not simply to add LGBTQ campaigns, publics, practitioners, or organiza-
tions to existing public relations scholarship. Nor is it only to ask whether
queer people are represented accurately or included adequately, though those
questions remain important. Rather, to queer public relations is to trouble
the field’s assumptions and interrogate the norms and trace the assumptions
that govern the discipline.
Queer theory is especially useful because it challenges the field’s recurring
desire for coherence, stability, and managerial resolution. Much public rela -
tions scholarship has imagined communication as a practice through which
organizations build relationships, reduce conflict, restore legitimacy, manage
crises, and produce mutual understanding. Queer theory does not reject these
practices. Rather, it asks what these ideals presume. What forms of conflict
are softened in the name of dialogue? What kinds of difference must become
palatable in order to be included? When does visibility become exposure, sur-
veillance, or regulation? What kinds of publics remain unintelligible because
they do not conform to the field’s preferred models of rationality, profession-
alism, or strategic action?
A queer theoretical approach therefore illuminates social actors as com-
plex, embodied, contradictory, and often noncompliant. It helps show that
visibility can regulate as well as empower, that inclusion can discipline as
well as recognize, that transparency can expose as well as hold accountable,
and that diversity can be branded in ways that perform equity without trans-
forming the structures that produce exclusion. As such, queer theory directs
attention to the conditions under which communication becomes legitimate
and to the costs imposed on those who must translate themselves into recog-
nizable forms.
This book takes up queer theory in this broader sense. It is not a best-practice
guide for more inclusive public relations. It does not offer a managerial frame-
work for reaching LGBTQ publics more effectively, branding diversity more
authentically, or repairing organizational legitimacy after controversy. Instead,
the book uses queer theory to ask different questions: Who is authorized to
speak? Whose knowledge counts as legitimate? Which identities are made
intelligible through public relations? Which forms of visibility are rewarded,
and which are punished? What must marginalized publics disclose, soften, pro-
fessionalize, or translate in order to be recognized?
In this sense, the book is deliberately resistant to the managerial logics that
have organized dominant public relations theory and practice. It refuses the
promise of easy solutions because the problem it examines is not simply a fail-
ure of representation or inclusion. The problem is the normative orientation

The Vocabulary of This Book
This book uses several terms that carry both theoretical and political weight.
Before turning to the chapters, I want to clarify how I use this vocabulary and
why it matters for queering public relations.
I use LGBTQ as a standard term to refer to sexual and gender minorities
throughout the book, unless a chapter, case, or example refers to a more spe-
cific population, in which case I use the terminology most appropriate to that
context. This choice is both practical and political. I understand transgender,
nonbinary, and gender-diverse people as part of queer life to the extent that
they claim, inhabit, or find usefulness in that affiliation. This is an inten -
tional inclusion that rejects anti-trans tendencies within some lesbian, gay,
and bisexual spaces. At the same time, I recognize that acronyms can become
symbolic gestures when the needs and experiences of particular groups, espe-
cially gender-diverse populations, remain unaddressed. The terminology of
this book therefore aims to be inclusive without pretending that any acronym
can fully contain the complexity of queer lives.
Several theoretical concepts also require definition at the outset: nor-
mativity, intelligibility, recognition, visibility, and disclosure. These terms
recur across the chapters and form the conceptual vocabulary of the book’s
argument.
I use normativity to refer to the standards, assumptions, ideals, and
expectations through which communication, identities, publics, organiza -
tions, and practices become recognizable as legitimate. Normativity is not
simply a set of explicit rules. It often operates in the background as com -
mon sense or best practice. Public relations is normative because it does not
merely describe or facilitate communication. It helps establish what com -
munication ought to look like, what kinds of publics matter, what forms of
visibility are acceptable, and what kinds of organizational conduct appear
responsible. Throughout this book, normativity names the field’s investment
in categories such as professionalism, transparency, dialogue, consensus,
legitimacy, responsibility, and repair. These ideals are not inherently bad,
but they are never neutral.
I use intelligibility to describe the conditions through which subjects, pub-
lics, identities, organizations, and claims become recognizable as coherent,
meaningful, and legitimate within dominant social, institutional, or profes -
sional frameworks. To be intelligible is not simply to be visible or present;
it is to be understood within available categories of meaning. In this book,

intelligibility is a central concept because public relations helps organize the
terms through which life can be seen, heard, believed, and acted upon. Queer
theory is especially useful here because it asks how these terms of recogniz -
ability are produced, stabilized, and contested.
I use recognition to refer to the process through which identities, pub -
lics, claims, and organizations are acknowledged as legitimate, credible, and
worthy of attention or response. Recognition is not simply the act of seeing,
naming, or including a subject. It is a political and normative process that
determines whether a subject, group, claim, or harm can be understood as
meaningful within existing frameworks. In the book, recognition is always
conditional. Public relations often grants recognition through norms of pro -
fessionalism, respectability, transparency, authenticity, dialogue, responsibil-
ity, and market compatibility. This means recognition can be enabling, but it
can also be regulatory.
I use visibility to refer to the condition of being publicly seen, represented,
named, or made available for recognition. Public relations scholarship and
practice often treat visibility as a good: a sign of inclusion, transparency, or
representation. This book complicates that assumption. To become visible is
also to become available for interpretation, management, commodification,
surveillance, backlash, or discipline. Queer theory helps show that visibility
is always governed. Some forms of visibility are celebrated because they are
respectable, marketable, professional, or institutionally useful, while other
forms are treated as excessive, risky or radical. Across the book, visibility
appears as a central but ambivalent public relations problem – it can enable
recognition, but it can also expose subjects and publics to new forms of regu-
lation and harm.
I use disclosure to refer to the act, demand, and process of making iden-
tity, knowledge, affiliation, harm, or organizational conduct publicly know -
able. Dominant public relations often values disclosure through norms of
transparency, yet disclosure is never neutral. Some subjects are expected to
disclose to be understood as authentic or trustworthy. Others are punished
for disclosing. Some are granted privacy or opacity, while others are treated
with suspicion if they do not make themselves fully knowable. Disclosure can
produce recognition and accountability, but it can also produce exposure,
surveillance, punishment, or intensified vulnerability. In this book, disclosure
is about the power to determine who must reveal what, under what condi -
tions, and with what consequences.
Taken together, these concepts form the vocabulary of the book. Nor-
mativity names the standards that organize legitimacy. Recognition names
the process through which identities, publics, claims, and organizations are
acknowledged as legitimate. Intelligibility names the conditions of recogniz-
ability. Visibility names the ambivalent condition of being publicly seen. Dis-
closure names the uneven demand to make oneself, one’s community, one’s
harm, or one’s conduct knowable.

Central Argument of the Book
This book asks what public relations scholarship might gain by taking queer
theory seriously as a way of interrogating the norms through which publics,
organizations, identities, and claims become recognizable. Public relations
has long been shaped by normative assumptions about legitimacy, profes -
sionalism, organizational success, and social order. As a result, public rela -
tions scholarship has often treated publics and organizations as if they were
stable or manageable categories, rather than asking how those categories are
produced, governed, and contested in the first place.
The central argument of this book is that public relations functions as a
regime of intelligibility. It does not simply communicate organizational values,
manage relationships, repair crises, represent marginalized groups, or secure
legitimacy. Public relations helps produce the norms through which organi -
zations, publics, identities, and claims become legible as worthy of response.
Queer theory matters because it makes this normative work visible. It shifts
attention from inclusion to intelligibility, from representation to production,
from visibility to disclosure, and from organizational legitimacy to the condi-
tions under which legitimacy can be claimed at all. Rather than beginning with
the needs of organizations, the management of relationships, or the repair of
legitimacy, queer public relations scholarship begins with the production of
norms: how they are made, repeated, naturalized, contested, and refused.
Chapter Roadmap
Chapter 1 establishes the theoretical problem to which the book responds.
It argues that public relations scholarship has been shaped by assumptions
about legitimacy, professionalism, and social order. The field has historically
centered organizations, normalizing consensus, effectiveness, and managerial
usefulness, even as critical scholarship has challenged these assumptions. The
chapter shows that public relations is not simply a communication practice; it
is a normative project that helps define what counts as legitimate. The queer
intervention begins where these critiques leave off – with the question of how
norms make subjects, publics, organizations, and practices intelligible.
Chapter 2 develops the book’s queer theoretical framework. It shows that
queer theory does not simply expand public relations to include LGBTQ pub-
lics, practitioners, campaigns, or organizations. Rather, queer theory chal -
lenges the categories through which these subjects are produced in the first
place. Engaging with the work of Michel Foucault, Judith Butler, and Eve
Kosofsky Sedgwick, three canonical theorists whose work has profoundly
shaped queer theory, the chapter develops a queer analytic for public relations
organized around questions of normativity, intelligibility, disclosure, visibility,
and resistance. It asks: What norms are being cited? What identities are made
intelligible? What forms of visibility are rewarded or punished? What must
be disclosed, concealed, or left unknowable? Where does resistance emerge?

Chapter 3 applies this queer analytic to corporate social responsibility. It

CSR is authentic or inauthentic and instead asks how CSR governs the

Chapter 4 reframes crisis communication as a struggle over intelligibil -

tion. Crises reveal whose harms count, whose anger is treated as reasonable,
whose credibility is recognized, whose exposure is required, and what forms
of repair are understood as legitimate. Queering crisis communication means
asking not only how organizations recover, but also what restoration pre-
sumes, whom it protects, and whether the order being restored should be
restored at all. The chapter therefore shifts crisis communication from the
management of reputational threat to the politics of harm, vulnerability,
responsibility, and repair.
Chapter 5 turns to activism and argues that activist public relations does
not simply communicate preexisting identities or interests but helps bring
political subjects, publics, and futures into being. The chapter intervenes
in public relations scholarship on activism by moving through three posi -
tions: activism as threat, activism as public relations practice, and activism
as a challenge to public relations intelligibility. Its central concern is whether
activism can be included in public relations without being domesticated by
the field’s managerial assumptions. Through the #WontBeErased case, the
chapter shows activist public relations as a performative and world-making
practice of survival, refusal, and futurity.
Chapter 6 brings the book’s argument to its most explicit formulation by
theorizing public relations as a regime of intelligibility. The chapter examines
how public relations filters visibility through norms such as transparency,
authenticity, dialogue, and professionalism. It argues that visibility becomes
legitimate only when it passes through these filters, and that disclosure is dif-
ferentially rewarded or punished depending on race, sexuality, citizenship,
respectability, and institutional fit. To develop this argument, the chapter
places Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick’s work on disclosure and the epistemology of
the closet in dialogue with cultural theorist C. Riley Snorton’s critiques of
visibility, racialization, and the politics of recognition.
Together, the chapters argue that queer theory does not merely add new top-
ics to public relations scholarship. It changes the questions the field can ask.
Across CSR, crisis communication, and activism, the book shows that public
relations helps produce the norms through which publics, identities, organiza-
tions, and claims become recognizable. Each chapter is accompanied by an

The Stakes of the Project
In 2018, I argued that queering public relations meant “rattling the chain
of a sleeping beast.” 5 I no longer think that metaphor goes far enough. To
queer public relations is not only to disturb the discipline. Queering public
relations requires examining the cage that has held it together: the bars, bolts,
and chains that have given public relations its sense of order, professionalism,
and legitimacy. The task is not simply to wake the beast; it is to ask what
structures have made the beast possible, what forms of power it protects, and
what kinds of life it has kept outside.
Black, lesbian, feminist Audre Lorde’s warning that “the master’s tools
will never dismantle the master’s house” is instructive here. 6 Lorde reminds
us that genuine transformation cannot be achieved by relying on the same
frameworks that sustain domination. Those tools may allow temporary
access, partial recognition, or limited inclusion, but they do not remake the
structures that produce exclusion in the first place. For public relations, this
warning matters. The field has often sought legitimacy through the very
norms this book interrogates. However, these norms have not simply organ-
ized the field intellectually; they have also helped public relations sustain and
reproduce dominant power structures and social arrangements.
The stakes of queering public relations, then, are not limited to represen -
tation. The question is not only whether LGBTQ publics, practitioners, or
campaigns appear within public relations scholarship. Nor is the question
simply whether organizations communicate more inclusively. The deeper
issue is whether the field is willing to examine the normative frameworks
through which inclusion becomes possible and conditional. Queering public
relations asks what must be dismantled, not merely who must be added.
This book is written in a moment when those questions feel especially
urgent. Corporate commitments to diversity, equity, and inclusion have
become increasingly fragile, contingent, and politically vulnerable. In
recent years, I have been asked by journalists to explain the apparent retreat
from corporate advocacy and LGBTQ inclusion. Only a short time ago, cor-
porations, professional associations, and media organizations sought exper -
tise on LGBTQ inclusion, diversity training, and public-facing commitments
to equity. More recently, many of those same commitments have been with-
drawn. This shift reveals the limits of inclusion when it depends on reputa -
tional value, consumer comfort, political convenience, or institutional risk
management.
The personal and the political are not separate here. I wrote this book in
part because I did not know what else to do with the contradiction of being

10 Queering Public Relations
asked to explain, professionalize, and translate LGBTQ inclusion at the very
moment when the conditions for that inclusion were being publicly contested
and institutionally withdrawn. L’Etang reminds us that “the position of the
researcher is central to the nature of the story-telling” and that this requires
more reflexivity than is common in much academic writing. 7 My position
in this book is not outside the story it tells. The rollback of protections,
programs, and public commitments to LGBTQ people is not only an object
of scholarly analysis. It is part of the world in which this book was written.
These retreats have consequences. They create uncertainty for LGBTQ
employees, students, practitioners, activists, and communities who are left
to wonder whether institutional commitments to inclusion were ever dura -
ble. They expose the limits of corporate social responsibility when equity is
treated as an optional initiative rather than a structural obligation. They also
reveal how quickly visibility can become risk. What was celebrated as diver-
sity in one moment can be recoded as controversy in another. What was once
branded as inclusion can be withdrawn in the name of neutrality or balance.
This is why the book does not treat visibility, recognition, disclosure, or
inclusion as straightforward goods. Visibility can bring recognition, but it
can also invite surveillance, backlash, and discipline. Disclosure can enable
accountability, but it can also produce exposure and vulnerability. Inclusion
can open doors, but it can also require marginalized subjects to become pal-
atable. Public relations is central to these processes because it helps manage
the conditions under which organizations decide what to say, what to hide,
what to affirm, what to disavow, and whose lives become worth defending.
The stakes also extend beyond corporate communication. Universities,
schools, cultural institutions, public health agencies, and government bodies
have become key sites in struggles over gender, sexuality, race, and knowl -
edge more broadly. Fields such as queer theory, gender studies, critical race
theory, ethnic studies, and inclusion work have been targeted as ideologi -
cal, excessive, dangerous, or illegitimate. These attacks are not incidental
to the concerns of this book. They are struggles over intelligibility  – over
whose knowledge counts, whose bodies are protected, whose histories may
be taught, whose speech is credible, and whose presence is framed as a threat.
For these reasons, this book is not a best-practice guide. It does not offer
public relations professionals a new toolkit for reaching LGBTQ publics,
repairing reputational damage, or branding inclusion more effectively. Nor
does it assume that the field can resolve its normative problems through better
representation alone. Instead, the book asks what public relations becomes
when its own categories are placed under pressure.
The stakes of this project are therefore theoretical, disciplinary, and deeply
political. Theoretically, the book argues that queer theory gives public rela -
tions scholarship a vocabulary for studying normativity, intelligibility, rec -
ognition, visibility, and disclosure. Disciplinarily, it asks public relations to
confront the managerial and organizational assumptions that have shaped
its development. Politically, it insists that public relations is not merely a

communication practice but a practice of world-making – a field that helps
determine which subjects are credible, which publics are legitimate, which
harms are acknowledged, and which futures are possible.
