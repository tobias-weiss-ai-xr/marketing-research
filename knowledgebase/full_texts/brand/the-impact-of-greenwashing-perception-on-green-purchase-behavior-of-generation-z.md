Abstract
This study builds an integrated theoretical framework and metho dological design to analyze
the impact of greenwashing awareness on the actual green consum ption behavior of Gen Z in
Hanoi. Based on the combination of the Theory of Planned Behavi or, Signaling Theory and
Attribution Theory, the study develops a multi-level structural  model to explain the process
by which awareness of greenwashing transforms into a decline or  breakdown in green
purchasing behavior. The model proposes indirect impact mechani sms through three
intermediate variables including green skepticism, green trust and perceived green risk, and
also considers the moderating role of environmental knowledge. This study employs a
deductive conceptual research method, combined with a systemati c and critical literature
review process. The research result is a theoretical model with  a system of 11 hypotheses,
oriented to test the model using PLS-SEM techniques with a mini mum sample size of 400
Gen Z consumers in Hanoi. Thereby addressing three key gaps: clarifying Gen Z’s reaction to
greenwashing in the context of emerging urban markets; approach  the intention-behavior gap
by selecting actual green consumption behavior as the final outcome variable; and explain the
dual regulatory role of environmental knowledge. The study provides a foundation for further
empirical tests, and also proposes management implications for environmental information
transparency, greenwashing control and strengthening sustainable brand trust.
Keywords: greenwashing perception, green c onsumer behavior, green skepti cism, Gen Z
consumers, intention-behavior gap

1. Introduction
In the context of global environmental degradation and increasi ngly complex climate change,
green consumption has transformed from a voluntary trend into a  core behavioral norm to
promote a circular economy and sustainable development (Peattie , 2010; White et al., 2019).
Facing the rapid increase in community ecological awareness, bu sinesses around the world
have proactively integrated environmental protection commitment s into marketing strategies,
brand positioning and product development (Delmas & Burbano, 2011).
However, the explosion of green marketing has also led to the s pread of the phenomenon of
“Greenwashing” where businesses intentionally make false, exaggerated or ambiguous claims
about environmentally friendly at tributes to gain commercial pr ofits (Lyon & Montgomery,
2015). This greenwashing phenomenon not only manipulates public  trust but also causes
negative backlash, eroding trust in the sustainable product mar ket (Chen & Chang, 2013;
Nyilasy et al., 2014). Generation Z (Gen Z)—the young consumer force born from the
mid-1990s to 2010 is rising to beco me the market segment with t he deepest behavioral shape
and social influence (Francis & Hoefel, 2018).
Characterized as “digital natives” with high levels of educatio n, the ability to access
multi-dimensional information, and a special sensitivity to issues of social responsibility, Gen
Z shows strong support for green lifestyles (Borah et al., 2024 ; Lopes et al., 2024). However,
the ability to look up and analy ze information sensitively in t he digital environment also
makes Gen Z easily identify, dissect, and react harshly to gree nwashing marketing tactics of
businesses (Su et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2020).
In Vietnam, Hanoi is a special economic and cultural center, wh ere Gen Z accounts for a
large proportion of the populati on and plays a pioneering role in leading new consumption
trends (Nguyen et al., 2019). However, empirical studies to exp lain the psychological impact
mechanism of greenwashing awareness on Gen Z’s actual green con sumption behavior in
emerging markets are still very fragmented. The widespread exis tence of the
“intention-behavior gap” raises an urgent theoretical question: What cognitive and emotional
mechanisms transform perceived greenwashing into a breakdown in  G e n  Z ’ s  a c t u a l  g r e e n
purchasing behavior? (ElHaffar et al., 2020; Wiederhold & Marti nez, 2018). This study was
conducted to address the above research gap.
The article builds an integrated theoretical framework based on  the Theory of Planned
Behavior (Ajzen, 1991), Signaling Theory (Spence, 1973), and At tribution Theory (Kelley,
1967). The goal of the research is to clarify the indirect impa ct paths through Green
Skepticism (Goh & Balaji, 2016), Green Trust (Chen, 2010), Perceived Green Risk (Zhang et
al., 2018), and the moderating role of Environmental Knowledge (Lin & Niu, 2018) on Gen
Z’s green consumption behavior in Hanoi. The study makes an imp ortant contribution to the
theoretical treasure of sustain able marketing management and pr ovides practical policy
suggestions for businesses in Vietnam.

2. Literature Review
2.1 Theoretical Foundations
To build a solid theoretical foundation, this study performs a systematic integration of three
classic management and consumer behavior theoretical models. Fi rst, Ajzen’s (1991) Theory
of Planned Behavior (TPB) asserts that behavioral intention is the most important and direct
predictor of actual behavior. However, in the field of sustainable consumption, TPB shows its
limitations when it cannot fully explain the phenomenon of “int ention-behavior gap”, where
consumers have a positive attit ude but abandon the actual purch ase decision due to the
appearance of negative cognitive barriers (ElHaffar et al., 202 0; Wiederhold & Martinez,
2018).
To supplement the limitations of TPB, Spence’s Signaling Theory  (1973) is introduced to
explain the operation of inform ation in asymmetric market condi tions (Information
Asymmetry). Environmental protec tion statements or eco-labels i ssued by businesses act as
“signals” representing product quality (Ernst et al., 2022). Wh en Greenwashing behavior
occurs, these green signals are distorted or faked, reducing in formation value and activating
consumers’ cognitive defense mechanisms regarding brand authent icity (Guo & Luo, 2023;
Nyilasy et al., 2014).
Next, Kelley’s (1967) Attributi on Theory explains the internal psychological reaction when
consumers receive noisy green signals. Accordingly, when detect ing inconsistencies between
media messages and actual actions of businesses, consumers will  p e r f o r m  t h e  p r o c e s s  o f
attributing motives (Camilleri, 2022; Skarmeas & Leonidou, 2013 ). If businesses are accused
of carrying out green campaigns f or the purpose of “self-servin g attribution” instead of “for
social benefits” (altruistic attribution), negative psychological variables including skepticism,
erosion of trust, and increased perceived risk will immediately explode (Chen & Chang, 2013;
Zhang et al., 2018).
2.2 Multidimensional Nature of Greenwashing and Consumer Perception
To understand the mechanism of consumer perception formation, t he Greenwashing
phenomenon needs to be dissected from many marketing and brand management perspectives.
Critical analysis of the empirical work of Delmas and Burbano ( 2011) and Lyon and
Montgomery (2015) shows that greenwashing is not simply blatant  false statements, but
operates through a system of sophi sticated manipulation strategies. The most common is the
vague statement strategy, where businesses abuse terms that hav e not been legally
standardized such as “natural” , “eco-friendly”, or “low carbon footprint” to create a false
sense of security for customers (Schmuck et al., 2018). In addi tion, the form of providing
green data that cannot be verified by a third party or selectin g information in a way that only
emphasizes a small green feature to cover up large-scale pollut ing production processes is
creating serious information tur bulence in the market (Parguel et al., 2011; Lyon &
Montgomery, 2015).
Greenwashing Perception arises when consumers identify a break between the public image
that the brand builds and the actual operations of the business  (Chen & Chang, 2013; Nyilasy

2.3 Mediating Psychological Mechanisms and Intention-Behavior Gap
The relationship from Greenwashing awareness to actual green co nsumption decisions is a
complex psychological process including sequential and parallel  impact chains. The first link
is Green Skepticism, defined as a consumer’s cognitive state of  doubt regarding the
authenticity of environmental messages (Obermiller & Spangenber g, 1998; Goh & Balaji,
2016). When businesses are discovered to be greenwashing, this skepticism immediately
explodes, acting as a natural defense mechanism to help consume rs avoid being deceived by
marketing tricks (Aji & Sutikno, 2015). Green skepticism not on ly erodes positive attitudes
toward products but also directly suppresses the motivation to buy ecologically (Nguyen et al.,
2019).
The second link heavily influenc ed by greenwashing awareness is  Green Trust, which is
customers’ firm belief in the brand’s ability to implement envi ronmental protection
commitments (Chen, 2010). Trust is the core intangible asset th at determines the success of
green marketing. However, according to Signaling Theory, when green signals are discovered
to be lies, green trust will completely break down (Ernst et al., 2022). Chen and Chang (2013),
as well as Tarabieh (2021), emphasize that restoring green trus t after being devastated by
greenwashing is extremely difficult, creating a persistent psyc hological barrier that prevents
re-establishment of purchasing behavior.
At the same time, information insecurity triggers Green Perceiv ed Risk, which reflects
consumers’ concerns about financi al loss, poor functionality, o r emotional disappointment
when paying higher prices for “fake green” products (Chen & Cha ng, 2012; Zhang et al.,
2018). When perceived risk exceeds the psychological safety thr eshold, consumers will
choose the safe solution of withdrawing from green transactions (Zhang et al., 2018).
The biggest limitation in this se ries of empirical studies on i ntermediate variables is the
overreliance on the dependent variable Green Purchase Intention  (GPI). Although the works
of Mostafa (2007), Yadav and Pathak (2017), and Kumar et al. (2 021) reinforced the
mediating role of skepticism, trust and risk on purchase intention, but they lacked a direct test
of actual Green Purchase Behavior (GPB). In marketing practice,  intention has never been
synonymous with execution. The “intention-behavior gap” phenome non occurs commonly

2.4 Gen Z Characteristics, Environmental Knowledge, and Critical Research Gaps
Gen Z is rising to become the most market-shaping consumer group in the digital era (Francis
& Hoefel, 2018). Characterized by “digital natives”, Gen Z sens itively accesses social
network information, possesses a high educational background an d shows deep concern for
sustainable development goals and climate change (Borah et al.,  2024; Lopes et al., 2024).
Unlike previous generations, Ge n Z tends to view consumption be havior as a means of
expressing self-worth and social responsibility (Francis & Hoefel, 2018). However, this sharp
information processing ability also increases Gen Z’s level of strictness regarding corporate
honesty. They do not easily accept one-sided marketing messages  but always actively seek
authentic evidence from the community (Su et al., 2023).
In the cognitive structure of Gen Z, Environmental knowledge pl ays a role as a specific
regulatory lens (Lin & Niu, 2018;  Lavuri et al., 2023). Environ mental knowledge represents
an individual’s level of understa nding about ecological problem s, causes of pollution, and
solutions to protect nature (Amyx et al., 1994). In traditional  studies, environmental
knowledge is often considered a f actor that promotes green purc hase intention (Lin & Niu,
2018). However, when placed in the context of facing the Greenw ashing phenomenon and
from the perspective of Attribution Theory, the role of environ mental knowledge becomes
complex and two-sided (Aji & Sutikno, 2015; Wang et al., 2020).  Gen Z individuals with a
deep ecological understanding possess better message analysis a bilities, helping them easily
detect loopholes, ambiguities, or inconsistencies in businesses’ green advertising (Wang et al.,
2020). As a result, for customer groups with high environmental  knowledge, greenwashing
awareness will trigger skepticism and attribution of profiteeri ng motives much more harshly
than for groups with less knowledge (Aji & Sutikno, 2015). This increased moderating role of
environmental knowledge has never been thoroughly empirically t ested for Gen Z in
developing cities like Hanoi.
From a critical review of the previous literature system, this study identifies three core
theoretical gaps that need to be resolved: (1) First gap, most of the current theoretical
frameworks on Greenwashing are developed in Western markets. Th ere is a lack of an
integrated theoretical model e xplaining the psychological and b ehavioral reactions of Gen Z,
a pioneering but sensitive consumer group, to the phenomenon of  greenwashing in an
emerging market with an eco-label institution in the process of  being perfected like Hanoi,
Vietnam. The second gap is that previous studies often discrete ly approached the variables of
skepticism, trust or risk, and stopped at measuring Purchase In tention (GPI). This paper fills
the gap by simultaneously connecting three parallel psychologic al mediating mechanisms
(GS, GT, GPR) to explain the rupture that leads directly to Act ual Green Buying Behavior
(GPB). The third gap is the lack  of empirical testing of the mo derating role of Environmental

3. Method
This study employs a deductive co nceptual research approach com bined with a systematic
and critical literature review . Its methodological objective is  not to conduct quantitative
testing on empirical data, but rather to construct a robust the oretical framework, identify
knowledge gaps, and propose a standardized research model along  with a set of structural
hypotheses to facilitate future empirical testing.
The theoretical research process follows three specific methodo logical steps: First, the study
gathers and screens literature from reputable academic database s indexed in ISI/Scopus (such
as Web of Science, Scopus, Scien ceDirect, Springer, and Wiley).  Search keywords are
combined using specific parameters, including “Greenwashing per ception”, “Green purchase
behavior”, “Green skepticism”, “Green trust”, “Green perceived risk”, “Theory of Planned
Behavior”, “Signaling Theory”, “Attribution Theory”, and “Gen Z  consumers”. Selected
works focus on theoretical and empirical articles published in high-quality specialized
journals from 1991 to the present.
Second, the study employs comparative and critical analysis tec hniques to dissect existing
theoretical pathways. By examining the interplay between three foundational theories the
Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), Signaling Theory, and Attribu tion Theory the research
deciphers the psychological mechanisms that transform “greenwas hing” signals into
cognitive barriers. Simultaneousl y, the critical review process  highlights the lack of
integrated research addressing th e intention-behavior gap among  Generation Z consumers in
emerging markets such as Hanoi, Vietnam. Third, based on the re view results and identified
research gaps, the article operationalizes the research concept s and synthesizes a set of
standardized scales derived from international studies. The stu dy proposes a technical
procedure for subsequent quantitative research, specifically: designing a survey questionnaire
using a 5-point Likert scale; targeting Gen Z consumers (aged 1 8–29) in Hanoi with a
minimum sample size of N = 400; and outlining the application o f Partial Least Squares
Structural Equation Modeling (PLS -SEM) using SmartPLS software to test the proposed
structural model and hypotheses.
4. Results
Based on comprehensively addressing the three identified resear ch gaps, this study proposes
a structural theoretical model regarding the impact of Greenwas hing Perception on the Green
Consumption Behavior of Gen Z in Hanoi. The model is designed t o serve as a foundational
theoretical framework for subsequent empirical studies employin g PLS-SEM techniques for
data collection and analysis. In  the proposed structural model,  Greenwashing Perception
(GWP) acts as the direct independent variable. The impact of GWP on the ultimate dependent
variable—Actual Green Consumption Behavior (GPB)—is mediated th rough a two-stage
structural process. The first mediation stage comprises three p arallel psychological variables:
Green Skepticism (GS), Green Trust (GT), and Perceived Green Ri sk (GPR). The second

5. Discussion
The primary objective of this conceptual study is to resolve th e psychological mechanisms
and systemic pathways through wh ich greenwashing perception rup tures actual green
purchasing behavior among Genera tion Z consumers in Hanoi. By i ntegrating the Theory of
Planned Behavior, Signaling Theory, and Attribution Theory, the proposed multi-level model
addresses crucial gaps in the sustainable marketing literature,  particularly within emerging
Asian urban markets.
In conceptualizing greenwashing perception, this study views it  not merely as an isolated
cognitive evaluation, but as a c atalyst for a chain of adverse psychological reactions. In line
with Spence’s Signaling Theory, corporate eco-friendly claims function as signals intended to
reduce information asymmetry in the market. However, when Gen Z  consumers detect
greenwashing whether through vague claims, unverifiable data, o r hidden trade-offs these
green signals transform into deceptive noise. This signal disto rtion directly activates three
parallel internal defense mechanisms. First, in agreement with existing literature on consumer
skepticism, Gen Z digital natives actively deploy green skeptic ism as a protective cognitive
filter to avoid falling victim to manipulative marketing. Secon d, greenwashing fundamentally
undermines the relational bond between consumers and sustainabl e brands, leading to a
severe erosion of green trust a nd a long-term breakdown in bran d credibility. Third,
information insecurity triggers heightened green perceived risk , reflecting consumers’
deep-seated concerns about financial, functional, and emotional  losses when paying price
premiums for products with questio nable environmental attribute s. In the context of Hanoi,
an emerging market where institutional mechanisms and eco-label ing standards are still
evolving, these three psychological friction points operate wit h heightened intensity, forming
a formidable psychological barrier that suppresses green purchase intention.
A central theoretical contribution of this study lies in extend ing the traditional Theory of
Planned Behavior framework by in corporating actual green purcha se behavior as the final

6. Conclusion
This study has successfully established a comprehensive concept ual framework explaining
how greenwashing perception disrupts actual green consumption behavior among Generation
Z consumers in Hanoi. By synthesizing the Theory of Planned Beh avior, Signaling Theory,
and Attribution Theory, the research unveils a multi-stage impa ct pathway wherein
greenwashing perception induces t hree parallel psychological ba rriers green skepticism, trust
erosion, and perceived risk whi ch subsequently suppress green p urchase intention and
ultimately break down actual purchasing behavior. Additionally,  environmental knowledge
has been conceptualized as a critical moderator that intensifie s consumer skepticism upon
detecting greenwashing practices. Overall, the study makes a si gnificant theoretical
contribution by directly addressi ng the intention-behavior gap within the context of an
emerging market.
From a practical perspective, the findings offer vital insights  for corporate managers, green
marketers, and regulatory authorities in Vietnam. Businesses ta rgeting Generation Z can no
longer rely on vague or unverified  eco-claims; instead, brands m u s t  a c h i e v e  r a d i c a l
operational transparency by obtai ning credible third-party eco- certifications and disclosing
lifecycle assessment data. Market ers must actively focus on reb uilding green trust and
mitigating perceived risks by offering clear performance guaran tees and transparent supply
chain reporting. At the macro level, policymakers in Vietnam mu st accelerate the
establishment of clear legal definitions, unified eco-labeling standards, and strict penalties for
misleading green claims to protect consumers and foster a healthy sustainable marketplace.
Despite these meaningful contributions, the study possesses cer tain inherent limitations that
open promising avenues for future research. As a conceptual pap er, the eleven proposed
structural hypotheses require emp irical validati on. Future rese arch should execute the
proposed Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling methodology using a sample of
