Abstract
This study aims to test the extent to which the digital content of Egyptian
brands conforms to the standards of Answer Engine Optimization (AEO), as the
most recent approach to digital visibility in search environments powered by gener-
ative artificial intelligence, by applying it to the official website of Kazyon, one of the
most prominent low-cost retail brands in the Egyptian market. The study relied on a
methodological triangulation approach through three tools: (1) a descriptive-analyt-
ical structural content analysis of the official website using a checklist of eleven stan-
dards derived from AEO literature; (2) testing the responses of generative search
engines (Google AI Overviews, Bing Copilot, and Perplexity AI) through nine natu-
ral queries classified according to search-intent patterns; and (3) in-depth interviews
with a purposive sample of twelve participants from the company’s digital mar -
keting and content teams, conducted during the period from January to May 2025.
The results showed the absence of structured data architecture and of a Frequently
Asked Questions section in FAQ Schema format from the website, and a low level
of reliance on the official website as a source of information in generative engine
results, in favor of digital intermediaries. They also revealed a total absence of the
brand in non-directed positioning queries, despite price being the axis of its strategic
positioning, while the interviews indicated partial awareness of the AEO concept
and a behavioral readiness to adopt it that is conditional on institutional support
and training. The study offers an applied model integrating Uses and Gratifications
Theory with the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) to explain the gap between
content production and its institutional reception in the AEO context, and proposes
an operational roadmap for adopting AEO in Egyptian companies.
Keywords: Answer Engine Optimization; generative search; Egyptian brands;
digital visibility; Technology Acceptance Model.

 Introduction
General Context of the Study
The digital environment has witnessed a structural transformation in the
mechanisms of information searching, whereby search engines have shifted from
a link-retrieval model based on keyword matching to generative models capable
of producing direct, personalized answers through generative artificial intelligence
technologies, such as Google AI Overviews, Bing Copilot, and Perplexity AI. This
transformation has produced a new concept in digital marketing-Answer Engine
Optimization (AEO)-which goes beyond the traditional logic of Search Engine Op -
timization (SEO), based on ranking, toward a logic based on citation and selection
within the generated answer itself (Popescu, 2026; Chatwin & Bliey, 2026).
This transformation entails a radical reshaping of the relationship between the
brand and the consumer: the user is no longer obliged to navigate to the website,
but instead obtains a direct answer that may include the brand name or exclude it
entirely from the context of the answer. Visibility within the generative answer thus
becomes a strategic variable, subject to measurement and marketing management
(Kumar, 2026). The stakes of this transformation are amplified in emerging markets,
including the Egyptian market, where competition intensifies in the fast-moving
consumer retail sectors, and where the informational infrastructure of many brands
remains grounded in traditional promotional logic, without technical or informa -
tional preparation for the new generative environment.
Study Problem
Despite the growing body of literature on AEO and Generative Engine Opti -
mization (GEO) in Western contexts, a dual research gap persists: a contextual gap,
reflected in the scarcity of Arab and Egyptian studies that have addressed AEO
through field application; and a conceptual gap, reflected in the absence of integra -
tion between the technical track of visibility optimization and the institutional track
of practice adoption within work teams. Most previous studies have either focused
on the technical measurement of digital visibility (Kumar, 2026; Kultavirta, 2026),
or on consumer behavior toward AI chatbots (Tafesse & Sandhu, 2026; Pham et al.,
2026), without testing the interactive relationship among content structure, organi -
zational readiness, and actual visibility outcomes.
From this standpoint, the study’s problem crystallizes in the following main
question:
“To what extent does the digital content of the Kazyon brand conform to An -
swer Engine Optimization (AEO) standards, what are the implications of this for its
visibility as a trusted source in generative search results, and how do the marketing
teams within the company interact with the requirements of this transformation?”
Study Significance
At the theoretical level:  The study contributes to filling the aforementioned
research gap by: (a) grounding the concept of AEO in Arab literature as a field inde-

Study Objectives
1- Analyzing the informational structure of the Kazyon website and evaluating
its conformity with AEO standards (textual formulation, structured data, the

2- Measuring the level of presence of the Kazyon brand in generative search en -

Theoretical Framework and Literature Review
From SEO to AEO: The Conceptual Shift in Digital Visibility
Answer Engine Optimization (AEO) is operationally defined in this study as:
“the set of technical and editorial practices that prepare digital content for selection
and citation within answers generated by artificial intelligence systems, through
structured data, question–answer architecture, and alignment with user intent.”
Unlike SEO, which targets ranking on results pages, AEO targets passing the stages
of retrieval, documentation, and entity verification that large language models rely
on before formulating an answer (Chatwin & Bliey, 2026). The study by Kultavirta
(2026)-applied to 105 companies listed on American stock exchanges-confirmed that
structured data markup (Schema markup) and content architecture were the most
influential factors in the accuracy of generatively extracted information, while Ra -
makrishna (2026) showed that content optimized according to the QIO model (based
on identifying audience intent and bridging informational gaps) achieved statistically
significant higher levels of visibility compared with non-optimized content. AEO lit -
erature points to six core principles for content preparation: structured data, formulat-
ing content in a question-and-answer pattern, alignment with search intent, reliance
on trusted sources (E-E-A-T), user experience optimization, and the temporal updat -
ing of content (Surfer SEO, 2024; CXL, 2025; Built In, 2024). These principles rest on the
premise that generative models grant priority to content they can verify structurally
and contextually, not merely to content that is most coherent in terms of keywords.
Measuring Brand Visibility in Generative Engines
In a large-scale analytical study covering more than 100,000 generative re -
sponses linked to more than 100 brands, Kumar (2026) identified a structural dispar-
ity in visibility according to brand strength (73% for global brands versus 11% for
small brands), and found that 78% of citations were directed to companies’ official
websites-a finding with direct relevance to the present study, which assumes that
the absence of preparation deprives a brand of its natural share of citation even if it
is commercially popular. Popescu (2026) likewise affirmed that AEO/GEO does not
represent an alternative to SEO but rather a complementary logic that redefines the
value of visibility from a “fixed ranking” to a “probability of selection across diverse
queries.” Islam et al. (2024) further emphasized that the shift toward generative
models necessitates a reconsideration of digital content strategies in their entirety,
not only at the technical level but also at the editorial and strategic levels.
The Behavioral Dimension: Consumers and Generative AI
Studies in the behavioral track have agreed on the centrality of trust and per -
ceived information quality in shaping purchase decisions through generative tools.
Tafesse and Sandhu (2026)-drawing on a sample of 188 users-showed that informa -
tion quality (comprehensiveness, credibility, recency) positively affects decision confi-
dence and decision satisfaction, while Pham et al. (2026) confirmed the mediating role
of trust in the relationship between chatbot characteristics and the purchasing behav-
ior of Generation Z. In the Egyptian context, Salam-El-Abd and Youssef (2026)-draw-
ing on a sample of 366 consumers and integrating TAM with the SOR model-demon-
strated that interactivity and information quality enhance perceived usefulness, trust,

Theory One: Uses and Gratifications (U&G)
Uses and Gratifications Theory assumes an active audience that consciously
selects the content that satisfies its cognitive and functional needs (Katz et al., 1974;
Makkawi & Abdel Meguid, 1998; Abdel Hamid, 2000). The theory rests on several
key assumptions: (1) the audience is active and directed toward specific goals; (2) the
individual chooses media and content on the basis of expectations of gratification;
(3) media compete with other sources of gratification; and (4) judgments of media
value originate from the individual, not from external parties. The theory classifies
the needs that users seek to gratify into four main categories: cognitive needs (seek-
ing to understand the environment and acquire information), affective needs (emo-
tional experiences and pleasure), personal integrative needs (enhancing status and
confidence), social integrative needs (communication and interaction with others),
and tension-release needs (escape from pressures).
The employment of the theory in this study rests on the premise that the gen -
erative search user engages in “purposeful, immediate search activity,” expecting
instant gratification (a direct answer about offers, branches, and comparisons). If the
brand’s website provides structured content in question–answer form prepared ac -
cording to AEO, gratification is achieved through the official source and the brand’s
visibility as a trusted source is reinforced. If the content lacks preparation, the user
resorts-through the mediation of the generative engine-to alternative sources, and
what this study terms “Informational Representation Displacement” of the brand
occurs in favor of intermediaries. The theory thus explains the relationship between
the quality of preparation (independent variable) and the brand’s presence in gener-
ated answers (dependent variable).
Theory Two: The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)
The Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989) posits that perceived useful -
ness and perceived ease of use form the cognitive response that produces the affec -
tive attitude, then behavioral intention, and finally actual use (Abdel Hamid, 2000).
The model comprises four main components: external variables (which influence
perceptions), cognitive perceptions (perceived usefulness and perceived ease of
use), the affective response (attitude toward use), and the behavioral response (be -
havioral intention and actual use). The model is employed in this study to explain
the readiness of Kazyon’s marketing teams to adopt AEO, while taking into account
the criticisms directed at the model (its limited organizational factors) by incorporat-
ing contextual external variables: institutional support, training, technical readiness,
and inter-team coordination-an employment that comes closer to the model’s orga -
nizational extensions (TAM 2 / Organizational TAM). This employment is consis -

Research Gap and the Position of the Current Study
The chronological survey of the literature reveals a three-stage evolution: early
acceptance studies of chatbots (Sidlauskiene et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2022), followed
by comparisons between generative tools and traditional engines (Kim & Priluck,
2025), and then the shift toward the generative search environment itself (the 2026
studies). AEO literature, however, has remained technical in character, Western in
context, and poor in communication-theory grounding; integration has likewise
been absent between the two levels of analysis: the level of the digital product (the
website and the engines) and the level of the institutional producer (the human
teams). The current study positions itself at this intersection, presenting the first
Arab three-tool field test of AEO in the retail sector.

Data Collection Tools
First: The Structural Content Analysis Checklist (AEO Checklist):
The checklist comprised eleven standards derived from the specialized litera -
ture on AEO and GEO, and was constructed on the basis of the following sources:
the study of Kultavirta (2026), which emphasized the importance of structured data
and content architecture; the study of Chatwin and Bliey (2026), which identified
the standards of entity verification and citation in generative answers; the compre -
hensive CXL (2025) guide to AEO standards; Surfer SEO’s (2024) recommendations
on formulating content in a question–answer pattern; and Ramakrishna’s (2026)
model based on audience-intent analysis. The standards were classified into four
main groups: structural-technical standards (informational architecture, structured
data, rich results); editorial standards (linguistic formulation, the question–answer
model, search-intent alignment, temporal updating); experiential standards (user
experience, cross-channel integration, identity presence); and strategic standards
(composite marketing value).
Second: The Generative Response Test Card:
Designed to document the appearance of the official website (explicit mention/
citation/absence) and the type of sources relied upon (official/intermediary/gener -
al generative), with documentation through screenshots on different dates to track
temporal stability. The nine queries were formulated according to the search-intent
patterns adopted in AEO literature: informational (learning about the new offers),
local (locating the nearest branch), functional (learning about delivery services),
seasonal (Ramadan offers), general comparison (the cheapest supermarket), direct
comparison (Kazyon versus Carrefour), instructional (how to benefit from the of -
fers), cross-sector comparison (Kazyon versus B.TECH), and behavioral-explanato -
ry (the motives for preferring Kazyon).
Third: The Semi-Structured In-Depth Interview Guide:
Consisting of open-ended questions covering the following axes: awareness of
the concept, degree of adoption, use of technical tools, challenges, performance track-
ing, and future visions and proposals. It was administered via Google Forms after
contacting participants through LinkedIn and receiving responses via WhatsApp.
Validity and Reliability
Validity of the analysis tool and the interview guide: The two instruments were
presented to an arbitration committee of ten professors specializing in digital media,
marketing communication, and public relations at Egyptian and Arab universities,
and were approved after minor modifications (face validity and content validity).
Reliability of the content analysis: The temporal re-coding method was employed
(one week apart) with an independent external coder who re-coded 20% of the content;
the average Holsti coefficient of inter-coder agreement reached 94.4% (see Table 1).

Results
Structural Content Analysis Results for the Kazyon Website (Q1)
The analysis according to the eleven standards revealed a mixed picture dom-
inated by generative deficiency:
- Informational architecture: The homepage lacked a top navigation bar lead-
ing to pivotal sections (About Us, Branches, Payment Methods, FAQ), and
near-total reliance on offer images prevailed, without alternative texts (Alt
Text) or indexable text links, which prevents machine reading of the content.
- Linguistic formulation: The phrasing was direct and understandable to the
human user (“Kazyon’s offers today”), but devoid of explanatory context
and decision-support phrases (“the offer is valid until stocks run out,” de -
tails of inclusion and exclusion), and integration between in-image texts and
the site’s textual headings was absent.
- Temporal updating: The website recorded a sole positive indicator in the
form of regularly dated updates of the offers (at least seven time-document-
ed offers during the analysis period), reflecting commitment to the recency
criterion, though this remained isolated from structural support.
- The question–answer model and structured data: Any FAQ section was en-
tirely absent, as was any employment of Schema.org markup (Offer/Prod -
uct/FAQPage) or JSON-LD/RDFa formats, which renders product and price
content “visible to humans only” and irretrievable by generative systems.
- Search-intent alignment: The website displayed initial formal signals (nat-
ural-language headings such as “today’s lowest offers”) without detailed
textual content that actually answers the intent (classified lists, comparative
content, explanatory paragraphs).

- User experience and cross-channel integration: The website recorded good
compatibility with mobile phones (loading speed, interface flexibility, clear
interactive buttons), but lacked a footer containing links to the governing
pages (return policy, shipping). In contrast, the brand demonstrated notable
integration through an omnichannel model (the app, Facebook, LinkedIn)
with visual identity consistency-an informational asset that remains unin -

- Marketing value:  The content concentrated on a single price dimension
(“the cheapest brand”) without highlighting other value dimensions (quali-

ability to generate rich answers of the “benefit + reason + evaluation” type.
Analytical summary: The website achieved a high score on only two standards
(temporal updating and cross-channel integration), a medium score on three (linguis -
tic formulation, mobile experience, identity presence), and a low or null score on six
standards that represent the technical core of AEO (informational architecture, ques -
tion–answer, structured data, search intent, rich results, composite marketing value).

Key takeaways from the matrix:
- Informational representation displacement:  In eight of the nine queries,
the official website was absent as a source, and the brand was represented
through intermediaries (offer sites, maps, social platforms) or through gen-
eral generative content not subject to institutional verification.
- The non-directed positioning gap:  In the general comparative query
(“cheapest supermarket”), Kazyon was excluded entirely in favor of com -
petitors, although its marketing positioning is in fact based on price-deci -
sive evidence of the absence of a semantic link between the brand and its
positioning concepts in the engines’ digital memory.
- Disparity in engine capabilities:  Perplexity AI excelled in rich analytical
generation, Bing Copilot dealt flexibly with consumer queries through rich
cards, and Google interacted effectively with the local (maps) and instruc -
tional (instant answers) patterns-meaning that successful preparation must
be multi-platform rather than single-platform.
- The seasonal gap: Despite the intensity of the Ramadan campaigns on social
channels, this intensity did not transfer to the website in an indexable struc -
ture, squandering an opportunity to dominate a critical purchasing peak.
In-Depth Interviews (Q3, Q4, Q5)
Thematic analysis was conducted on the answers of the twelve participants
and yielded six themes:
- Theme One: Cognitive awareness of AEO (the cognitive response in
TAM). Awareness varied across job levels. The technical specialists (the
digital marketing director, the senior digital marketing specialist, and the
brand development manager) demonstrated a precise understanding of
the fundamental difference (“visibility as an answer, not a link”; “user in -
tent, not keywords”), whereas department leaders acknowledged that the
term is not officially used and that awareness depends on “individual fol -
low-ups.” Others (product management, foods, B2B) reported recent theo -
retical knowledge without applied capability. The dominant pattern: partial
awareness, hierarchically distributed.
- Theme Two: Degree of actual adoption. Practices were confined to limited
individual initiatives (reviewing the FAQ page, experimenting with FAQ
Schema on specific pages, rephrasing headings), against near-complete con-
sensus on the absence of an institutional plan and the non-inclusion of AEO
among official priorities or performance indicators.
- Theme Three: The question–answer model.  The sample split into three ap -
proximate thirds: one third observes an emerging partial application; one
third sees it as confined to certain sections, with a predominance of traditional
descriptive phrasing; and one third noticed no structured architecture of this
pattern-reflecting the absence of institutional standardization of the concept.
- Theme Four: Technical tools.  The use of structured data was confined to
preliminary experiments that were “not systematic and without an over -

in TAM). The majority expressed high readiness for adoption, condition -

can be classified into: technical proposals (rebuilding the FAQ with Sche -

Discussion of Results
The Integrated Theoretical Interpretation
The study’s results derive their interpretive depth from the integration of the
two theoretical frameworks. From the perspective of Uses and Gratifications The -
ory, it becomes evident that the Egyptian user searching for offers, branches, and
comparisons did in fact obtain cognitive and functional gratifications-but through
intermediaries, not through the official source. This is what the study terms “infor -
mational representation displacement”: the generative engine performed its func -
tion, the user’s need was gratified, and the sole loser is the brand, which lost the
authority of informational narration about itself. This result is consistent with the
assumptions of Uses and Gratifications Theory that audiences choose media and
content on the basis of their expectations of gratification, and that media compete
with other sources of gratification. Here, the generative engine and the intermediar-
ies competed with the brand’s official website to provide informational gratification,
and the absence of generative preparation on the website tipped the balance toward
the intermediaries.
The results also align with Kumar (2026) regarding the centrality of official
websites in citations (78%), and reveal, conversely, that unprepared brands are ex -
cluded from this share even when they are the very subject of the question. The
non-directed positioning gap (Kazyon’s exclusion from the “cheapest supermarket”

From the perspective of the Technology Acceptance Model, the hierarchical
variance in awareness and the non-institutionalized individual initiatives are ex -
plained by the fact that the perceived usefulness of AEO was high among specialists
(which accounts for their positive behavioral intention), while perceived ease of use
remained low across the sample (owing to the absence of training and oversight
tools). The interaction of the external variables (weak institutional support, the pri -
ority of operational expansion) produced a state of “Suspended Acceptance”: a con-
ditional affective and behavioral readiness that has not converted into actual institu-
tional use. This reading is consistent with Al-Asdoudi (2023) and with Salam-El-Abd
and Youssef (2026) in the Egyptian context, and extends them by demonstrating that
the barrier to adoption in the AEO case is not the perception of usefulness but the
enabling infrastructure. The interviews reveal that the technical specialists showed
high perceived usefulness and, consequently, positive behavioral intention, whereas
perceived ease of use remained low among all participants as a result of the absent
training and tools-producing a gap between intention and action. This finding sheds
light on the importance of external variables in TAM: institutional support, training,
and inter-team coordination constitute the enabling conditions necessary for con -
verting intention into actual use, which goes beyond the original TAM toward its
organizational extensions (TAM 2).
The comparison among the threefold results (the website, the engines, the in -
terviews) reveals a complex interactive pattern: the absence of technical preparation
in the website (first level) led to weak visibility in the engines (second level); weak
visibility, in turn, weakened the institutional incentive for adoption (third level);
and the absence of institutional awareness reinforced the continued absence of tech-
nical preparation-a vicious circle that requires intervention from both ends. This
circular interaction explains why technically improving the website alone is insuf -
ficient without institutional enablement, and why institutional awareness alone is
insufficient without translation into technical procedures.

strongest explanatory candidate for the absent citation. The results also support the

Practical Implications: A Proposed Model for Institutional AEO Adoption
Drawing on the methodological triangulation, the study proposes an opera -
tional three-level model:
- The technical level (architecture): An informational restructuring that sep-
arates content from images; embedding Schema.org markup (Offer, Prod -
uct, FAQPage) in JSON-LD format; and building an FAQ section designed
according to the seven search-intent patterns documented in this study.
- The editorial level (content): An editorial guide based on “the complete an-
swer” (benefit + reason + context + timing); indexable seasonal landing pages
linked to campaigns; and comparative and positioning content that consoli -
dates the semantic link between the brand and its strategic concepts (such as
“the cheapest in Egypt”) in order to prevent positioning displacement.
- The institutional level (enablement):  Including AEO in the marketing
team’s KPIs; assigning a weekly generative-tracking officer; specialized
training on intent-analysis tools; a mandatory coordination mechanism
among marketing, content, and technical development; and a limited-scope
pilot program (three pages / one month) to demonstrate impact before gen-
eralization-the most frequently repeated proposal in the interviews.
Conclusion
Key Conclusions
- The Kazyon website suffers a structural deficiency in the governing AEO stan-
dards (structured data, question–answer, intent alignment), despite possess -
ing two positive assets (temporal updating and cross-channel integration).
- The brand is represented in generative answers through intermediaries, and
is entirely excluded from non-directed positioning queries, which threatens
its digital identity with “informational representation displacement” and
“strategic positioning displacement.”

- A pattern of “suspended acceptance” prevails within the company: partial,

- The decisive barrier to adoption is organizational-enabling (training, coor -

- The transition to the generative search environment requires a holistic

Scientific Contributions
- Grounding the AEO concept in Arab literature and providing a reference
analysis checklist of eleven standards that is generalizable and replicable.
- Presenting an integrated theoretical framework (TAM × U&G) to explain
AEO at both the user and institutional levels, thereby responding to the
theoretical-grounding gap in this field.
- Conceptually documenting two new phenomena: “informational represen-
tation displacement” and “suspended acceptance.”
- Documenting the phenomenon of a brand’s exclusion from its own posi -
tioning in non-directed queries-a strategic loss more serious than the mere
absence of citation-which is added to the literature as the concept of “strate-
gic positioning displacement.”
Study Limitations
The limitations include: confining the case to a single brand in a single sector;
the unavailability of the AI Overviews feature to the Egyptian user on some test
dates (which was addressed through repeated documentation and methodical ob -
servation); the rapidly evolving nature of generative engines, which may alter some
of the observed patterns over time; and the reliance, in the interviews, on a purpo -
sive sample from which statistical generalization is not intended.
Proposed Future Studies
- Multi-brand, multi-sector comparative studies in the Egyptian and Arab
markets to measure differences in generative readiness.
- A pre/post experimental design to measure the effect of the recommended
AEO interventions on actual visibility over a controlled period of time.
- Introducing the consumer as a unit of analysis to measure the effect of gen-
erative visibility on brand trust and purchase intention (linking the techni -
cal and behavioral tracks).
- Testing the applicability of the proposed model in different Arab cultural
and organizational contexts.
- Studying the impact of AEO interventions on actual digital performance
indicators (traffic, conversion rates, brand awareness) over an extended pe-
riod of time.
