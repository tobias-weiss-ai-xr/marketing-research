Abstract
Purpose: The study aims to provide a systematic and integrative account of athlete branding by
synthesizing conceptualizations, processes, and management stra tegies across diverse contexts.
Unlike prior reviews, it incorporates both Web of Science (WoS)  and Google Scholar (GS), ensuring
coverage of peer-reviewed and gray literature to capture a more inclusive research landscape.
Design/methodology/approach: The review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines with a deductive
design guided by three research questions. Four conceptual incl usion categories from branding and
sport management informed the coding frame. Data were drawn fro m WoS and Google Scholar
sources published up to 2024, enabling a comparative analysis o f coverage and focus across the two
databases.
Findings: Six thematic areas were identified: athlete brand identity and measurement, brand growth
and evolution, athlete–fan relationship, communication and medi a influence, strategic athlete brand
management, and contextual/cultural factors. The findings highlight the dominance of Western, male
and elite athlete contexts; an emphasis on quantitative, deduct ive approaches and limited attention
to digital-native, cross-cultural and female athlete branding.
Originality/value: This is the first review to combine WoS and GS coverage in athl ete branding,
offering a comprehensive thematic synthesis and a comparative d atabase perspective. By identifying
overlooked areas and proposing future research directions, this  study provides actionable pathways
for diversifying contexts, expanding methodological approaches and enhancing the global relevance
of athlete branding research.

Athlete branding introduction and outline
Athlete brands have emerged as a distinct strand of human brand  research, conceptualized
in diverse ways that emphasize complementary, yet distinct pill ars. The first perspective
highlights the symbolic meaning of the athlete brand, portrayin g it as a public persona built
around recognizable elements such as a name, face, or image (Ar ai et al. 2014; Hasaan et al.
2018b). The second stresses identi ty markers and associations, framing athlete branding as
attributes such as personality or reputation that distinguish a thletes in the marketplace and
promise functional and emotional value (Williams et al. 2015a). The third pillar emphasizes
the branding aspects, focusing on brand personality and positioning athletes as human brands
with marketable identities (Carlson and Donavan, 2013). Finally , audience-centered
definitions present athlete branding as including an athlete's publicly expressed personality
(Hasaan and Kerem, 2017) or a coll ective opinion toward an athl ete (Hasaan et al. 2018a).
Against this backdrop, athlete branding is situated within a broader branding ecosystem, with
athletes often grouped with celebrity (Carlson and Donavan, 201 3), product (Hasaan, 2021),
and cultural brands (Vila-Lopez et al. 2024), or hybrid forms that fuse sport, celebrity, and
commerce (Parmentier and Fischer, 2012). Within this ecosystemic view, athlete branding is
frequently approached as a dynamic and relational process shape d through interactions
among athletes, fans, media, and cultural intermediaries (Anderski et al. 2023).
However, this ecosystemic expansion has also contributed to a c onceptual overextension of
the athlete branding construct, making it increasingly difficult to distinguish between studies
that meaningfully advance athlete branding theory and those tha t engage with it only
tangentially. As a result, the term “athlete branding” is often  a p p l i e d  t o  a  w i d e  r a n g e  o f
research involving visible or marketable athletes, even when br anding processes, strategies,
or management dynamics are not explicitly theorized or examined . This creates a need for a
systematic review that critically scrutinizes studies explicitly concerned with athlete branding
processes rather than aggregating all research that merely references branded athletes. This
review seeks to clarify the boundaries of athlete branding scho larship and identify which
theoretical and methodological approaches genuinely inform bran d development and
management, as well as unresolved issues that persist despite t h e  g r o w i n g  v o l u m e  o f
literature. Drawing on a broad a nd inclusive evidence base also  allows this study to capture
forms of scholarship that may be unevenly visible within domina nt academic outlets,
contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of how athlete branding knowledge has
evolved across different contexts.
Despite these divergent uses of athlete branding across discipl ines, the field remains
conceptually and methodologically fragmented. Theoretical lenses have been borrowed from
several domains, such as marketing, sociology, psychology, and media studies, without a
shared framework for integration. Studies differ in how they define an athlete brand and the

and shifts over time.
Methodology
Systematic literature reviews can be reasoned inductively, deductively, or abductively (Sauer
and Seuring, 2023). Inductive reviews derive categories from observations, abductive reviews
reconcile surprising findings with existing theories, and deduc t i v e  r e v i e w s  b e g i n  w i t h
established frameworks and exami ne whether studies confirm, ref ine, or challenge those
propositions. Our review followed abductive logic, combining th eoretically informed
structuring with iterative sensitivity to patterns emerging across the literature.

RQ1 Current state of research on athlete branding
Category 1: athlete branding concepts & elements
The foundations of athlete brandi n g  s c h o l a r s h i p  c e n t e r  o n  c l a r if y i n g  w h a t  c o n s t i t u t e s  a n
athlete brand. Rather than a single fixed framework, research c onverges on three recurrent
pillars: performance, appearance , and lifestyle factors. These core elements operate as the
baseline of athlete image, yet studies have also revealed impor tant contextual expansions,
such as combining performance and lifestyle to sustain athlete credibility (Hashemian and
Shojaei, 2015; Hawkins-Jedlicka et al. 2023), demonstrating the importance of digital
interactions and fan relations hips (Constantinescu, 2016), and showing how sport-specific
attributes and moral values apply in athlete branding within cu ltural and ethical frameworks
(Keshtidar et al. 2020). These contributions indicate that athlete branding elements are both
stable in the persistence of performance, appearance, and lifestyle across settings and flexible
in incorporating new dimensions such as digital presence, cultu ral identity, and moral
credibility. This duality suggests that conceptual work on athlete branding has moved beyond
identifying static attributes to recognizing how brand elements  are socially and contextually
constructed, reflecting the evolving expectations of fans, media, and sporting cultures.
Category 2: athlete branding development models
A second stream of scholarship has advanced integrative models o f  a t h l e t e  b r a n d i n g
development, shifting the focus from what a brand is  to how it evolves . Early contributions
positioned athletes as human brands, with prestige and distinct iveness as key predictors of
fan identification (Carlson and Donavan, 2013). Arai et al. (2014) extended the brand image
model into a life-cycle framework (Hasaan et al. 2021), highlighting how athlete capital and
reinvention sustain brand longevity. Subsequent models broadene d the developmental lens
linking media, communication, and social agents to brand loyalty and sponsor outcomes (e.g.
Hasaan et al. 2016, 2018a) and an intellectual capital perspective, emphasiz ing knowledge,
relationships, and r eputation (Aprile et al. 2022). More recent work by Bredikhina et al.
(2023a) conceptualized athlete branding as a process of authenticity management negotiated
between fans, sponsors, and the media. Together, these models r eflect a clear progression
from static representations of brand attributes to dynamic, multilevel processes that capture
growth, reinvention, and stakeho lder negotiation. They undersco re that athlete brands are
not simply owned but are actively constructed, maintained and c ontested within ever-
changing social and commercial landscapes.
Category 3: athlete branding processes
Research on athlete branding pro cesses has shifted from definin g brand attributes to
examining the mechanisms through which brand meaning is created  a n d  s u s t a i n e d .  E a r l y
work underscored the importance of media representation and per sona construction,
showing how visibility in mass media channels shapes athlete equity (Parmentier and Fischer,
2012). Subsequent studies have focused on micro-level drivers, such as behavior, personality,
lifestyle, and performance, as the building blocks of brand identity (Nazemi et al. 2020a) and

authenticity actors for athlete branding (Hasaan and Fişne, 2021). Linsner et al. (2020) further
operationalized these processes into measurable dimensions, inc luding integrity, athletic

highlighted the interactive nature of branding created through digital interactions between

as something athletes project to branding, as an ongoing negotiation shaped simultaneously
by athletes, fans, the media, and commercial agents.
Category 4: athlete branding management strategies
The final stream explores how athletes actively manage their br ands through brand co-
creation. A consistent theme is impression and reputation manag ement (Boroujerdi et al.
2024b), where athletes consciously  curate their behavior and co mmunication to align with
the desired brand meanings (Xie et al. 2024). Other scholars have emphasized authenticity
management, showing how athletes negotiate the tensions between  commercial demands
and their personal identity (Dašić et al. 2021; Hasaan et al. 2018b). The third focus is strategic
capitalization, with studies highlighting how athletes leverage social, cultural, and intellectual
capital to strengthen sponsorship ties and career longevity (Ratten, 2015). Finally, digital and
social media have become central to brand management, where athletes directly engage with
fans to control the narratives and maintain relevance (Anderski et al. 2023; Doyle et al. 2022).
These studies underline that athlete branding management is les s about static positioning
and more about ongoing negotiations regarding authenticity, marketability, and stakeholder
expectations.

Discussion
The discussion below is organize d around the findings of the re search questions, presenting
the proposed future directions derived directly from the concep tual, thematic,
methodological, and contextual gaps identified through the systematic review. Regarding the
current state of athlete branding research (RQ1), despite conve rgence on the core triad of
performance, appearance, and lifestyle (Arai et al. 2014; Nazemi et al. 2020b), no universally
accepted framework delineates which elements are central, perip heral, or context-
dependent. Many models extend or modify dimensions (e.g. moral credibility, digital
presence, and intellectual capital) (Keshtidar et al. 2020; Aprile et al. 2022), but these
extensions are not always integrated into a cumulative structur e. As the review revealed
considerable variation in the conceptualization of athlete bran d elements across existing
models, future research should focus on a clearer taxonomy that  distinguishes between
foundational brand elements  (likely stable across contexts) and adaptive brand dimensions
(fluid and context-specific). This boundary setting reduces conceptual inflation and allows for
more precise cross-study comparisons.

For RQ2, the review highlights not only the shortcomings within  the predefined themes but
also a set of emergent illustrations that extend the conceptual boundaries of the field. While
some of these illustrations have been indirectly touched upon o r in a limited number of
studies, they remain largely underdeveloped, offering promising  avenues for further
exploration. These directions emerge directly from low-frequenc y, yet conceptually
meaningful, topics identified during the abductive synthesis. A lthough they did not warrant
new higher-order themes, they consistently pointed to areas in which the literature remains
underdeveloped. Philanthropy and social impact have emerged as underexplored dimensions
of athlete branding (Kunkel et al. 2022). Although high-profile ath letes frequently engage in
charitable initiatives, few studies have examined how philanthr opy translates into positive
brand outcomes, such as sustained brand equity, credibility, an d fan loyalty. Questions
regarding authenticity, scale, and media framing remain open for enquiry. Periphery Branding
illustrates how athletes achieve recognition and symbolic value  in markets outside their
primary competitive spaces, such as Maradona's enduring reputat ion in China (Ferrari et al.
2021). Future research could assess how peripheral audiences construct athlete meaning and
how these dynamics differ from core market strategies. Technology-Driven Branding extends
beyond social media into AI, VR, blockchain, and digital collec tables, raising questions about
ownership, authenticity, and co-creation. Studies could investi gate how digital innovations
reshape athlete–fan interactions and brand monetization. Mental  Health and Branding have
gained visibility; however, little is known about how disclosures of stress, burnout, or recovery
affect perceptions of authenticity and commercial potential. Fi nally, Intersectional Identities
remain underexplored. Gender, race, religion, and nationality i ntersect to create
differentiated branding pathways, yet most models assume a univ e r s a l i s t  l o g i c  b a s e d  o n
Western male athletes. Together, these emergent illustrations h ighlight gaps that remain at
the periphery of existing work but are essential to advance ath lete branding research in line
with contemporary cultural, technological, and ethical realities.

Research limitations
This study has several limitations. First, including GS was a p ragmatic choice because of the
limited coverage of athlete branding research in WoS. Only peer -reviewed journal articles
were retained for analysis, but the broader indexing practices of GS may introduce greater
variability in publication quality and review standards. Thus, the findings should be
interpreted with an awareness of these differences in quality a ssurance processes across
databases. Second, as with quali tative syntheses, human judgmen t shapes the themes.
Although transparency and reflexivity were emphasized, the inte rpretive nature of theming
inevitably carries inherent subjectivity. Furthermore, although  peer debriefing, expert
consultation, consensus discussions, and an audit trail were us ed to enhance reliability, the
review relied on primary coding by a single researcher, rather than independent dual coding.
Fu ture reviews may strength en  met hodo lo gical rigor by incorporating multiple coders and
formal intercoder agreement procedures. Finally, the scope of this review prioritized mapping
the athlete branding research landscape, which meant that some theoretical and practical
considerations were not fully explored.
