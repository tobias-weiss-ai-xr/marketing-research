Abstract
Founded in 1672 and rebranded in 1894, Rongbaozhai (荣宝斋) developed from a
Qing dynasty stationery shop into a leading cultural institution specializing in fine
art, calligraphy, and scholarly collectibles. Over more than three centuries, it navi-
gated major historical transformations, shifting from the literati cultural economy
of the Qing dynasty to modern commercial systems and later expanding into Hong
Kong’s international market. Drawing on Bourdieu’s field theory and Zhang Jiji-
ao’s dual-structure model, this study argues that these transitions can be explained
through four dimensions of brand authenticity: continuity, credibility, integrity,
and symbolism. Rather than treating authenticity as a branding attribute, the paper
reconceptualizes these dimensions as institutional conditions that enable the repro-
duction and conversion of cultural capital. It further identifies the Confucian-Mohist
principle of yi yi qu li (以义取利, securing profit through righteousness) as the ethi-
cal foundation of integrity, linking authenticity to the moral logic of traditional Chi-
nese commerce. Using a historical case study organized into five periods, with the
Hong Kong branch serving as a critical negative case, the study shows that cultural
capital remains convertible into economic value across changing socioeconomic
fields only when all four conditions are preserved. When they weaken, especially
across field boundaries, this convertibility declines, limiting the transfer of legiti-
macy and value.

1  Introduction: capital portfolio, organizational habitus,

1.1  Research background
China’s official system for recognizing “Time-honored Brands” (Lao Zihao, 老字号)
presents a central analytical puzzle: how can heritage-based enterprises preserve the
cultural and symbolic capital that underpins their legitimacy while remaining eco-
nomically viable across changing market conditions? Unlike conventional firms, these
organizations function as institutional carriers of intangible cultural heritage, deriving
value not only from commercial performance but also from historical continuity, tradi-
tional knowledge, and cultural authority. They are formally recognized through a hier-
archical system of certification, at the apex of which is the national designation “China
Time-honored Brand” (Zhonghua Lao Zihao, 中华老字号),1 awarded to enterprises
with more than fifty years of history, distinctive traditional products, skills, or ser -
vices, and significant cultural heritage. The longevity of these firms, therefore, raises
a broader theoretical question concerning the conditions under which cultural capital
can be reproduced, legitimized, and converted into economic value across different
historical and institutional contexts.
Among China Time-honored Brands, Rongbaozhai provides an especially valu-
able case for examining how cultural capital can be reproduced and converted into
economic value across changing historical fields. Founded in 1672 as Songzhuzhai
(松竹斋) during the Kangxi reign and rebranded as Rongbaozhai in 1894, the enter-
prise combines exceptional institutional longevity with repeated adaptation to shift-
ing political, economic, and cultural environments (Zheng 2019). Throughout its
history, Rongbaozhai has derived legitimacy not only from commercial success but
also from its role as a custodian of artistic traditions and cultural authority, mak -
ing it representative of the dual commercial and heritage functions characteristic of
China Time-honored Brands (Song and Kim 2022). Its development spans several

retirement of key leaders. High operating costs, changing consumer preferences, and
a structural misalignment between Rongbaozhai’s heritage-based model and Hong

of operations, in 2000 and 2018. These difficulties suggest that symbolic legitimacy
accumulated in one field cannot automatically be converted into economic value in
another. Although Rongbaozhai possessed considerable historical prestige and cul-
tural authority, these assets proved less effective in a market increasingly shaped
by investment logics, global branding, and contemporary artistic tastes, where tra-
ditional Chinese art occupied a relatively marginal position. The reopening of the
Hong Kong branch in 2013 reflected a strategic attempt to address this challenge by
shifting emphasis from commercial transactions alone to cultural communication,
heritage interpretation, and brand positioning. Through exhibitions, curated experi-
ences, and cultural programming, Rongbaozhai sought to reconstruct the conditions
under which its cultural capital could acquire value in a different field. The Hong
Kong case therefore serves as a critical contrast to the company’s broader historical
trajectory, illustrating both the possibilities and limits of converting cultural capital
across distinct institutional and market environments.
1.2  Research gap and questions
Existing scholarship on China Time-honored Brands has largely concentrated on
issues of brand revitalization, consumer perception, heritage marketing, and digital
transformation, emphasizing how historical brands can maintain market relevance
in contemporary consumption environments (Song and Kim 2022; Wang and Zhang
2023; Zhao and Xiang 2024). While this literature has generated important insights
into consumer responses to heritage signals and the strategic renewal of traditional
brands, it tends to treat authenticity primarily as a marketing resource or perceptual

outcome. Consequently, limited attention has been paid to the deeper institutional
mechanisms through which authenticity enables heritage organizations to preserve
legitimacy and reproduce value across changing historical and socioeconomic con-
texts. In particular, existing studies rarely examine how cultural capital accumulated
under one set of social conditions can remain effective when organizations encoun-
ter new political, economic, or cultural fields. This limitation is especially signifi-
cant for China Time-honored Brands, whose long histories are characterized not by
stability but by repeated adaptation to shifting institutional environments.
Bourdieu’s field theory provides a potentially powerful framework for address-
ing this issue because it conceptualizes cultural, symbolic, and economic capital as
relational resources whose value depends on field-specific structures and rules of
recognition (Bourdieu 1993). Although field theory has been widely applied to cul-
tural production and has increasingly informed research on heritage governance and
cultural institutions in China (Zhang 2014, 2016),2 its implications for understand-
ing heritage brands remain insufficiently developed. Existing research generally
examines field dynamics and brand authenticity as separate phenomena rather than
investigating how authenticity itself may function as a mechanism linking the repro-
duction of cultural capital to organizational survival. As a result, the process through
which heritage-based organizations maintain legitimacy while navigating transitions
between different fields remains theoretically underexplored.
Parallel constraints persist within the brand authenticity literature. Morhart et al.’s
(2015) influential four-dimensional framework conceptualizes authenticity through
the dimensions of continuity, credibility, integrity, and symbolism and operationalizes
them primarily as components of consumers’ perceived brand authenticity. The frame-
work has therefore been employed largely to explain consumer attitudes, trust, loyalty,
and purchasing behavior. However, less attention has been given to the possibility that
these dimensions also possess an institutional and field-dependent character. Rather
than viewing authenticity solely as a consumer perception, this study reconceptualizes
it as a set of structural conditions that determine whether accumulated cultural capi-
tal can be recognized, legitimized, and converted into economic value across different
historical contexts. This alternative interpretation is particularly relevant for heritage
organizations whose survival depends not only on market acceptance but also on the
preservation of cultural authority and historical legitimacy.
Furthermore, existing studies have seldom examined the cultural and ethical
foundations of authenticity in non-Western contexts. While integrity is generally
defined in terms of sincerity, responsibility, and commitment to stakeholders, the
normative content underlying this dimension remains inadequately specified. In the
Chinese context, the long-standing Confucian and Mohist principle of  yi yi qu li  (
以义取利), or securing profit through righteousness, offers a historically embedded
moral logic that may explain how commercial legitimacy and cultural authority are
mutually constituted. Yet the relationship between this ethical tradition, authenticity,
and the reproduction of cultural capital has received little systematic analysis.

To address these gaps, this study asks the following research questions: How do
the four dimensions of brand authenticity—namely continuity, credibility, integrity,
and symbolism—function as structural conditions that enable or constrain the insti-
tutionalized reproduction of cultural capital across changing fields? In what ways do
the Confucian and Mohist ethics of  yi yi qu li constitute the substantive content of

Chinese commercial civilization? Finally, what explains variation in the convert-

Kong branch of Rongbaozhai represent a case in which this convertibility became
constrained when one or more of these conditions weakened? By addressing these
questions, the study seeks to reconceptualize brand authenticity as a field-theoretic
mechanism linking cultural legitimacy to economic value across historical change.
1.3  Theoretical framework: conceptual mapping and analysis
This study argues that the long-term survival of heritage enterprises cannot be
explained by historical longevity alone. Rather, the capacity of cultural capital to
retain legitimacy and generate economic value depends on the institutional condi-
tions through which it is reproduced and recognized across changing social contexts.
To explain this process, the study integrates Bourdieu’s theory of capital and fields
with Zhang Jijiao’s dual-structure model of Chinese social organization. Together,
these perspectives provide a framework for examining how Rongbaozhai accumu-
lated, reproduced, and transformed cultural legitimacy and how, under different
political, economic, and cultural conditions, that legitimacy was either successfully
converted into economic value or its conversion was constrained.
Bourdieu’s field theory, developed through a critique of structuralism and eco-
nomic reductionism and drawing on insights from Marx, Weber, and Durkheim,
explains how social positions, forms of capital, and habitus shape action within rela-
tively autonomous social fields. Capital is understood as accumulated resources that
enable actors to secure position and influence within a field (Bourdieu 1984, 1986).
From this perspective, Rongbaozhai’s historical development can be understood as
a process of acquiring, reproducing, and converting different forms of capital under
changing field conditions.
Four forms of capital are particularly relevant to this study. Cultural capital refers
to the artisanal knowledge, aesthetic expertise, and authority associated with tra-
ditional artistic production (Bourdieu 1984, 1993). Social capital consists of net-
works connecting the enterprise to artists, collectors, scholars, and cultural institu-
tions (Anheier et al. 1995). Symbolic capital refers to the legitimacy and prestige
recognized by actors within a field as worthy of trust and respect (Bourdieu 1992,
1986).  Political capital, particularly salient in the Chinese context, refers to the
capacity to secure state support, policy protection, and institutional endorsement,
thereby shaping the conditions under which organizations acquire legitimacy and
access strategic resources (Zhang 2016; Suchman 1995). These forms of capital are
neither fixed nor independent. Their value depends on the structure of the field in

which they operate and on the possibilities for converting one form of capital into
another.
Bourdieu’s distinction among the three states of cultural capital further clari-
fies the mechanisms through which heritage enterprises reproduce advantage over
time. Embodied cultural capital consists of knowledge, skills, and dispositions
internalized through practice, such as artistic connoisseurship and craft expertise.
Objectified cultural capital exists in material forms, including artworks, rare books,
archives, and cultural artifacts. Institutionalized cultural capital refers to official rec-
ognition conferred by authoritative organizations, certifications, and formal status
(Bourdieu 1986). Rongbaozhai’s historical trajectory illustrates the interaction of
these states. Artistic expertise and aesthetic judgment were embodied in its crafts-
men and experts; high-quality artworks, publications, and collections constituted
objectified cultural capital; and recognition from state and cultural authorities pro-
vided institutionalized legitimacy. The enterprise’s durability depended on its abil-
ity to reproduce and convert these forms of cultural capital as the surrounding field
evolved. Symbolic capital is especially important for understanding heritage brands.
Rather than constituting a separate category of capital, symbolic capital emerges
when economic, cultural, social, or political resources are collectively recognized as
legitimate within a field (Bourdieu 1986). In Rongbaozhai’s case, symbolic capital
derives from its reputation as an authentic custodian of Chinese literati culture. The
prestige accumulated through centuries of cultural activity enables the organization
to command trust, authority, and recognition beyond the material value of its prod-
ucts. From this perspective, the symbolism dimension of brand authenticity reflects
the symbolic power generated when cultural capital acquires broader civilizational
significance.
Building on this foundation, the study reconceptualizes the four dimensions of
brand authenticity identified by Morhart et al. (2015), namely continuity, credibility,
integrity, and symbolism, as structural conditions rather than consumer perceptions.
Continuity refers to the preservation and transmission of cultural capital across time.
Credibility concerns the recognition of expertise and authenticity by relevant actors
within a field. Integrity reflects the moral legitimacy necessary to sustain trust and
social acceptance. Symbolism denotes the broader cultural meanings through which
accumulated capital acquires symbolic authority. Together, these dimensions shape
whether cultural capital can be reproduced institutionally and converted into eco-
nomic value under particular field conditions.
While Bourdieu’s framework provides a powerful explanation of capital accumu-
lation and conversion, it offers less guidance for understanding the distinctive rela-
tionship between state power, social networks, and historical change in China. To
address this limitation, the analysis incorporates Zhang Jijiao’s (2016) dual-structure
model, which distinguishes between the state-centered logic of the umbrella society
(sanshi shehui, 伞式社会) and the network-based relational logic of the honeycomb
society (fengwo shi shehui, 蜂窝式社会). The model highlights the coexistence of
hierarchical political authority and interpersonal relational networks within Chinese
social organization. It therefore helps explain how cultural enterprises navigate shift-
ing configurations of state intervention, market competition, and social legitimacy.

approach provides a more nuanced explanation of how heritage enterprises survive

1.3.1  Bourdieu’s field theory and the concepts of habitus and legitimacy
Bourdieu’s field theory provides a relational framework for understanding how
forms of capital acquire value and legitimacy within specific social contexts. A field
is a structured system of objective relations in which actors compete over valued
resources according to field-specific rules and criteria. Participation is sustained
by illusio, the shared belief that the stakes of the field are meaningful and worth pur-
suing, while actors’ strategies are shaped by habitus, the durable dispositions formed
through socialization and historical experience. Within a field, dominant actors exer-
cise symbolic power by defining which forms of capital are recognized as valuable
and which practices are regarded as legitimate (Bourdieu 1986). The effectiveness of
capital, therefore, depends not only on possession but also on its recognition within
the field.
Applied to China Time-honored Brands, this perspective treats heritage not as
a static asset but as a resource whose value depends on historically specific struc-
tures of recognition. The relevant field is organized around a cultural  illusio derived
from the Confucian merchant tradition (rushang chuantong, 儒商传统), which
links economic activity to literati values, moral cultivation, and social responsibil-
ity (Bourdieu 1992). Within this framework, commercial success gains legitimacy
when it is aligned with broader cultural and ethical principles. China Time-honored
Brands, therefore, derive value not solely from market performance but from their
capacity to embody a culturally recognized synthesis of commercial competence,
cultural authority, and moral responsibility.
The mechanism through which this legitimacy is reproduced is habitus. Through
apprenticeship systems, family transmission, professional training, and participa-
tion in established communities of practice, China Time-honored Brands transmit
specialized knowledge, aesthetic judgment, artisanal skills, and ethical norms across
generations. These embodied dispositions constitute a form of cultural capital that
cannot be reduced to formal rules or technical expertise alone. Equally important,
they internalize the ethical principle of  yi yi qu li, or securing profit through right-
eousness, which historically defined the moral boundaries of legitimate commercial
behavior. This inherited habitus enables actors to reproduce not only technical com-
petence but also the cultural values that make such competence socially meaningful
and trustworthy.
Viewed from this perspective, the dimensions of brand authenticity can be inter -
preted as institutional expressions of field dynamics rather than merely as consumer
perceptions. Continuity reflects the successful intergenerational transmission of

embodied cultural capital through habitus, ensuring that valued knowledge, prac-
tices, and traditions remain recognizable across time. Integrity derives from the
moral structure of the field and establishes the normative boundaries within which
economic activity is considered legitimate. It therefore represents more than ethi-
cal conduct in a general sense; it embodies the historically embedded principle
that commercial gain must remain compatible with cultural and moral obligations.
Together, continuity and integrity sustain the internal conditions necessary for the
long-term reproduction of cultural capital.
By contrast, credibility and symbolism concern external processes of recogni-
tion. Credibility depends on whether cultural capital is acknowledged as authentic
and authoritative by relevant actors, including customers, cultural experts, industry
peers, and state institutions. Symbolism emerges when accumulated cultural capital
acquires wider social recognition and becomes associated with collective identities,
historical memory, or civilizational values. In Bourdieu’s terms, symbolism repre-
sents the transformation of accumulated capital into symbolic capital through pro-
cesses of social recognition. Whether a time-honored brand achieves credibility and
symbolism, therefore, depends not only on its own practices but also on prevailing
structures of legitimacy within the field and on the judgments of actors capable of
conferring recognition.
This perspective highlights a central proposition of the present study: the four
dimensions of brand authenticity are not simply attributes of a brand but field-the-
oretic conditions governing the reproduction and convertibility of cultural capital.
Continuity secures the transmission of embodied cultural resources, integrity pro-
vides moral legitimacy, credibility establishes recognized authority, and symbolism
transforms accumulated capital into socially acknowledged prestige. Together, these
dimensions mediate the relationship between cultural capital and economic value.
When they are reinforced by the surrounding field, cultural capital can be success-
fully reproduced and converted across historical transitions. When they weaken,
particularly during movement across different institutional and cultural fields, the
convertibility of cultural capital becomes constrained, creating the conditions under
which heritage organizations may struggle to sustain legitimacy and economic
viability.
1.3.2  Zhang’s dual‑structure model
Bourdieu’s field theory was originally developed in the specific socio-historical con-
text of modern Western societies, where fields are presumed to enjoy relative auton-
omy. Its application to a transitional society such as China, which Li Peilin (李培林)
characterizes as still moving from tradition to modernity, therefore calls for explicit
justification. To this end, we supplement Bourdieu’s framework with Zhang Jijiao’s
dual-structure model, which addresses the dimension of state–society relations that
the original formulation tends to underplay. The case of Rongbaozhai, spanning pre-
modern, transitional, and fully modernized (Hong Kong) settings, offers a valuable
test of this integration. By clarifying the origin and scope of Bourdieu’s theory and
how the dual-structure model complements it, we strengthen the framework’s appli-
cability to the Chinese context without altering its core logic.

The reproduction of cultural capital in China must be understood within the
broader context of state–society relations. Zhang Jijiao’s dual-structure model
(Fig.  1) distinguishes between the  umbrella society and the  honeycomb soci-
ety, two interdependent modes of social organization that shape how legitimacy,
authority, and resources are acquired (Zhang 2014, 2016). The umbrella society
is characterized by hierarchical authority, administrative coordination, and state
regulation, making political capital a crucial source of institutional legitimacy
and resource access. By contrast, the honeycomb society is sustained by decen-
tralized networks of trust, reciprocity, reputation, and shared cultural norms, in
which social and cultural capital function as the primary mechanisms of coordi-
nation. Rather than operating as separate spheres, these two structures interact to
form a hybrid institutional environment in which organizations must simultane-
ously satisfy the demands of state authority and socially embedded communities.

This dual structure provides the institutional context within which the four

cultural capital through artisanal lineages, professional communities, and cultural
networks embedded in the honeycomb society, while also benefiting from institu-
tional preservation and policy support provided by the umbrella society. Credibil-
ity emerges through recognition from both structures: market actors, collectors, and
cultural communities evaluate the authenticity of an enterprise through repeated
exchanges, while state institutions certify and reinforce its legitimacy through offi-
cial recognition.  Integrity, rooted in the civilizational ethic of  yi yi qu li ( 以义取

Rongbaozhai’s historical development illustrates this dual dynamic. Its designa-
tion as a major cultural institution and alignment with state cultural policies gen-
erated political and symbolic capital within the umbrella society, while its long-
standing relationships with artists, collectors, scholars, and literati communities
embedded it within the honeycomb society through networks of cultural and social
capital. Its longevity rested on the interaction of these two structures and the sus-
tained operation of the four dimensions of authenticity. Zhang’s model therefore
complements Bourdieu’s field theory by showing that cultural capital becomes
convertible through the alignment of state endorsement, societal recognition, and
field-specific legitimacy. This alignment supports the institutional reproduction of
cultural capital and its transformation into economic and symbolic value. The Hong
Kong case demonstrates the consequences of its breakdown: as legitimacy became
detached from the foundations that had sustained Rongbaozhai on the Chinese main-
land, the effectiveness of continuity, credibility, integrity, and symbolism dimin-
ished, limiting the organization’s capacity to reproduce and convert cultural capital
across fields.
1.3.3  Reconceptualizing brand authenticity as structural conditions
To explain how cultural capital is reproduced, legitimized, and converted into eco-
nomic value across changing fields, this study draws on the four dimensions of
brand authenticity identified by Morhart et al. (2015) and Bruhn et al. (2012). While
the branding literature generally treats continuity, credibility, integrity, and symbol-
ism as consumer perceptions that shape brand evaluations, this study treats them as
structural conditions governing the institutionalized reproduction of cultural capital.
Viewed through Bourdieu’s field theory, these dimensions are not merely attributes
observed by consumers; they are mechanisms that determine whether cultural capi-
tal can retain legitimacy and remain convertible across different social, political, and
economic environments.
Credibility refers to the extent to which an enterprise’s practices consistently
align with its claimed identity, thereby generating trust among stakeholders and

International Journal of Anthropology and Ethnology           (2026) 10:16  Page 11 of 35
establishing its legitimacy in commercial exchange. As a structural condition,
credibility enables cultural capital to be recognized as authentic and worthy of
recognition within a given field.
Integrity, by contrast, concerns the moral foundations of legitimacy. In the
context of China Time-honored Brands, it cannot be reduced to conventional
notions of reliability or promise-keeping. Rather, it is rooted in the Confucian
principle of “righteousness above profit” (yi yi wei shang, 以义为上), expressed
in the commercial ethic of “securing profit through righteousness” (yi yi qu li, 以
义取利). This tradition holds that economic gain is legitimate only when pursued
in accordance with broader moral obligations and social responsibilities. Histori-
cally, yi ( 义, righteousness) encompassed fair dealing with consumers, responsi-
ble treatment of employees, integrity in relations with business partners, contri-
butions to local communities, and commitment to the wider interests of society
and the nation. In contemporary terms, these obligations correspond closely to
the core dimensions of corporate social responsibility.
Integrity, therefore, functions as more than a moral ideal. It constitutes a nor -
mative structure that constrains organizational behavior and defines the condi-
tions under which commercial activity is regarded as legitimate. For heritage
enterprises such as Rongbaozhai, adherence to righteousness was not a moral cost
but a source of institutional resilience. Embedding commercial practice within a
recognized ethical framework enabled the organization’s cultural capital to retain
legitimacy, resist erosion, and remain convertible across changing historical
fields. The Confucian ideal of the unity of righteousness and benefit (yili tongyi,
义利统一) and the Mohist principle of universal love and mutual benefit (jian
tianxia, jiao xiang li, 兼天下, 交相利) together provide the intellectual founda-
tion of this dimension.
Symbolism  refers to the capacity of a brand to embody meanings that extend
beyond individual consumption and become associated with collective identities,
cultural memory, and civilizational values. Through symbolic recognition, cultural
capital acquires broader legitimacy and is transformed into symbolic capital. In this
reconceptualization, consumer perceptions do not define authenticity; rather, they
serve as empirical indicators of whether these structural conditions are functioning
effectively.
This theoretical move enables a productive dialogue between the four-dimen-
sional framework and China’s official accreditation criteria for the China Time-
honored Brand designation. The criteria require that a brand (a) have been estab-
lished for more than 50 years, (b) maintain distinctive craftsmanship or skills, (c)
possess a foundation in traditional Chinese culture, and (d) enjoy a favorable market
reputation (Ministry of Commerce 2006). Each criterion maps onto one of the four
dimensions: historical duration corresponds to continuity; a foundation in traditional
Chinese culture corresponds to integrity (fidelity to a civilizational value system
rather than mere organizational consistency); distinctive craftsmanship corresponds
to symbolism (material embodiment of a cultural tradition through craft skills); and

market reputation corresponds to credibility (transactional trustworthiness). Two
additional requirements under the Chinese framework—formal trademark registra-
tion and sustained economic performance—fall outside the four-dimensional scope
and are treated in this study as legal and market preconditions rather than authentic-
ity conditions per se.
1.3.4  The integrity dimension: the philosophical foundations of “securing profit
through righteousness”
The preceding analysis suggests that brand authenticity is best understood as a field-
dependent mechanism whose meaning and operation vary across institutional and
cultural contexts. In the tradition of China Time-honored Brands, authenticity does
more than establish market credibility; it facilitates the reproduction and conversion
of cultural capital within a civilizational framework.
Most notably, the Confucian–Mohist principle of  yi yi qu li, or securing profit
through righteousness, offers a distinctive interpretation of  integrity. Whereas
Western studies typically define integrity as consistency between brand promises
and organizational behavior, China Time-honored Brands derive legitimacy from
adherence to a broader moral order rooted in traditional Chinese culture. The  yi (义,
righteousness) underpinning commercial activity is therefore not simply an organi-
zational value but a civilizational principle that governs the relationship between
ethics and profit. Under this logic, economic gain becomes legitimate only when
achieved through culturally recognized standards of conduct. Rongbaozhai’s dona-
tion of a Mi Fu handscroll (Fig.  2) to the Palace Museum exemplifies this principle:
the act represented the enactment of values that sustain long-term symbolic and eco-
nomic returns. Integrity thus serves as a structural condition through which cultural
capital can be converted into economic value while retaining its moral legitimacy.
Symbolism likewise acquires a broader meaning than the lifestyle or subcultural
associations commonly discussed in Western branding literature. For Rongbaozhai,

1.3.5  Operationalizing the framework
Having established brand authenticity as a set of structural conditions governing the
preservation and transformation of cultural capital, we operationalize this frame-
work  through three analytical steps. First, each stage of Rongbaozhai’s develop-
ment is examined as a distinct field configuration in which different forms of cap-
ital acquire varying value and legitimacy. The analysis considers how continuity,
credibility, integrity, and symbolism shape the capacity of cultural capital to endure
under changing political, economic, and cultural conditions. Second, the interaction
between the umbrella society and the honeycomb society in Zhang’s model is traced
across historical periods to show how shifting state–society relations influence
the mechanisms through which cultural capital generates economic and symbolic
returns. Third, Rongbaozhai’s expansion into Hong Kong is treated as a boundary-
crossing case that tests the limits of these mechanisms in a field whose institutional
foundations differ substantially from those of Beijing.
The central mechanism examined throughout is  yi yi qu li ( 以义取利), or secur-
ing profit through righteousness. Within the framework, this principle operates at
multiple levels: it is embodied in professional habitus through apprenticeship, con-
noisseurship, and the transmission of artisanal knowledge; institutionalized through
credibility and integrity, which sustain trust and moral legitimacy; and expressed
symbolically when such practices are recognized as culturally representative and
prestigious. Rongbaozhai’s trajectory suggests that cultural capital remains effective
only when these dimensions are jointly maintained and recognized by both state and
societal actors. The Beijing–Hong Kong comparison therefore serves as an analyti-
cal test of how variations in field structure affect the capacity of cultural capital to
generate enduring economic and symbolic value.
The framework thus provides a localized refinement of existing research on
legitimacy and cultural reproduction by integrating Bourdieu’s concepts of capital
and fields with Zhang’s dual-structure model. By treating authenticity as a set of

approach to understanding how cultural legitimacy is maintained and translated into

2  Methodology
This study adopts a qualitative case study approach to investigate the structural
tensions arising from Rongbaozhai’s historical development, with the Hong Kong
branch as a case of transregional expansion. A case study methodology is particu-
larly suited to examining complex social phenomena within their real-world con-
text, especially when the boundaries between the phenomenon and its context are
not clearly defined (Yin 2018). The Rongbaozhai Hong Kong case was selected
purposively because it enables an in-depth exploration of the collision between
heritage-based symbolic capital and the commercial logic of a globalized art mar -
ket. This approach facilitates a fine-grained analysis of how organizational habi-
tus and capital conversion operate, and sometimes fail, when transposed across
distinct cultural and economic fields.
Data collection drew on multiple documentary sources as part of a triangu-
lation strategy intended to enhance analytical rigor and credibility (Flick 2018).
The materials included institutional histories, memoirs, archival documents,
media reports, exhibition catalogs, and official publications. A key source was  A
century of memories of Rongbaozhai by Zheng (2019), which provides a detailed
account of the organization’s governance, leadership transitions, and strategic
development from the late Qing period to the present. These primary materials
were supplemented by secondary literature on Bourdieusian field theory, China
Time-honored Brands, and the Hong Kong art market (e.g., Bourdieu 1993;
Zhang 2019; Poposki and Leung 2022), providing the theoretical and contextual
foundation for the analysis.
Data analysis followed a theory-driven thematic approach. Drawing on the
framework developed in Sect.  1.3, the four dimensions of brand authenticity,
namely continuity, credibility, integrity, and symbolism, were operationalized as
structural conditions through which cultural capital is preserved, validated, and
transformed (Morhart et al. 2015). These dimensions were analyzed in conjunc-
tion with Bourdieu’s concepts of habitus, field, and capital conversion and Zhang
Jijiao’s dual-structure model of umbrella society and honeycomb society. Histori-
cal and contemporary materials were systematically coded to identify patterns of
institutional adaptation, continuity, and tension.
The analysis proceeded in three stages. First, Rongbaozhai’s historical trajec-
tory was reconstructed to examine the formation and evolution of organizational
habitus across successive political and economic regimes. Second, key episodes
of adaptation, including the establishment and development of the Hong Kong
branch, were analyzed to assess how the four authenticity dimensions shaped the
organization’s capacity to maintain and transform cultural capital under chang-
ing field conditions. Third, a comparative perspective was employed by relating
Rongbaozhai’s experience to strategies observed among other Chinese heritage

International Journal of Anthropology and Ethnology           (2026) 10:16  Page 15 of 35
brands, international luxury firms, and leading cultural institutions. Rather than
constituting a formal comparative case study, this exercise served as an analytical
heuristic for understanding alternative responses to similar structural pressures.
By situating Rongbaozhai within a broader spectrum of organizational models,
the analysis highlights how its challenges emerged not from heritage itself but
from the difficulty of aligning inherited institutional logics with changing field
conditions.

3.1  The unbroken seal: how Rongbaozhai navigated three centuries of Chinese

Rongbaozhai’s institutional longevity in Beijing was built on a continuous accu-

the studio operated initially as a private stationery and calligraphy shop. How -

the court. This marked the start of a long-term dependency on what Zhang (2019)
terms an “umbrella-style” (伞式) patron-client relationship, in which the state pro-
vided resource allocation and institutional protection in exchange for loyalty and ser-
vice. The political capital thus accumulated formed the bedrock of Rongbaozhai’s
subsequent symbolic capital.

3.2  Rongbaozhai at the strategic crossroads of reform and opening up
The policy of reform and opening up launched in 1978 fundamentally restructured
China’s cultural field. As the transition to a market-oriented economy expanded
commercial competition, heritage institutions such as Rongbaozhai were required to
adapt to an environment in which cultural and economic value became increasingly
intertwined. Long sustained by symbolic capital rooted in literati traditions and state
support, Rongbaozhai faced the challenge of maintaining cultural legitimacy while
generating sustainable economic returns. Cultural organizations were therefore
compelled to negotiate the competing demands of heritage preservation and mar -
ket participation within a landscape increasingly shaped by global economic forces
(Westad and Chen 2024).
This transformation extended far beyond surface-level operational tweaks. It rep-
resented a broader reconfiguration of the cultural field, as traditional hierarchies
were reshaped by consumer markets, international exchange, and new forms of cul-
tural production. For Rongbaozhai, survival depended on converting accumulated
prestige into economic value without undermining the legitimacy from which that
prestige derived. This balancing act required the development of hybrid operational

homogenization, media logic privileged spectacle and digital engagement, and psy -
chological drives fueled aspirational desire for globally recognized brands. By cling-
ing to literati aesthetics and traditional forms of cultural authority, Rongbaozhai
struggled to translate its heritage into forms of value recognized by contemporary
consumers. Its limited engagement with auctions, digital platforms, international
partnerships, and innovative branding constrained its ability to expand beyond its
established cultural niche.
As Cai (2023) and Du et al. (2025) argue, organizational sustainability depends
on the capacity to convert and integrate multiple forms of capital. This constraint
suggests that heritage can become a source of disadvantage when it is not accompa-
nied by strategies that adapt cultural value to changing market conditions. Although
Rongbaozhai possessed considerable historical prestige, it lacked the organizational
flexibility and branding capabilities needed to compete in an art market increasingly
shaped by auctions, digital engagement, international networks, and experiential
consumption. Its commitment to cultural purity and traditional modes of operation
limited its ability to generate sustainable revenue and respond effectively to new
forms of market competition (Harris 2017).
The case highlights the contingent nature of authenticity and cultural legitimacy.
Rather than functioning as fixed assets whose value is preserved automatically over
time, both require continual renewal through the effective mobilization of cultural,
symbolic, social, and economic capital within changing institutional and market
environments. Long-term viability, therefore, depends not only on heritage preserva-
tion but also on organizational adaptability, transparency, and governance, challeng-
ing the assumption that historical prestige alone can sustain competitive advantage.
This argument also reveals the dark side of heritage branding: cultural legitimacy
can obscure institutional weaknesses when it is not supported by effective oversight
and strategic adaptation. As Du et  al. (2025) demonstrate, China Time-honored
Brands often exhibit higher earnings opacity because reputational incentives may
encourage the concealment of poor performance. Heritage branding thus remains a
valuable resource only when accompanied by governance mechanisms and adaptive
strategies that sustain both organizational resilience and public credibility.
To remain relevant, heritage organizations must adopt hybrid strategies that inte-
grate interpretive innovation, technological engagement, and systemic regulation to
reconcile economic imperatives with cultural values (Du et al. 2025). Evidence from
successful cases reinforces this principle. Chinese heritage brands such as Daoxi-
angcun and Wangzhihe exemplify a state-led model that combines cultural authen-
ticity with modernization through product diversification and digital marketing
under strong regulatory oversight. In contrast, global heritage brands like Hermès
and LEGO rely on market-driven approaches that emphasize e-commerce, experi-
ential retail, and collaborative innovation to sustain symbolic capital in competitive
environments. Art institutions such as the Louvre and Tate Modern illustrate a third
pathway, employing technological innovations including VR tours and adaptive
reuse of spaces within mixed governance structures that blend public funding and

Rongbaozhai’s experience in Hong Kong reveals a central paradox of heritage

excessive commodification can undermine the cultural values that confer legitimacy.
As Bourdieu (1992) suggests, symbolic capital requires continual reproduction
within changing fields, while Niu (2017) argues that cultural sustainability depends
on governance structures capable of balancing economic efficiency with social and
cultural objectives. Evidence from China Time-honored Brands further indicates
that effective oversight enhances transparency and organizational credibility (Du
et al. 2025). Rongbaozhai’s trajectory, therefore, suggests that heritage sustainability
depends on the combined capacity to reinterpret authenticity, engage new audiences
through technological and organizational innovation, and maintain governance
mechanisms that protect cultural integrity. The relative absence of these supporting
conditions in Hong Kong’s laissez-faire art market exposed the organization’s sym-
bolic capital to speculative pressures without adequate institutional mediation, dem-
onstrating that historical prestige alone is insufficient to sustain long-term viability
in changing cultural and commercial environments.
3.3  Institutional mediation and cultural sustainability
Hong Kong’s contemporary art field has evolved into a complex cultural ecosys-
tem where institutional mediation determines visibility and sustainability. This envi-
ronment is structured by competing forms of capital, including economic, cultural,
social, and symbolic capital, which shape authority over what is considered authen-
tic, innovative, or canonical (Bourdieu 1993; Buchholz 2022). Cultural sustainabil-
ity depends on institutions’ ability to negotiate between heritage and innovation,
ensuring traditional forms remain relevant amid globalized discourses and shifting

and transnational networks, was not the outcome of a purely artistic evolution but

Hong Kong art field. The “pluralism” exhibited by Hong Kong’s contemporary art

of the colonial cultural order. It is precisely this historically constituted field struc-

tradition and state endorsement—would struggle to secure the kind of institutional
confirmation it had enjoyed in Beijing.

accumulated through historical continuity had to compete with alternative forms of
cultural authority generated through cosmopolitanism, curatorial innovation, and

This was an exploration nearly thirty years earlier than the abstract ink experiments
of the mainland’s ‘85 New Wave’ (Li 2018; Art Education Research 2018). Rather
than treating tradition as a fixed inheritance, the movement transformed ink into
a medium of cultural negotiation between Chinese civilization and global moder -

which repositioned contemporary city life as a legitimate subject of Chinese artistic
expression. Within this framework, authenticity derived not from the preservation of
historical forms alone but from the creative reinterpretation of cultural traditions in
response to changing social realities.

of the umbrella society. In Hong Kong, however, legitimacy was increasingly
shaped by market performance, brand visibility, global recognition, and investment
value. The Confucian merchant tradition therefore ceased to operate as an imme-

exchange, and East–West cultural interaction. Here, value was less often derived
from the preservation of cultural orthodoxy than from the ability to reinterpret Chi-

facing Rongbaozhai was not simply whether its heritage aligned with consumer
expectations, but whether its underlying cultural assumptions retained authority
within a field governed by different principles of recognition.

Chinese cultural traditions were continually reinterpreted through cross-cultural
exchange rather than simply preserved. Wong Chiu-tong’s Ode to the City exhibition

traditional ink techniques to the representation of contemporary urban experience,
Wong demonstrated that cultural authenticity can be regenerated through creative
reinterpretation rather than sustained solely through continuity with the past. In this

From this perspective, Rongbaozhai’s prolonged posture of observation reflected
more than strategic inertia. It revealed an inability to reposition its symbolic capital
within a field where legitimacy increasingly depended on cultural translation rather
than historical inheritance alone. By failing to engage fully with the forms of hybrid-

Kong’s artistic development since the modern ink movement, the institution missed
an opportunity to transform its heritage into a culturally resonant resource. As a
result, its symbolic capital gradually lost relevance within a field whose dominant
actors were actively redefining the relationship between Chinese tradition and global
modernity. Just as brand legitimacy mediates consumer acceptance in commercial
markets, symbolic legitimacy mediates institutional survival in cultural fields. In
Hong Kong, that legitimacy depended not on preserving tradition unchanged but on
demonstrating the capacity to participate in the ongoing struggle to define the cul-
tural meaning of Chinese modernity itself.
This challenge extends beyond marketing and reflects a broader relationship
between cultural heritage and institutional governance. Saci et  al. (2021) show
that China Time-honored Brand firms exhibit lower levels of earnings manage-
ment because deeply embedded cultural norms can function as informal governance
mechanisms, particularly when reinforced by internal promotion, state ownership,
and executive shareholding. Their findings suggest that cultural legitimacy remains
effective only when supported by institutional structures. For heritage organizations,
symbolic prestige alone is insufficient in fields shaped by global competition and
speculative market logics. Sustainability, therefore, depends on governance arrange-
ments that combine transparency, accountability, interpretive legitimacy, and adap-
tive capacity through regulatory oversight, strategic alliances, technological engage-
ment, and cross-sector collaboration (Du et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2024; Niu 2017).
Rongbaozhai’s re-entry into Hong Kong highlights the limitations of symbolic
capital when transferred across fields governed by different regimes of legitimacy.
The four dimensions of authenticity that historically enabled the conversion of
cultural capital in Beijing were significantly weakened in Hong Kong.  Continu-
ity  lacked institutional mechanisms for reproducing traditional authority;  credibil-
ity depended on market intermediaries and international networks rather than state
recognition; integrity, rooted in the Confucian principle of  yi yi qu li, was deprived
of the normative foundations provided by the umbrella society; and  symbolism,
detached from state-sanctioned cultural authority, could no longer secure recogni-
tion on its own. Consequently, legitimacy in Hong Kong derived less from historical
lineage and literati prestige than from international visibility, curatorial innovation,
and participation in global cultural networks.
This transformation reflects the broader shift from industrial capitalism to cul-
tural capitalism identified by Wang and Hao (2025) through their engagement with
Reckwitz’s concept of the “singularity society.” In this context, value is generated
through uniqueness, symbolic distinction, and market visibility rather than inher -
ited status. Yet such forms of distinction remain embedded within commercial
and speculative logics, privileging global circulation and cultural innovation over

did not gradually lose relevance; rather, it entered a field whose principles of evalua-

The challenge facing Rongbaozhai was thus one of institutional translation rather
than organizational modernization alone. Although digital transformation, curato-

when it can be translated into forms of cultural, symbolic, and economic capital that
are recognized within the prevailing field structure (Reckwitz 2020; Wang and Hao

on the preservation of historical prestige alone, but on their capacity to continually
renegotiate the meaning and relevance of tradition within changing cultural fields,
thereby remaining active participants in the production of cultural value rather than
passive witnesses to their own obsolescence.
4  Conclusion
The case of Rongbaozhai demonstrates that the sustainability of cultural enterprises
depends not simply on the accumulation of cultural and symbolic capital, but on the
extent to which such capital can be recognized and converted within a particular
field. While Rongbaozhai accrued substantial authority through its artisanal exper -
tise, literati heritage, and state-supported cultural status on the Chinese mainland,
its experience in Hong Kong reveals the limits of capital mobility across fields gov -
erned by different regimes of legitimacy. Its difficulties were not primarily the result
of managerial shortcomings or strategic misjudgments. Rather, they stemmed from
a structural incompatibility between the value system underpinning the organiza-
tion’s authenticity and the principles of recognition operating within Hong Kong’s
art field. This incompatibility was itself historically constituted. Shaped by colo-
nial governance and long-standing transnational exchange, Hong Kong developed
a distinct cultural environment in which standards of value and legitimacy became
increasingly detached from the civilizational framework of traditional Chinese cul-
ture and oriented instead toward market rationality, cosmopolitanism, and global
cultural circulation.
At the center of this incompatibility was the integrity dimension of authentic-
ity. On the Chinese mainland, Rongbaozhai’s legitimacy was rooted in the Confu-
cian principle of  yi yi qu li, embedded within a broader civilizational framework
reinforced by state recognition and the institutional logic of the umbrella society.
Under these conditions, cultural authority, symbolic prestige, and economic activity
were mutually reinforcing. In Hong Kong, by contrast, legitimacy was increasingly
determined by market visibility, curatorial innovation, international circulation, and

transnational networks. Consequently, the moral and cultural foundations that ena-
bled Rongbaozhai to convert cultural capital into symbolic and economic value on
the Chinese mainland no longer functioned as recognized sources of authority.
This finding reinforces Bourdieu’s central proposition that capital derives its
value not from any intrinsic quality but from the field in which it operates (Bourdieu
1992; Grenfell 2008). At the same time, the study extends this insight by demon-
strating that capital may, under certain conditions, become structurally non-converti-
ble. Rongbaozhai entered Hong Kong with substantial reserves of symbolic and cul-
tural capital, yet these resources could not easily be transformed into forms of value
recognized by local mechanisms of legitimacy. Capital conversion is therefore not
merely a question of organizational capability; it is conditioned by the institutional
structures that define what is valuable within a given field.
The study also contributes to the development of Bourdieusian theory by high-
lighting the significance of political capital in shaping cultural fields. Rongbaozhai’s
historical resilience on the Chinese mainland was closely tied to state-supported sys-
tems of cultural recognition, suggesting that political capital was embedded within,
rather than external to, the cultural field. Its diminished effectiveness in Hong Kong
reveals the limits of relying on cultural and symbolic capital when the political foun-
dations that support their legitimacy cannot travel across fields. Capital conversion,
in other words, is contingent upon institutional arrangements and cannot be under -
stood independently of the structures that sustain it.
Similarly, the analysis advances Zhang Jijiao’s dual-structure theory (Zhang
2016, 2019) by demonstrating how Rongbaozhai’s legitimacy historically
depended on the interaction between the umbrella society and the honeycomb
society. On the Chinese mainland, state coordination and artisanal networks oper -
ated as complementary sources of authority, enabling the reproduction and con-
version of cultural capital. In Hong Kong, however, this institutional configura-
tion could not be reproduced. Colonial rule had reshaped the territory’s legal,
educational, and cultural foundations, displacing the state-centered mechanisms
that supported the umbrella society and producing a field in which legitimacy
derived primarily from market recognition, international visibility, and trans-
national networks. Consequently, the organizational logic through which Rong-
baozhai accumulated and converted capital on the Chinese mainland could not
be effectively transferred across the border, illustrating how historically specific
institutional conditions determine the convertibility of capital across fields.
More broadly, the case suggests that the key challenge facing heritage insti-
tutions is not simply balancing tradition and innovation but sustaining the con-
ditions under which cultural resources can acquire legitimacy in changing envi-
ronments. Hong Kong’s modern ink movement, from Lui Shou-kwan to Wong
Chiu-tong, illustrates that cultural continuity was achieved not through the pres-
ervation of tradition in its inherited form but through its reinterpretation within
new aesthetic and institutional contexts. The central issue is therefore one of
institutional translation: the capacity to transform inherited cultural resources
into forms that remain meaningful and recognizable within a different field.
