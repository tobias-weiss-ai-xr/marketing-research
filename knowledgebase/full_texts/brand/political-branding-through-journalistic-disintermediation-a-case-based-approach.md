Research in political marketing has demonstrated the importance of journalistic
intermediation in building political image and identity. Journalists have long
been an integral part of political marketing by virtue of being a bridge
between political leaders and their voters. However, political leaders across
the globe have leveraged digital tools to reach out to their voters directly. This
process, called disintermediation, has been critical in building political brand
of Prime Minister Narendra Modi in India and has helped him win three
election cycles between 2014 and 2024. He enjoyed tremendous public
support across three elections, with no notable interaction with journalists.
This study, using the single-case research method, investigates how Mr. Modi
built his brand by reaching out to his voters directly, analysing
disintermediation-related information obtained from international and Indian
English media for about 12 years between 2012 and 2024. The study employs
thematic analysis, integrating political–theoretical frameworks to explain
political brand identity, message personalization, brand authenticity, and
disintermediation, among others. The study proposes an integrated framework
explaining how sustained public messaging, symbolic rhetoric, and direct
digital and mass gathering formats substituted for interview-based media
engagement. The major contribution of the study is to bring communications
sovereignty to the fore, rather than cooperation with traditional media and
journalism, which contradicts the theory of mediatization.

1 Introduction
Although the academic research in political marketing is a relatively new
phenomenon, it has been richly contributed to by many scholars (Mauser, 1983;
Newman and Sheth, 1985, 1987; Farrell and Wortmann, 1987; O’Shaughnessy, 1990)
and many others who have analysed and presented various facets of political
marketing. The use of political communication as a tool to create and nurture
perceptions, images, and narratives has been presented in detail (Harrop, 1990;
Worcester and Mortimore, 2005). Personal branding in political marketing has also
been studied (Needham, 2005; Peng and Hackley, 2009). Many studies have focused on

For many scholars, the role of the media and journalists in
political communication goes far beyond providing information
and enabling debate. Gurevitch and Blumler (1990) elaborated
on eight roles that the media plays in a democratic society,
whereas Curran (2000) provided a more intensive role of the
media in the democratic process as a watchdog, public
representative (the fourth estate), and source of public
information. Some other scholars detailed a more inclusive role
in improving the quality of the electoral system, political parties,
parliament, judiciary, and other branches of the state, even civil
society, and safeguarding their democratic performance
(McQuail, 2000; Street, 2010).
Given the importance of the media in society, it is only to be
expected that political actors use the media for their public
relations and to frame their personal image (McNair, 1996).
Numerous other works suggest the importance of public
relations in exploring, defining, and communicating image
(Grunig, 1992; Haberman and Dolphin, 1988). Furthermore,
Pich et al. (2019) stated, “While extant research has tended to
focus on identity or image, there are calls to take a more holistic
perspective to ground future studies.” Farhan and Omar (2021)
commented that the effect of political brand relationship
investment on voter citizenship behaviour has not been
examined enough. Perloff (2023) noted that, while the political
marketing field is rich with insightful models, conceptual maps,
and frameworks drawing from strategic marketing, it lacks
hypotheses “a priori” about marketing effects, contingent
conditions, and underlying processes. This paper attempts to
fulfil these gaps, especially the effects of direct marketing.
Not many have studied direct marketing in political
marketing. This paper explores how the Indian Prime Minister
has pursued a rather unconventional branding strategy in his
political career. He has been the most popular leader since he
assumed office in 2014. From 2014 to 2024, his approval ratings
have exceeded 75% every year, except for 2017 (63%), 2019
(64%), and 2021 (74% in January and 71% in September).1 In
2023, he was the most popular leader in the world, with an
approval rating of 76%.2 His personal popularity is twice that of
his party, and “he is by far the world’s most popular elected
leader.”3 This study contributes to scholarship on populist
communication styles (Engesser et al., 2017; Hameleers, 2018)
by demonstrating that direct-to-citizen messaging functions
simultaneously as brand storytelling and as a populist strategy of
bypassing “elite” institutional intermediaries. This convergence
challenges the traditional distinction between political marketing
and populist performance, indicating that contemporary political
communication increasingly integrates marketing strategies with
performative populist practices.
The novelty of this study extends beyond its empirical focus to
the theoretical insights it generates. The Modi case challenges the
conventional assumption in the mediatization literature that
successful political brand-building depends on media
cooperation or, at a minimum, media tolerance. Instead, the
findings demonstrate that strong political brand equity (Keller,
1993) can be achieved through strategic communicative
sovereignty. It involves the deliberate creation of direct,
repeated, and emotionally resonant communication channels
that circumvent traditional journalistic mediation. This concept
extends Needham’s (2005) citizen-as-consumer model and
connects with recent scholarship on political disintermediation
(Enli, 2017) and the “personal vote” in digital environments
(Larsson and Kalsnes, 2014). Future research should test these
propositions comparatively across political systems with varying
levels of press freedom and digital penetration.
The Prime Minister has garnered popularity by avoiding
interactions with journalists, an unprecedented practice in any
democratic society. This phenomenon needs to be studied to
understand how the Prime Minister has achieved this rare
accomplishment. This study aims to explore the ways and means
employed by Prime Minister Modi, by answering the following
research questions:
RQ1: How has Mr. Modi bypassed journalistic intermediation
in communicating with citizens?
RQ2: Through which communication strategies has Modi
constructed credibility and political brand identity?
RQ3: How does political rhetoric contribute to the perception
of direct leader–citizen communication?
The study is organized so that Section 2 explains the methodology,
followed by Section 3, which explores the theoretical
underpinnings of political marketing, followed by the
establishment of the role of media in political marketing,
looking at personal branding in the political marketing
literature. Section 4 studies the strategies adopted by Prime
Minister Modi to communicate and discusses the results and
findings, while Section 5 is dedicated to conclusions and an
agenda for further research.
2 Methodology
By employing a longitudinal case study methodology (Yin,
2018) spanning three electoral cycles (2014, 2019, and 2024), the
study extends political branding theory beyond the campaign
window, demonstrating that brand equity can be built, sustained,
and refreshed through non-electoral communicative routines.
Although prior studies have examined political marketing and
populist communication independently, the convergence of these
phenomena has not been systematically addressed in the extant
literature (Marland, 2016; Scammell, 2015).
Mr. Modi’s popularity persists despite his “lack of interaction”
with journalists. Exemplifying this, when PM Modi interacted

2.1 Case methodology
As case studies allow for a longitudinal study of the
phenomenon for better understanding (Ghauri, 2004) and
provide an opportunity to examine the phenomenon from
different perspectives and factors over a long period of time
(Ghauri, 1983), this method has been used in the study. Also,
when the concepts and variables involved are difficult to
quantify, rendering experimental or survey methods
inappropriate, the case study method proves very effective
(Bonoma, 1985). This study also meets the criteria necessary for
the use of a single case in research: when a case is critical for
explaining or questioning an established theory; when a case is
an extreme or a unique case; when a case is revelatory; when an
inductive approach and specific explanations are possible
(Ghauri, 2004).
In addition, the case method involves data collection through
various sources, such as speeches, in-person interviews,
observations, and secondary sources such as reports, archives,
marketing communications, and news reports. This way, the
research question can be investigated in depth from various
perspectives to characterize and explain the unique features and
many dimensions of the case under study for a cohesive
interpretation (Selltiz et al., 1976).
As established in the above sections, the subject of the study,
Mr. Modi, does n’t give interviews. Also, reaching out to the
team involved in designing his communication is futile, and
interviewing experts would likely yield only the information
available in the media. Thus, this study relies entirely all the
above-mentioned secondary data collection tools.
2.2 Inclusion criteria
Relevant information was collected from reputed international
media outlets—such as The Economist, Time, Financial Times,
Washington Post, Wall Street Journal, New York Times, and
Reuters—as well as Indian English media outlets—such as India
Today, The Hindu, The Times of India, Business Today,
Economic Times, Hindustan Times, Live Mint, First Post, and
The Print, among other reputable publications—covering the
period from 2012 to 2024.
The case study methodology employed in this study (Yin,
2018) offers depth and contextual richness but raises well-
documented questions about the external validity of the findings
(Flyvbjerg, 2006). A single-case design is best suited to theory
building and analytical generalization, allowing for the extension
of theoretical propositions to new contexts rather than statistical
generalization from a sample to a population (Yin, 2018). The
findings reported here are therefore offered as theoretical
propositions grounded in one richly documented case, rather
than as empirical generalizations about all political systems or
all instances of disintermediated political branding. Readers
seeking to apply these findings in other national contexts should
treat them as hypotheses to be tested rather than as conclusions
to be transferred directly.

3 Literature review: politics and
marketing
Scholars from various disciplines have been studying political
marketing for some time now, but the increased interest is evident
from the 1990s (Baines and Egan, 2001; Kavanagh, 1995; Lees-
Marshment, 2001; Lock and Harris, 1996; Morley, 2002;
O’Shaugnessy and O’Shaughnessy, 2003; Scammell, 1999;
Wheeler, 1997). Consequently, political marketing is considered
a subdiscipline of marketing, attracting many researchers
(Henneberg, 2008). The only difference is that, in political
marketing, candidates, ideas, and philosophies are marketed
rather than products and sales, as in commercial marketing.
This proposition is in consonance with Harrop (1990), who says
that party image and policies are offered by political marketing.
Farrell and Wortmann (1987) stated that party image, leader
image, and manifesto proposals or selected issues will constitute
the product in political marketing. Similarly, Worcester and
Mortimore (2005) identified issues, party image, and leader
image as offerings.
Comparisons of political marketing with commercial
advertising have generated considerable interest among
researchers, leading to contrasting conclusions about whether
the two spheres are similar. Some researchers opine that
advertising in both political and commercial marketing evokes
similar affective, cognitive, and behavioural responses. They
further state that marketing techniques employed by political
parties closely resemble those of corporate entities.
However, some scholars argue that the contexts differ between
voting and consumption. Therefore, attitude formation and
decision-making will not be similar (Lock and Harris, 1996), as
voting cannot be postponed like buying decisions. Although a
monetary price does n’t exist in voting, a “political price”—
expressed as feelings of hope or insecurity regarding national
and economic interests (Wring, 1997)—means that voting is
perceived as a “psychological purchase” (Reid, 1988). An
election represents a collective choice, and the elected candidate
cannot be altered or revoked, even if the candidate is not the
preference of a particular segment of voters (Lock and Harris,
1996).
Further, the political process is highly difficult to understand;
i.e., voting decisions might be underinformed or uninformed; the
scope for brand introduction or creation might be very
constricted, as political parties (and, in some cases, candidate
choices) are heavily governed by law. Further, political
marketing can be ideological, propagandistic, and rhetorical
(Harris et al., 2000), whereas commercial marketing focuses on
functional, instrumental, and intrinsic values (O’shaughnessy,
2003). In short, the political choice of a voter is not always
rational, unlike buying choices.
Notwithstanding these differences, both business entities and
political parties exploit marketing campaigns to appeal to their
respective constituencies (Norris, 1999). Marketing campaigns
naturally lead to brands and branding, prompting Kotler to
emphasize the application of brand management theories and
branding frameworks to positively influence voter attitudes and
behaviour in a political context (Kotler and Kotler, 1999; Kotler
and Levy, 1969).
Schattschneider (1975) described democracy as a competitive
political system in which political organizations and leaders
propose alternative public policies to enable a participative
decision-making process. Harrop (1990) elaborated on this
notion, saying that the parties try to position themselves “in the
electoral market,” and Beresford (2000) stated that each party
tries to relate itself to a particular segment.
It has been well established that media messages have a high
impact on voters as they do on consumers (Hackley, 2005;
Lewis, 2002; Messaris, 1997; Morley, 2002; O’Shaugnessy and
O’Shaughnessy, 2003). Newman (1994) opined that
technological improvements in media, especially television, are
the main reason for the accelerated use of media in political
marketing. The role of television in political marketing,
advertising, and branding was extensively studied by Newman
(1999) and O’Shaughnessy (1990).
Since its origin in the United States,6 branding has become
popular in political marketing. Both political parties and
contesting candidates are being branded (Peng and Hackley,
2009). As Needham (2005) pointed out, branding techniques are
increasingly used to reinforce certain key attributes of the
candidate and to build trust and relatability with voters.
To meet the demands of electoral politics, a candidate’s image
is cultivated to be vibrant with political rhetoric, symbolism, and
emotional appeals to better engage voters. In political advertising,
the core goal of branding is to consistently protect a candidate’s
image amid an ever-changing political environment. Political
advertising aims to build public confidence and influence voting
by marketing ideas, attitudes, and solutions to public issues.

3.1 Role of media in political marketing
Ullah (2009) argued that the media is a principal institution in
democracy, as citizens come to better understand society through
media coverage. The media provides information to citizens,
encourage and contribute to public debates by offering diverse
perspectives, give opinion makers a platform, and act as a
watchdog—exposing the inner workings of various public
institutions (Curran, 2000) to safeguard democratic values
(McQuail, 2000; Street, 2010). Media serves as both the
representative of the public in national discourse and a source
of information for citizens. Democracy requires active public
engagement for its success, and that engagement is driven by
political literacy, which involves media literacy. Lund and Carr
(2008) opined that the process of the public becoming politically
literate should begin with education.
Carr (2009) stated that media literacy is critical for people to
understand the world, to change positions, to arrive at their
own conclusions, to identify truth, not to succumb to herd
mentality, and to better judge critical issues. Media not only
provides information but also guides the public in interpreting
it, facilitating opinion formation by the public. Further, media
provides a venue for all sections of society to express opinions
as a democratic institution (McQuail, 2000; Street, 2010). This
role of the media allows for an inclusive public debate on all
important topics and issues. In contrast, low media literacy
makes people and democracy vulnerable.
Couldry (2009) emphasized the role of media, saying that the
sustenance of the modern nation-state depends on the existence of
media, which, in itself, is a critical component of the wider
organization of economic, social, and political structures.
According to Kellner (2009), media is a powerful force in
contemporary societies, as it is integrated into all spheres of
social life, ranging from the economy and politics to culture.
Media shapes society’s understanding of reality and serves as the
mirror through which the public perceives it (Apuke, 2017).
Because of this reality-portraying role, political leaders use
media to emphasize their election campaigns and tarnish their
opponents so that rivals stay in a bad light in public opinion.
All parties seek media endorsements to strengthen their images
at the cost of their opponents (Ullah, 2009).
Media plays a prominent role in shaping long-term
perceptions of political issues, as acknowledged (Kavanagh,
1995; Kraus and Davis, 1976). It has also been suggested that
the way the media reports events, or omits some events from
reporting, is crucial in shaping public perceptions (McQuail,
1977). This is because voters tend to believe that the issues that
get more coverage in the media are more important than those
that get less coverage (Mancini and Swanson, 1996). Issues not
covered by the media do not make it into public discourse. The
same is applicable to parties as well: the parties that garner
more coverage will get more visibility than their competitors
(Semetko and Schönbach, 1994).
Political leaders have been leveraging various media to
advance their political agenda. It began with the development of
the newspaper market. One statement or one announcement
could reach thousands through a newspaper. Advances in radio
technology allowed political leaders to “talk” (not converse) with
a large number of people, and radio became the centre of
political marketing. With the commercialization of television,
politicians’ interest in the media has increased manifold due to
“visibility” to many people. Also, with television broadcasting,
the message is fully under the control of the party or the
candidate (Kaid and Holtz-Bacha, 1995).
Technology has provided an efficient way of campaigning to
achieve political goals (Towner and Dulio, 2013). Apart from
reinforcing the existing image (Kavanagh, 1995; Scammell, 1995)
or creating the image, political advertising has disseminated
issue-based information to the voting public. Visibility on screen
has been helping to imprint the physical image of political
leaders in the minds of people, and thus branding and
personalization become easier (Swanson and Mancini 1996).
Personalization leads to attachment between the public and
political leaders. Television debates increasingly became the
focal point of the election campaign (Maarek, 1995), even
though evidence could not be found to suggest that debates
dramatically altered the outcome of the campaign (Newman,
1994). However, many authors agree that television is the key
political marketing tool (Newman, 1994; O’Shaughnessy, 1990;
Scammell, 1995) as it paves the way for cultivating and
nurturing a desirable image for a political leader.
3.2 Personal image creation in political
marketing
Political marketing witnessed a new turn in the United States,
as political communication began to revolve around party leaders
(Maarek, 1995; Newman, 1994)—a phenomenon subsequently
followed by political communication across the globe. The
United Kingdom has caught on by televising the Prime
Minister’s Question Time in the House of Commons, and the
party leaders have since been the central focus of political
communication (Kavanagh, 1995; Scammell, 1995). With the
leaders occupying the central stage of communications, voters’
likes and dislikes towards the individual leader replace
ideological bonds with a political party, even while casting votes
(Harris et al., 2000). Thus, in some cases, the brand of the
political leader has become much bigger than the party they
represent. People outside the United Kingdom may remember
Margaret Thatcher, for instance, but may not remember the
party she represents. The same would be the case with Ronald
Regan or J. F. Kennedy for people outside the United States. In
view of the importance of personal branding in politics, political
parties always seek to project the right image of their leader(s)
(Swanson and Mancini, 1996). Further, Pitch et al. (2020)
provided a personal brand identity framework with six
dimensions: (1) legacy and experience, (2) continuous brand
building, (3) coherency of personal values, (4) develop and
maintain personalized relationships, (5) visibility and presence,
and (6) goals and aspirations.
The growing prominence of personal branding in politics has
been described by some authors as democratization of the
relationship between elites and citizens (Van Zoonen, 2005), but
some others did not like the idea, such as Habermas (1989,
1992) and Postman (1985), as it undermines philosophies and

policies of political parties. However, scholars have been
highlighting that the image of a leader favourably influences
voting decisions (Cutlip, 1994; Kaid and Holtz-Bacha, 1995).
Smith (2001) argued that the brand image of the party and
leader will determine voting more than policies. Needham
(2006) favoured the idea that brand image will simplify
decision-making for voters.
Popkin (1991) and Scammell (1995) proposed that the
image of the leader will substitute for their performance in
the minds of voters (Bartels, 1987; Carmines and Stimson,
1980). Scammell (1995) explained that the image of a
candidate stands on reputation, trustworthiness (used
extensively by Bush in 2000), and credibility rather than on
appearance, which includes body language or dress. Harrop
(1990) argued that the performance of the candidate adds to
credibility, further enhancing the image. Although voters give
more prominence to the image of a leader than to policies,
this may not be admitted explicitly.
All political marketing focuses on establishing the image of a
leader (Newman, 1994) that is relatable to all critical voter
segments and effectively counters the image of the opponents.
Voters relate to the image of the leader much more quickly than
to issues (O’Shaughnessy, 1990); hence, image advertising leaves
a stronger imprint on voters than issue advertisements (Sabato,
1981). Maarek (1995) suggested that, compared with other
candidates’ image advertising, facing a tough challenge will yield
better results than trying to build an image. Indeed, Towner and
Dulio (2013) argued that every candidate strives to distinguish
themselves from opponents to be better remembered by voters
during elections. Therefore, “campaigns are fundamentally about
contrasts.” Often, parties and candidates attempt to undermine
the credibility and consistency of opponents’ image (Baines
et al., 1999). Consequently, to maintain image credibility and
consistency, all parties and candidates constantly resort “spin-
doctoring” and “rapid rebutting.” As Blumenthal (1980)
proposed, all parties and candidates are in “permanent
campaign” mode. Essentially, political marketing has become
permanent campaigning in which the people and party in power
have an advantage, as Nimmo (1999) succinctly stated:
officeholders turn “…office itself into a full-time campaign
platform.”
However, brands cannot be built solely on differentiation; they
need substance for long-term survival. Speed et al. (2015)
proposed that political brands must have (a) credibility to
advocate for the policies of the party and (b) authority to
promise on behalf of the party; in other words, voters must
perceive the leader to be the face of the party. No candidate or
leader can become a brand without these two conditions being
met; however, as seen previously, political leaders are products
that parties offer to the people. That means, in political
marketing and political communications, leaders are both
products in themselves and also brand advocates. In this
context, in political marketing, human brands have to embody
the philosophy of their party and must be able to build
favourable associations with the voters for the party to edge out
competition in elections. Further, the leader or candidate needs
to be relatable to the core voter segments (or voter groups) of
the party to fare better in elections. The more relatable the
leader is to voters, the better the chances of the party. Along
with relatability, Smith (2001) identified brand dimensions
necessary for a political leader to build a good image with
voters, including “capable leader,” “good in a crisis,” and
“patriotic,” among others.
The advent of social media augers well for political brand
building, as it establishes a direct channel between the leader
and voters. In 2008, Barack Obama extensively used social
media, which helped create a brand: “… the Obama campaign
went beyond simple messaging with one additional
advancement in 2008: the building of the ‘Obama brand’”
(Speed et al., 2015). Furthermore, it has been observed that after
the acute difficult times of COVID-19, social media is among
the most used tools for interaction between individuals
(Moslehpour et al., 2025).
3.3 Effective direct communication—
theoretical underpinning
The literature offers many theories that explain how political
brands are built, as well as disintermediation in the process of
political brand building through digital technologies. These
theories provide an in-depth understanding of the acceptance of
these brands by the public at large while also reinforcing how
the public “owns” such brands when there is no mediation
between the brand and its target audience.
Parasocial relationship theory (Giles, 2002; Horton and Wohl,
1956): Regular, unmediated communication from a political
leader, particularly via radio or social media, fosters parasocial
interaction. The audience develops a sense of personal
acquaintance with the communicator despite the one-directional
nature of the channel. This parasocial relationship generates
trust, liking, and loyalty that parallel (and may substitute for)
direct interpersonal contact. For Modi, the cumulative parasocial
capital from direct communication constitutes a durable
brand asset.
Framing theory (Chong and Druckman, 2007; Entman, 1993):
Modi’s direct communication consistently frames issues in terms
of national development, individual aspiration, and collective
pride. Thus, it aligns with dominant cultural values in post-
liberalization India. By controlling the communication channel,
the BJP controls the frame, preventing the oppositional
reframing that journalistic mediation typically introduces. This
frame-control function is central to brand equity maintenance
(Keller, 1993).
Symbolic interactionism and political identity (Blumer, 1969;
Norris, 2000): Political brands function as identity symbols;
supporting Modi becomes an expression of a particular vision of
Indian national identity. Direct communication reinforces this
symbolic association more effectively than mediated
communication because it is unfiltered, personalized, and ritual
—the characteristics that strengthen symbolic resonance.
Uses and gratifications theory (Katz et al., 1974; Vaccari and
Valeriani, 2021): Audiences seek political media for surveillance
(staying informed), social utility (shared political identity), and
parasocial companionship. Modi’s diverse channel portfolio,
i.e., radio for reach, social media for interactivity, and

community events for embodied experience, satisfies a broader
array of these gratifications than a single media strategy could
achieve, thereby maximizing audience engagement and

Digital political communication and disintermediation: The
emergence of social media has fundamentally restructured the
political communication ecosystem. Chadwick (2013) identified
a “hybrid media system” in which legacy and new media logics
co-exist and compete; more recently, scholars have documented
how platforms such as Twitter/X, Facebook, Instagram, and
WhatsApp have enabled politicians to communicate directly
with constituents, bypassing editorial mediation (Boulianne,
2020; Enli, 2017).
This phenomenon of political disintermediation has been
empirically studied in contexts as diverse as Trump’s Twitter
presidency (Ott, 2017), Bolsonaro’s WhatsApp campaigns
(Cesarino, 2020), and Macron’s direct-to-citizen YouTube
addresses (Lalancette and Raynauld, 2019).
The Indian context introduces a distinctive institutional
dimension: a relatively adversarial relationship between the
ruling BJP and sections of the English-language press
incentivized the adoption of disintermediation as a strategic
choice rather than merely a technological opportunity (Mehta,
2022; Neyazi, 2020).
Mediatization and its limits: The mediatization of politics
thesis (Esser and Strömbäck, 2014; Strömbäck, 2008) holds that
political actors increasingly adapt their behaviour to satisfy the
logic of media, such as prioritizing conflict, simplicity,
personalization, and novelty. However, recent scholarship has
identified conditions under which political actors resist or invert
mediatization pressures (Hjarvard, 2013; Strömbäck and
Dimitrova, 2011).
Self-mediation (Enli and Skogerbø, 2013): Where political
actors possess sufficient brand equity and direct-channel
infrastructure, they can bypass media logic entirely, a condition
Enli and Skogerbø (2013) termed ’self-mediation’ and that
Vaccari and Valeriani (2021) analysed in the context of social
media political communication. The Modi case represents an
extreme manifestation of this self-mediation strategy, warranting
theoretical attention.
Populist communication: Populist communication is
characterized by appeals to the homogeneous “people,” attacks
on corrupt “elites,” and an emotionally charged, simple,
repetitive style (Engesser et al., 2017; Mudde and Kaltwasser,
2017). Recent quantitative studies have demonstrated that
populist communication styles generate higher social media
engagement than non-populist messages (Ernst et al., 2017;
Hameleers, 2018). Importantly, Moffitt (2016) argued that
populist leaders engage in the “performance of crisis,” projecting
urgency, decisiveness, and ordinariness to construct a particular
political persona. Modi’s direct communication programmes
display many of these characteristics: the intimate radio address
format of Maan Ki Baat enhances accessibility; the Chai Pe
Charcha format conveys ordinariness; the repeated nationalist
frames convey urgency. This connects political marketing theory
with the growing literature on populism as a communicative
style rather than merely an ideology.
Platform politics: The concept of platform politics refers to
how the technical and commercial logics of digital platforms
shape political communication practices (Klinger and Svensson,
2015; Kreiss and McGregor, 2018). Algorithmic amplification,
micro-targeting, and data-driven voter segmentation have
transformed political campaign strategy globally (Baldwin-
Philippi, 2015; Howard and Hussain, 2013). In the Indian
context, the BJP was an early and sophisticated adopter of social
media-based campaigning described as the most extensive
WhatsApp political communication network in the world. More
recently, scholars have examined the role of IT cells,
coordinated inauthentic behaviour, and viral political memes in
the digital ecosystem of the BJP (Pal et al., 2019; Udupa, 2019).
These platform-level strategies do not merely amplify Modi’s
brand messages but also create effective communities of brand
advocates who generate secondary content, thereby multiplying
reach at negligible marginal cost.
Personal political branding: Personal political branding has
attracted growing scholarly attention, particularly in the context
of presidentialization and the personalization of politics (Balmas
et al., 2014; Poguntke and Webb, 2005). Marland (2016)
examined brand management in Canadian federal politics and
identified the centralization of the communication strategy as a
precondition for effective personal brand management. Nielsen
and Vaccari (2013) distinguished between the politician’s “brand
community” and their broader electoral coalition, arguing that
strong personal brands allow politicians to perform better than
their party’s natural vote share—a phenomenon evident in
Modi’s sequential electoral performances (2014, 2019, and 2024).
Lalancette and Raynauld (2019) examined how political leaders
use Instagram to perform “ordinary” and “intimate” leadership.
The findings are directly applicable to Modi’s social media
persona.

4.2 Rise on to the national politics
Mr. Modi created an image of a progressive, prosperity-
oriented Chief Minister by projecting the economic growth of
Gujarat during his tenure. As Baumann et al., 2018 observed,
“Modi cleverly marketed the economic development of Gujarat
as his personal achievement, implying that his ability to deliver
governance could bring the whole country forward. By stressing
the success of his ‘Gujarat model,’ Modi also tried to take public
attention away from accusations of his role as Chief Minister
during the Gujarat riots of 2002.” The degree of economic
progress in Gujarat has always been contagious, but there has
been general consensus on the direction.
4.2.1 Success as the chief minister
Gujarat recorded an economic growth rate of 10.1% between
2004 and 2012, compared to a national average of 7.6%. Heavy
investments in infrastructure, a focus on private investment, and
an emphasis on e-governance have transformed the state’s
industrial sector, which grew by 12.65% between 2005 and 2009,
up from 3.95% between 2001 and 2004. In agriculture, farmers
were promised and provided with 8 h of uninterrupted
electricity, while almost all villages received 24/7 power supply.
Electricity helped Gujarat achieve the fastest growth in the
agricultural sector compared to any other state in the country.
Between 2001 and 2010, the agriculture sector of Gujarat
expanded at 10.1%. The living conditions of the rural
population have improved dramatically.11 e-Governance largely
curbed corruption at higher hierarchical levels in the
bureaucracy and among elected representatives.12 Mr. Modi has
used these achievements to stake a claim to the Prime
Minister’s position.

4.2.3 Political idiom
Mr. Modi addressed himself in the third person in his public
speeches. Sud (2022) observed that by addressing himself in third
person, Mr. Modi tries “to evoke feelings of unquestioned loyalty,
and to almost elevate himself above personhood” among his
followers. By doing so, he has become above questioning. He
ensured that he was relatable to the people; to this end, along
with addressing himself in the third person, he spoke to the
masses in their language, i.e., Hindi. Using common idioms,
metaphors, and catchy slogans, he has been successful in being
identified by common people for his ordinariness. He has
constantly been reminding people that he is one among them
and an embodiment of the people, rather than a representative
of the people. This resulted in compelling brand promotions
(Vittorini, 2022). By identifying himself with common people,
Mr. Modi reminds observers that, unlike the other engaged in
“dirty” politics for fame or fortune for themselves and their
progeny, he is in politics to serve the public. He often uses the
term Seva (service) in his speeches, to such an extent that after
becoming the Prime Minister in 2014, he has often described
himself as the Pradhan Sevak (prime servant). As a celibate
bachelor, he has consistently stated that he has no desire for
worldly possessions or fame, remaining totally dedicated to “the
people” (Sud, 2022). His loyalty to people has been so
pronounced that Time Magazine acknowledged, “in a country
where nepotism and dynastic politics are the norm, Modi’s
family is invisible.”15
4.2.4 Engagement in political rallies
To engage people, he often asks questions during his speeches
to elicit answers. He makes a statement and asks people to switch
on the torch on their mobiles if they agree with him. These
approaches turn speeches into conversations, with people
perceiving themselves as part of the discussion or dialogue
rather than passive spectators. He has used phrases such as
Chappan Inch Ki Chati (56-inch chest) and Gahr Mein Ghus Ke
Marenge (an idiom for forceful and unhesitating retaliation), which
evoke a huge uproar from the public, often turning into feverish
admiration accompanied by unstoppable “Modi….Modi….Modi”
chants from thousands of attendees. While the chants continue,
Mr. Modi pauses in his speech. This pause, coupled with the
chants, leaves a lasting effect on both the attendees and those
watching the live telecast or re-broadcast, making members of the
audience feel as though he is speaking directly to each one of
them. These phrases serve a dual purpose. First, because they are
commonly used by the public, listeners feel as though they are
hearing from one of their own. Second, these phrases emphasize
certain attributes. For instance, Chappan Inch Ki Chati
symbolizes courage.
Another hallmark of his speeches has been his avoidance of
the names of his political opponents. He mocks his opposition
by smearing them, and people would get who the target is,
almost on cue—for instance, using the word Shezada (prince)
for Mr. Rahul Gandhi. His oratory skills have established such a
connection with people that they can identify the person even if
his/her name has not been mentioned. In 2013, addressing a
party meeting, he said, “When the time came to form the
government, they (the opposing political part—Congress) chose
a PM who could do what the family wanted; they chose another
nightwatchman.” This was an obvious reference to the former
Prime Minister, Dr. Manmohan Singh, and the Gandhi family,
which controls the Congress Party.16 Further, he mocked Singh’s
efficiency as Prime Minister with just one word, using an
analogy from cricket, in which a nightwatchman has nothing to
accomplish and is not expected to. These references soon
became part of day-to-day conversations of the public,
establishing a deep, subliminal connection with Mr. Modi.

continents. He addressed a host of questions from around the
globe, establishing him as a tech-savvy politician. By 2014, he
had more than a million Twitter followers,17 providing another
avenue for direct connection with the public.

4.3.1 Chai Pe Charcha
During the campaign, at least three phrases became so popular
that they entered the common lexicon and are still used today by
various sections of the public in different contexts. One of them is
Chai Pe Charcha (a tête-à-tête over a cup of tea). When an erudite
Congress leader, Mr. Mani Shankar Aiyer, mocked Mr. Modi by
stating, “Give my word, Modi will never be PM in the 21st
century. If he wants to sell tea here (AICC meet), we can
arrange for it,”20 Modi’s team responded with Chai Pe Charcha,
“reaching out to voters using the potent symbolism of Modi’s
rise from a lowly background” (Sen, 2016). In these
conversations, Mr. Modi interacted directly with citizens who
could ask questions. As part of the campaign, Mr. Modi
participated in over three such events, which were telecast in
over 500 cities and towns and at more than 4,000 tea stalls
across the country (Price, 2015).
Rajdeep Sardesai, a prominent Indian journalist known for his
sharp remarks on Mr. Modi, commended the impact of these
charchas. He wrote, “A tea stall where a potential Prime
Minister sits with his fellow citizens drinking chai—what could
be a greater equalizer?” (Sardesai, 2015). Apart from portraying
the journey of Mr. Modi, from poverty to power (Sardesai,
2015), these charchas brought him closer to potential voters.
The imagery of a prime ministerial contender sitting at a
roadside tea stall and having routine discussions with common
people proved deeply endearing and has been etched in public
memory. The phrase Chai Pe Charcha has since become a
common expression among friends and family for any routine
conversation over tea, through which Mr. Modi established a
subliminal connection. Even after close to a decade, the phrase
instantly evokes his image in the public consciousness.

powerful people overruled the interests of the nation. Against
this backdrop, achhe din acquired an additional meaning: that in
the days to come, the country’s interests will be protected. Sen
(2016) summarized, “If development was an overarching theme
of the electoral campaign, Modi was also able to connect to the
mood of the voters by resorting to pithy slogans. One such
powerful slogan was ‘Achhe Din Aane Waley Hai (Good times
are coming).’” The phrase went viral across both social media
and conventional media (Kaur, 2015) to such an extent that
Swapan Dasgupta, an erudite journalist-turned-politician leaning
towards the BJP, stated that “achhe din has been decisive in
BJP’s victory.”23 Nearly a decade after its coinage, the phrase
still evokes Mr. Modi’s image in 2024.
4.3.3 Sabka Sath, Sabka Vikas
The third phrase that stuck with people is Sabka Sath, Sabka
Vikas (translating to together with all, development for all).
While many pundits interpreted this slogan as an outreach
effort towards the Muslim community, some opined that its
appeal was much broader, designed to reach every corner of the
country, every community, every region, every subterrain, and
every subaltern section. This phrase, centred around vikas
(development), captured the imagination of the public at large,
specifically urban youth, at a time when the incumbent
government’s economic policy leaned towards rural
development. As widely reported, the slogan was instrumental in
influencing neutral voters to vote for Mr. Modi.
Other influential phrases were also used, such as Ab ki Baar,
Modi Sarkar (this time, a Modi government), to reach out to
the public. On analysing these slogans, one finds a distinct
pattern. They draw from the language of the common person,
are easy to remember, are highly contextual, carry deep
meaning, offer hope, evoke positive feelings about the future,
and are easy to pronounce. All of these qualities, especially the
last one, faciliated a quick and effective word-of-mouth
advertisement. This people-to-people spread was important to
the candidacy of Mr. Modi’ as he has not been offered a
positive image by the media.
Cornfield (2021) formulates 10 elements of a speech that can
help convince voters to favour a particular leader. These include
(1) an orienting welcome; (2) a onerous personal backstory; (3)
shout-outs to (some) personal benefactors and role models; (4)
credentials for the office; (5) a signature alert; (6) favourable
contrasts; (7) conduct pledges and policy promises; (8) magic
words: the declaration sentence (and “The American Dream”);
(9) perorations; and (10) theme songs. Of these 10, Mr. Modi
has ticked many.
4.3.4 Hindutva and his personality
Mr. Modi has been associated with the Rastriya Swayamsevak
Sangh (RSS) since childhood. The RSS, a non-political
organization (Curran, 1950), was founded in 1925 by Baliram
Hedgewar (1889–1940), a physician, to fight against British rule.
The RSS ideology centres on the creation of a “Hindu nation”
through the protection of Hindu political, cultural, and religious
interests (Atkins, 2004). Following independence, the RSS
developed into an influential Hindu nationalist umbrella
organization, with affiliate bodies running schools, charities,
labour unions, youth wings, and several other clubs to spread its
ideology. By the end of 2024, the RSS had 72,354 shakhas24
(literally meaning branches, referring to a place where local
congregations happen) spread across the country, with over 5
million swayamsevaks25 (volunteers) organizing regular events
dedicated to upholding Hindu culture.
The RSS, according to its publications, has been working
towards the restoration of Hindu pride after centuries of self-
effacement. Anyone associated with RSS has likewise been
perceived as working towards this same cause. Consequently,
Mr. Modi was viewed as upholding the Hindu cause in the
Hindu-majority country, and the RSS started promoting his
candidature through its network. His image as a man fighting
for the Hindu cause steadily grew through his constant
references to Veer Savarkar in his speeches. Veer Savarkar, a
controversial figure during the Indian freedom struggle, was
castigated by the independent Indian Government over
allegations that he was a prime conspirator in the assassination
of Mahatma Gandhi, among others—accusations that Hindu
ideologues have always challenged since independence. Anyone
who dares to take Savarkar’s name publicly has been viewed as a
staunch Hindu ideologue.
The much-politicized religious undercurrents in Indian
society helped Mr. Modi become recognized as a Hindu leader
in no time, especially in North Indian states where religious
friction had been apparent since the 1990s. This image of Mr.
Modi as a Hindu ideologue, combined with his promotional
campaigns, made him popular among the masses.
In 1980, the RSS started its political wing, the BJP, and thus
any BJP candidate would be promoted by the RSS. However,
with Mr. Modi, the relationship has been much deeper, as he
himself is an RSS Pracharak. As Shani (2021) noted, Pracharaks
are full-time RSS appointees who are highly disciplined and
dedicated to the cause, forsaking family life and remaining
unmarried. This aspect of Mr. Modi’s persona is highly
respected by the Indian mass, as traditional Indian society holds
profound reverence for those who make personal sacrifices for a
greater cause. Mr. Modi has thus been viewed as an individual
who surrendered his personal life for the nation. When Time

4.3.5 The results
The Economist acclaimed, “The storm broke, marking a
national political shift as big as any since independence in 1947.
Mr Modi smashed the long domination of Congress.”26 The BJP
secured a 31% vote share, which translated into 282 seats out of
543. When included its allies, the tally rose to 336 seats.
Congress garnered a 19% vote share and won 44 seats. This
marked the first time that a single party outnumbered Congress

could produce only fragmented outcomes and weak coalition
governments.”27
4.3.6 The Prime Minister who speaks to people
The goodwill that Mr. Modi accumulated during the campaign
phase has helped garner a positive image, even during difficult
times for the common person. On the evening of 6 November
2018, the Prime Minister announced the demonetization of Rs.
500 and Rs. 1,000 notes. He stated that the move was intended
to curb black money. Suddenly, small- and medium-scale
businesses across the country felt a severe negative impact as
close to 86% of the money in circulation lost its legal tender
status. Consequently, for months together, all economic
activity came to a screeching halt, millions lost their
livelihood, and citizens were forced to stand in never-ending
queues outside banks. Even in those times of massive distress,
the common public did not blame the Prime Minister. Singh
et al. (2018), through sentiment analysis after demonetizati on,
concluded that, “The result of our analysis shows that a large
share of Indian people was happy with this policy. During the
initial days the sentiment was more towards the negative side
as the common man had to suffer many hardships.
Ultimately, as the new banknotes were made available, the
overall sentiment of the people became positive.” Hordes of
newspaper articles and videos express similar sentiments, with
the common public believing that these troubles are
temporary and that curbing black money will help them in
the long run.
As Prime Minister, Mr. Modi’s inclination to speak to people
directly has taken new approaches. Months after assuming
office, the Prime Minister launched a new radio programme
named Mann Ki Baat (straight from the heart) on 3 October
2017. The 20–30-min episode, broadcast on radio on the last
Sunday of each month, has become an instant hit with the
masses. In his addresses, the Prime Minister talks about a
wide variety of topics, ranging from motivation, national
security, sports, and cleanliness to government policies, the
eradication of tuberculosis, the valour of soldiers, and Indian
heritage. Through a website, ideas are sought from the general
public on what should be included in the speech of the
Prime Minister.

talk show. The All India Radio that telecasts the show has sold the

second prime minister, after Jawaharlal Nehru, to form three
successive cabinets.

country had to navigate two waves of the COVID-19 pandemic
and associated lockdowns during 2020 and 2021. Much like
demonetization in 2018, the economic consequences of the
national lockdowns were severe for the middle class and the
poor. Unable to sustain lives in the closed-down cities, millions
of migrant workers traversed the vast expanse of India on foot
during the first lockdown of 2020. The following lockdown in

inadequate healthcare system could not handle the pandemic,
generating public anger. Time Magazine noted, “That anger and

class was left lurking, as The Hindu opined, “In India, while
rural and poor households received a marginal increase in direct
cash transfers as part of an existing employment guarantee
scheme, and organised businesses in affected sectors received a
government-backed line of credit, the average working classes—
the middle and upper-middle class Indians—were left to fend
for themselves.”36
5 Conclusion
Drawing on four theoretical traditions, this study proposes an
integrative framework for understanding how political actors build
brand equity outside the mainstream media ecosystem. These four
constructs are treated as complementary rather than competing:
(i) Political brand identity (Kapferer, 2012; Needham and
Smith, 2015): Brand identity encompasses the sender-
controlled signals—name, visual identity, narrative,
personality, values, and relational promise—that a
political actor deliberately projects. This study
operationalizes brand identity through Modi’s persona
construction across three electoral cycles.

(ii) Mediatization and disintermediation (Enli, 2017;
Strömbäck, 2008): Mediatization theory posits that
political actors adapt their behaviour to media logic
(Altheide and Snow, 1979). Disintermediation reverses
this dependency: actors build direct channels to
citizens, reducing reliance on journalistic gatekeepers.
Modi’s Maan Ki Baat and social media presence
exemplify disintermediated political communication.
(iii) Populist communication style (Engesser et al., 2017;
Hameleers, 2018): Populist communication involves an
appeal to “the people” against corrupt elites and
operates through simplification, emotionality, and vox
populi rhetoric. The present study analyses how Modi’s
direct-communication programmes deploy populist
stylistics to strengthen brand authenticity.
(iv) Platform politics and micro-targeting (Kreiss and
McGregor, 2018; Vaccari and Valeriani, 2021): Political
actors now leverage platform affordances, including
algorithmic reach, user-generated amplification, and
targeted advertising, to build granular audience
relationships. This dimension examines how the BJP’s
digital ecosystem amplified Modi’s personal brand far
beyond traditional broadcast.
The BJP presented Mr. Modi as its prime ministerial candidate
during the 2014 Indian election. In accordance with Harrop’s
(1990) idea that party image and policies are communicated
through political marketing, the BJP offered the electorate a
nationalistic, right-oriented, non-dynastic, and anti-corruption
party, paired with a candidate whose personal identity aligned
with the party’s propositions. This is also in accordance with
the observations of Farrell and Wortmann (1987) and
Worcester and Mortimore (2005) that party image and leader
image form the core product. It can be argued that the public
was offered a strong product during the 2014 election,
particularly against the backdrop of an environment rife with
corruption scandals involving the incumbent government.
Furthermore, as Newman (1994) opined, all political marketing
focuses on establishing a leader’s image that resonates across
critical voter segments while effectively countering the image
of opponents. Mr. Modi not only succeeded in creating an
image that cut across voter segments but also created a
comparative difference with his prime contender, Rahul
Gandhi, by calling the latter “Shehzada.”
According to Reid (1988), voting is perceived as a
“psychological purchase”; often, consumers would want to opt
for a comparatively less risky product. The BJP’s prime
ministerial candidate appealed to this public sentiment with the
slogan Achhe Din Aane Wale Hai. Through the slogan, Mr.
Modi built public confidence to influence voting by focusing on
the marketing of ideas, attitudes, and solutions. One of the
reasons the slogan Acche Din Aane Wale Hai caught the public
imagination is that it reflected their sentiments.
As Harris et al. (2000) stated that political marketing can be
ideological, propaganda, and rhetoric, Mr. Modi did lean on all
three at some point in his campaign. His talk of nationalism,
religion, and acquisitions regarding the incumbent government
falls in this category. Further, he did offer an alternative policy
in his bid for the prime ministership, as Schattschneider (1975)
described the democratic process. His campaign seemed to
concur with the established views.
However, the campaign did deviate in the media usage
segment. He completely avoided giving interviews and
appearing on television talk shows as a prime ministerial
candidate. Perhaps nowhere in the world’s democratic nations
did a significant contender for the top post of the country
skirt journalis ts. The literature has established that media
messages have a high impact on voters (Lewis, 2002;
O’Shaugnessy and O’Shaughnessy , 2003). Newman (1994)
opined that the media is being extensively used in political
marketing. O’Shaughnessy (1990) suggested that television has
a greater impact. Yet, Mr. Modi has, by and large, not given
interviews to the media.
Carr (2009) argued that democracy requires active public
engagement, which is driven by political and media literacy. Mr.
Modi, faced with hostile media that has indulged in adverse
reporting, engaged with the public in his own way. He used
public rallies to engage in conversation with the people rather
than just address them. He used technology, oratory, language,
rhetoric, and his humble background to engage with his
audience. Often, these engagement methods were so novel (for
instance, using hologram technology to address multiple
gatherings simultaneously) that the methods themselves have
become a talking point among the public, establishing a
connection with Mr. Modi. This finding is in accordance with
Lucarelli et al. (2021), who argued, “information technologies
work as generic political products in which different topics/
agendas – debated both inside the movement but also in
traditional and social media geared towards voters – unfold
from the interaction among members and voter-consumers.”
Needham (2006), Smith (2001), Cutlip (1994), and Kaid and
Holtz-Bacha (1995) emphasized the role of the image of a leader
in influencing voting decisions. It should be noted that Mr.
Modi succeeded in portraying a favourable image. He has built
his administrative credentials by citing the Gujarat growth
example; he has established a clean image by emphasizing his
severed ties with his family. He cultivated a pro-development
image as a leader and connected with job-seeking youth,
promising good times ahead.
Scammell (1995) and Popkin (1991) opined that the image of a
leader, coupled with reputation, trustworthiness, and credibility,
exerts a positive impact on voters. Mr. Modi cultivated all of
these qualities through a carefully curated campaign strategy.
Moreover, by projecting these attributes strongly into the public
sphere, he succeeded in winning the elections twice. Also, he
has ticked all the boxes in the framework proposed by Pitch
et al. (2020).
5.1 Critical perspectives and alternative
interpretations
While the preceding analysis demonstrates the strategic
effectiveness of Modi’s disintermediated communication approach
in building brand equity, this section introduces critical and
alternative scholarly perspectives that complicate a
straightforwardly celebratory reading. A balanced assessment
requires engagement with three sets of concerns: the democratic

implications of media bypassing, the normative critique of populist
communication, and the contested empirical claims about the
actual reach and depth of direct communication channels.
(a) Democratic implications of media bypassing. The
normative political communication literature cautions
that direct political communication, while efficient as a
branding mechanism, may undermine the
accountability functions that independent journalism
performs in liberal democracies (McNair, 2017; Rosen,
2010). When political actors systematically bypass
editorial mediation, they also bypass the adversarial
questioning, fact-checking, and oppositional framing
that constitute journalism’s democratic watchdog role
(Curran, 2011; Norris, 2000). Reporters Without
Borders’ successive downgrading of India’s press
freedom ranking, from 140th in 2014 to 159th in 2023,
reflects a structural shift in the government–media
relationship, which many press freedom scholars read
as a consequence, at least in part, of the governing
party’s disintermediation strategy (Mehta, 2022; RSF,
2023). From this perspective, what this study frames as
’strategic communicative sovereignty’ may
simultaneously be read as a deliberate erosion of the
intermediary institutions that sustain democratic
deliberation. Acknowledging this tension is essential for
a theoretically complete account.
(b) The dark side of populist communication. The populist
communication literature documents not only the
electoral effectiveness of populist messaging but also its
corrosive effects on political pluralism and minority
representation. Mudde and Kaltwasser (2017)
distinguished between the “inclusive” populism of Latin
American left movements and the “exclusive” populism
characteristic of right-wing movements in Europe and
Asia, which constructs the “people” in ethnically or
culturally bounded terms. Critics of the BJP’s political
communication, including Jaffrelot (2021) and Tharoor
(2018), argued that the construction of a Hindu
nationalist “people” in Modi’s messaging functions to
exclude religious minorities from the imagined
community of the brand’s target constituency. The
affective communities constructed through populist
political branding may thus simultaneously strengthen
in-group solidarity and deepen intergroup polarization
(Eatwell and Goodwin, 2018; Hameleers, 2018). This
study does not independently test this claim, but
intellectual honesty requires acknowledging it as a
competing interpretation of the same communicative
strategies that this manuscript frames through the lens
of brand equity.
(c) Contested claims about reach and depth. A third critical
perspective concerns the empirical reach and audience
engagement of the direct communication programmes
celebrated in this manuscript. The listenership figures of
Maan Ki Baat, although government-reported as
substantial, have been questioned by independent
researchers, who note the limited audited data available
on sustained audience engagement with the programme
(Mehta, 2022). Similarly, WhatsApp-based political
communication, while extensive in distribution, operates
through “dark social” channels that are difficult to audit
for reach, credibility impact, or persuasion effects
(Bradshaw and Howard, 2019; Howard and Hussain,
2013). Social media follower counts routinely cited as
evidence of political brand strength are also
methodologically problematic, given documented
concerns about inauthentic amplification (Pal et al., 2019;
Udupa, 2019). This paper acknowledges these
measurement limitations and resists conflating platform-
level activity metrics with demonstrated voter attitude
change, which would require survey or experimental
evidence not available in a case study design.
This study examined Modi’s disintermediated political
communication strategy primarily through the lens of political
brand equity, and the findings confirm its effectiveness in building
durable brand salience, perceived authenticity, and voter loyalty
across three electoral cycles. However, effectiveness as a branding
outcome is analytically distinct from desirability as a democratic
practice. The same communicative mechanisms that generate
brand equity, repetition without adversarial challenge, emotional
resonance without deliberative engagement, parasocial intimacy
without journalistic accountability also carry risks for the health of
democratic institutions (Curran, 2011; McNair, 2017). This study
does not adjudicate between these readings; it is a study in political
marketing, not normative democratic theory. Nevertheless, future
research should integrate both dimensions and examine whether
the brand equity gains associated with disintermediated political
communication come at measurable costs to media pluralism,
political trust, and minority political inclusion.
