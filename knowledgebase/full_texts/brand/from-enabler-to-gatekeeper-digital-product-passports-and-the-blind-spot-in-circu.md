1 Introduction
When the World Trade Organization (WTO) Committee on Technical Barriers to

Thatjuxtapositionisthestartingpointofthisarticle.
The passport is the centerpiece of the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation
(ESPR), the framework instrument of the EU’s circular economy agenda, which replaced
the earlier Ecodesign Directive and extended its logic from energy-related products
to almost all goods placed on the Union market (Regulation(EU), 2024/1781). It will
carry the material and lifecycle data on which reuse, repair and recycling decisions
depend, and textiles are the ﬁrst sector in line. The ﬁrst content speciﬁcation for textile
apparel, issued by the Joint Research Center (JRC) in May 2026, sets out 49 data points
across a tiered reporting structure, ahead of a delegated act expected around 2027
(González-TorresandArcipowska,2026 );therevisedWasteFrameworkDirective,inforce

on the same trajectory (Directive(EU), 2025/1892). Because the obligation attaches to

globalized structure has steadily eroded supply-chain transparency, traceability is widely
identiﬁed as a prerequisite for any credible circular transition (Dasetal., 2025 ). The
case for the passport, in these terms, is strong. Yet the scholarship on passports has

2 The digital product passport as a
condition of market access
In its own terms the passport is an enabler of circularity:
the data carrier attached to the product is to make material
composition and lifecycle attributes legible to the recyclers,
repairers, regulators and consumers who handle it after the point
ofsale[ Regulation(EU),2024/1781 ].Thereislittlereasontodoubt
thatthisisitspurpose.
Its legal form, however, is that of a market-access condition.
The obligation attaches to the placing of a product on the Union
market, so that a product without a compliant passport may not
lawfullybesold,anditappliesirrespectiveofwheretheproductwas
made [Regulation(EU), 2024/1781]. This is a shift in kind rather
thandegree.Traceabilityrequirementshavehistoricallyoperatedas
post-market obligations, veriﬁed through surveillance once goods

into a pre-market digital condition that must be satisﬁed before

TheCommissiondoesnottreatthisconsequenceasincidental.
It has acknowledged that the requirements will have signiﬁcant
implications for trading partners particularly developing ones
(EuropeanCommission,2025 ),sinceallgoodsplacedontheUnion
market, imports included, must comply, and it has committed to
assessingtheeﬀectsonthirdcountriesandtoattendingtotheneeds
of smaller operators in the design of speciﬁc requirements. These
are meaningful commitments, and the argument that follows does
notassumetheirabsence.
It is therefore worth stating plainly what is, and what is not,
contested here. The legitimacy of the environmental objective is
not in question, nor is the proposition that circularity depends on
reliable product information: a passport that no one can populate
serves no one. What is at issue is narrower and concerns the
instrument’s design, the granularity of what must be reported, the
sequence in which obligations take eﬀect, and the distribution of
theresultingcostacrossavaluechainwhoseupstreamparticipants
had no part in setting the requirement. It is to that mechanism of
distributionthatthearticlenowturns.
3 The regulatory cascade: from public
obligation to private condition
The ESPR allocates the DPP obligation to the economic

single value chain, between parties of markedly unequal capacity.

Three consequences follow, and they are analytically distinct
from the familiar observation that regulation imposes compliance
costs. First, the incidence of the measure diverges from its
formal allocation: the obligation is European, the expenditure is
largely extra-European, and assessments of regulatory burden that
examine the duty-holder will not detect the displacement. Second,
exclusion operates on a diﬀerent timetable from the legislation.

period before application; contractual conditions, by contrast, are
imposed as soon as buyers begin de-risking their own future

no state decision is taken at the moment of exclusion—a purchase
orderisnotatechnicalregulation.
These eﬀects matter for circularity, and not only for
equity. A passport is worth precisely as much as the data it
carries: recyclability scores, recycled content claims and substance
declarations are the inputs on which downstream sorting, reuse

4 A blind spot in trade law
The argument of this article is not that the DPP violates WTO
law. It is that the measure may well be consistent with it, and that
thisconsistencyispreciselywhatshouldgivepause.
The relevant disciplines are those of the Agreement on
Technical Barriers to Trade (WTO, 1995). A threshold point
should be made explicit, since the analysis assumes it. The
passport is capable of qualifying as a technical regulation within
the meaning of Annex 1.1 of the TBT Agreement: it applies to
an identiﬁable product, it lays down product characteristics and
related administrative provisions, and compliance is mandatory—
the threeelements the AppellateBody drew from thatdeﬁnition in
EC—Asbestos (WTO, 2001) and EC—Sardines (WTO, 2002). The
deﬁnition expressly extends to marking and labeling requirements,
which the data carrier aﬃxed to the product supplies. On that
footingthedisciplinesofArticle2areengaged.
Article 2.2 requires that technical regulations be no
more trade-restrictive than necessary to fulﬁll a legitimate
objective. Environmental protection and resource eﬃciency are
unambiguously legitimate, and the passport’s contribution to
them is plausible; the contestable element is therefore narrower
than the speciﬁcation as a whole. Where granularity is set at
model level, the reporting burden is largely absorbed at the design
stage. The necessity question attaches to the batch-level subset,
and speciﬁcally to whether chain-of-custody certiﬁcation and
per-batchchemicalreportingcontributetothecircularityobjective
by a margin that justiﬁes pushing veriﬁcation into upstream
operations whose readiness the JRC’s own assessment records as
low. Necessity analysis under Article 2.2 is capable of engaging
that question; it has not yet been asked. That analysis is relational:
it weighs the measure’s contribution to its objective against its
trade-restrictiveness and against the gravity of the consequences of
non-fulﬁllment and asks whether a reasonably available alternative
would make an equivalent contribution while restricting trade
less (US—Tuna II,WTO, 2012; EC—Seal Products,WTO, 2014).
Whether a less trade-restrictive alternative is reasonably available
is the question on which such an analysis would turn, and several
candidates present themselves: a compact core of data points with
the more demanding elements phased in, model-level rather than
batch-level granularity, longer transition periods diﬀerentiated
under Article 12.3, and recognition of equivalent third-country
schemes and of conformity assessment carried out abroad under
Article 6.1. Each would have to be shown to contribute to the
objective to an equivalent degree; that demonstration has not been
attempted,butneitherhasitsimpossibility.
Article 2.4 raises a further issue that is analytically open.
Members are required to use relevant international standards as a
basis for technical regulations, and Article 2.5 attaches a rebuttable
presumptionofconsistencytoregulationsthatdoso.Thetechnical
architectureofthepassportisbeingﬁxedthroughCEN/CENELEC

5 Discussion
The transition to a circular economy has been described as
an institutional lag: trade systems built for linear ﬂows have
not adapted to remanufactured goods, secondary raw materials
and reused components (TuerkandSporysheva, 2022). The digital
product passport is often presented as a corrective to that lag,
an instrument that ﬁnally makes circular attributes legible across
borders. The preceding analysis suggests a more qualiﬁed reading.
The passport does not merely encounter an unadapted trade
system; it introduces a new mechanism of its own, in which a
public obligation is transmitted down the value chain as a private
condition, and it does so in a form that the disciplines governing
the obligation cannot see. The contribution here is to name that
mechanism and to locate the point at which trade law loses sight
ofit.
This reframes, rather than contradicts, the existing literature.
Work on the legal and institutional consequences of passports
has noted that their design may reinforce asymmetries among
producers, regulators and consumers, while calling for closer
attentiontotheintersectionwithtraderules( Yuan,2025).Analysis
of cross-jurisdictional traceability has addressed conﬂicts between
comparable regulatory systems and argued for mutual recognition
over centralization (HuangandNottage, 2026 ). The cascade sits
beneath both: it concerns not the relation between regulators but
the transmission of a single regulator’s requirement to parties who
are not its addressees and who have no standing in its design. It is,
in this sense, the intra-chain counterpart to the inter-jurisdictional
problemothershavedescribed.

If the diﬃculty is one of categorization rather than intent, the
response is a matter of design, and four directions follow. The
ﬁrst is to extend the graduation the JRC has already proposed.
Its tiering diﬀerentiates by data type; what it does not do is
diﬀerentiate by the capacity of the actor required to produce the
data. A phase-in for the batch-level subset, keyed to supplier size
and country status under Article 12.3 of the TBT Agreement,
would carry the same logic one step further. The second is mutual
recognition of conformity assessment with accreditation of local
veriﬁers, so that veriﬁcation need not be routed through European
service providers—the channel through which much of the cost
is presently incurred (WTO, 2026a,b). This runs through the
Union’s accreditation infrastructure and the existing ILAC and
IAF arrangements, with Article 6.1 inviting it; the trade-oﬀ is the
trust and enforceability that recognition demands where veriﬁer
capacity is uneven. The third concerns capacity support, which
the Commission has promised through technology transfer and
capacity building (EuropeanCommission, 2022) but which runs
into the territorial limit of Article 22. Extending it would mean
attaching passport readiness to the Union’s external trade and
development instruments rather than to development assistance
alone; Article 11 of the TBT Agreement is the unused legal hook,
and the trade-oﬀ is additionality and the risk of aid conditionality.
The fourth is a multilateral, interoperable minimum data model
developed through the World Customs Organization and the
InternationalOrganizationforStandardizationratherthanthrough
regional standardization alone (Gupta, 2024). These bodies are
chosendeliberately:theWCOgovernsthecustomsdatamodeland
stands at the border where the passport would in fact be checked,
and ISO, unlike CEN/CENELEC, is an international standardizing
body whose deliverables could attract the Article 2.5 presumption,
with ISO/TC 323 already working on circularity data (ISO, 2025).
The trade-oﬀ is the slower pace of consensus standard-setting
against the speed of the EU’s regulatory calendar, and the risk
that European deliverables establish a de facto lock-in before any

environmental objective, and none disputes that traceability is a
precondition of circularity: circular decisions cannot be made on
data that does not exist. They ask instead that the instrument be
builtsothattheactorsonwhomitdependsareabletocomply,and
that its informational value not be quietly eroded at the point of
collection. Trade policy, if strategically reformed, can shift from a

who pays for circular transparency, and who is excluded when
