Co-Branding Marketing Strategies: Audience Expansion,
Partner Selection, Brand Awareness, and Consumer Trust
Shuyi Chen
China Agricultural University, Beijing, China
Abstract. This study examines co-branding as a communication strategy for expanding audiences,
selecting partners, increasing brand awareness, and building consumer trust. Using a comparative
qualitative case study of Luckin Coffee and Duolingo, LV and Supreme, Gucci's luxury collaborations,
and Perfect Diary, the paper analyzes how brands construct perceived fit, invite consumer
participation, and convert attention into long-term brand influence. The findings show that successful
co-branding depends less on product similarity than on image compatibility, audience
complementarity, coherent storytelling, and credible product experience. Positive cases use humor,
scarcity, cultural status, and offline or digital participation to make consumers understand the
partnership. Riskier cases show that repeated or superficial collaborations may create visibility while
weakening brand equity. The paper argues that co -branding should be evaluated as a
communication sequence from partner selection to perceived fit, awareness, participation, trust, and
brand equity.
Keywords: Co-branding, brand awareness , partner selection , consumer trust , brand equity ,
audience expansion, perceived fit, consumer participation.
1. Introduction
A successful co -branding campaign can make consumers notice a brand they might otherwise
ignore, but attention alone does not explain why some collaborations strengthen a brand while others
create confusion or weaken credibility. When two brands collaborat e, they do more than share a
product, logo, or promotional event. They share audiences, cultural meanings, reputations, and risk.
This makes co -branding especially important in a market shaped by social media circulation,
fragmented consumer communities, l imited-edition products, and public participation. A luxury
brand can borrow streetwear energy, a beauty brand can borrow an entertainment intellectual property,
and a coffee chain can borrow the humor and personality of a language -learning app. These
partnerships can make brands more visible and culturally relevant, but they can also confuse
consumers when the partners appear mismatched or when the collaboration feels superficial.
The Luckin Coffee and Duolingo campaign shows the positive potential of this strategy. The two
brands had little functional connection, yet their collaboration became meaningful because the
campaign turned the partnership into a public story. It used a wedding-themed narrative, playful social
media dialogue, pop -up check-in spaces, limited -edition products, and the recognizable Duolingo
mascot to invite consumers into a shareable event. Luckin  gained a fresher digital personality and
access to young consumers who follow playful online culture. Duolingo moved from an app -based
identity into offline consumer life. This case matters because it shows that co -branding does not
always require product  similarity. It can also work through emotional resonance, humor, audience
overlap, and visible participation.
At the same time, this paper is not only about Luckin and Duolingo. That campaign is one positive
example within a broader communication problem: how do brands use co -branding to reach more
audience groups, choose suitable partners, expand awareness, and build consumer trust? Other cases
show different parts of this problem. LV and Supreme illustrate how a luxury brand can reach younger
consumers through a culturally powerful partner. Gucci's collaborations with Balenciaga and Adidas
show how co-branding can increase revenue while also creating risks of brand dilution. Perfect Diary
shows how frequent or shallow co-branding can increase exposure but weaken long-term brand equity
when the product experience does not support the campaign promise. These cases suggest that co -
branding succeeds only when attention, fit, quality, and trust work together.

This study argues that effective co -branding expands brand audiences and strengthens brand
influence when partners are selected for image compatibility, audience complementarity, and shared
emotional value, not novelty alone. The analysis further shows that co-branding should be understood
as a communication process that moves through four connected stages: constructing perceived fit,
inviting consumer participation, converting participation into awareness, and supporting that
awareness with trust. When the se stages align, co-branding can help brands enter new communities
without losing credibility. When they do not align, collaboration may produce short -term visibility
while weakening the meaning and equity of the original brand. To develop this argument, t his paper
uses a comparative qualitative case study of Luckin Coffee and Duolingo, LV and Supreme, Gucci's
luxury collaborations, and Perfect Diary's co-branding practices.
2. Literature Review
The first issue in co-branding research is partner selection. Scholars show that a brand should not
choose a partner only because the partner is famous. Paydas Turan's meta-analysis [1] finds that brand
image fit is more important than individual brand traits or product fit. This finding changes how co -
branding should be understood. A collaboration does not have to connect two similar products, but it
must conne ct two compatible meanings. Consumers need to understand why the partners belong
together at the level of image, personality, value, or lifestyle. This explains why the Luckin  Coffee
and Duolingo campaign can be treated as a positive case. The brands do not share a product category,
but the campaign created image fit through humor, youth culture, a playful wedding narrative, and a
shared invitation to participate online.
Kania et al. [2] also emphasize fit by showing that brand fit mediates the relationship between
consumer attitudes toward the original brands and attitudes toward the co -branded product. Their
findings suggest that co-branding works through transfer. If consumers already like one partner, that
positive feeling can transfer to the collaboration and possibly to the other partner, but only when the
partnership appears coherent. This helps explain how co-branding can touch new audience groups. A
brand can use t he partner's audience as a bridge, but the bridge must feel trustworthy. If the
partnership looks random, consumers may notice it without accepting it. Therefore, the best co -
branding partner is not simply the brand with the biggest audience. It is the bra nd whose image can
connect with the original brand's desired audience in a believable way.
Audience expansion is one of the clearest benefits of co-branding. Jia and Ran [3] analyze the LV
and Supreme collaboration and show how a luxury brand used a streetwear partner to attract young
consumers, expand markets, and increase brand influence. This case shows that co-branding can help
an established brand enter a community that might otherwise see it as distant or old -fashioned.
Supreme supplied youth culture, street credibility, and scarcity, while LV supplied luxury status and
heritage. The result  was not only a product collaboration but also an audience exchange. For this
paper, LV and Supreme provide an important example of how partner selection can help brands reach
consumers outside their usual group.
However, audience expansion becomes weak when a collaboration relies only on surface attention.
Jia and Ran [3] note that the LV and Supreme cooperation remained somewhat superficial, and this
limitation points to a broader risk in co-branding. Cai [4] argues that successful co-branding requires
quality, pricing, inventory planning, topicality, and attention to consumer psychology. This means
that a campaign must connect promotional excitement to a satisfying consumer experience. Limited
products and social  media buzz can attract consumers quickly, but poor planning can create
disappointment. A collaboration that sells out may look successful, but if consumers feel manipulated
by scarcity or disappointed by product quality, the campaign may damage trust.
The literature also shows how brands use limited editions, fan culture, and social participation to
increase awareness. Wang and Meng [5] explain that fashion co -branding often relies on limited -
edition hunger marketing and fan economy to generate sales an d attention. Scarcity encourages
consumers to act quickly, and fan identity encourages them to share that action publicly. This logic

is visible in many modern collaborations. Luckin and Duolingo used limited-edition products, themed
stores, and social media interaction to make consumers feel that participation itself was part of the
product. Consumers did not only buy a drink or a cup. They joined a public joke, took photos,
commented on brand posts, and helped the campaign circulate.
Negative and risky cases are equally important because they show what co-branding should avoid.
Lu [6] argues that Perfect Diary's co -branding increased exposure but damaged long -term brand
equity because some collaborations were shallow, insufficiently in novative, and not supported by
strong product quality. This case shows that attention can become a trap. If a brand collaborates too
often or uses partners only as decorative IPs, consumers may begin to see the brand as dependent on
borrowed attention. Bai [7] makes a similar point in the luxury context. Gucci's collaborations with
Balenciaga and Adidas increased revenue, but they also created risks of brand dilution, negative
spillover, and consumer dissatisfaction. These studies show that co-branding must protect the original
brand's meaning while adding new value.
Trust is the final standard for evaluating co -branding. Basiya et al. [8] find that co -branding can
improve customer-based brand equity, which then supports repurchase intention and loyalty. This
finding is important because it connects collaboration to lo ng-term consumer relationships. A
campaign is not truly successful if it only produces one round of attention. It must also make
consumers more willing to remember, choose, and trust the brand later. Sriwomatu  [9] extends this
argument in the digital landscape by showing that co -branding can create consumer value and trust
when the partners are strategically compatible. Digital platforms matter because they allow
consumers to respond publicly, evaluate the part nership together, and test whether the brand's tone
feels authentic.
Financial and market outcomes also matter, but they should be interpreted as part of a larger
communication process. Fakhravar  and Tahami [10] argue that co -branding announcements can
generate positive financial responses and that integration, exclusivity, and longer duration can
strengthen performance. This suggests that the market reads co -branding as a signal of strategic
confidence. Yet financial success cannot explain the entire process. A collaboration may improve
short-term sales while weakening brand meaning. For that reason, this project treats co-branding as a
communication strategy that must be judged through several co nnected outcomes: audience reach,
perceived fit, participation, awareness, trust, and brand equity.
Together, the literature suggests a clear argument for this research. Effective co-branding depends
on four connected strategies. First, brands must choose partners with image compatibility and
audience complementarity. Second, they must communicate the pa rtnership through a story or
experience that consumers can understand. Third, they must use scarcity, fan culture, and social media
participation to expand awareness without making the campaign feel empty. Fourth, they must protect
consumer trust by suppor ting the campaign with quality, coherence, and long -term brand value.
Existing research provides concepts for studying these issues, but more work is needed to compare
positive and risky cases across different industries. By placing Luckin and Duolingo beside LV and
Supreme, Gucci, and Perfect Diary, this study explains not only why co -branding attracts attention,
but when that attention becomes meaningful brand influence.
3. Method
This study uses a comparative qualitative case study method supported by descriptive secondary
evidence from campaign materials, press coverage, social media content, and published case research.
This design follows Yin's view [11] that case study research  is useful for explaining how a
contemporary phenomenon works in its real-life context, especially when the boundaries between the
campaign, the brand, and public response are not completely separate. The coding process also draws
on Braun and Clarke's thematic analysis [12] and Saldana's approach [13] to qualitative coding. These
sources shape the method used here: I first identify repeated themes inside each case, then compare
those themes across cases to explain larger patterns in co-branding communication.

The data sources were limited to publicly available materials. For Luckin Coffee and Duolingo,
the analysis focused on official campaign posts and announcements, limited-edition product images,
wedding-themed campaign materials, mascot use, themed store or pop-up check-in content, consumer
comments and reposts on social media, and business or marketing news coverage describing the
campaign's public response. For LV and Supreme, the materials included official collaboration
announcements, product -release and  lookbook materials, fashion media coverage, discussion of
scarcity and drop culture, and scholarly case analysis of audience expansion. For Gucci's
collaborations with Balenciaga and Adidas, the materials included official collection materials,
fashion pr ess coverage, revenue or performance discussion reported in secondary sources, and
scholarly analysis of dilution and negative spillover risk. For Perfect Diary, the materials included
examples of IP -based co -branding, product and packaging images, brand o r campaign posts,
consumer commentary discussed in secondary sources, and academic analysis of brand equity
problems.
The study examined four co-branding cases that represent different outcomes. Luckin Coffee and
Duolingo served as a positive non-complementary digital-retail case because the campaign connected
two unrelated product categories through humor, story, and participation. LV and Supreme served as
an audience -expansion case because it connected luxury heritage with streetwear credibility and
youth culture. Gucci's collaborations with Balenciaga and Adidas served as high -visibility luxury
cases that show both financial opportunity and brand dilution risk. Perfect Diary served as a warning
case because its repeated co -branding practices increased exposure while raising questions about
product quality, innovation, and long-term brand equity.
The coding process was guided by three research questions. RQ1 asked how brands choose co -
branding partners and construct perceived fit. Codes included image compatibility, product fit,
audience overlap, audience complementarity, shared values, cultural re levance, and narrative
coherence. RQ2 asked how co -branding campaigns expand awareness and reach new consumer
groups. Codes included limited editions, fan participation, social media interaction, offline events,
influencer or mascot use, scarcity, consumer  sharing, and press visibility. RQ3 asked how co -
branding affects trust and brand equity. Codes included credibility, product quality, consumer
satisfaction, brand dilution, negative spillover, loyalty, repurchase intention, and long -term brand
meaning.
Each case was first coded individually and then compared across the full set of cases. Descriptive
quantitative evidence, such as reported sales effects, revenue discussion, engagement numbers,
limited-edition availability, and public visibility, was used only as supporting context rather than as
original statistical data. The purpose of coding was not to produce a complete measurement of all
consumer responses. Instead, the coding identified repeated communication patterns across cases and
connected those patterns to the academic concepts in the literature review. This means that the results
should be understood as interpretive findings about co-branding communication. The method shows
how campaigns construct meaning, invite participation, and create or dam age trust, while also
recognizing that future research could strengthen this analysis through interviews, surveys, complete
platform analytics, or direct sales data.
4. Results
The coding of campaign materials, public commentary, press coverage, scholarly case research,
and available descriptive indicators produced four major findings. First, perceived fit was more
important than product similarity. Second, audience expansion wor ked best when the partner
functioned as a believable bridge into a new consumer community. Third, awareness increased most
strongly when campaigns gave consumers a clear participation script, such as buying limited products,
sharing social media content, v isiting offline spaces, or joining fan -based conversation. Fourth,
consumer trust depended on whether the collaboration strengthened brand meaning after the initial
attention. These findings show that co -branding is not a single act of joining two names. I t is a

sequence of communication choices that begins with partner selection and ends with consumer

The first result was that perceived fit could be constructed through cultural and emotional meaning
even when product fit was weak. Luckin Coffee and Duolingo provided the clearest example. Coffee
and language learning do not naturally belong to the same product category, but the campaign created
fit through a wedding story, humorous interaction, and the Duolingo mascot's recognizable digital
personality. The brands appeared connected because the campaign gave consumers a simple
explanation for the relation ship: two playful, youth -oriented brands were staging a public joke that
consumers could join. In the coding, this case showed strong scores for narrative coherence, cultural
relevance, mascot use, and social media interaction, even though product fit was low.
LV and Supreme showed a different form of fit. The product categories were closer because both
brands operate in fashion, but their symbolic identities were different. LV represented luxury heritage,
high status, and established craftsmanship. Supreme repr esented streetwear, youth culture, scarcity,
and subcultural credibility. The coding showed that the partnership worked because the difference
between the brands was not random. It created audience complementarity. Supreme helped LV speak
to younger consumers who might see traditional luxury as distant, while LV gave Supreme access to
luxury legitimacy and global prestige. This finding supports the idea that strong co -branding often
depends on complementary difference rather than simple similarity.
The Gucci and Perfect Diary cases showed the limits of perceived fit. Gucci's collaborations with
Balenciaga and Adidas had strong visibility because the partner brands were recognizable and
culturally powerful, but the coding also identified brand dilution and negative spillover as important
risks. In luxury branding, repeated or highly visible collaboration can make a brand feel less exclusive
if consumers cannot clearly identify what the collaboration adds to the original brand's meaning.
Perfect Diary s howed a similar issue in a different market. Its co -branding practices generated
exposure, but the coding showed weaker results for innovation, product -supported credibility, and
long-term brand equity. In these cases, partner recognition created attention , but attention did not
automatically become trust.
The second result was that audience expansion occurred when brands used the partner's audience
as a bridge rather than as a borrowed crowd. Luckin used Duolingo's digital fan base and meme -
friendly personality to enter playful online culture. Duolingo used  Luckin's retail locations and
everyday consumption setting to move from an app -based experience into offline life. LV used
Supreme to enter a youth streetwear community that valued scarcity and cultural insider status. These
cases show that audience expansion work s when consumers can understand why the partner's
audience should care about the collaboration. The bridge must feel meaningful. If a brand only
borrows a partner's popularity, consumers may notice the collaboration but not accept the relationship.
The third result was that awareness depended on participation. The strongest cases did not only
announce a partnership. They gave consumers something to do. Luckin and Duolingo created limited-
edition products, themed stores, photo opportunities, and socia l media dialogue. These elements
transformed the campaign from an advertisement into a public event. LV and Supreme used scarcity,
limited availability, and the cultural status of streetwear drops to create urgency and public
conversation. In both cases, consumers became part of the circulation system. They bought, posted,
commented, visited, photographed, and shared. The coding therefore showed that brand awareness
grew through consumer performance, not only through brand messaging.
However, the results also showed that participation can become shallow when it is disconnected
from product quality or brand purpose. Perfect Diary illustrates this problem most clearly. Frequent
co-branding can train consumers to expect novelty, but it ca n also make the original brand seem
dependent on external images. If the product experience does not deepen the campaign meaning, the
collaboration becomes decorative. Gucci's luxury collaborations raise a related issue. High -profile
partnerships can create excitement and sales, but they can also create consumer dissatisfaction if the
collaboration weakens the perceived uniqueness of the brand. In both cases, the coding showed a gap
between visibility and credibility.

5. Discussion
These results support Paydas  Turan's argument [1] that brand image fit is more important than
product fit, but they also extend that argument by showing how image fit can be actively performed.
Luckin and Duolingo did not begin with an obvious product relationship. The campaign had to create
the relationship through narrative, humor, and consumer participation. This suggests that image fit is
not only a pre-existing quality that brands either have or d o not have. In digital co -branding, image
fit can be produced through communication when the campaign gives consumers a coherent story
and a recognizable emotional tone. The wedding narrative mattered because it made the partnership
legible. Consumers did not need the products to match because the campaign supplied a social scene
that explained the match.
The findings also support Kania et al.'s claim [2] that consumer attitudes can transfer across co -
branded products when the partnership appears coherent. Luckin and Duolingo created conditions for
positive transfer because each brand contributed a clear value to the collaboration. Luckin contributed
everyday retail access, speed, and a popular consumption setting. Duolingo contributed a distinctive
mascot, a humorous online voice, and a fan base familiar with playful brand communication. The
transfer was not automatic. It depended on the campaign's ability to make the two identities feel
mutually useful. This helps explain why co-branding can reach new audiences without requiring the
brands to become identical.
The audience-expansion cases show that co-branding is most powerful when difference is strategic.
LV and Supreme worked because the brands were different in ways that created new value. LV did
not simply use Supreme as decoration. It used Supreme's cultura l position to access a younger and
more streetwear-oriented audience. Supreme did not simply borrow LV's logo. It used LV's luxury
status to raise the symbolic value of the collaboration. This aligns with Jia and Ran's analysis [3] of
the collaboration's market-expansion function. At the same time, the case also reveals a limit. If the
collaboration remains only a surface combination of recognizable symbols, it may generate hype
without producing deeper brand development.
This distinction between attention and development is important because many co -branding
campaigns appear successful at the level of visibility. A limited drop sells out, a hashtag circulates,
or consumers take photos at a themed location. These outcomes matter, but they do not prove that the
collaboration strengthened the brand. Wang and Meng's discussion [5] of limited -edition hunger

The Perfect Diary case demonstrates the danger of confusing repeated visibility with brand equity.
Lu [6] argues that Perfect Diary's co -branding increased exposure while damaging long -term brand
equity because some collaborations were shallow and insufficiently supported by product innovation.
The coding in this study supports that conclusion. Perfect Diary's problem was not that co -branding
itself is harmful. The problem was that the brand risked making collaboration feel like a substitute
for product str ength. When consumers see too many partnerships that function mainly as visual
decorations, they may begin to question the original brand's independent value. This is why co -
branding needs to create new meaning rather than only attach borrowed cultural sym bols to existing
products.
The Gucci cases show that this risk also applies to luxury brands. Bai [7] notes that Gucci's
collaborations with Balenciaga and Adidas increased revenue while creating risks of brand dilution,
negative spillover, and dissatisfaction. Luxury brands depend heavily on symbolic control, heritage,
and exclusivity. Co -branding can refresh these meanings, but it can also blur them. If consumers
cannot distinguish collaboration from overextension, the brand may gain short -term conversation
while weakening its long-term identity. This does not mean luxury brands should avoid collaboration.
It means that they must protect the central meaning of the brand while using partners to open new
cultural possibilities.
Trust therefore becomes the key difference between successful co -branding and temporary hype.
Basiya et al. [8] connect co -branding to customer -based brand equity, repurchase intention, and
loyalty. Sriwomatu [9] similarly emphasizes consumer value and tru st in digital co -branding
partnerships. The present analysis supports these arguments because the stronger cases offered
consumers more than novelty. Luckin and Duolingo offered a humorous and participatory experience
that fit both brands' public personalities. LV and Supreme offered a culturally meaningful exchange
between luxury and streetwear. The weaker or riskier cases showed that attention becomes fragi le
when it is not supported by product credibility, innovation, and clear brand meaning.
This study's main contribution is the idea that co-branding should be evaluated as a communication
sequence. Many discussions of co -branding focus on whether the two brands fit, whether the
campaign gained attention, or whether sales increased. Those quest ions are useful, but they are
incomplete when separated from one another. The cases in this study show that co -branding works
through a sequence: partner selection constructs the possibility of fit; campaign storytelling makes
that fit visible; consumer pa rticipation expands awareness; and product experience determines
whether awareness becomes trust. A campaign can fail at any stage. A famous partner cannot fix
weak coherence. A viral moment cannot fix poor quality. A sold-out product cannot guarantee loyalty.
This sequence also clarifies why co-branding is a communication issue rather than only a business
tactic. Co -branding organizes how consumers interpret relationships between brands. It asks
consumers to believe that two identities can share a temporary wor ld. That world may be playful,
exclusive, fashionable, luxurious, or fandom -driven, but it must be understandable. The most
effective campaigns give consumers a clear role inside that world. The role may be to participate in
a joke, join a drop, visit a pop-up, display taste, or share a fan identity. When consumers understand
the role and the product supports the promise, the campaign can expand awareness and strengthen
trust. When the role is unclear or the product feels weak, the collaboration becomes noise.
For brand managers, the implication is that partner selection should begin with meaning rather
than fame. The best partner is not always the largest or most surprising partner. It is the partner whose
audience, personality, and cultural position can help t he brand communicate a believable extension
of itself. Brands also need to plan the consumer experience after the initial announcement. A
collaboration should answer practical communication questions: What story explains the partnership?

What action can consumers take? What value does the product itself provide? How will the
collaboration protect the original brand's identity? These questions matter because consumers judge
co-branding publicly and collectively, especially on digital platfo rms where praise, criticism, and
confusion can circulate quickly.
The study also suggests directions for future research. Because this project relied on public
campaign materials, press coverage, scholarly case evidence, and secondary descriptive indicators
rather than original interviews, complete platform analytics, or direct sales data, future studies could
measure consumer interpretation more directly. Researchers could compare how different generations
respond to playful or meme-based collaborations, or how cultural context changes the meaning of fit.
Future research could also study failed co -branding campaigns more closely, because failure may
reveal the boundaries of authenticity, scarcity, and trust more clearly than success. These questions
would help communication researchers understand how consumers decide whet her a collaboration
feels meaningful, manipulative, or forgettable.
6. Conclusion
This paper began with a question about how brands use co-branding to expand audiences, choose
suitable partners, increase awareness, and build consumer trust. The final analysis shows that
effective co -branding is not created by novelty alone. It is create d when a partnership moves
coherently from image fit to audience bridge, from audience bridge to participation, and from
participation to trust. Luckin Coffee and Duolingo demonstrate that even non-complementary brands
can create meaningful fit when they b uild a shared emotional scene that consumers understand. LV
and Supreme show how complementary difference can expand audiences when both brands
contribute clear cultural value. Gucci and Perfect Diary show that visibility can become risky when
collaboration weakens brand identity or is not supported by product quality.
The importance of this research is that it offers a more complex way to judge co -branding
campaigns. Instead of asking only whether a collaboration gained attention, researchers and brand
managers should ask what kind of attention it created and what remai ned after the campaign ended.
Co-branding is meaningful when it helps consumers see a brand differently while still trusting what
the brand stands for. Future co -branding research should continue to investigate the boundary
between authentic cultural participation and temporary promotional hype. For practitioners, the lesson
is direct: choose partners for coherent meaning, design participation with a clear consumer role, and
protect trust after the campaign gains attention. A collaboration succeeds when con sumers do not
only remember that two brands appeared together, but understand why the relationship mattered.
