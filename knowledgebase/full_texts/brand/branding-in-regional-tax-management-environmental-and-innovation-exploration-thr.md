The reputation and effectiveness of regional tax management systems significantly influence
local investment climates and public trust in governance. This study investigates the perspectives,
roles, and expectations of key stakeholders in shaping a credible and legitimate regional tax
environment in Indonesia. Situated within the broader discourse on decentralised governance,
public engagement, and management control systems, this research employs stakeholder
typology theory, centred on power, legitimacy, and urgency, alongside social network theory to
analyse how policy actors interact across different stages of tax regulation. Drawing on qualitative
data from in-depth interviews and document analysis in three Indonesian cities, South Tangerang,
Payakumbuh, and Yogyakarta, the study examines the implementation of tax enforcement
mechanisms under Government Regulation No. 55 of 2016 and Law No. 19 of 1997 concerning
the issuance of coercion warrants. Findings reveal that the Regional Revenue Agency serves
as a central institution, reinforced by political support from the City Regent, institutionalised
procedures, and digital infrastructures. Crucially, the study identifies management control systems,
including administrative oversight, digital transformation, outreach initiatives, and innovation, as
vital to maximising local tax revenues. Stakeholder engagement emerges as a key determinant
of regulatory legitimacy and policy effectiveness. This research contributes a novel, integrative
perspective by linking stakeholder dynamics and management control frameworks within local
taxation reform, an area that remains underexplored in the public policy literature. It advances
our understanding of how local governments and non-state actors collaborate within evolving
governance networks to strengthen the credibility, transparency, and performance of regional tax
systems.

Research Context
The decentralisation of public authority in Indone -
sia has fundamentally transformed the role of regional
governments, positioning them as central actors in
the pursuit of locally driven development. As fiscal re-
sponsibility shifts from the central government to the
regions, local governments are now tasked not only
administering public services but also with mobilising
their own-source revenue (Pendapatan Asli Daerah,
PAD). Among the primary contributors to PAD is re-

frameworks and tax policy instruments, local tax reve-
nues often fall short of their potential due to enduring
challenges in enforcement, compliance, and institutio-
nal capacity.
In this context, management control systems in
regional taxation have emerged as critical, yet under -
-researched, tools for aligning administrative practice
with policy goals. Such systems incorporate both for -
mal mechanisms such as audit, performance measure-
ment, and coercion warrants and informal norms, ro-
utines, and stakeholder interactions that influence the
behaviour of both tax authorities and taxpayers. Felis
and Rosłaniec (2019) claim that the efficiency of local
taxes is not just a function of regulatory frameworks
but also of the perceived legitimacy and accountabili -
ty of implementing authorities. It follows that under -
standing how local governments operate management
control systems is critical to determining the channels
through which tax revenue can be optimised.
The Indonesian government’s efforts to enhance
regional taxation through initiatives like Government
Regulation No. 55 of 2016 and amendments to Law No.
19 of 1997 indicate a commitment to reform; however,
the execution of these measures exposes ongoing di -
screpancies between official adherence and actual en -
forcement. Van Rooij (2016) underscores this discon-
nect in regulation, emphasising that formal guidelines
frequently do not consider the practical circumstances
faced by both tax officials and taxpayers. Enforcement
effectiveness in most cases depends not so much on
legal sanction but on strategic communication, stake -
holder trust, and credibility of institutional routines.
These are precisely the areas where management con -
trol systems play a determining role.
At a time, characterised by heightened competition
among urban areas for investment and legitimacy,
subnational governments need not only to ensure tax
compliance but also to show competence, transparen-
cy, and equity in doing so. Brezzi et al. (2021) empha -
sise that stakeholder participation and trust between
citizens and their governments are crucial for the
legitimacy and functioning of democracies. Y et as
Alemanno (2015) warns, stakeholder involvement in
public policy tends to degenerate into token consulta-
tion instead of being integrated as a strategic, iterative
process. Successful stakeholder involvement involves
strong feedback channels, intersectoral collaboration,
and role clarification, factors that can be embedded
through effective management control systems.
Despite growing interest in participatory fiscal
governance, empirical research on how local govern -
ments in Indonesia develop and apply a regional tax
control systems remains sparse. Most existing studies
have either focused narrowly on technical perfor -
mance metrics or treated regional tax offices merely
as rule enforcers. For example, earlier works by Che -
ung (2009), Sinaga (2011), Ahmad and Putri (2025),
and Lohonauman (2016) concentrated on local own
revenue (PAD) growth following tax decentralisation,
while more recent studies such as Astini et al. (2024)
examine taxpayer behaviour in Land and Building
Tax. These accounts often overlook the relational and
institutional processes through which tax legitimacy is
cultivated and sustained.
This study addresses this gap by advancing a more
integrated perspective, one that positions management
control systems as both technical and relational tools
for enhancing tax performance. Drawing from Van’s
(2016) call to explore the lived practices of regulators,
and Felis and Gołębiowski’s (2021) argument for ali -
gning fiscal efficiency with citizen participation, this
research foregrounds the strategic use of a regional tax
control systems as a means of achieving revenue opti -
misation. It posits that tax collection outcomes are not
merely a function of statutory enforcement but also of
how well local authorities engage stakeholders, moni -
tor performance, and adapt practices in real time.
By investigating the implementation of local tax
enforcement in South Tangerang, Payakumbuh, and
Y ogyakarta, this study interrogates how local gover -
nments deploy control systems in conjunction with
stakeholder engagement strategies. In doing so, it
builds upon and extends the theoretical frameworks of
stakeholder typology (Mitchell et al., 1997) and social
network theory to map the interdependencies, power
dynamics, and feedback loops that define regional
tax governance. The novelty of this research lies in its
conceptualisation of a regional tax control systems not
simply as administrative tools, but as dynamic gover -
nance systems that mediate between policy design, in-
stitutional performance, and stakeholder expectations.
Ultimately, this study contributes to the current
literature by introducing a more sophisticated model
of regional tax implementation that acknowledges the
interplay between formal regulatory structures and
informal stakeholder networks. In policy terms, it
provides practical guidance for policymakers and tax
administrators wanting to develop local taxation sys -
tems that are responsive, transparent, and effective at
raising revenue. Through exploring the crossroads of
participatory governance and management control,
this study seeks to reconfigure the regional tax control
systems as a fundamental component of sustainable

2. Literature Review
2.1. Decentralisation, Local Taxation, and
Management Control Systems
The discussion of decentralisation continues, with
increasing attention to whether its implementation
in Indonesia has been successful in achieving the in -
tended consequences of local empowerment and im -
proved delivery of public services. Although regional
autonomy has been in place for over two decades, its
success is still in question, particularly in the dimen -
sion of fiscal decentralisation. Among the concerns is
a persistent issue regarding the low capacity of sub-na-
tional governments in optimising own-source reve -
nues, primarily regional taxes, despite increased fiscal
responsibilities.
Jia et al. (2018) hold that tax incentive and enfor -
cement policy strategies can increase compliance, but
this is only effective under the condition of strong
institutional control systems in the background. This
highlights the value of management control systems
(MCS), as systematic processes, tools, and rituals im -
plemented by local/regional government to guide ac -
tivities toward achieving strategic goals. In tax admi -
nistration at the regional level, MCS entail regulatory
frameworks, administrative procedures, information
systems, human resource management, and inter-a -
gency coordination that collectively aim to improve
performance, transparency, and accountability.
However, most Indonesian local governments are
still disproportionately reliant on top-down enforce -
ment approaches. Regulation implementation largely
benefits tax authorities as sole implementers with mi -
nimal engagement of non-bureaucratic stakeholders
(Van Rooij, 2016; Alemanno, 2015). Overreliance on
formal power will marginalise the perspectives, roles,
and abilities of other stakeholders, primarily taxpayers,
entrepreneurs, and civil society, and thereby undermi-
ne regulatory legitimacy and long-term compliance.
To overcome these limitations, this research advocates
for a more holistic approach that incorporates MCS
alongside participatory governance arrangements. By
doing this, it explores the interaction of stakeholder
involvement and social networks with local tax control
systems and how they contribute to both regulatory
efficacy and revenue outcomes. This perspective ena -
bles a closer investigation of how a regional tax control
systems can be shaped and established not simply as
technocratic tools but as socially embedded processes.
The novelty of this research is its empirical explora-
tion of the operational dynamics of management con-
trol systems, under the general theoretical umbrellas
of stakeholder typology and social network analysis,
as they pertain to local tax ecosystems. This research
fills an important void in existing literature by explo -
ring the socio-political aspects of tax regulation and
enforcement, thereby providing fresh insights on how
regional governments can shape tax systems that are
more credible, transparent, and effective.
2.2. Stakeholder Engagement: A Foundation
for Participatory Governance
Within democratic and decentralised decision-ma -
king, stakeholder engagement is a widely accepted ele-
mentary and functional necessity. These mechanisms
increase trust, accountability, and democratic legiti -
macy by directly involving citizens in shaping policies
and public services (Özden, 2026). Alemanno (2015)
contends that engagement not only improves the em -
pirical basis of policymaking, commonly referred to as
input legitimacy, but also public acceptance and com-
pliance, thus underpinning output legitimacy.
In local tax administration, stakeholder engagement
is a critical way of encouraging voluntary compliance,
setting expectations, and recognising actual complian-
ce constraints. Alemanno (2015) identifies stakeholder
engagement as a three-stage approach of communica-
tion, consultation, and participation. Communication
is the transmission of information in one direction,
while consultation occurs through input from stake -
holders, although within set policy lines. Participation
is the most developed stage, enabling two-way interac-
tion where dialogue and joint policy outcomes can be
achieved.
This participatory design, when incorporated in
MCS, can foster more adaptive and responsive gover -
nance. For example, incorporating citizens’ input in
performance monitoring systems or involving local
businesses in tax outreach campaign design can help
improve the effectiveness and legitimacy of regional
tax programmes.

and Urgency
To discover how different actors influence and are
influenced by tax policy, this study applies the stake -
holder typology model of Mitchell et al. (1997). This
model defines three attributes that determine stake -
holder salience: power, or the ability to influence poli-

Within the context of regional tax governance, mu -

This study uses Mitchell et al. (1997) model to map
and prioritise stakeholders in local tax systems. This
helps to determine which stakeholders are most im -
portant to co-produce effective MCS and how their en-
gagement can enhance system resilience as well as the
effect of policies.
2.4. Social Network Theory: Mapping the
Interactions That Shape Tax Control
In addition to stakeholder categorisation, the rese -
arch uses social network theory to examine stakehol -
ders’ inclusion in, and impact on, wider governance
networks. Borgatti and Ofem (2010) argue that social
networks structure information, influence, and resour-
ce flows in a policy system. This enables the determina-
tion of central actors (who possess institutional or in -
formational power), bridging actors (who link various
groups), and peripheral actors (who, while marginali -
sed, also offer distinctive perspectives).
In local taxation, knowledge of such relational dy -
namics is important to MCS development in that, for
instance, cooperative networks among tax administra -
tors, information technology providers, town planning
departments, and local leaders have the potential to
enhance enforcement and facilitate data integration
(Hassard & Wolfram Cox, 2013). Fragmented or isola-
ted relationships, in contrast, are likely to lead to policy
incoherence, inefficiency, and enforcement loopholes.
Through a network analysis in relation to the applica-
tion of tax policies in three Indonesian municipalities,
South Tangerang, Payakumbuh, and Y ogyakarta, this
research highlights the significance of relational capi -
tal and inter-organisational trust as a way to guarantee
effective tax governance arrangements. Furthermore, it
adds to the current scholarly literature by showing that
the management control systems are not merely admi-
nistrative tools, but also social objects forged through
the continuous interactions of various actors.
2.5. Synthesis and Research Gap
Although theoretical frameworks for stakeholder
engagement and network analysis are well-established,
empirical studies integrating these perspectives within
the design and operation of local tax management con-
trol systems remain limited. Much of the existing lite -
rature either focuses on legal reform or technical capa-
city-building, neglecting the socio-political dimensions
that underpin successful tax policy implementation.
This study addresses this gap by offering a multi-the-
oretical and empirical framework for understanding
how management control systems are co-produced
through stakeholder engagement and network dyna -
mics. It situates MCS not merely as internal bureaucra-
tic functions but as embedded, negotiated systems sha-
ped by actors with varying degrees of power, legitimacy,
and urgency.
In doing so, the study contributes to the growing
body of research that reconceptualises local tax gover -
nance as a collaborative, adaptive, and participatory
process. It also aligns with Indonesia’s national rese -
arch priorities, particularly in promoting innovation,
transparency, and accountability in public financial
management.
3. Method
3.1. Research Design
This study adopts a qualitative methodology utilising
a multiple-case study design to explore the dynamics of
stakeholder engagement and management control sys-
tems (regional tax control systems) in the enforcement
of regional taxation in Indonesia. A type IV , multi-case
study design, as conceptualised by Baskarada (2014),
facilitates a comparative analysis across distinct go -
vernance contexts while capturing the depth and com-
plexity of tax policy enforcement and stakeholder in -
teraction within and across municipal administrations.
The choice of a qualitative approach is grounded in
the objective to uncover not merely the formal structu-
res of tax regulation, but the lived realities, interactions,
and embedded practices that shape the effectiveness of
local fiscal control. Case studies are particularly well -
-suited to examining “bounded systems” where context
and process are inextricable from the phenomenon un-
der investigation. In this research, the bounded system
is the local tax enforcement environment, where admi-
nistrative routines, legal instruments, and stakeholder
relationships coalesce to form a city’s regional tax con-
trol systems.

3.2. Research Sites
Three regional governments were purposively selec-
ted for this study: South Tangerang (Banten), Payakum-
buh (West Sumatra), and Y ogyakarta (Special Region
of Y ogyakarta). These cities have recently revised the -
ir regional taxation policies in line with Government
Regulation No. 55 of 2016 and Law No. 19 of 1997, as
amended by Law No. 19 of 2000, which authorise the
use of coercive instruments such as coercion warrants
for non-compliant taxpayers. The selection was guided
by theoretical sampling, enabling the investigation of
municipalities at varying levels of fiscal maturity and
institutional innovation (Wuetherick, 2010). These mu-
nicipalities serve as sites where novel practices in sta -
keholder engagement and internal control mechanisms
are emerging, providing fertile ground for theory-buil-
ding.
By selecting diverse regions, the study benchmarks
distinct approaches to tax regulation and control, there-
by enhancing the transferability of insights and facilita-
ting an analysis of how local governments institutiona-
lise a regional tax control systems to optimise revenue
collection. The value of case study research lies not in
statistical generalisability, but in its capacity to captu -
re the richness of social practice and to enable analytic
generalisation.
3.3. Data Collection
Primary data were collected through semi-structu -
red interviews with both internal and external stakehol-
ders involved in the formulation, enforcement, and mo-
nitoring of regional tax policies. Internal stakeholders
included representatives from the Regional Revenue
Agency (Badan Pendapatan Daerah, Bapenda), Legal
Departments, and Inspectorates in each municipality.
In South Tangerang, interviews were conducted with
Bapenda officials, the Legal Bureau, and the Inspecto -
rate. In Payakumbuh and Y ogyakarta, interviews were
held with tax managers and officials from both the Le -
gal Department and Bapenda.
External stakeholders comprised academic consul -
tants and regulatory advisors who were involved in
the legislative review or provided expertise during the
drafting and revision of regional tax regulations. This
approach reflects a commitment to theoretical sam -
pling, ensuring that respondents are directly involved
in, or possess deep knowledge of, the subject matter
under investigation.
In addition to interviews, this study drew upon do -
cumentary analysis to triangulate findings and enhance
contextual understanding. Documents included regio -
nal tax regulations, implementation guidelines, audit
reports, local government policy briefs, and publicly

3.4. Data analysis
Data analysis followed a two-stage, iterative process
combining deductive and inductive strategies. Initially,
a theoretically informed coding scheme was developed
based on existing literature on stakeholder engagement
(Mitchell et al., 1997), management control systems,
and regional fiscal governance (Van Rooij, 2016; Felis &
Gołębiowski, 2021). Codes were refined through pilot
analysis and were subsequently adjusted as empirical
data emerged from interviews and document review.
Secondly, an inductive approach grounded in con -
structivist grounded theory was employed to capture
emergent themes that were not anticipated at the outset
of the study. This dual approach, reminiscent of Eisen-
hardt’s (1989) model of theory-building from case stu-
dies, allowed for the dynamic interplay between theory
and data, ensuring both analytical rigour and empirical
richness.
Particular attention was given to how regional au -
thorities legislated and planned their regime of control
over regional taxation, and how these were established
in, and reinforced through, networks of stakeholders.
For instance, the role of internal audit units in enhan -
cing compliance, the use of inter-agency task forces for
issuing coercion warrants, and the involvement of civil
society organisations in public education campaigns
were examined not as discrete activities but as elements
of broader control systems. The results were then analy-
sed using the stakeholder network model to quantify
the congruence among formal authority, legitimacy,
and real influence (Saunders et al., 2009; Bryman &
Bell, 2011).
By doing so, the study develops a nuanced explora -
tion of how participation and control in the regional
taxation field intersect, an intersection that is not well
explored in the available literature, particularly in de -
centralised states like Indonesia.
4. Research and Discussion
This research uses information gathered from 13 key
informants, both internal stakeholders, who are directly
related to the design, assessment, and implementation
of regional tax policies, and external stakeholders, inc -
luding academics and representatives of taxpayers. The
internal informants were staff members of the Regio -
nal Revenue Agency (Bapenda), the Legal Department,
the Inspectorate, and the Regional Regulation Forma -
tion Agency of the Regional House of Representatives
(DPRD) in three case study cities. Outside stakeholders
gave valuable information regarding responsiveness to
regulation, legitimacy, and public participation.
1. Stakeholder Dynamics: Power, Legitimacy, and
Urgency
The central thrust of this analysis is anchored in sta-
keholder salience theory (Mitchell et al., 1997), which
posits that actors can be differentiated based on their
possession of power, legitimacy, and urgency. Internal
stakeholders, especially from Bapenda and legal units,
exercise institutional power to influence regional tax
frameworks. However, as Chen (2012) has argued, po -
wer without legitimacy and a shared sense of urgency
may result in limited compliance and policy inefficacy.
Legitimacy in this study emerged as a product of
procedural robustness and institutional collaboration.
As evidenced in South Tangerang and Y ogyakarta, re-
gional tax legislation undergoes formal vetting proces -
ses, which include coordination with legislative bodies
and legal harmonisation with national frameworks.
This reflects the finding that legitimacy in public gover-
nance is sustained through transparent, consultative,
and evidence-based policymaking.
Urgency, meanwhile, is cultivated through proactive
dissemination strategies and public engagement me -
chanisms. Interviewees emphasised the role of infor -
mation technology platforms in streamlining taxpayer
services and fostering public acceptance. One infor -
mant from South Tangerang City commented:
“Tax services will become more user-friendly in the
future thanks to information technology platforms.
All payments are processed online; simply visit the of-
fice for a consultation… Our faces at the service have
to smile all the time. ”
This reflects an evolving service-oriented gover -
nance model where tax authorities function not
merely as enforcers but as facilitators of compliance
through citizen-centred innovations (Sarker, 2011).
2. Enforcers’ Innovations and Strategic Use of Ma-
nagement Control Systems
The findings reveal that management control systems
(MCS) are integral to the development and implemen-
tation of effective tax policies. The Regional Revenue
Agency (Bapenda) in South Tangerang, for instance,
employs a combination of strategic planning, stakehol-
der engagement, and operational monitoring to rein -
force regulatory compliance and revenue performance.

3. Gaps in Enforcement and Operationalisation
Despite regulatory advancements, enforcement me -
chanisms remain underutilised. Interviewees reported
that while regional tax authorities have occasionally
employed sealing sanctions against non-compliant
taxpayers, coercive enforcement using instruments
such as coercion warrants under Law No. 19 of 1997
(as amended by Law No. 19 of 2000) remains largely
dormant. This gap between legal provision and enfor -
cement practice may stem from political caution, admi-
nistrative inertia, or insufficient operational guidance.
Moreover, regulatory completeness is still a pressing
issue. Informants noted deficiencies in SOPs and re -
gional head decrees, which are critical for translating
tax laws into actionable procedures. According to one
academic informant, „Only a few SOPs supporting Ba-
penda’s technical activities are available on the official
website. ”
This finding is corroborated by evaluation results
from the South Tangerang Inspectorate, suggesting that
the absence of well-structured management control
tools weakens implementation and monitoring.
4. Stakeholder Engagement and Input/Output Le -
gitimacy
Drawing from Alemanno (2015), who emphasises
the role of stakeholder participation in enhancing both
input and output legitimacy, the study found a mixed
level of engagement across cities. While consultation
with entrepreneurs, particularly regarding hotel and
restaurant tax policies, was acknowledged, broader pu-
blic participation remained limited. This supports Ka -
ufmann et al. ’s (2005) argument that governance quality
hinges not only on regulatory design but also on citizen
trust, administrative autonomy, and the transparency of
regulatory outcomes.
The legal departments in Y ogyakarta and Payakum-
buh reported that early involvement of national legal
institutions had improved regulatory precision. Howe-
ver, they also recognised the need to shift from a 70/30
ratio (academic drafting vs. discussion) to a more ba -
lanced 50/50 model, to allow for more dynamic and
participatory debate. The Payakumbuh case especially
underscores the importance of regulatory harmonisa -
tion, where the legal department acted as a mediator
between regional technical units and national legal
standards.

4.3. Stakeholder Involvement and Engagement
Expectations
The research identifies a sequential stakeholder en -
gagement model in the regulatory lifecycle, delineated

actors, from the initial drafting of the academic paper
to final approval by the DPRD. Notably, engagement is
most active during formal consultation stages, while vo-

regulatory formulation, with limited mechanisms for

The current engagement model reflects only partial
adherence to the principles of inclusive governance.

most engagement activities remain at the level of toke -

public communication mechanisms exist, active two -

participate more actively in determining tax rates, base
thresholds, and non-taxable limits, issues with direct
implications for public equity and fiscal justice.
4.4. Towards an Effective Local Tax
Environment
Informants proposed a set of criteria for an effective
local tax environment, grounded in practical governan-
ce challenges and aspirations. These are summarised
below:
1. Administrative Enforcement : Respondents
emphasised the importance of strengthening enfor -
cement through local adaptations of national regula -
tions. Despite the existence of Government Regulation
No. 55/2016, implementation remains limited. Local
initiatives to include coercive measures in regional re -
gulations reflect a push towards greater administrative
rigour.
2. Institutional Autonomy: The existence of a flexi -
ble and independent tax agency, such as Bapenda, was
identified as a prerequisite for agile policy implementa-
tion. Many regions still operate under limited institu -
tional autonomy, restricting their ability to innovate or
respond to compliance challenges.
3. Executive Commitment: Regional heads must de-
monstrate political and financial commitment, not only
in endorsing tax policies but also in funding necessary
operational needs, such as updating property tax object
values. As NJOP values remain outdated, potential re -
venue losses are considerable.
4. Interdepartmental Coordination : Effective im -
plementation requires intensive collaboration among
Bapenda, Legal Division, Inspectorate, and enforce -
ment units such as Satpol PP . Without such coordina-
tion, regulatory implementation remains fragmented.
5. Public Engagement and Socialisation:  Ongoing
taxpayer education is key in overcoming passive re -
sistance. In many cases, misconceptions and ignorance
serve as excuses for non-compliance, highlighting the
importance of proactive outreach initiatives.
6. Innovation and ICT Integration: The informants
observed that the uptake of digital payment systems
and regulatory technology needs to be aligned with the
local socio-economic context in an effort to enhance
accessibility and minimise administrative costs.
7. Perceived Usefulness: Highlighting the material
benefits of tax compliance, i.e., allocated spending on
public goods, can enhance voluntary compliance. This
behavioural finding aligns with Torgler’s (2007) theory
of tax morale.
8. Reward Mechanisms : Encouraging compliance
by rewarding and giving preferential treatment, e.g.,
promotion priority for restaurants, was suggested as an

5. Conclusion5. Conclusion
This study sought to investigate stakeholder involve-
ment in the creation and implementation of regional
tax policies in three Indonesian cities: South Tange -
rang, Payakumbuh, and Y ogyakarta, situated within
stakeholder typology and social network theory, with a
further examination of the role of management control
system. The study offers novel insight into how local
tax environments are co-created by formal authority,
inter-stakeholder coordination, and dynamic trust re -
lationships.
Participants across the cities commonly linked the
quality of public services to the image of local tax au -
thorities. Y et, the perceptions of fairness in tax policy
remain contested. Several taxpayers voiced concern
over what they perceived as excessive local tax rates,
suggesting that the “highest possible rate” approach
risks eroding public trust and voluntary compliance.
This supports Torgler’s (2007) assertion that taxpayers’
perceptions of fairness and government accountability
are central to compliance behaviour. Notably, the chari-
sma or popularity of regional heads proved insufficient
as a driver of compliance. Instead, informants expres -
sed a preference for clear and transparent earmarking
of local tax revenue, echoing calls for more visible links
between tax and public spending.
From an internal stakeholder point of view, more
precisely those based in Regional Tax Management
Units (OPD), control power derives from a mix of
expertise, leadership support, and budget flexibility.
These elements of institutional capital enable agencies
to engage in policy innovations, make responsive regu-
lations, and promote coordination of implementation
tasks. Legitimacy of such regulatory action, in particu -
lar, tends to be determined not only by legal require -
ment but also by successful communication, track re -
cord, and negotiation with stakeholders. One regional
representative pointed out that „A good grasp of the
concepts and content of regulations will help to convin-
ce other stakeholders, ” thus underlining the performa-
tive aspect of legitimacy establishment. This view is in
line with the contention by De Bruijn and Ten Heuvel-
hof (2000) that effective policy networks rely heavily on
discursive framing alongside formal authority.
One of the contributions of this research is in char -
ting the evolution of stakeholder information networks
that underpin adaptive management control systems
(MCS) in local taxation. Instead of depending on hie -
rarchical command alone, local tax authorities now
work more through collaborative governance arrange -
ments such as coordination forums, joint assessment
processes, and inter-agency knowledge transfer. These
interactions produce a dynamic form of control, in ac -
cordance with Simons’ (1995) levers of control frame -
work, where belief systems (shared regulatory values),
boundary systems (legal constraints), diagnostic con -
trols (performance metrics), and interactive controls
(stakeholder interaction) operate together.
Furthermore, stakeholder engagement in tax inno -
vation has revealed latent tensions between regulatory
ambition and administrative capacity. While initiatives
such as digital tapping boxes and IT-based payment
platforms demonstrate innovation, their effectiveness
remains uneven and largely unevaluated. This highli -
ghts a key research gap. As Felis and Rosłaniec (2019:1)
note, “tax policy significantly affects city income; howe-
ver, the impact of these policies can change substantial-
ly over time. ” The need to study not just the presence
but the long-term effectiveness and equity outcomes of
tax innovations is thus both urgent and under-explo -
red.
Limitations of this study must also be acknowledged.
First, despite efforts to ensure data saturation, it is pos-
sible that certain stakeholder perspectives, particularly
from marginalised taxpayer groups, remain underre -
presented. Second, this research did not undertake a
detailed impact evaluation of the tax innovations di -
scussed, limiting its ability to draw conclusions about
their effectiveness in revenue generation or taxpayer
satisfaction.
Future research should therefore address these limi-
tations by adopting a mixed-methods approach, com -
bining stakeholder analysis with performance data and
longitudinal case studies. Such studies could explore
the interplay between evolving central government re -
gulations, such as Law No. 1/2022 on Fiscal Relations,
and local regulatory autonomy. This law explicitly desi-
gnates local taxing power as a key pillar of sub-national
