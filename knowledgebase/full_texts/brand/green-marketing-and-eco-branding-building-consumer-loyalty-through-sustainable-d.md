ABSTRACT:
The relevance of the study is determined by the growing role of environmental responsibility of
business and sustainable development values in shaping consumer behavior and long -term
relationships between brands and their target audience. The growth of env ironmental awareness of
consumers necessitates the transition from the declarative use of "green" messages to scientifically
based strategies of green marketing and eco -branding, based on transparency, authenticity and
verification of environmental attributes. The purpose of the study is to theoretically substantiate the
mechanisms of influence of green marketing and eco-branding on the formation of consumer loyalty
through the value integration of sustainable development. The methodological basis was the a nalysis
and synthesis, comparison, systematization, theoretical generalization and content analysis of scientific
literature, thematic research and structural -logical modeling. The conceptual differences and
relationships between green marketing and eco-branding are highlighted, their key tools are clustered,
and the role of environmental values in consumer behavior is identified.  The impact of green
marketing on long -term loyalty is multi -level and is carried out through the perceived value of the
green product, green trust, perceived brand authenticity, value congruence and environmental
satisfaction. The congruence of brand values and consumers' personal beliefs has been substantiated
as a factor in strengthening the emotional connection and sustainable connection with the eco-brand.
It was found that greenwashing increases consumer skepticism and perceived risk, reduces trust and
long-term brand value. A shift to an evidence -based green marketing model is proposed, which
includes verification of environmental claims, transparency of information, third-party verification of
environmental characteristics and consistency of communica tion with actual business practices. The
practical implications of this study are, firstly, that they can be used to improve eco-branding strategies
in order to reduce the risk of greenwashing and build long-term consumer loyalty based on sustainable
values.

Keywords: green marketing, eco -branding, sustainable development, consumer loyalty, environmental value, brand
trust, brand authenticity, greenwashing, environmental behavior, green brand equity.

The structure of consumer markets development is revealed by the increased
focus of society on environmental protection issues, responsible consumption and the
principles of sustainable development. Climate change and depletion of natural resources,

One of the consequences of such changes was the development of green

One of the important tools for promoting green marketing is eco-branding, which

with the principles of sustainable living. For a modern consumer, the choice of a brand is
increasingly determined not only by the price, quality or functional characteristics of the
product, but also by the compliance of the company's activities with his  own values and
beliefs. Environmental responsibility, transparency of business processes, ethical
production and confirmation of the declared environmental characteristics can increase
trust in the brand, its emotional appeal and the consumer's willingness to build long-term
relationships with it.
In this regard, developing consumer loyalty is of particular importance. A value
fit between a brand and a consumer can lead to repeat purchases, positive attitudes towards
the product, recommendations and reduced sensitivity to competing offers. At the sa me

situation where the declared environmental responsibility is not reflected in the actual
behavior of the company. Such a mismatch can undermine trust and damage the brand
image, which emphasizes the need for accuracy, transparency and consistency in
environmental marketing communications.
Thus, the relevance of green marketing and eco-branding research is determined
by the need to better understand the processes by which sustainable development values
are transformed into perceived environmental value, brand trust, and consumer loyalty.
Research on the relationship between environmental positioning, brand authenticity,
consumer value orientation, and consumer behavior provides a theoretical basis for an
improved marketing strategy that allows a company to develop sustainable competitive
advantages.

marketing as a marketing activity aimed at satisfying needs while minimizing negative
environmental impacts. The further evolution of the concept is associated with the

3. Materials and Methods

The methodological basis of the study was a combination of general scientific and
special methods, which allowed for a comprehensive consideration of the theoretical
foundations of green marketing and eco -branding, as well as the tools of their influence
on consumer loyalty. The method of analysis and synthesis was used to develop scientific
approaches to determining the essence of green marketing, eco-branding, perceived green
value, green trust, brand authenticity, value congruence, green satisfaction and  green
loyalty. A systematic approach was used to describe green marketing and eco-branding as
interrelated aspects of sustainable development marketing strategies.
Comparative analysis methods were applied to the theoretical and conceptual
characteristics of green marketing and eco-branding, their tools and the consequences of
influencing consumer behavior. The theoretical generalization method allowed to organize
the determinants of the formation of environmentally oriented and long -term loyal
behavior. Content analysis was used to clarify the roles of environmental value, trust, brand
authenticity, value alignment and greenwashing in the interaction b etween the enterprise
and the consumer. The case study method of the study is applied to the analysis of various
international brands in green marketing and eco -branding to study the manifestations of
practice.

4. Results

The modern concept of green marketing has developed as a result of the gradual
awareness of the role of business in ensuring environmental, social and economic aspects
of sustainable development. While traditional marketing focuses mainly on identifying and
satisfying customer needs while achieving the economic goals of the business, green
marketing extends this by “integrating what is known about the environmental impact of
production, distribution, consumption and disposal of products into traditional
marketing” using the terminology of green marketing. Green marketing is seen as a
marketing system that involves minimal damage to the natural environment while creating

value for business and society in the long term (Polonsky, 1994; Peattie, 2001). This
approach reflects the transition of the environmental marketing perspective from a narrow
interpretation of marketing “green” products to a comprehensive management conce pt
embedded in a corporate strategy for sustainable development.
The evolution of green marketing reflects a gradual shift from reactive to
proactive and strategic environmental business behavior. In the past, firms primarily
responded to environmental concerns, regulatory constraints, and societal pressures.
Today, environmental orientation is viewed as a potential source of competitive advantage,
innovation, and long-term customer relationships. (Peattie & Crane, 2005) argue that there
cannot be truly green marketing simply by including environmental concerns as part of the
marketing approach in product advertising, as green marketing involves real changes in
product, technology, and company behavior. (Prakash,  2002) also compares green
marketing to the need to align firms' marketing strategies with social and environmental
interests. Therefore, its conceptual framework is based on the principle of creating value
for the consumer, the firm, and the natural environment simultaneously.
In this sense, it is appropriate to distinguish between green marketing and eco -
branding. Green marketing is broader in the system of marketing activities and calls to
manage marketing activities in general taking into account  environmental aspects, while
eco-branding is aimed at developing a specific brand identity related to sustainable
development, environmental compliance and appropriate consumption. An eco -brand
transforms the environmental properties of the product and co rporate practices into a
system of functional, symbolic and emotional values that is easily understandable for the
consumer. That is why the brand acts as its native communicative city between the
company's real eco-activity and its perception by the consu mer. (Chen, 2010) identifies a
close relationship between green brand image, green satisfaction, green trust, and green
brand equity, which confirms the need to consider green branding not only as a means of
product identification, but also as a mechanism for accumulating long-term brand equity.
The distinction between the functional and communication-value components of
green marketing allows us to specify the place of eco -branding in the general system of
environmentally-oriented enterprise management. The generalization of scientific
approaches (Polonsky, 1994), (Peattie, 2001), (Prakash, 2002), (Peattie & Crane, 2005),

The green marketing toolkit is its systemic integral part. The traditional marketing
mix is undergoing transformation in line with the principles of environmental
responsibility. The term “green product” implies the development of products whose life
cycle includes reduced resource consumption, are reusable or recyclable, durable and
minimize the use of hazardous materials. Green price is the market price of the product
and the value of environmental services that the consumer receives by making responsible
choices. Green space is associated with reducing the environmental impact of logistics
procedures, rationalizing supply channels and choosing local or low -carbon distribution
schemes. Green promotion is the truthful communication of the environmental
characteristics of the product, the use of eco-labeling, certification and digital channels, as
well as socially responsible content (Majeed et al., 2022; Román-Augusto et al., 2022).
particularly relevant in the service sector, in particular in such a segment as the
restaurant business.  The environmental orientation of catering establishments can be
manifested in the use of local and seasonal raw materials, reduction of food waste, rejection
of excessive disposable packaging, application of resource -saving technologies and
transparent information to consumers about the environmental characteristics of the offer.
In this context, eco -branding makes it possible to transform actual sustainable
development practices into a component of the restaurant brand identity. At the same
time, long-term consumer loyalty is formed only when the environmental statements of
the establishment are confirmed by its real activities, and the declared values are consistent
with the actual customer experience.
Eco-branding enhances the effect of these tools by creating stable cognitive and
emotional associations. The main focus was on the environmental brand image, which is
the consumer's perception of the environmental commitments and features of the brand.
A positive environmental image is not created only by green symbols, colors and green

Perceived environmental value also plays an important role in the conceptual
model. The consumer evaluates an environmentally friendly product based on the
functional, emotional, social, and environmental benefits and costs associated with its
acquisition. Satisfaction is formed when the relationship is favorable, and this can be
translated into trust and loyalty. Chen (2013) empirically confirmed that perceived
environmental value is positively related to environmental loyalty both directly and
indirectly through environmental satisfaction and environmental trust. Therefore,
environmental value is not an additional characteristic of the product, but a separate
element of overall consumer value.
Green Trust is particularly relevant because consumers cannot independently
verify the environmental performance of a product. Information asymmetry forces the
buyer to rely on the manufacturer, on labeling, on certification and on corporate
communications. However, there is a risk of “greenwashing” due to the excessive use of
unverified environmental claims. (Chen & Chang, 2013) found that “green” trust is
weakened by “greenwashing” due to increased consumer confusion about “green” and
perception of “green ” risk. ( Akturan, 2018) also confirms the negative impact of
greenwashing on green brand value and purchase intention. Thus, transparency and
verifiability of environmental claims should be considered as fundamental aspects of eco-
branding, rather than as derivative attributes of the communication of such claims.
The concept of sustainable development expands the value content of green
marketing. It recognizes the need to reconcile social, environmental and economic
interests, so an environmentally sound marketing strategy must go beyond simple sales
promotion. Now it is about the formation of responsible consumption patterns, where a
person's choice of a product is associated with the perception of the long-term impact of
this product on the environment and society. Green marketing in this sense performs not
only commercial functions, but also informative and educational ones, as it contributes to
the dissemination of knowledge about the properties of the product, consumption, reuse
and recycling.
Sustainability values can serve as internal determinants of environmentally friendly
behavior. A consumer who personally associates environmental protection, responsibility
to future generations, and conscious business practices with their daily lives is more likely
to positively evaluate the environmental attributes of products. (Li et al., 2021) found that
environmental values are positively associated with the intention to purchase an
environmentally friendly product, highlighting the role of environment al trust in shaping
such behavioral intention. At the same time, positive environmental attitudes do not
always automatically translate into actual purchases. (Kalafatis et al.,  1999) also noted,
applying the theory of planned behavior, that it is necessary to take into account not only
the attitude towards the ecological product, but also the subjective norm, perceived
behavioral control, and the characteristics of the market environment.

The theoretical and conceptual foundations of green marketing and eco-branding
are based on changing the framework of orientation towards short -term sales of
environmentally-positioned products into sustainable formation of long -term consumer
value. Effective green marketing involves real greening of marketing activities as a whole,
eco-branding, in turn, transforms relevant practices into an image and system of values
that are accessible to the consumer. As the ecological quality of the product, the reliability
of information, certification, transparency, green brand image, perceived value, satisfaction
and trust are components of a single mechanism of influence on behavior. As a result, the
values of sustainable development cease to be only a normative characteristic of corporate
activities and turn into a factor of consumer choice, emotional attachment and long-term
green loyalty.
The impact of green marketing and eco-branding on consumer loyalty is a multi-
level process, as a number of  cognitive, emotional and value connections are formed
between a company's green initiative and repeat purchase behavior. Simply positioning a
product as environmentally friendly does not guarantee the automatic formation of brand
loyalty. The consumer first interprets information about the environmental properties of
the product and the company's activities, compares this information with his own
expectations and values, assesses the veracity of the statements and, finally, constructs the
expected level of environmental value and trust. Only a positive experience can transform
these components into satisfaction, emotional attachment, repeat choice and green loyalty.
That is why green marketing is better considered not as a direct cause of loyalty, but as a
set of marketing incentives, the effectiveness of which depends on the psychological and
behavioral processes of their perception (Chen, 2013; Zhang et al., 2023).
A fundamental part of this mechanism is a set of green marketing tools through
which a company expresses its environmental commitments. Environmental
characteristics of the product, resource-efficient production, responsible packaging, green
logistics, cer tification, labeling and dissemination of information about the impact of
products on the environment provide an informational basis for brand evaluation. At the
same time, the effectiveness of these tools is assessed not by the volume of environmental

Thus, environmental perceived value is considered one of the key elements of this
procedure. It is formed by subjectively comparing the benefits that consumers expect to
receive from using an environmentally better product with the financial, time and othe r
costs. Unlike traditional consumer value, environmental perceived value includes not only
the functional utility of the product, but also its environmental, social and symbolic
aspects. When a consumer buys an environmentally friendly product, there is o ften an
additional intangible benefit associated with the awareness that he has caused less harm to
the environment or supported a responsible business. (Chen, 2013) found that perceived
environmental value is positively related to environmental loyalty, and its influence is also
exerted through environmental satisfaction and environmental trust. Thus, environmental
value provides a platform for the formation of more sustainable brand attitudes.
Perceived value is particularly relevant in contexts where green products can
justify a price premium or where established consumption patterns are challenged. When
the buyer considers the additional environmental benefits to be significant, a higher price
is not necessarily a critical barrier. At the same time, proclaiming the sustainable properties
of a product without obvious benefits for the consumer may limit the intention to
repurchase. Thus, to be successful, a company must combine environmental and
functional value and demonstrate not only the social effect of responsibility in the choice,
but also some personal benefits for the consumer. (Lin et al., 2017) emphasize the
importance of perceived benefits and transparency in developing perceived “green” value,
connection with one’s own brand and brand loyalty. This means that an eco-offer becomes
particularly powerful when the consumer simultaneously sees the practical utility of its use
and finds that it corresponds to his or her own personal identity.
The next important element of the mechanism is green trust. The attributes of a
“green” product are among those characteristics that are difficult for many consumers
without specialized knowledge to verify. The buyer can rarely independently determine the
true carbon footprint of the product, the source of raw materials, whether the production
process meets the stated standards, or whether the packaging can be recycled. This creates
an information asymmetry between the firm and the consumer. Trust in this c ontext
reduces subjective uncertainty and the risk of making the wrong decision. Green trust, as
part of the “green” brand image and “green” satisfaction, is also an important factor
determining the equity of a “green” brand, as shown (Chen, 2010). Meanwhi le (Li et al.,
2021) emphasize the importance of “green” trust in the relationship between
environmental value and intentions to purchase “green” products.
Trust is built not only on the content of the marketing message, but also on its
evidence. Certification, traceability of raw materials, quantitative environmental indicators,
transparency of corporate reporting and consistency of company behavior can redu ce
uncertainty. In this regard, transparency becomes particularly important. The consumer

information disclosed but also on its source, verifiability, relevance, and comprehensibility
to consumers. Independent certification and recognized eco -labeling can reduce
information asymmetry by providing third-party confirmation of environmental attributes
that consumers are generally unable to verify directly. Traceability systems can strengthen
this effect by linking environmental claims to identifiable stages of sourcing, pro duction,
distribution, and product disposal, while quantitative environmental indicators provide
measurable evidence of environmental performance. Corporate sustainability reporting
and transparent disclosure of assessment methodologies can further support  credibility,
particularly when environmental claims concern complex characteristics such as carbon
emissions, resource efficiency, or supply -chain impacts. However, the relative credibility
of these forms of evidence may vary across industries and consumer groups. Product-level
eco-labels may be particularly relevant in frequently purchased consumer goods, whereas
traceability, lifecycle information, and independently verified performance indicators may
become more important for products and services chara cterized by higher involvement,
technological complexity, or environmental uncertainty. Their effectiveness may also
depend on consumers’ environmental knowledge, familiarity with certification schemes,
perceived independence of the information source, and ability to interpret environmental
information. Therefore, evidence contributes to lasting loyalty when it is not only credible
but also understandable, relevant to the purchasing decision, consistently communicated,
and confirmed by the consumer’s subsequent experience with the brand. In this process,
verification reduces uncertainty, transparency facilitates evaluation, and consistent brand
behavior reinforces green trust over time, creating conditions under which initial
confidence can develop into repe at purchasing, positive word -of-mouth, and long -term
green loyalty.
The reverse process occurs when it comes to greenwashing. Vague claims,
exaggerating environmental benefits, selective disclosures, or the use of natural imagery
without any real changes in production can all contribute to consumer skepticism.
Greenwashing negatively impacts trust in the green market as a result of increasing
consumer confusion and environmental risk perception (Chen & Chang, 2013). (Akturan,
2018) also finds a negative relationship between greenwashing, green brand equity, and
purchase intention. Thus, false environmental communication poses a double risk: it not
only reduces the effectiveness of the current marketing campaign, but also undermines the
accumulated trust that could be replenished with much larger investments. In a loyalty
reward system, combating greenwashing is not just an ethical issue, but rather a necessary
condition for protecting long-term brand equity.
Authenticity also has a significant impact on the perception of an eco-brand, and
can be defined as the degree of congruence between the brand’s stated environmental
positioning, its actual behavior, and its history of interaction with consumers.

Perceived brand authenticity and green trust should be treated as closely related
but conceptually distinct constructs. Perceived brand authenticity reflects consumers’
assessment of whether a brand’s environmental identity is genuine, consistent, and
supported by its actual behavior, whereas green trust refers to consumers’ willingness to
rely on the credibility of the brand’s environmental claims, commitments, and
performance. Thus, authenticity primarily concerns the perceived congruence between
what the brand claims to represent and what it actually does, while trust concerns the
consumer’s confidence that the brand will provide reliable environmental information and
fulfill its environmental commitments.
Consumers can assess claim –behavior consistency through multiple observable
signals. These include correspondence between environmental advertising and actual
product characteristics, continuity of environmental commitments over time, consistency
between p roduct design, packaging, sourcing, logistics, and corporate sustainability
policies, independent certification, disclosure of measurable environmental indicators, and
the absence of contradictions between corporate statements and publicly observable
practices. Repeated consistency across these touchpoints strengthens perceived
authenticity because sustainability is interpreted as an embedded component of brand
identity rather than a temporary promotional strategy. Conversely, inconsistencies, vague
environmental claims, selective disclosure, or discrepancies between communication and
observable corporate behavior can operate as signals of greenwashing and weaken
perceived authenticity.
The interaction between authenticity and trust can therefore be understood as a
reinforcing process rather than as an equivalence between the two constructs. Perceived
authenticity provides consumers with evidence that environmental positioning is genuine,
thereby strengthening the basis for green trust. Green trust, in turn, reduces perceived
uncertainty and facilitates reliance on future environmental promises made by the brand.
When authenticity and trust are subsequently confirmed by positive product an d brand
experience, their combined effect can support green satisfaction, repeat purchasing,
positive word-of-mouth, and long -term loyalty. Greenwashing disrupts this mechanism
because detected inconsistencies between claims and behavior simultaneously undermine

An even deeper level of mechanism is the alignment of brand values with the

self-brand connection and, subsequently, brand loyalty. Thus, eco -branding can lead to
loyalty not only through better product characteristics, but also by allowing the consumer
to symbolically express who they are.

quality. If a product does not meet basic expectations in terms of quality, convenience, or

on the boundaries of sustainable loyalty, it is through sustainable loyalty t hat sustainable
loyalty emerges at the intersection of innovation management. Therefore, along with
functional and environmental value, sustainable loyalty should be given more attention.

convey green consumer confusion  and green perceived risk negatively affects the

public use, independent certification, quantitative indicators and available information on
the method of assessing environmental performance will be justified.
At the same time, evidence must be combined with clarity of communication.
Overly complex technical details may be formally true, but do not create value for the
buyer. Good eco-branding should translate environmental performance into meaningful
benefits for the consumer, without distorting them into unsubstantiated advertising claims.
Transparency of environmental information increases perceived environmental value and
brand association, which is associated with future brand loyalty (Lin et al., 2017). Thu s, a
balance should be found between the scientific substantiation of environmental claims and
their accessibility for the target audience.

and level of environmental awareness of different consumer segments. The impact of
environmental communication is not the same for all market segments, as it depends on
environmental concern, knowledge, corporate social responsibility, trust and price

importance for shaping sustainable purchasing behavior among the younger generation
(Borah et al., 2024). This justifies the transition from general environmental messages to
differentiated communication, with an emphasis on those aspects of sustainability that are
most relevant to the target audience.

5. Discussion

Thus, the results give reason to believe that green marketing in the current
conditions should no longer be considered as a separate tool for promoting environmental
products, but as an integrative model of forming relationships between the brand and the
consumer. This approach corresponds to the concept of (Polonsky, 1994) and (Peattie,

2001), according to which the evolution of green marketing is a feature of the transition
from a reactive position regarding individual environmental problems to the inclusion of
the principles of sustainable development in marketing activities. At the sam e time,
research data extended this approach to eco -branding as a process of transforming the
environmental activity actually carried out by the company into a system of values
perceived by the consumer.
The established relationship between perceived environmental value,
environmental trust, satisfaction and loyalty is consistent with the results of (Chen, 2010)
and (Chen, 2013). However, this study allows us to consider the process in a more holistic
way, taking into account  brand authenticity and the consistency of consumers' personal
beliefs with the environmental values of the brand. In this regard, the results of (Lin et al.,
2017) are relevant for linking transparency with perceived environmental value, connection
with o ne's own brand and brand loyalty. Thus, eco -brand loyalty develops not only
through rational evaluation of product attributes, but also through consumers'
identification of values and emotional attachment to the brand.
Green trust also deserves special attention. The results of the study show that
trust is in fact the link connecting a company’s environmental claims and long -term
consumer behavior. This is consistent with the findings (Guerreiro & Pacheco, 2021),
which highlight the importance of green trust in the formation of purchase intentions and
in green word-of-mouth. However, trust is fragile in conditions of information asymmetry,
when the consumer is unable to personally verify most of the environmental characteristics
of the product. Therefore, certification, traceability, specificity of environmental indicators
and transparency in corporate activities should be considered not as information tools, but
as elements of managing long-term relationships with consumers.
Greenwashing remains a significant problem. The negative impact of false and/or
exaggerated environmental claims on trust is found to be consistent with (Chen & Chang,
2013), which links greenwashing to increased consumer confusion and risk perception.
The same issue is explored by (Akturan , 2018), who point to the negative impact of
greenwashing on green brand value and purchase intentions. This means that aggressive
environmental positioning without a proper evidence base can lead to marketing effects
that contradict expectations. Thus, a promising path for innovation in green marketing
would be to move from a declarative way of communication to an evidential way, where
environmental claims could be verified by consumers or an independent party.
At the same time, the results do not allow us to consider the impact of green
marketing to be the same for all consumers. The importance of the environmental element
is influenced by environmental concern, knowledge, price sensitivity, social norms and
individual values. The results (Borah et al., 2024) emphasize the importance of consumers'
environmental knowledge, social responsibility and trust for the behavior of Generation
Z, as does this study. Thus, not only traditional socio-demographic variables, but also the
level of environmental concern and the type of sustainability value can be used to segment
consumers in order to predict their willingness to pay a price premium.
The interpretation proposed in this study is also consistent with, but extends, the
most recent research published in 2026. (Eyasu, 2026) confirms that the effects of green
marketing on customer loyalty are largely indirect and operate through brand image and
relational mechanisms such as trust, perceived value, and customer satisfaction. This

therefore not the identification of green trust, perceived value, satisfaction, or
greenwashing as entirely new determinants. Rather, the proposed framework integrates
these established relationships with perceived brand authenticity and value congruence and
distinguishes between two interconnected pathways: a constructive pathway in which
verifiable environmental practices support perceived value, authenticity, trust, satisfaction,
and ultimately loyalty, and a disruptive pathway in which inconsistency between
environmental claims and actual corporate behavior generates skepticism and percei ved
risk and weakens trust and brand equity. In this respect, the study shifts the analytical focus
from isolated determinants of green purchasing toward the process through which
sustainable development values can become embedded in enduring consumer –brand
relationships.

proposed relationships among green perceived value, green trust, perceived brand
authenticity, value congruence, green satisfaction, and green loyalty were derived from the
synthesis of previous empirical and conceptual studies and illustrated through selected
business practices rather than tested using original primary data. Accordingly, the
integrative mechanism proposed in this study should be interpreted as a theoretically
grounded framework rather than as a fully validated causal model. Although prior research
provides empirical support for individual relationships among these constructs, their
simultaneous interaction within one integrated model has not yet been directly test ed
across different industries, countries, and consumer segments. This limitation restricts the
extent to which the proposed relationships can be generalized to all market contexts. At
the same time, it provides a clear basis for further empirical research aimed at validating
the model through survey data, structural equation modeling, longitudinal observations,
or cross-market comparative designs.

The summary of the results allows us to offer a broad understanding of the
mechanism of green loyalty formation as a sequence of interrelated processes: the
company's environmental practices serve as the basis for green marketing communication;
green marketing communication affects the perceived green value and image of the green
brand; transparency and certification of environmental claims lead to green trust and
perceived authenticity; the correspondence of brand values to consumer beliefs promotes
connection with one's own brand; positive interaction experiences transform these
elements into satisfaction and long-term loyalty. This approach extends existing models by
integrating cognitive, trust, and value -emotional dimensions of the formation of
commitment to an eco-brand.
Future research should empirically test the proposed multi -level framework and
examine the boundary conditions under which green marketing and eco-branding are more
or less effective in generating long-term loyalty. Particular attention should be paid to the
moderating roles of environmental concern, green knowledge, perceived consumer
responsibility, green trust, price sensitivity, and familiarity with environmental certification.
These factors may influence both the interpretation of e nvironmental claims and the
extent to which perceived value, authenticity, and trust are translated into behavioral
outcomes. Comparative studies across generations, countries, industries, and product
categories would help determine whether the proposed re lationships are stable across
different market contexts or depend on cultural, demographic, and sector -specific
conditions. Further studies should also move beyond purchase intention and include
behavioral and economic outcomes such as willingness to pay a  price premium, actual
repeat purchasing, positive word -of-mouth, switching resistance, and long -term loyalty.
Longitudinal research would be particularly valuable for determining whether trust and
perceived authenticity generated by environmental communic ation persist over time and
whether repeated brand experience strengthens or weakens the proposed relationships.
The integrated framework could also be tested using structural equation modeling and
multi-group analysis to compare the strength of direct, mediating, and moderating effects
across consumer segments and market settings.

6. Conclusions

The study identified that green marketing and eco -branding are related elements
of modern marketing focused on the principles of sustainable development. Green
marketing includes an ecological product, pricing, distribution and promotion, while eco-
branding contributes to the development of an ecological brand identity, a positive brand
image and emotional connections with the consumer. Their success is not measured by
the number of ecological messages, but rather by how well the promoted principles
correspond to the real practices of the firm.
The impact of green marketing on consumer loyalty is multi -level. Important
intermediate components of this mechanism are perceived environmental value, trust,
satisfaction, brand authenticity, and the fit between brand value and consumer personal
beliefs. The functional benefits of the product, combined with environmental and
symbolic value, contribute to the formation of brand loyalty, repeat purchase,
recommendation, and long-term commitment.

Greenwashing continues to be identified as a serious risk. The gap between a
company’s environmental claims and its actual behavior fuels consumer skepticism,
undermines trust, and can negatively impact brand reputation. For this reason,
environmental posi tioning should be based on transparency, evidence, third -party
verification of environmental attributes, and consistency in corporate behavior.
The shift from a declarative to an evidence-based model of green marketing and
eco-branding was discussed. Key elements should be verified environmental claims,
credible eco-labeling, transparency of information, communication adapted to the values
of target groups, and systematic management of the risk of greenwashing.
