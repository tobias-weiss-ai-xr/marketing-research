Abstract
This study introduces and validates a novel quantitative survey-based technique (select-and-rank, SAR) designed to measure
brand preference as an outcome of consumer-based brand equity (CBBE). The SAR method combines simplicity with predic-
tive accuracy by leveraging a ranking-based approach leaning on a Zipf distribution—a model demonstrated to effectively
represent market shares. Its development addresses the need for a practical and reliable tool in large-scale brand studies,
distinguishing itself from traditional methods by offering improved measurement capabilities and reduced cognitive load.
Using data from a market representative sample, this initial study demonstrates SAR’s ability to accurately capture brand
preference dynamics while comparing its performance against widely used existing measurement techniques. The findings
reveal SAR’s enhanced accuracy, robustness, and scalability, making it a valuable addition to the CBBE research toolkit.
The study also highlights managerial implications, offering brand managers a technique to capture consumer preferences to
assist in optimizing brand positioning and strategy in competitive markets. Finally, recommendations for future research are
provided to extend SAR’s application to additional dimensions of CBBE.

Introduction
Measuring brand equity is essential for marketers and prac-
titioners, to capture greater consumer preference (Farquhar
1989; Keller & Lehmann 2003; Sharp 2010). Consumer
surveys play an important role in this measurement, par -
ticularly the measurement of customer-based brand equity
(CBBE) (Aaker 1991; Keller 1993; Farjam & Hongyi 2015;
Parris & Guzmán, 2023; Allen 2024). As such, a wide vari-
ety of survey-based methodologies has been developed over
the last decades and is widely used in practice to improve
strategic decision-making related to brand management
(Park & Srinivasan 1994; Lehmann et al. 2008; Oh et al.
2020).
Prevailing quantitative approaches for measuring CBBE
in practice can be primarily categorized as scale, or rating,
and choice-based techniques (Kühberger & Gradl 2013 ).
Simple scale-based approaches such as rating brands on
one or several items (such as Likert-type or semantic dif-
ferential) are both widely cited and extensively applied in
practice; such scale-based approaches typically use a single
choice instrument asking respondents to select an individual
answer option in relation to a given statement using Likert-
type or semantic differential scale (Lassar et al. 1995; Yoo
& Donthu 2001; Lehmann et al. 2008; Joshi et al. 2015;
Heo et al. 2022). Alongside scale-based tasks, choice-based
technique such as conjoint analysis and maximum difference

Despite the ubiquitous use of these approaches, their
inclusion presents a cost-quality trade-off, in particular to
practitioners. The development and planning of consumer
surveys is often guided by cost considerations directly influ-
enced by questionnaire length alongside practical implica -
tions such as the simplicity of the task from a respondent
perspective (Lavrakas 2008; Plumeyer et al. 2019). Scale-
based techniques are simple to utilize, functionally flexible,
and intuitive for practitioners to perform analysis, interpret,
and communicate results. However, these tasks are often
tedious and take more time for respondents to complete,
especially for product categories with an extensive number
of relevant brands. Another key challenge stems from the
nature of the task in testing individual brands in isolation,
producing results with lower external validity compared to
alternative techniques such as conjoint (Lasser et al., 1995;
Yoo & Donthu 2001; Lehmann et al. 2008).
Alternatively, conjoint exercises, both ranking and
choice-based, test brands in a competitive environment and
are regarded as highly externally valid in gauging consumer
preference compared to scale-based techniques; conjoint
approaches are very time intensive and complex for con-
sumers to process, and too, require specialized knowledge,
software, and resources to administer, analyze, and inter -
pret (Cobb-Walgren et al. 1995; Karniouchina et al. 2009).
Ranking-based methodologies offer a promising alternative
to cope with the inherent challenges of scale and conjoint-
based approaches. Despite the benefits of ranking instru-
ments offering a simpler alternative to complex choice tasks,
with less costs based on less time to administer, minimal
CBBE literature has explored the opportunity to develop a
technique built on an explicit ranking task.
Against this background, we build on the work of
Hofmeyr et al. ( 2008) to develop a direct select-and-rank
technique (SAR) to measure CBBE based on Zipf distri-
bution, which has been recognized as a valid framework
for describing market shares, to serve as a reliable basis
for practitioners and researchers engaged in brand-related
research (Kohli & Sah 2003).
To do this, we designed a questionnaire in which prompts
respondents to indicate the brands they are aware of within
an aided brand list (selection phase), and subsequently ask
respondents to rank each of the aware brands (ranking phase)
based on their preference. Stated differently, participants are
presented an exhaustive list of the toothpaste brands offered
in the United Kingdom market, then asked to rank only the
brands that they specify they have heard of in the past (i.e.,
aware brands). The benefit in prompting and capturing ranks
directly is that it forces trade-offs across brands similar to
that of more complex consumer preference techniques such
as conjoint or MaxDiff (Finn & Louviere 1992; Cobb-Wal-
gren et al. 1995; Menictas et al. 2012; Farsky et al. 2017).
Furthermore, our new technique addresses the polarized
cost-quality challenge in its simplicity to administer, signifi-
cantly lower time-intensiveness, and too in achieving high
levels of predictive external validity against actual market
share across a broad competitive set.
With this context, our article addresses the shortcom-
ings of existing practitioner-used technique for measuring
CBBE in empirically testing the predictive validity of a new
technique against existing scale and choice-based method-
ologies. While scientific literature offers an abundance of
for-purpose methodologies, their practical application in
real-world contexts often present challenges given the sheer
scale of real-world studies, typically inclusive of extensive
brand lists, diverse category and market focuses, alongside
large sample size requirements, each in which amplify ten-
sions within the cost-quality trade-off. With this, the primary
purpose of this article is to develop a highly practical prefer-
ence technique (i.e., top-down measurement) that performs
effectively within true market research conditions, serve as
a foundational dependent variable to aid bottom-up dimen-
sion-level brand research, and offer a viable bridge between
academic rigor and practical application.
The remainder of this article is organized in providing an
overview on existing approaches and rationale on the selec-
tion of techniques for comparison, details on the develop-
ment of the new technique alongside a conceptual compari-
son against existing techniques, followed by the empirical
validation study, a discussion of the results, followed by a
conclusion and recommendations.
Techniques for measuring customer‑based
brand equity
CBBE research encompasses a broad range of methodolo-
gies, reflecting the complexity of assessing brand equity
from various perspectives. While our primary focus is on
techniques that directly capture brand preference, it is impor-
tant to highlight that CBBE research more broadly includes a
number of additional approaches that typically assess equity
at the individual dimension level (e.g., image). As conceptu-
alized in the foundational work of Aaker (1991) and Keller
(1993), these include dimensions such as brand awareness,
loyalty, quality, image, and associations.
These individual components build toward overall brand
preference, or choice, which represent the outcome of CBBE
frameworks. Traditional techniques include scale-based,
ranking-based, and choice-based methodologies, alongside
methods that extend to implicit measures and qualitative

techniques. This distinction between dimension-level assess-
ments (i.e., bottom-up) and direct preference measures (i.e.,
top-down) highlight an important duality within CBBE
research, wherein individual brand attributes collectively

• Scale based  including Likert and semantic differential
scales wherein participants provide a direct rating against

• Ranking based including direct ranking and constant sum

• Choice based including conjoint, or discrete choice mod-

simulate decision-making by forcing trade-offs between

Scale-based approaches, particularly the Likert scale and
the semantic differential scale, are widely used in CBBE
research to capture brand preference, attitudes, and percep-
tions (Likert 1932; Osgood et al. 1957). Likert scales enable
respondents to indicate their agreement using a set of state-
ments toward brand-related items and attributes, allowing
researchers to assess individual dimensions such as loyalty,
quality, and associations (Yoo & Donthu 2001). Further -
more, while typically used outside of preference measure-
ment, semantic differential scaling offers additional nuance
through the ability to measure perceptions against bipolar
attributes (e.g., “unreliable” to “reliable”) and is generally
used to capture brand image and association strength (Kel-
ler 1993).
Despite their popularity, scale-based approaches often
encounter limitations such as response biases, including
straight-lining or halo effects, particularly in long ques-
tionnaires (Cole et al. 2012; Schonlau & Toepoel 2015).
In addition, these approaches tend to lack external validity
relative to ranking and choice-based methods based on their
inability to mimic realistic decision-making contexts, along-
side response biases such as acquiescence (i.e., yea-saying),
disacquiescence (i.e., nay-saying), and central tendency bias,
which distort the measurement of true preferences, limiting
their applicability to capture preference directly (Coltman
et al. 2011; Netemeyer et al., 2024).
In building on brand preference specifically, ranking-
based and choice-based approaches provide an alternative
to scale-based methods by focusing on comparative evalu-
ations. Pairwise comparisons combine elements of both
choice-based and ranking-based techniques, as the task
requires participants to make discrete choices, yet the out-
put is typically used to derive rankings. Respondents are
asked to select their preferred option when presented with
a series of pairs, enabling researchers to construct a rank
order across a broader set of items (David 1988). This dual-
ity lies in the choice-based mechanism of the task and its
ranking-based application in analyzing the aggregated data.
While this approach is effective in capturing detailed com-
parisons, it is considered complex relative to scale-based
methods and becomes impractical for large brand lists,
requiring more comparisons, and thus, increasing cognitive
load (Thurstone 1927; Agarwal & Rao 1996). Conversely,
constant sum scaling, a ranking-based technique, asks par -
ticipants to allocate a fixed number of points across a set of
items (e.g., statements, brands) providing insights into the
relative value assigned to each item (Roth 1995; Louviere
& Islam 2008). This method allows researchers to capture
relative importance but can become difficult for respondents
to explicitly assign values.
In addressing some of these challenges, choice-based
methods allow researchers to replicate real-world decision-
making contexts and handle larger brand sets. MaxDiff and
best–worst scaling techniques prompt respondents to select
their “most” and “least” preferred item from a broader set of
items, allowing researchers to identify which brand or brand
attribute is most valued by consumers (Finn & Louviere
1992; Louviere et al. 2015). The method provides detailed
preference data, but its relative complexity in both design
and analysis can make it difficult to apply. Similarly, discrete
choice modeling and conjoint-based approaches also force
trade-offs in simulating real-work purchasing decisions. This
approach offers respondents a series of different product
configurations and prompts them to choose their preferred
option (Green & Rao 1971; Louviere & Woodworth 1983;
Louviere & Islam 2008). Conjoint techniques are particu-
larly useful in studies interested in measuring the multiple
attributes in which make up a product or service such as
brand, price, and quality (Voleti et al. 2017). Despite these
benefits, like MaxDiff, its design complexity and the need
for specialized software can make it difficult for inexperi-
enced practitioners to administer.
In addition to these approaches, CBBE research also
includes methods better suited to dimension-level meas-
urement such as pick-any or free-choice approaches within
brand image research, wherein respondents make binary
decisions on whether an image attribute is associated with
a brand under investigation (Driesener & Romaniuk, 2006;
Farsky et al. 2017; Plumeyer et al. 2019). Implicit Associa-
tion Tests (IATs) also feature in CBBE literature, capturing
subconscious brand attitudes by tracking response times
to image attributes (Greenwald et al. 1998; Priluck & Till
2010). These techniques offer insight into dimensions that
drive preference but are distinct from directly capturing pref-
erence itself.
Furthermore, qualitative methods, such as focus groups
or projective techniques, facilitate respondents in expressing

brand perceptions more openly, capturing subtle and sub-
conscious associations (Plumeyer et al. 2019). While valu-
able for exploring consumer attitudes, these methods are less
relevant in studies focused on explicit brand preference, as
they primarily capture brand perceptions against overarch -
ing preference. SAR, with its focus on direct ranking, is thus
more appropriate for studies aiming to understand explicit
consumer preference.
SAR was developed to address limitations in traditional
CBBE measurement approaches, combining a ranking-based
structure with the predictive accuracy of a Zipf distribution.
SAR minimizes cognitive load for respondents and allows
for robust preference data, balancing both practicality and
validity. This makes SAR a valuable tool for both research-
ers and practitioners interested in scalable preference meas-
urement. For this study, we selected a subset of methods
for initial comparison, focusing on one scale-based and one
choice-based selected based on their popularity in both sci-
entific literature and in applied contexts (Keller 1993; Yoo
& Donthu 2001; Plumeyer et al. 2019). Using a Likert scale
to represent scale-based methods and conjoint analysis to
represent choice-based methods provided a streamlined and
practical comparison. This selection aligns with our objec-
tive of validating SAR’s applicability relative to established
methods, offering a preliminary but insightful foundation
for further research.
Techniques for comparison
To assess the validity of our proposed new technique, we
compare against a choice-based conjoint (CBC) and a scale-
based approach, each in which are well established, and
widely cited, survey-based CBBE measurement methodolo-
gies in practice (Voleti et al. 2017).
Early forms of conjoint took shape in a ranking for -
mat, to which participants were offered a series of hypo-
thetical product or service combinations and asked to rank
each in order of preference. In building on this foundation,
further developments saw the introduction of the choice-
based approach, or discrete choice experiments, in which
prompt participants to select a single preferred product
across a series of possible combinations—better reflecting
a real purchase interaction (Green & Rao 1971; Louviere &
Woodworth 1983). Alongside these approaches, significant
interest in the technique motivated the development of more
complex alternative approaches (e.g., adaptive), but for our
purposes, we focus exclusively on the CBC approach given
its popularity and breadth of application in both scientific
and applied market research contexts (Voleti et al. 2017).
Despite a strong and consistent interest in conjoint, sub-
stantial limitations exist in which create barriers in applying
the technique. Of these barriers, the most notable stems from
its complexity, with the appropriate inclusion of the tech-
nique contingent on considerations such as choice design,
estimation, and interpretation. Administering a conjoint
exercise has largely been simplified through the develop-
ment of commercial software products (e.g., Sawtooth Soft-
ware), but complexities regarding design, setup, and inter -
pretation still require specialized knowledge and a thorough
understanding of the methodology (Rao 2014). Additionally,
past comparative efforts using conjoint for CBBE are quite
insular, predominantly focused on within-approach compari-
sons (e.g., ranking conjoint against choice-based conjoint)
alongside that of other trade-off-based techniques such as
best-worse scaling (Elrod et al., 1992; Cheng et al., 2023).
Previous comparisons to other CBBE approaches, however,
such as scale-based or simple ranking techniques beyond
conjoint are scarce.
Conversely, scale-based approaches are relatively much
simpler techniques. Survey scales do not require special-
ized knowledge or software to implement or administer, and
calculations are straightforward leaning merely on full scale
sums, averages, or box-specific analysis (i.e., top-2-box) (De
Haan et al. 2015). The most common form of the scale-based
approach is the Likert scale, with origins in which stem from
attitude measurement, in prompting participants to indicate
their level of agreeance across a typically five to seven-point
symmetric scale (Likert 1932; Joshi et al. 2015). Based on
its prevalence, scale-based approaches are frequently used as
an anchor in many empirical survey-based instrument com-
parisons (Priluck & Till 2010; Farsky et al. 2017).
Unlike trade-off exercises (e.g., conjoint approaches),
scale-based questions require respondents to evaluate brands
individually across relevant scale items. This becomes dif-
ficult for categories with extensive brand lists, with direct
impact to costs through an increased questionnaire length
(Lavrakas 2008). As an extension, the nature of longer non-
trade-off exercises have the potential to impact response
quality as they offer participants the opportunity to provide
the same answer across brands (i.e., straight-lining) due to
factors such as survey fatigue (Cole et al. 2012; Schonlau
& Toepoel 2015). Similarly, another underlying limitation
stems from the halo effect, specifically for multi-item evalua-
tions, in that of responses to individual scale items motivated
by the participants overall impression of the brand (Farsky
et al. 2017). Table  1 provides an overview across the key
characteristics of scale-based and choice-based conjoint
methodologies for measuring CBBE.

Development of a new technique
In developing our new technique, we build on the work of
Hofmeyr et al. (2008) who introduce the application of uti-
lizing a Zipf distribution to survey data, in developing an
‘attitudinal equity’ measure using a 10-point scale, to indi-
rectly derive rankings across a competitor set (i.e., brands)
before applying the power law to transform these rankings
into predicted brand shares. Alongside their motivation in
developing a valid technique, the authors too emphasize the
importance of practicality in survey-based instruments as
key to minimizing questionnaire lengths. Hofmeyr et al.
(2008) and others further demonstrate in past research the
applicability of the power law to actual brand market shares
(Kohli and Sah 2003). However, Hofmeyr et al. do not
include a direct ranking task in their study, but rather statis-
tically transform scale-based brand preference into ranking
scores afterward.
In building on this work, we develop our own tech-
nique addressing both aspects of the above-mentioned
practitioner’s tension; the accuracy of the instrument (i.e.,
external predictive validity) alongside its practicality (i.e.,
ease and speed to administer, analyze, and interpret). We
refer to our technique as select-and-rank (SAR), wherein
we embed the instrument within a traditional brand fun -
nel question set (i.e., awareness, consideration, purchase).
Despite sitting within a traditional brand funnel, the over -
arching instrument comprises only two questions, first ask -
ing respondents to indicate on a binary scale which brands
they are aware of, and subsequently asking them to rank
each brand based on their likelihood to purchase filtered on
previously indicated aware brands only. We then transform
brand ranks into brand scores using the power law (i.e.,
calculated using 1/rank), and use the sum of these scores
as a basis to establish predicted brand market shares for
each individual respondent.
Conversely, the Hofmeyr et  al. (2008) ‘attitudinal
equity’ measure first establishes a set of relevant brands
through two initial questions prompting participants on
both purchase and consideration, before leveraging an
‘overall brand performance’ scale-based question used to
calculate brand ranks afterward. However, unlike SAR,
a direct ranking task for respondents explicitly ranking
brands by their preference is missing, where we do so with
a single question leaning on brand awareness exclusively,
a key and established customer-based brand equity dimen-
sion (Aaker 1991). An important consideration in utiliz -
ing awareness too stems from the work of Romaniuk and
Sharp (2004, 2010) and the impact of mental availability,
or the share in which a brand is recalled as part of a pur -
chase decision, highlighting that mental availability cannot
be measured exclusively with an awareness metric alone.
As such, in building on the established relevant brand
set, we derive through an awareness question, contrary to
Hofmeyr et al. (2008), we then ask respondents to directly
rank each brand based on their likelihood to purchase,
too, forcing trade-offs across brands similarly to higher
complexity preference measures such as a conjoint-based
approaches (Cobb-Walgren et al. 1995 ; Rao 2014 ). Our
two-step SAR approach, wherein respondents initially
select aware brands, and subsequently rank the relevant
brand set, too, conceptually addressing mental availability
in first capturing the brands consumers think of, before
capturing preference anchored against a purchase context
(Sharp 2010).
In our empirical study, we evaluate each technique (i.e.,
select-and-rank, scale-based, and CBC) across two key
criterions, testing the external predictive validity against
actual market share, alongside a practical assessment of
each instrument. Our updated SAR technique contrary
to Hofmeyr’s et al. ‘attitudinal equity’ measure enables
respondents to rank directly, against that of indirect calcula-
tions to establish ranks, alongside the benefit in better emu-
lating a realistic purchase environment and decision-making
process through forcing trade-offs, like that of instruments
such as conjoint-based and MaxDiff exercises.

Empirical study
Research design
This initial study conducted in United Kingdom empiri-
cally tests and compares the predictive validity of the new
SAR technique against two commonly used survey-based
preference techniques including a scale-based and choice-
based conjoint instrument. Although used as a basis for
development, we made a deliberate choice to exclude the
original approach proposed by Hofmeyer et al. (2008) from
our comparison. This decision was made to streamline our
initial evaluation study in including only one instrument
representative of each approach classification (i.e., rank -
ing, scale-based, choice-based). We used toothpaste as the
product category for this study given its importance of
brand in the category, its regular inclusion in consumers’
shopping baskets alongside existing observational data of
actual brand shares allowing to validate our technique in
a larger competitive set given our focus on practitioner
application (Park & Srinivasan 1994; Lehmann et al.
2008).
In terms of brand selection, to cover 80% of the local
market, we include the top nine toothpaste brands in the
United Kingdom based on actual market shares, inclusive
of the two leading supermarket brands: Colgate, Senso-
dyne, Aquafresh, Oral-B, Macleans, Arm & Hammer, Cor -
sodyl, Tesco, and Sainsbury’s. The inclusion of a broader
brand list, against market leaders exclusively, allows us to
test the practitioner-relevant accuracy of the long tail (i.e.,
brands with smaller shares).
Predicted shares are based on an online survey con-
ducted in January 2023 and includes a nationally repre-
sentative sample of category buyers in the United King-
dom (n = 1,522) provided in cooperation with online panel
provider Dynata. Actual market shares for validation are
based on Kantar Worldpanel tracking of the number of buy-
ers of each brand in the same investigation period. While
similar comparative academic studies sufficiently leverage
data of smaller sample sizes, typically made up of student
populations, given our practitioner focus we chose instead
to simulate a realistic applied market research study. The
self-administered questionnaire compares technique leaning
on a between-subject design (i.e., each respondent evalu-
ates only one preference technique exclusively) indicative
of a hypothetical practitioner-based study. Our sampling
approach leans on common practitioner practices, admin-
istering the online survey representatively against United
Kingdom census data, with respondents assigned one indi-
vidual preference technique evenly based on demographic
distributions (i.e., age, gender, and region), and ensuring
structural demographic equality across techniques tested.
Alongside the inclusion of our SAR technique, for the
scale technique, we lean on Yoo and Donthu’s (2001)
widely used overall brand equity (OBE) scale, based a
consolidated iteration of their 10-item multidimensional
customer-based brand equity (MBE) scale consisting of a
refined four items—“it makes sense to buy brand instead
of any other brand, even if they are the same,” “even if
another brand has the same features as brand , I would
prefer to buy brand,” “if there is another brand as good
as brand, I prefer to buy brand,” and “if another brand is
not different from brand in any way, it seems smarter to
purchase brand”—with brand indicating the brand under
investigation. With this scale, we ask respondents to assess
each individual brand against a seven-point Likert scale
using 1 = “Strongly disagree” and 7 = “Strongly agree.”
We then sum the scores for the 4 statements across each
individual brand, which are then used to calculate aggre-
gate predicted brand market shares.
Despite the development of more contemporary con-
joint variations, such as incentive-aligned and duel-
response iterations, given our emphasis on application, we
opted to employ a simpler choice-based conjoint (CBC) as
it remains the most common type of conjoint used by prac -
titioners in applied brand based and consumer research
(Cobb-Walgren et al. 1995; Agarwal & Rao 1996; Ding
et al. 2005; Wlömert & Eggers 2016; Voleti et al. 2017).
Additionally, while both SAR and the scale-based tech -
nique focus exclusively on a single attribute (i.e., brand),
the nature of conjoint-based instruments require the inclu-
sion of multiple variables. As such, to enable an equitable
comparison and bolster our focus on practicality, we chose
to include the minimum necessitate of only one additional
variable (i.e., price). Using Sawtooth Software, we gener -
ate a complete enumeration design to minimize the overlap
of hypothetical combinations, including 12 choice tasks
with each task including the brand as an attribute (i.e., 9
levels) alongside an additional price attribute (Chrzan &
Orme 2000). For the price attribute, we use conditional
pricing leaning on the average price for each brand as an
anchor, and include 4 additional levels (i.e., 5 levels total)
at 60%, 80%, 120%, and 140% of the brand-specific aver -
age for each.
Within each of the 12 choice tasks, respondents are
presented with a choice of 4 product concepts, alongside
a ‘None’ option, and asked to select which they would
most likely purchase. We then use Sawtooth Software to
calculate individual utility scores using hierarchical Bayes
estimations. These scores are calculated using a combina-
tion of each individual brand and its relevant average price
to simulate share of preference, which we use as predicted
brand market shares (Hein et al. 2020). Further detail on
each approach as it appeared in the questionnaire is provided
in Appendix A.

The development of our new SAR technique offers poten-

preference, alongside scientific brand equity research, as a
dependent variable in aiding future dimension-level inves-
tigations. While this initial validity study offers promising
results, several important factors exist in which should be
considered in approaching ranking-based preference meas-
urement, which too offer potential opportunities for future
research.
The most notable of these considerations stem from
category implications, and the nature of the category or
industry under investigation. Alongside the importance of
mental availability, Sharp (2010) introduces the importance
of physical availability, or the ease in which a product is
readily available and accessible to purchase. Hofmeyr et al.
(2008) also highlights this point, through the inclusion of a
question within their technique specific to barriers, and the
potential unavailability of a preferred brand. Although this
notion of barriers is of less relevance to our study, given our
focus on toothpaste, which is generally a widely consumed,
affordable, and easily available product across, this is not the
case for all categories, especially those outside of consumer-
packaged goods.
As an example, inversely, the automotive category suffers
from multiple barriers, such as physical availability along-
side price implications, in which could impact consumer
preference rankings across a competitive brand set. In other
words, perhaps a consumer has an overarching preference
toward a luxury vehicle, but realistically does not have the
means to purchase their preference. As such, an opportunity
exists in refining our approach in addressing potential barri-
ers to improve the generalizability of the technique.
Additionally, the toothpaste market in the United King-
dom is reasonably concentrated, with the 9 brands included
in our study making up 80% of the total market. While this
is a relatively longer brand list in comparison to prior CBBE
empirical studies, from a practitioner perspective, other cat-
egories are far more fragmented and thus require assess-
ments across a much broader brand set. This poses several
challenges, notably in that of the limitations of survey-based
direct ranking tasks, and a potential threshold in the number
of items consumers can realistically rank (Lavrakas 2008).
While the importance of awareness as a foundational
CBBE dimension is clear, one potential solution to reduc-
ing items to be ranked stems from the Hofmeyr et al. (2008)
approach, leaning instead on consideration against aware -
ness exclusively. As such, two future research opportunities
exist, in that of a comparative study in testing the impact
of an awareness versus consideration filter within the SAR
technique, alongside a comparison of SAR with the Hofmeyr
et al. (2008) approach directly, which too, could help in bet-
ter understanding the implications in trade-off versus non-
trade-off survey-based exercise.
Additionally, the Hofmeyr et al. (2008) approach in fil-
tering on consideration against awareness may also help to
address differences in dynamics across categories. In extend-
ing the above example, while physical availability is less rel-
evant to the toothpaste category (with the exception of own
brand labels typically only available at individual retailers),
the automotive category suffers a host of potential barri-
ers. With this context, in the absence of explicitly capturing
and calibrating for possible barriers, a ranking instrument
that filters on consideration could conceptually offer a more
pragmatic and resource-conscious solution to mitigate these
issues. With this, it is too important to note that conjoint-
based exercises inherently, notably in the context of our
study, offer the opportunity to partially address these issues
through the ability to lean on multiple variables (e.g., the
inclusion of price alongside brand) against a single variable
alone (i.e., brand).
Conclusion and recommendations
Our research offers a promising initial demonstration in
the benefit of the new select-and-brand technique against
existing methodologies for both practitioners and as a basis
for future scientific research. Our findings highlight the
potential of the SAR method to contribute significantly to
brand preference measurement within the broader context
of CBBE. To further develop and validate this approach,
the following recommendations outline key areas for future
research and practical exploration. These directions aim to
enhance SAR’s applicability, address current limitations,
and to better align with evolving research and practitioner
needs:
• Expand comparative studies future research should
explore SAR’s application across diverse industries and
geographies to validate its generalizability beyond this
initial market (i.e., United Kingdom) and category (i.e.,
toothpaste).
• Incorporate additional techniques include further com-
parisons with alternative measurement approaches (e.g.,
semantic differential scaling, MaxDiff, constant sum
scaling) to deepen understanding of SAR’s advantages
and limitations.
• Explore applications to dimension-level research evalu-
ate and compare SAR’s effectiveness for dimension-level
research (e.g., image, loyalty). While this study focuses

• Optimize cognitive load refine SAR’s ranking process
(e.g., filtering based on consideration) to further mini-

brand sets, to ensure suitability for complex and different
market contexts.
• Analyze longitudinal effects apply SAR in longitudinal
studies to assess the techniques robustness in dynamic
market environments and tracking preference over time.

Select‑and‑rank technique (SAR)
Please rank the following toothpaste brands in order of your
preference.
Where 1 would be your most preferred and 5 your least
preferred.
[Respondent is presented filtered list of brands based on
initial aided awareness question].
Overall brand equity scale (Yoo & Donthu 2001)
Please indicate how much you agree with the following
statement in relation to each toothpaste brand.
1. It makes sense to buy brand instead of any other brand,
even if they are the same.
2. Even if another brand has the same features as brand, I
would prefer to buy brand
3. If there is another brand as good as brand, I prefer to buy
brand
4. If another brand is not different from brand in any way,
it seems smarter to purchase brand
[Respondent is presented repeated scale for each indi-
vidual brand where brand represents the brand under
investigation].
Choice‑based conjoint (CBC)
In thinking about toothpaste, which of the following prod-
ucts would you most likely choose?
[Respondent is presented 12 choice tasks (i.e., screens)
with each task including 4 brand and price combinations
alongside a ‘None’ option].
