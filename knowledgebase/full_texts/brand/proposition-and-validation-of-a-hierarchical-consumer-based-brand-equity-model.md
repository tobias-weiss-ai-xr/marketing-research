Abstract

Objective: This study proposes and validates a hierarchical consumer-based brand
equity model (CBBE-H), analyzing its influence on consumers’ purchase intention
in the apparel sector. The research seeks to fill a gap in the literature, which often
treats brand equit y dimensions in isolation, by proposing a unified model that
considers multiple interrelated dimensions.
Method: A quantitative approach was adopted, with a non -probabilistic sample of
407 apparel consumers. Data were analyzed using Structural Equation Modeling
(PLS-SEM), allowing for the testing of the model’s feasibility and its structural
relationships.
Results: The findings confirm the validity of the hierarchical consumer-based brand
equity model and indicate that it significantly influences purchase intention. All
analyzed dimensions showed a significant impact, reinforcing the importance of an
integrated approach to measuring brand equity.
Theoretical/Methodological Contributions:  This study contributes to the
literature by offering a comprehensive model that enhances the understanding of
brand equity construction and measurement, differing from previous approaches by
considering a second-order construct.
Relevance/Originality: Unlike prior studies that examine isolated dimensions, this
research proposes a reflective -reflective hierarchical model, promoting a more
holistic understanding of the impact of brand equity on purchase intention.
Managerial/Social Implications: The findings provide marketing managers with a
consolidated framework to strengthen consumer -based brand equity and influence
purchase decisions, aligning branding strategies with consumer perceptions.

associations, and awareness. These elements are shaped by continuous interactions with the brand,
influenced by promotional activities an d other touchpoints, consolidating their impact in the
marketplace (Febraa et al., 2023; Troiville, 2024). In recent years, brand equity has become a

Brands play a central role in purchase decision -making, influencing preferences and
behaviors. As identification elements, they act as “spokespersons” for diverse audiences and
represent strategic assets (Davcik et al., 2015). Beyond providing competitive advantage, they add
value to consumption (Veloutsou et al., 2013). Their definition, often limited to visual aspects,

varies according to context and audience (Jones & Bonevac, 2013), while others emphasize its

does not account for these interconnections. The gap lies in the lack of empirical evidence for a
hierarchical structure in which interdependent dimensions jointly influence purchase intention in

3 Methodological Procedures for Data Collection and Analysis

The sample, non -probabilistic and selected through judgment sampling, had its size
estimated using the G*Power 3.1 software (Faul et al., 2009), following Kock and Hadaya (2018)
for Structural Equation Modeling (PLS -SEM). Considering a medium effect size ( f2 = 0.15), a
significance level of 0.05, statistical power of 0.80, and seven predictors, the minimum estimated
sample size was 103 respondents. To ensure robustness and predictive validity, the
recommendation of Ringle et al. (2014) was followed, and the  sample was increased to 309
participants, exceeding the minimum requirement. The scales were adapted from validated
instruments, totaling 51 items measured on a five -point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 =
strongly agree), as detailed in Appendix A.
Data collection was conducted through an online cross -sectional survey (Google Forms),
and the data were organized and coded using Microsoft Excel®. The analyses were performed
using SmartPLS 4.1.1.5 software, widely used in marketing studies due to its ro bustness in
handling non-normal data, suitability for moderate sample sizes, and ability to estimate complex
and hierarchical models (Hair et al., 2019; Guenther et al., 2025). PLS -SEM was chosen for its
predictive nature and flexibility in dealing with co mplex hierarchical models, making it
particularly suitable for research aimed at maximizing the explanatory power of latent variables
(Sarstedt et al., 2019; Hair et al., 2019). The estimation algorithm adopted was the path weighting
scheme, as recommended by Hair et al. (2019).
Latent variables (LVs) were measured indirectly through observed variables (OVs),
derived from the questionnaire items. The LVs included Brand Loyalty, Perceived Quality, Brand
Associations and Awareness, Brand Image, Brand Credibility and Reputation, Self -Expression
with the Brand, Brand Love, and Purchase Intention, with their indicators properly coded (e.g.,
LEA1, LEA2, LEA3...). The second -order hierarchical construct was termed Hierarchical
Consumer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE -H). Hierarchical models repr esent multidimensional
constructs by consolidating multiple dimensions into a unified concept (Becker et al., 2012;
Sarstedt et al., 2019). In this study, a reflective–reflective model was adopted, in which the lower-
order constructs (LOCs) include the var iables LEA, QLD, ARM, IMG, CRM, AUT, and AMO,
specified as reflective indicators of the higher-order construct (HOC), CBBE-H.

of the LOCs are also assigned to the HOC, being replicated in the measurement model (Becker et

4.1.1 Measurement Model

In hierarchical models, such as the one adopted in this study, it is necessary to separately
evaluate the measurement models of the lower -order constructs (LOCs) and the higher -order
construct (HOC). Both follow PLS -SEM criteria, including reliability, con vergent validity, and
discriminant validity (Hair et al., 2019; Sarstedt et al., 2019). The first stage of PLS-SEM consists
of evaluating the measurement model, which examines the relationships between constructs and
their respective indicators to verify t he quality of the measures used (Hair et al., 2019; Hair &
Alamer, 2022).
In reflective–reflective hierarchical models such as this, the relationships between the HOC
and LOCs are interpreted as factor loadings, although they are operationalized as path coefficients,
and should be assessed based on criteria of convergent validit y, composite reliability, internal
consistency, and discriminant validity (Sarstedt et al., 2019).
To assess indicator reliability in the measurement model, cross -loadings were examined.
Indicator loadings should be higher within their respective constructs than in others (Chin, 1994).
Indicators with loadings between 0.40 and 0.70 were analyzed on a ca se-by-case basis, as they
may affect construct reliability (Hair & Alamer, 2022). According to recommendations in the
literature, items with lower factor loadings were progressively removed, with the model re -
estimated at each step to verify the impact of these exclusions on the remaining loadings. In this
procedure, items CRM9 (0.266), ARM6 (0.339), IMG1 (0.407), and IMG2 (0.475) were
successively removed, ensuring methodological transparency and reinforcing the credibility of the
model (Hair & Alamer, 202 2). The final factor loadings were consolidated and are presented in
Appendix C. First -order constructs were treated as indicators of the higher -order construct, in
accordance with Sarstedt et al. (2019), with values presented in Table 1.

After verifying the reliability of the items, the internal consistency of the model is assessed.
Composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha are the main measures used to ensure the consistency
of the variables (Hair et al., 2019; Hair & Alamer, 2022). Cron bach’s alpha is considered a less
precise reliability measure, as the items are not weighted. In contrast, composite reliability weights
the items according to their factor loadings, resulting in higher values and being more suitable for
PLS-SEM (Hair & Alamer, 2022).
Considering composite reliability as the more appropriate measure for PLS-SEM, only the
value of the higher -order construct was calculated. This calculation was performed manually,
following the guidelines of Sarstedt et al. (2019), since SmartPLS does not automatically provide
these values for higher -order constructs in reflective –reflective models. Therefore, the formulas
and procedures recommended by the authors were followed to manually calculate composite
reliability and the AVE of the higher-order construct. Values of Cronbach’s alpha and composite
reliability above 0.70 are considered acceptable, ensuring internal consistency (Hair et al., 2019;
Hair & Alamer, 2022). These criteria were met, allowing the analysis to proceed.
Convergent validity was assessed using the Average Variance Extracted (AVE), which
represents the proportion of variance explained by the indicators of each construct (Hair et al.,
2019). AVE values equal to or greater than 0.50 indicate adequate convergen t validity,
demonstrating that the construct explains more than 50% of the variance of its indicators. All
remaining factor loadings met this criterion, supporting the continuation of the analysis.
Discriminant validity, which assesses whether constructs are distinct, was examined using
the Fornell -Larcker criterion and the heterotrait -monotrait ratio (HTMT). The Fornell -Larcker
criterion compares the square root of the AVE of each construct with its  correlations with other
constructs, ensuring that the square root of the AVE is greater than the inter-construct correlations.

4.1.2 Structural Model

The analysis of the structural model estimated the relationships between latent constructs
according to the criteria established by Hair et al. (2019) and Hair and Alamer (2022). In
reflective–reflective hierarchical models, only the higher -order construct  (HOC) is evaluated,
since the lower-order constructs (LOCs) are part of the measurement model, and it is not necessary
to assess collinearity among them (Sarstedt et al., 2019). The analysis considered: (i) the
significance and strength of the paths obtained via bootstrapping, (ii) explanatory power (R2), (iii)
effect size (f2), and (iv) predictive relevance (Q2). The use of PLSpredict is not recommended for
this type of model due to potential imprecision in benchmark calculations (Marcoulides & Chin,
2013).
The statistical significance of the path coefficients was assessed using the bootstrapping
procedure with 10,000 resamples, applying the bias -corrected percentile confidence interval
method, a two-tailed test at a 5% significance level, and a fixed random seed. Coefficients were
considered statistically significant when the 95% confidence interval did not include zero and p -
values remained below 0.05 (Hair & Alamer, 2022). The result of the relationship between the

The results align with studies that highlight the potential of hierarchical models in specific

brand equity dimensions operate in an interdependent manner. Brand Loyalty confirms its strategic

(Carroll & Ahuvia, 2006).
The main theoretical contribution of this study is the empirical demonstration of the
hierarchical and integrated organization of brand equity, highlighting it as a dynamic relational
system in which cognitive, affective, and symbolic dimensions reinforce one another to generate
Purchase Intention. The proposed model offers a holistic view of consumer -based brand equity
and responds to the need for theoretical and methodological integration highlighted by Zheng et

5.2 Practical Contributions

Managerial implications stem from the relationships observed within the CBBE -H
construct and their relative weights on Purchase Intention. The results indicate that Brand
Credibility and Reputation and Brand Love are the primary drivers of brand equity in the apparel
sector, followed by Perceived Quality and Brand Image, while Brand Loyalty and Self-Expression
with the Brand exert a positive but less intense influence. This configuration provides managers
with a priority framework for resource allocation and brand strategy development.
First, Brand Credibility and Reputation should be at the core of branding strategy.
Consistency in delivery, clear communication, and alignment between promise and experience are
essential to maintaining trust. In highly substitutable markets, reliability reduces perceived risk
and stabilizes consumer preferences, reinforcing quality, transparency, and after -sales practices
(Veloutsou & Moutinho, 2009; Raj & Roy, 2015).
Second, the affective bond (Brand Love) should be cultivated through authentic narratives
and sensory experiences that evoke positive emotions. Distinctive aesthetics and a sense of
belonging create emotional capital and sustain brand relevance in saturated environments (Carroll
& Ahuvia, 2006; Iglesias et al., 2019).
Perceived Quality and Brand Image function as supporting dimensions: tangible
performance and aesthetic coherence strengthen value perception and trust in the brand, reducing
dependence on promotional strategies (Aaker, 2021; Buil et al., 2008).
Brand Associations and Awareness should be reinforced in everyday contexts, such as
work, leisure, and social events, enhancing mental availability and brand recall (Vriens et al.,
2019). Associating the brand with daily life situations is as important as investing in functional
attributes, as it expands the brand’s presence in the consumer’s cognitive repertoire.
Finally, Brand Loyalty and Self-Expression with the Brand should be viewed as outcomes
of symbolic and affective value, rather than starting points. Loyalty programs and CRM initiatives
are more effective when they strengthen feelings of belonging and identification rather than relying
solely on financial incentives. In this way, repurchase behavior reflects affinity and trust, not
merely convenience (Veloutsou & Moutinho, 2009; Thomson, MacInnis & Park, 2005).
These findings indicate that brand management in the apparel sector must balance
rationality, emotion, and identity, prioritizing symbolic and relational coherence. The CBBE -H

6 Conclusion

This study proposed and validated a Hierarchical Consumer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE-
H) model, conceived as a second-order construct, to assess its influence on Purchase Intention. All
hypotheses were confirmed, indicating that brand equity is a multidimensional construct composed
of cognitive dimensions (Perceived Quality, Brand Credibility and Reputation, Brand Associations
and Awareness), affective dimensions (Brand Love), and symbolic dimensions (Self -Expression
with the Brand and Brand Loyalty). The mode l demonstrated both explanatory and predictive
power, showing that CBBE-H exerts a significant influence on Purchase Intention in the apparel
sector.
From a theoretical perspective, this research advances the literature by empirically
demonstrating that consumer -based brand equity should be understood as an integrated and
dynamic hierarchical structure, in which cognitive, affective, and symbolic dimens ions reinforce
one another. This approach broadens the understanding of brand equity by offering a framework
more aligned with the relational and experiential nature of consumption, addressing the theoretical
gap concerning the lack of hierarchical measurement models.
From a practical standpoint, the results provide clear guidance for brand management in
categories with strong symbolic appeal. Managers should prioritize the development of Brand
Credibility and Reputation based on consistency and coherent communication; foster Brand Love
through authentic experiences; and strengthen Perceived Quality and Brand Image as rational
pillars of trust (Veloutsou & Moutinho, 2009; Carroll & Ahuvia, 2006; Aaker, 2021). Self -
Expression with the Brand and Brand Loyalty should be und erstood as outcomes of emotional
bonding and the symbolic value of the brand. Thus, the CBBE -H offers a useful managerial
framework to guide branding strategies that balance rationality, emotion, and identity.
