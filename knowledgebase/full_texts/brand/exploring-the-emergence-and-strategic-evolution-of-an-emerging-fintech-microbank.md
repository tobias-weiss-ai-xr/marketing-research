 Abstract
This paper examines the emergence and strategic evolution of InnBucks MicroBank Limited
(InnBucks), a Zimbabwean Microfinance organisation that evolved from a restaurant -change
management digital wallet into a licensed deposit-taking microfinance bank serving around 3 million
users within 4 years. Operating in what is arguably one of the most extreme VUCA (volatile, uncertain,
complex, ambiguous) environments globally, characterised by currency failures, chronic
hyperinflation, regulatory unpredictability, and severe infrastructure deficits, InnBucks offers a
compelling case for exploring how organisations emerge, adapt, and scale amid chaos. The analysis
incorporates five theoretical frameworks: Chaos Theory, Entrepreneurial Orientation Theory,
Disruptive Innovation and Frugal Innovation, the Viable System Model, and Strategic Decision -
Making Theory. These are utilised to explain InnBucks' institutional pivots, its evolution into a
registered deposit-taking microfinance, its utilisation of Simbisa Brands' re staurant network as
financial infrastructure, and the recent shift to cloud -native banking through the Mambu/AWS
partnership. This study adopts a qualitative conceptual research design grounded in theory synthesis
and a single-case analysis of an emerging organisation, using a desk-based approach. The analysis
follows abductive logic culminating in the ARISE (Agility, Resilience, Innovation, Systemic,
Entrepreneurial) conceptual framework, which combines five theoretical perspectives into a practical
model to steer strategic emergence in resource -limited, institutionally fragile environments. It
concludes that InnBucks' trajectory challenges traditional assumptions about the prerequisites for
financial innovation and provides insights for emerging organisations operating within Sub-Saharan
Africa's growing fintech ecosystem.

1. Introduction
Sub-Saharan Africa's emerging markets present radical challenges, as institutional voids and infrastructure deficits paradoxically
create both the necessity and the opportunity for disruptive solutions (Doh et al., 2017). In Zimbabwe, organisations operate in a
volatile, uncertain, complex, and ambiguous environment characterised by extreme economic fluctuations, liquidity crises, and rapid
regulatory transitions (Mwenje & Manyanga, 2023).
Within this environment, InnBucks MicroBank Limited emerged, evolved, and now serves approximately three million users as a
licensed deposit-taking microfinance institution. This paper provides a theoretically grounded, critical evaluation of how InnBucks
emerged, operates, and scales within Zimbabwe's VUCA environment, integrating analyses of environmental sustainability pressures,
the implications of artificial intelligence, and ethical governance considerations, culminating in a conceptual framework synthesised
from various theories.
The study applies five theoretical frameworks Chaos Theory, Entrepreneurial Orientation Theory, Disruptive Innovation, the Viable
System Model, and Strategic Decision-Making Theory and culminates in the proposal of the ARISE (Agility, Resilience, Innovation,
Systemic, Entrepreneurial Orientation) conceptual framework for guiding strategic emergence in resource-constrained contexts.
The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 provides the background, a historical overview of InnBucks, tracing its evolution from
a retail-based solution within Simbisa Brands to an independent financial service provider, its role in the economy and the risks and

2. Literature Review
2.1 Theoretical Background
Zimbabwe’s financial sector has been characterised by a "currency muddle," a term that captures the tensions and frequent currency
changes during a prolonged monetary crisis (Nyamunda, 2021). For decades, the country has experienced liquidity crises and
hyperinflation, which have eroded public confidence in formal banking systems (Chitimira & Torerai, 2023; Kawara et al., 2025). In
this environment, the scarcity of physical cash, particularly US dollar coins and small denominations, led to a distinctive market
failure in the retail sector termed the "change problem." Retailers often struggled to give change to customers, forcing them to accept
credit notes or unwanted items instead of cash. Against this backdrop of macroeconomic instability and the demand for digital
solutions, Simbisa Brands developed InnBucks.
2.1.1 A Brief History of InnBucks
InnBucks MicroBank Limited (InnBucks) is a registered deposit-taking microfinance Microbank in Zimbabwe and is regulated by
the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe (RBZ) under the Microfinance Act (Chapter 24:29) (InnBucks 2025, Annual Report). Zimbabwe's
dollarised economy faced chronic shortages of US dollar coins and small-denomination notes. When customers paid with larger bills
at Simbisa outlets, the restaurants could not give exact change. InnBucks was created as a closed-loop system to address the "change
problem" across the extensive network of outlets operated by Simbisa Brands Limited. It functioned as a digital wallet, allowing
customers to load their change into the InnBucks app or via SMS, which could then be used for future purchases or transferred to
other users. However, the platform's rapid growth transformed it from a basic retail solution into a comprehensive money transfer
service. In April 2022, the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe intervened, ordering InnBucks to cease its money transfer operations because
Simbisa had failed to regularise its service by obtaining the necessary approvals (RBZ Press Statement, 2022).
Following this suspension, the organisation underwent a strategic restructuring and, after securing a Deposit Taking Microfinance
Licence through a partnership with Ndoro Finance (Ndoro Microfinance Bank Limited et al., 2022), eventually returned to the market
as InnBucks MicroBank Limited.
2.1.2 Role in the local and regional economy
Financial Inclusion for the underserved
InnBucks has played a critical role in meeting the needs of underserved customer segments, who are often excluded from formal
banking due to high transaction costs and a lack of trust in traditional institutions (Kawara et al., 2025;). By leveraging Simbisa's
retail footprint, InnBucks provides a "low-barrier" entry point for low-income earners to deposit, withdraw, and transfer funds
(Chitimira & Torerai, 2021).
Facilitation of remittances and payments
The platform is a vital conduit for domestic remittances, enabling individuals to send money through a network that is often more
accessible than formal banks. Transactions such as cash deposits, withdrawals, purchases and bill payments are also processed. This
mirrors the success of other fintech models in Sub-Saharan Africa, such as M-Pesa and EcoCash, which have shown that lower
transaction costs and "reachability" are key drivers of the fintech revolution (Sithole et al., 2023).
Financial intermediation
InnBucks offers a comprehensive digital banking platform covering personal and business banking, loans, remittances, merchant
payments, treasury, international banking and value-added services. Its core competitive advantage lies in the integration of Simbisa's
physical restaurant outlets, which serve as cash-in/cash-out points, with a mobile-first digital platform accessible via USSD, Android
and iOS apps, and internet banking (InnBucks, Annual Report, 2025).
2.1.3 Risks and Challenges: Operating at the Edge of Chaos
InnBucks faces several significant challenges that reflect the broader complexities of the Zimbabwean business environment.
Regulatory Hurdles and Compliance
The main challenge has been navigating Zimbabwe's ambiguous and complex regulatory framework. The 2022 suspension of
InnBucks highlighted regulators' "inadequate preparation" for the rapid growth of fintech products (Chitimira & Torerai, 2023).
Additionally, as a licensed microfinance institution, InnBucks now faces stricter compliance requirements, including anti-money

in the country (Chitimira & Torerai, 2023).
Macroeconomic volatility
InnBucks operates in an environment characterised by extreme price volatility and currency depreciation, punctuated by successive
regime shifts, from the multi-currency system through the Zimbabwean dollar to the gold-backed ZiG introduced in April 2024

denominated revenues and the RBZ's tight monetary policy constrained lending. While InnBucks mitigates currency risk by using
USD as its functional currency, it cannot fully escape exposure to ZiG-denominated transactions and regulatory capital requirements.
Competitive risk
InnBucks faces stiff competition from established players such as EcoCash, which continues to dominate the mobile money
landscape, and from fintech-focused entities such as Mukuru and Senditoo (Chitimira & Torerai, 2023; Sithole et al., 2023). Mukuru
obtained a Deposit Taking Microfinance Licence, thereby competing in the lending sector. Maintaining market share requires
ongoing innovation in an environment where consumer "switching behaviour" is often driven by price and brand trust
(Nyakuwanikwa et al., 2024).
Digital infrastructure and trust
While digital financial inclusion correlates positively with bank stability, it is hindered by poor digital infrastructure and low public
"digital literacy" (Mnkandla et al., 2025;). Building and maintaining "digital trust" remains a persistent challenge, as many
Zimbabweans remain sceptical of new financial products due to historical losses caused by overnight policy changes (Chitimira et
al., 2024). InnBucks’ future success depends on its ability to navigate a rigorous regulatory landscape, withstand macroeconomic
shocks, and maintain the trust of a consumer base that has become wary of financial instability.
3. Research and Methodology
3.1 Research Design and Data Sources
This study adopts a qualitative conceptual research design grounded in theory synthesis, integrating concepts across multiple
theoretical perspectives and conducting a single-case analysis of an emerging organisation, using a desk-based approach. Tronvoll
& Plangger (2024) posit that the primary purpose of conceptual papers is to advance theory development rather than to report
empirical testing. Contributions are constructed through logical argumentation and the integration of existing theories.
The study's starting point is the evolution of an emerging organisation in an institutionally volatile environment, and the choice of
theories is justified by their capacity to address distinct aspects of that phenomenon. The analysis relies exclusively on secondary
evidence, including publicly available organisational materials, industry and market reports, scholarly literature, contextual economic
evidence, and documented organisational milestones.
Each theory was analysed individually for its central constructs, underlying assumptions, explanatory mechanisms, and limitations.
An across-theory comparison matrix was developed to identify areas of convergence, complementarity, and tension. The analysis
followed an abductive logic. Evidence on InnBucks' origins, operating model, growth trajectory, technology adoption, entrepreneurial
culture, and risk responses was iteratively compared with the theoretical constructs to refine the theory.
As no primary data collection was undertaken, claims are treated as analytically grounded interpretations rather than independently
verified causal findings. Validation was treated as a dynamic, ongoing process of matching theory with empirical observation rather
than a one-off procedural check at the end of the study (Andersen et al., 2018).
The purpose of the case is analytical rather than statistical generalisation. Accordingly, this study seeks to develop theoretically
transferable propositions for emerging-economy firms operating under institutional volatility, rather than to claim population-level
causal effects (Johannessen & Olsen 2023).
The single-case focus prioritises depth and theoretical insight over breadth. Second, reliance on secondary data means the analysis is
constrained by the availability, accuracy, and completeness of publicly documented evidence. Third, because the findings are
interpretive, they are presented as analytically grounded propositions for further investigation rather than empirical conclusions.
4. Framing InnBucks as an Emerging Organisation
Emerging organisations are characterised by innovation-driven business models, entrepreneurial leadership, resource constraints, an
ambition for scalability, a tolerance for risk, and the capacity to disrupt established market structures (Lay & Tafese, 2025). InnBucks
aligns with each of these characteristics in ways that merit detailed examination.

4.1 Innovation and Disruption
InnBucks' core innovation of converting physical cash change from restaurant transactions into digital wallet balances exemplifies
what Christensen (1997) describes as a disruptive innovation entering at the lowest tier of the market. Percentage-based fee structures
dominate African mobile money markets (Alade & Kavame Eroglu, 2023) and the subsequent launch of the KaOne subscription
model in June 2024, offering unlimited transactions for a flat fee of US$1 per month, disrupted the prevailing pricing model. This
pricing innovation, while attracting a 20% increase in active customers, also reduced fee income by approximately 40% in the first
half of FY2025 (InnBucks Annual Results, 2025) demonstrating the creative destruction dynamics inherent in innovative business
models. However, the sustainability of the KaOne model remains to be seen.
4.2 Resource constraints and Institutional Bricolage
Instead of building a traditional branch network, which is extremely costly in Zimbabwe’s financially constrained environment,
InnBucks utilised Simbisa Brands’ extensive restaurant outlet network as cash-in and cash-out points. This approach effectively
employed an agent-based distribution model to expand financial access at lower costs (FinScope Zimbabwe, 2022; Gomber et al.,
2021; Simbisa Brands, 2023). This exemplifies institutional bricolage: the inventive recombination of existing resources for new
purposes, aligned with effectual logic under conditions of resource scarcity (Sunguh & Xu, 2024).
4.3 Scalability and Growth Trajectory
InnBucks' growth metrics demonstrate strong scalability. The user base expanded from 1.8 million in September 2023 to
approximately 3 million by May 2025. Total assets grew by 313% to ZiG 2.46 billion in FY2025; loans and advances surged by
327% to ZiG 1.5 billion; and profit after tax nearly tripled (InnBucks Annual Report, 2025). The May 2025 partnership with Mambu,
built on AWS cloud infrastructure, explicitly positions InnBucks for cross-border expansion across the 9–10 African countries where
Simbisa Brands already operates (Mambu Press Release, 2023). Engaging local retailers as agents and forming partnerships for
international remittances extended the organisation’s market reach.
4.4 Disruption of Traditional Financial Services
Emerging fintech organisations challenge established financial institutions by offering accessible digital alternatives that reduce
friction in payments and savings (Gomber et al., 2021; Kuckertz et al., 2020). InnBucks’ app -based transactions and constant
availability at merchant locations directly address the limitations of traditional bank branch models. In Zimbabwe’s context of
intermittent cash shortages, InnBucks’ ability to enable reliable USD transactions positions it as a disruptive force within the national
financial ecosystem, complementing yet distinct from earlier mobile money platforms such as EcoCash (World Bank, 2022; Kabanda
& Brown, 2021).
4.5 Entrepreneurial Leadership and Regulatory Adaptation
The RBZ's regulatory ban, which ordered the immediate cessation of InnBucks operations in April 2022 for operating without an
Authorised Dealer with Limited Authority licence (RBZ, 2022, April), would have been terminal for many startups. However, the
organisation executed a remarkable institutional pivot by acquiring Ndoro Microfinance Bank to secure deposit-taking licences,
restructuring ownership, and relaunching as a fully compliant financial institution. (Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe, 2022; Sengere,
2022). This adaptive capacity in the face of existential regulatory threat exemplifies the entrepreneurial leadership that defines
emerging organisations (Saleh & Athari, 2023).
The deposit-taking microfinance route demonstrates some entrepreneurial leadership. InnBucks successfully entered banking at the
lower end of the spectrum. Setting up a microfinance bank requires fewer resources than a traditional commercial bank. (RBZ,
Minimum Licensing Requirements for Banking Institutions, June 2025).
5. Theoretical Engagement
This section critically applies five theoretical and conceptual frameworks to explain the emergence, structure, decision-making,
innovation, and adaptation of InnBucks to external challenges. The selection of theories is deliberate: collectively they address the
organisation's internal dynamics (VSM), strategic posture (Entrepreneurial Orientation), innovation pathway (Disruptive Innovation),
environmental interaction (Chaos Theory), and leadership cognition (Strategic Decision-Making Theory).
5.1 Chaos Theory and Complexity
Chaos theory, as applied to organisations by Stacey (1996) and extended by contemporary scholars (Taskan et al., 2022; Sharma et
al., 2025), holds that complex adaptive systems operate through positive and negative feedback loops, coevolve far from equilibrium,
and self-organise in unpredictable yet patterned ways. The concept of the 'edge of chaos', the zone between rigid order and complete
disorder where systems exhibit maximum creativity and adaptability provides a powerful metaphor for InnBucks' operati ng
environment.

Modern chaos theory in organisational research is increasingly framed through "Chaordic Economics," in which artificial intelligence
and digital platforms are used to manage systems that are simultaneously chaotic and ordered (Treiblmaier, 2025).
Zimbabwe's macroeconomic environment is characterised by the 'sensitive dependence on initial conditions' that chaos theory
identifies as fundamental to complex systems. Small regulatory decisions by the RBZ, such as the April 2022 InnBucks ban, produce
cascading, nonlinear effects throughout the financial ecosystem. The June 2020 government suspension of all mobile money services
(RBZ, 2020), ostensibly to combat illegal foreign exchange dealing, disrupted millions of users and paradoxically created market
space for new entrants like InnBucks. The Stacey Matrix (Stacey, 1996) places virtually all of InnBucks' strategic decisions in the
'complex' to 'chaotic' zone, where traditional rational planning is insufficient and emergent, adaptive approaches are essential.
InnBucks' trajectory illustrates self-organisation within chaos. The transformation from a loyalty wallet to a licensed bank was not
the product of a predetermined strategic plan but rather an emergent response to a regulatory crisis. This aligns with Stacey's (1996)
argument that in chaotic environments, organisations must rely on bounded instability, maintaining sufficient structure to avoid
dissolution while preserving enough flexibility to adapt. InnBucks' dual-channel accessibility (USSD and mobile app), dual-currency
capability (ZiG and USD), and partnership-based scaling strategy all function as adaptive mechanisms within an inherently chaotic
system.
Recent empirical evidence from Zimbabwe suggests that Complexity Leadership serves as a critical moderator of organisational
adaptability during periods of dramatic social change (Chingwena & Scheepers, 2022). Firms like InnBucks thrive by operating at
the "edge of chaos," utilising feedback loops to self-organise in response to non-linear environmental shifts (Chingwena & Scheepers,
2022; Treiblmaier, 2025).
5.2 Entrepreneurial Orientation Theory
Entrepreneurial Orientation (EO) theory, originating in the seminal work of Covin and Slevin (1989) and refined by Lumpkin and
Dess (1996), identifies five dimensions of strategic posture that distinguish entrepreneurially oriented firms: innovativeness, risk-
taking, proactiveness, competitive aggressiveness, and autonomy.
InnBucks demonstrates high EO across all five dimensions. Innovativeness is evident in the ‘change wallet’ and the remittance
platform, the restaurant-as-infrastructure approach, and the cloud-native Mambu migration. Risk-taking is evident in the decision to
acquire a microfinance bank licence after the regulatory ban, rather than withdraw from the market. Proactiveness is demonstrated
by the early move into South African remittances via Clicksendnow in September 2023 and the Mambu partnership, which positions
InnBucks for pan-African expansion. Competitive aggressiveness is reflected in the KaOne pricing, which directly undercuts
EcoCash's percentage-based fee model and accepts short-term revenue cannibalisation (40% fee-income decline) to build market
share. Autonomy was structurally enabled by the Simbisa spin-off, which gave InnBucks independent governance under a dedicated
Board (InnBucks Annual Report, 2025).
Covin and Slevin's (1989) finding that entrepreneurial posture is more effective in hostile environments is particularly instructive.
Saleh and Athari (2023), studying EO in Lebanon's crisis-affected economy, found that EO significantly enhances new venture
performance through opportunity exploitation, with transformational leadership moderating this relationship. InnBucks' growth
during this period supports this theoretical prediction, although the KaOne-induced revenue decline warrants caution about the
sustainability of aggressive competitive strategies in resource-constrained environments.
Recent studies on EO in emerging economies emphasise that proactivity and entrepreneurial adaptability are essential for navigating
institutional voids and resource constraints (Alkharafi et al., 2024; Chrisben et al., 2025). For a fintech operating in a volatile retail
industry, EO is a strategic necessity for sensing market shifts and pivoting ahead of competitors (Chrisben et al., 2025). This is
particularly relevant in hostile environments, where strategic agility is directly correlated with organisatio nal performance
(Atanassova et al., 2024). InnBucks’ trajectory reflects these characteristics.
5.3 Disruptive Innovation and Frugal Innovation
Christensen's (1997) disruptive innovation theory and its recent application to African fintech contexts by Alade & Eroglu (2023)
provide essential explanatory power. Alade & Kavame Eroglu (2023) examined the implications of fintech for Africa, finding that
disruptive innovations in financial services simultaneously threaten established banking institutions and create unprecedente d
opportunities for financial inclusion in underserved markets. InnBucks entered the bottom of Zimbabwe's financial services market,
solving a coin-change problem, and is progressively migrating upmarket towards full-service digital banking with savings, loans,
and remittances.
Issami and Tandamba (2023) examined mobile money as a disruptive fintech for inclusive finance in Africa, showing how mobile-
based financial services are fundamentally transforming access to finance across the continent. InnBucks serves a population in which
82% of rural adults earn US$100 or less per month, only 46% hold traditional bank accounts, and 845,000 adults remain entirely
financially excluded (FinScope Zimbabwe, 2022)
The KaOne model, which offers unlimited digital banking for US$1 per month, is tailored to this price-sensitive market segment,
reflecting the critical role of perceived affordability in driving mobile fintech adoption in Sub-Saharan Africa, as identified in a recent

5.4 Viable System Model (vsm)
Beer's (1984) Viable System Model offers a structural perspective for analysing InnBucks' organisational architecture and its potential
for long-term sustainability. The VSM, recently extended by Espinosa (2023) for sustainable self-governance in businesses and
societies, identifies five interconnected systems essential for organisational survival: System 1 (Operations), System 2
(Coordination), System 3 (Control), System 4 (Intelligence), and System 5 (Policy). Epinosa (2022) demonstrated that the VSM
provides a holistic approach to organisational sustainability, grounded in the principles of viability, recursiveness, and autonomy, all
of which are directly relevant to InnBucks' context.
At System 1 (Operations), InnBucks delivers its core value through the digital wallet, lending, remittance, and merchant payment
services. System 2 (Coordination) is achieved through integration with Zimswitch for national payment interoperability and with the
Mambu cloud platform for unified banking operations. System 3 (Control) encompasses RBZ regulatory compliance, the credit risk
management framework, and internal audit functions; the company's capital adequacy ratio of 17.6% exceeds the 15% regulatory
minimum (InnBucks Annual Report, 2025), demonstrating controlled risk management. System 4 (Intelligence) represents the
organisation's environmental scanning capability, including market intelligence on competitor moves, technological horizon scanning
(the Mambu/AWS partnership reflects System 4 functioning), and the potential deployment of AI-driven analytics. Sydelko et al.
(2024), applying VSM to interagency responses to complex problems, demonstrated how System 4's intelligence function becomes
critical in environments characterised by rapid change and uncertainty. System 5 (Policy) is embodied in the board governance
structure and in the ethical framework that guides decisions on data protection, lending practices, and stakeholder relations (InnBucks
Annual Report, 2025).
Critically, the VSM reveals potential vulnerabilities. InnBucks' acknowledgement of 'partial compliance' with certain regulatory
requirements (InnBucks Annual Report, 2025) suggests that System 3 (Control) may be under strain as the organisation scales rapidly.
The absence of a published sustainability strategy suggests that System 5 (Policy) has not yet fully integrated environmental and
social governance considerations, despite the parent company, Simbisa Brands, having an active ESG agenda. Beer (1984) argued
that organisations become unviable when any of the five systems fails or when the recursive connections between systems break
down, a risk that Arghand et al. (2022), in their application of VSM to IT risk management in banking, found to intensify during
periods of rapid scaling and technological transformation.
To maintain viability, an organisation must structure its operations so that autonomous units can respond independently to
environmental variety, since any viable system contains and is contained within a viable system (Vik et al., 2021), with System 1
units interacting directly with their respective environments. This systemic approach ensures that coordination and control
mechanisms remain functional despite rapid regulatory and policy changes, enabling resilience and adaptive response in times of
instability (Castro, 2019).
5.5 Strategic Decision-Making Theory
Strategic decision-making under uncertainty has been examined through multiple theoretical lenses, from Sarasvathy's (2001)
effectuation theory to recent integrative frameworks. Sunguh and Xu (2024) examined entrepreneurial orientation during the post-
COVID crisis in developing countries. They found that effectuation's affordable loss and flexibility dimensions, along with bricolage,
respond more strongly to internal locus of control.  This significantly impacts individual entrepreneurial orientation during crises and
periods of scarcity. Cherbib (2024) further extended this understanding by demonstrating the interplay between effectual and causal
reasoning in large organisations under unexpected uncertainty, finding that firms adaptively switch between decision logics
depending on context. InnBucks' strategic decisions reflect effectual reasoning rather than classical causal logic. Sarasvathy (2001)
distinguished between causal reasoning (starting with a desired end and seeking means) and effectual reasoning (starting with
available means and imagining possible ends). The original InnBucks concept, using digital wallets to solve a coin-change problem,
exemplifies effectuation: Simbisa Brands started with its existing means (restaurant network, customer traffic, digital platform
capability) and imagined an achievable end (digital change management). When the regulatory ban invalidated this trajectory,
leadership pivoted again, leveraging the available means of Nd oro Microfinance Bank's licence to create an entirely new
organisational form. In conditions of extreme uncertainty, effective decision-making integrates scenario analysis with vigilant
learning to strengthen organisational resilience (Atanassova et al., 2024). In a VUCA world, employee behaviour and organisational
culture are the primary drivers of successful strategy implementation (Boikanyo, 2024). This requires adopting 'fast and frugal'
heuristics and processing information in real time to maintain strategic agility (Atanassova et al., 2024). The decision to launch
KaOne at US$1 per month, despite knowing it would cannibalise existing fee income, reflects a dynamic decision-making process.
While the decision facilitated market entry, it poses some threat to fee income and its sustainability is questionable.

6. Developing a Conceptual Model: The Arise Framework
Drawing on the theoretical frameworks outlined above and the practical realities of InnBucks' operating context, this paper proposes
the ARISE (Agility, Resilience, Innovation, Systemic Viability, Entrepreneurial) framework as a conceptual model to guide strategic
emergence and sustainability in VUCA environments. The framework, presented visually in Figure 1 and explained below,
synthesises theoretical insights into an integrated, actionable architecture.

Figure 1: The ARISE Framework - Agility, Resilience, Innovation, Systemic, Entrepreneurial (ARISE). Source: Author's
construct.
Agility (A) is the organisation’s ability to adapt to internal or external change while remaining competitive. In strategic management,
agility is conceptualised as a meta-capability that enables an organisation to proactively anticipate, sense, and respond to rapid
environmental shifts by redefining its corporate and functional strategies to create value (Diego & Almodóvar, 2021). InnBucks has
demonstrated strong agility in a volatile environment.
Resilience (R) is defined as the capacity to thrive amid turbulence through strategic adaptation and operational excellence (Jengwa
& Pellissier, 2022). By leveraging mobile money technology, InnBucks builds a sustainable entrepreneurial ecosystem that is resilient
to economic pressures and local resource constraints (Chitimira & Torerai, 2023). The ability to pivot and return to the market in the
face of regulatory challenges demonstrates InnBucks' resilience.
Innovative Frugality (I) refers to the development of high-value, low-cost digital solutions that bridge institutional voids and promote
financial inclusion (Chrisben et al., 2025; Luiz et al., 2021). By utilising entrepreneurial adaptability to navigate gaps in formal
financial structures, InnBucks provides essential services to consumers who are often excluded by traditional banking fees and
requirements ( Chrisben et al., 2025).

Systemic Viability (S) - Drawing from modern applications of the Viable System Model, this component ensures that the organisation
can manage environmental variety through recursive, autonomous units (Hakiki et al., 2024).
Entrepreneurial Orientation (E) serves as the behavioural driver of the framework, emphasising proactivity and innovative risk-taking
in emerging economies (Alkharafi et al., 2024).
6.1 Components of the Arise Framework
The outermost layer represents the VUCA macro-environment: currency volatility, regulatory uncertainty, infrastructure deficits, and
climate risk. Informed by Taskan et al.'s (2022) systematisation of VUCA elements in emerging markets, this layer sets the external
conditions within which all organisational activity occurs.
The second layer is the Chaos and Complexity Layer, drawn from Stacey (1996) and recent applications. This layer acknowledges
that nonlinear dynamics, sensitive dependence on initial conditions, and the emergence of self-organised patterns characterise the
relationship between the organisation and its environment (Thiétart & Forgues, 1995; Long et al., 2018).
Rather than attempting to control or predict the environment, the ARISE framework positions the organisation at the 'edge of chaos',
maintaining adaptive capacity through feedback loops that connect outcomes back to strategic inputs.
6.1.1 Input
The third layer comprises three strategic input pillars. The first pillar, Entrepreneurial Orientation, drawn from Lumpkin and Dess
(1996), Covin and Slevin (1989), and recent empirical extensions (Bataineh et al., 2025), encompasses the five EO dimensions
applied to InnBucks' strategic posture. The second pillar, Disruptive and Frugal Innovation, drawn from Christensen (1997) and its
African fintech applications (Alade & Eroglu, 2023; Issami & Tandamba, 2023), captures the innovation pathway from bottom-of-
the-pyramid market entry through upmarket migration, including the integration of AI-enabled credit analytics. The third pillar,
Strategic Decision-Making under VUCA, drawn from Sarasvathy (2001), Sunguh and Xu (2024), and Cherbib (2024), addresses the
cognitive and processual dimensions of leadership decision-making amid bounded rationality and radical uncertainty.
6.1.2 Processes
These three pillars feed into the VSM-Informed Organisational Core, which forms the fourth and central layer. This is the processing
core. Based on Beer's (1984) five systems and Espinosa's (2023) extension to sustainable self-governance, this core guarantees that
operational delivery (S1), coordination mechanisms (S2), regulatory compliance and risk management (S3), environmental
intelligence and AI-driven analytics (S4), and governance policy and ethical oversight (S5) are all operational and interconnected
recursively (Ramírez-Gutiérrez et al., 2020) At the core of all these activities are dynamic capabilities which enable the organisation
to sense opportunities, seize them and transform (Teece et al., 2018).
Two cross-cutting dimensions, sustainability and green pressures (on the left) and AI and digital technology (on the right), directly
influence the organisational core. These concepts have a direct impact on the organisational strategy and processes, which are central
in VSM.
6.1.3 Outputs
The framework's outputs are four strategic outcomes: financial inclusion (reaching underserved populations), organisational viability
(regulatory legitimacy and financial sustainability), sustainable growth (product and geographic diversification with ESG
integration), and ethical governance (data protection, AI ethics, and stakeholder trust). Critically, these outcomes loop bac k
recursively to the Chaos and Complexity Layer, reflecting the continuous adaptation that chaos theory identifies as essential for
organisational survival in far-from-equilibrium environments.
The ARISE framework's strength lies in integrating multiple theoretical perspectives into a coherent whole that recognises th e
genuine complexity of operating in VUCA environments. Unlike linear strategic planning models, it acknowledges that emergence
is iterative, recursive, and path dependent. Unlike pure chaos theory, it offers structural support for organisational design (via the
VSM). The framework can be adapted to other emerging fintech organisations in Sub-Saharan Africa, provided the specific VUCA
dimensions, EO configurations, and innovation pathways are tailored to the particular operating environment.
7. Critical Discussions
7.1 Innovation and Technology
InnBucks' innovation trajectory from USSD wallet to cloud-native banking demonstrates a shift from incremental to architectural
innovation. The Mambu/AWS partnership represents a fundamental reconfiguration of the organisation's technological architecture,
enabling API-first product development, real-time data analytics, and future AI integration. Panday (2024), studying financial
technology adoption in South African banking, found that disruptive technologies are embraced when they are fit-for-purpose and
deliver client-centric value, which aligns with InnBucks' progressive platform migration. However, the absence of documented AI
deployment for credit scoring or fraud detection, despite the platform's technical capability, suggests that InnBucks remains in the

early stages of digital maturity. The potential for AI to enable automated lending decisions for previously unbanked populations is
significant but carries risks of algorithmic bias, particularly if training data reflects existing socioeconomic inequalities (Cele, 2024).
7.2 Strategic Planning and Agility
InnBucks' strategic trajectory challenges the dichotomy between deliberate and emergent strategy recognised by scholars. The
organisation's development has been largely emergent, responding to regulatory shocks, opportunistic in acquiring Ndoro
Microfinance Bank, and experimental in launching KaOne. However, the Mambu partnership and pan-African expansion plans signal
a growing element of deliberate strategic intent. Al-Awlaqi et al. (2025), in their systematic review of leadership agility in VUCA
worlds, identified dynamic capabilities, systems thinking, and change management as key mediators that enhance strategic agility.
This tension between agility and planning characterises organisations operating at the edge of chaos (Stacey, 1996), where excessive
planning leads to rigidity, while excessive emergence results in incoherence. The ARISE framework addresses this tension by
integrating strategic agility within a structured VSM architecture.
7.3 Organisational Structure and Culture
The transition from Simbisa Brands’ subsidiary to an independently governed microfinance bank has been vital for regulatory
credibility and strategic autonomy. However, the Simbisa heritage offers advantages such as restaurant infrastructure, brand
recognition, and cross-selling opportunities. Challenges include potential conflicts between restaurant and banking priorities, as well
as reputational contagion risk. The appointment of a new CEO in January 2025, who possesses extensive pan-African banking
experience, signals a cultural shift towards professional financial services management (InnBucks Annual Report, 2025).
Whether the entrepreneurial culture, risk tolerance, and improvisational ability underpinning InnBucks’ ascent can coexist with the
compliance-driven culture mandated by banking regulation remains uncertain. Cherbib (2024) characterises this tension as the
interplay between effectual and causal reasoning within a single organisation.
7.4 Regulatory and Legal Issues
The regulatory dimension is both InnBucks' greatest vulnerability and its most instructive feature. The April 2022 ban underscored
the existential nature of regulatory risk in Zimbabwe, where the RBZ can order immediate operational cessation (RBZ Press Release,
April 2022). The subsequent regulatory journey, including obtaining DTMFI licensing, achieving capital adequacy (InnBucks Annual
Report, 2025), and navigating an evolving landscape that includes the Cyber and Data Protection Act (2021) and S.I. 17 of 2025, the
mobile banking regulations, illustrates both adaptive capacity and ongoing compliance strain.
The InnBucks FY2025 report's acknowledgement of 'partial compliance' with some requirements warrants monitoring (InnBucks
Annual Report, 2025).
The patchwork of overlapping legislation (Microfinance Act, Banking Act, National Payment Systems Act, Money Laundering Act)
creates compliance complexity that disproportionately burdens smaller institutions. Ncube et al. (2024), examining fintech adoption
and financial inclusion in Sub-Saharan Africa, found that effective regulatory frameworks are essential for ensuring that fintech does
not displace but rather enhances traditional financial inclusion. Balancing Zimbabwe's patchwork of overlapping legislation makes
it particularly challenging.
The Zimbabwean banking sector is currently restructuring its compliance frameworks to align with the fintech revolution, drawing
heavily on regional benchmarks, particularly from South Africa (Marange & Hamadziripi, 2026). However, the tension between
advancing digital financial services and safeguarding consumer rights remains a vital legal challenge (Chitimira & Torerai, 2023).
Regulatory strategies must balance these aims to prevent the exclusion of the poor during rapid technological changes (Chitimira &
Torerai, 2023).
7.5 Customer Acquisition and Relations
InnBucks' customer acquisition strategy has been remarkably effective, growing from zero to three million users in under four years
(InnBucks Annual Report, 2025). The Simbisa restaurant network provides a unique organic acquisition channel: customers
encounter InnBucks through routine dining transactions rather than through conventional financial marketing. The KaOne
subscription model has proven effective for acquisition (InnBucks Annual Report, 2025) but poses a sustainability challenge if per-
user revenue declines faster than user number growth.
Initially, customer acquisition was driven by the change transactions and the subsequent remittances function, with every cashier
acting as a customer acquisition agent. However, InnBucks became more aggressive in customer acquisition. By onboarding external
retailers such as TM Pick n Pay, Edgars, Jet, and Spar, the organisation expanded its market footprint  (TechZim, 2024). The
organisation also launched corporate payroll services and integrated international remittances through partners such as Mama Money
and Clicksendnow.
InnBucks reported a 327% increase in loans and advances for the year ended June 2025, alongside its migration to a scalable wallet
platform and the deployment of a Customer Relationship Management platform managed by human and AI agents (InnBucks Annual

7.6 Risk Management and Performance Monitoring
InnBucks manages a layered risk profile covering currency risk (ZiG devaluation, dual-currency exposure), credit risk (rapidly
expanding loan book growing 327% in FY2025), operational risk (electricity instability, mobile network disruptions), and
competitive risk. The swift growth of the loan book requires particularly careful oversight:  the quality of this portfolio under
Zimbabwe's macroeconomic stress conditions remains to be tested through a full economic cycle. The RBZ's tight monetary policy,
with restrictive interest rates and high statutory reserve requirements, constrains lending capacity while simultaneously increasing
borrower default risk.  Performance monitoring via AI-driven analytics, enabled by the Mambu platform, could significantly improve
real-time risk assessment, but requires investment in data infrastructure, talent, and ethical governance frameworks.
Achieving operational excellence requires a strategy implementation model tailored to volatile and ambiguous environments.
Monitoring the quality of expanding loan books remains a priority as firms scale amid hyperinflationary stress (Jengwa & Pellissier,
2022; Nyamunda, 2021).
7.7 Sustainability and Environmental Considerations
InnBucks' sustainability contribution is best assessed through the environmental, social, and governance lens increasingly applied to
financial technology firms. In emerging markets, fintech development has been shown to enhance corporate ESG performance, with
the effect strongest among smaller firms and technology companies (Wang et al., 2022). This is encouraging for InnBucks, whose
digital-first, asset-light operating model aligns well with the ESG expectations now attached to fintech.
InnBucks' environmental contribution stems largely from the inherent sustainability advantages of digital financial services. In
Zimbabwe, where rural residents face limited access to financial services, digital financial services represent a substantial
environmental and social intervention. Environmentally, digital payment platforms can finance sustainable projects, including
renewable energy initiatives and eco-friendly agricultural practices, thereby linking financial inclusion with environmental benefits
(Dudu et al., 2024). More broadly, fintech innovation supports sustainable development by improving resource efficiency, reducing
carbon footprints, and expanding access to green products and services (Offiong et al., 2025).
The social pillar is where InnBucks' impact is greatest. Financial inclusion is at the centre of the social dimension of ESG (Mellina
et al., 2024). Cross-country evidence from 103 developing economies shows that financial access is positively correlated with social
and governance sustainability indicators, including education expenditure, internet penetration, sanitation, gender equity, and anti-
corruption (Drago et al., 2025). By extending mobile payments to underbanked populations in Zimbabwe, InnBucks a dvances
financial inclusion and, through it, the social sustainability targets of the Sustainable Development Goals. Espinosa (2023) argued
that unless organisations establish proper self-governance structures that incorporate sustainability, there is limited hope of achieving
the Sustainable Development Goals, a principle directly relevant to InnBucks' need to develop a formal ESG strategy.
The RBZ's Climate Risk Management Guideline (2023) and the Zimbabwe Stock Exchange's mandatory ESG disclosures establish
an evolving regulatory expectation for sustainability reporting that InnBucks must address as it continues to develop. Simbisa Brands'
shift to biodegradable packaging and solar energy installations provides a corporate governance model that InnBucks might extend
through green lending products, carbon footprint reporting, and the integration of climate risk into its credit assessment frameworks.
8. Conclusion
This study examined the emergence and strategic evolution of InnBucks MicroBank Limited in Zimbabwe’s highly volatile,
uncertain, complex, and ambiguous (VUCA) environment. The case suggests that institutional fragility, currency instability, and
infrastructure constraints do not solely hinder fintech development. When combined with entrepreneurial action, frugal innovation,
and adaptive governance, they can also create opportunities for inclusive financial innovation. InnBucks’ progression from a
restaurant-based digital-change wallet to a licensed deposit-taking microfinance institution illustrates how emerging organisations
can turn an immediate market failure into a scalable financial-services platform. Its growth was enabled by the strategic use of
Simbisa Brands’ retail network, an institutional pivot following regulatory intervention, and the progressive development of digital
and cloud-based capabilities.
The study’s principal theoretical contribution is the ARISE framework (Agility, Resilience, Innovation, Systemic Viability and
Entrepreneurial Orientation). The framework provides a context-sensitive account of strategic emergence in resource-constrained
and institutionally fragile markets. In particular, it reconciles emergent strategy with organisational control. Firms need flexibility to
respond to non-linear shocks, but also sufficient systemic coordination, risk management and policy oversight to remain viable and
legitimate.
For managers, the findings underscore the need to build adaptive capability without compromising governance. Fintech leaders
should combine rapid experimentation with regulatory preparedness and leverage existing physical networks and partnerships as
low-cost financial infrastructure. Developing real-time market intelligence to respond to currency, competitive and policy shocks is

key. For InnBucks, this entails strengthening VSM System 4 intelligence through responsible data analytics for fraud detection, credit
risk assessment and customer segmentation; expanding customer relationship management capacity in line with user growth; and
closely monitoring asset quality, liquidity, operational resilience and dual-currency exposure.
For policymakers, the adoption of balanced, innovation-supportive frameworks that enable fintechs to test and scale products without
compromising consumer protection, financial stability or anti-money-laundering obligations. The adoption of harmonised licensing
requirements and coordinated oversight across payment, banking, data-protection and microfinance laws would reduce uncertainty
for emerging firms while preserving effective supervision. Finally, fintech regulation should adopt stronger standards for algorithmic
accountability, cybersecurity, data privacy, climate risk management, and ESG disclosure, ensuring that financial inclusion is both
sustainable and rights-respecting.
The study relies on a single, context-specific case, and its findings may not be uncritically generalised to all fintechs or African
markets. Second, the analysis is largely based on publicly available company, regulatory and secondary sources, which may be
incomplete, selectively reported or rapidly superseded in Zimbabwe’s changing macroeconomic and regulatory environment.
Thirdly, ARISE is a conceptual framework and has not yet been empirically validated. The longer-term consequences of rapid loan-
book growth, cloud migration, AI adoption, ESG practices and regional expansion also remain uncertain.
Future research should empirically test the ARISE framework through comparative and longitudinal studies of fintechs in Zimbabwe
and other Sub-Saharan African markets. Mixed-methods research with managers, regulators, agents and customers would clarify
how agility, resilience, trust, governance and inclusion interact in practice. Quantitative studies could develop and validate measures
of the five ARISE dimensions and test their relationships with financial inclusion, organisational viability, customer retention and
risk outcomes. Such work would transform ARISE from a contextually grounded conceptual model into an evidence -based
framework for managing strategic emergence in VUCA environments.
InnBucks' journey demonstrates that highly volatile environments can foster the most resilient organisations, as long as an
entrepreneurial mindset, innovation, and adaptable decision-making are consistently cultivated. The company's story continues, and
its ultimate success or failure will depend on its ability to sustain the adaptive resilience that has guided it thus far and to develop the
institutional maturity necessary for long-term sustainability.
