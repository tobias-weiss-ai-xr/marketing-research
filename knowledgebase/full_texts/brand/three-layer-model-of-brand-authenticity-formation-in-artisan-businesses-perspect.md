	Background:	Brand	 authenticity	 is	 increasingly	 relevant	 to	 the
sustainability	of	artisan	enterprises,	yet	empirical	research	offers	limited
insight	into	how	loyal	customers	come	to	perceive	a	brand	as	authentic,
particularly	in	Indonesia's	artisan	sector.
Objective:	This	 study	 investigates	 how	loyal	 customers	 construct
perceptions	 of	 brand	 authenticity	 through	 a	 single-case	 examination	 of
Alazkalogy,	an	artisan	brand	located	in	Ponorogo,	East	Java.
Methods:	The	research	used	a	qualitative	single-case	design	and	applied
the	 Critical	 Incident	 Technique	 (CIT).	 Evidence	 was	 obtained	 from	 in-
depth	interviews	with	ten	loyal	customers	whose	relationships	with	the
brand	averaged	seven	years;	the	interview	data	were	examined	through
open,	axial,	and	selective	coding.
Results:	The	 findings	 reveal	 a	 three-layer	 model	 of	 brand	 authenticity
formation,	 consisting	 of	 experience-based	 authenticity,	 process-based
authenticity,	 and	 decision-based	 authenticity.	 These	 three	 layers
represent	a	cumulative	and	temporal	process	in	which	customers	develop
deeper	 perceptions	 of	 authenticity	 as	 their	 relationship	 with	 the	 brand
evolves	 over	 time.	 Customers	 with	 longer	 relationships	 accessed	 more
advanced	 layers	 of	 authenticity,	 whereas	 newer	 customers	 primarily
experienced	the	initial	layer.
Conclusion:	This	 study	 extends	the	 brand	 authenticity	 literature	 by
proposing	 a	 conceptual	 model	 that	 explains	 how	 perceptions	 of
authenticity	evolve	through	accumulated	customer	experiences	within	the
context	of	artisan	businesses.

INTRODUCTION
Artisan	 businesses	 face	 asymmetric	 competitive	 pressures:	 on	 one	 side,	 they	 compete
against	mass-market	brands	with	substantial	marketing	budgets;	on	the	other,	they	must	maintain
the	 uniqueness	 that	 is	 precisely	 the	 reason	 customers	 choose	 them.	 Under	 these	 conditions,
authenticity	 becomes	 a	 distinctive	 competitive	 resource:	 customers	 evaluate	 whether	 a	 brand
behaves	 truthfully,	 remains	 coherent	 over	 time,	 and	 acts	 in	 accordance	 with	 its	 stated	 values
(Beverland,	2005;	Li	et	al.,	2024;	Södergren,	2021).
Research	 on	 brand	 authenticity	 has	 grown	 significantly	 over	 the	 past	 two	 decades,	 yet
most	remains	focused	on	large	corporate	brands	in	Western	contexts	(Li	et	al.,	2024;	Södergren,
2021).	Halwani	and	Cherry	(2024)	confirm	that	research	still	requires	deeper	exploration	of	how
consumers	 build	 and	 verify	 authenticity	 perceptions	personally,	 especially	 for	 brands	 without
formal	marketing	departments.	Meanwhile,	Hasanah	et	al.	(2023)		identify	that	five	decades	of

In	 this	 study,	 brand	 authenticity	 is	 understood	 as	 consumers'	 judgment	 that	 a	 brand
communicates	and	behaves	in	a	truthful,	coherent,	and	value-consistent	manner	(Li	et	al.,	2024;
Södergren,	2021).	Beverland	(2005)	and	Ranfagni	and	Salvietti	(2023),	in	a	pioneering	study	of
26	 luxury	 wine	 producers,	 identified	 that	 authenticity	 is	 built	 through	 narratives	 reflecting
craftsmanship,	 place	 uniqueness,	 and	 commitment	 to	 quality,	 while	 simultaneously	 avoiding
excessive	 commercial	 impressions.	 This	 finding	is	 relevant	 to	 artisan	 fermentation	 businesses
that	share	similar	production	characteristics:	small-batch,	craftsmanship,	and	benefit	orientation.
Beverland	and	Farrelly	 (2010)	and	Mann	 (2023)	extended	 this	 argument	 by	 showing	 that
consumers	actively	select	authentic	cues	that	align	with	their	personal	goals,	meaning	authenticity
is	not	only	communicated	through	the	brand	but	also	actively	constructed	by	consumers.
Morhart	 et	 al.	 (2015)	and	Halwani	and	Cherry	 (2024)	operationalized	 perceived	 brand
authenticity	(PBA)	as	four	connected	facets:	continuity,	credibility,	integrity,	and	symbolism.	This
scale	has	proven	reliable	across	brands	and	cultural	contexts,	making	it	an	appropriate	analytical
framework	 for	 research	 in	 the	 Indonesian	 context.	Napoli	 et	 al.	 (2014)	complemented	 this
perspective	 by	 identifying	 three	 factors	 of	 consumer -based	 brand	 authenticity:	 quality
commitment,	sincerity,	and	heritage,	all	highly	relevant	to	Alazkalogy's	artisan	values.	Kumar	and
Kaushik	 (2022)	further	 reported	 that	 self-congruence	 shapes	 the	 strength	 of	 the	 association
between	perceived	brand	authenticity	and	customers'	engagement	with	the	brand.
Using	 sustainability-related	 Instagram	 communication	 as	 their	 setting,	Bulmer	 et	 al.
(2024)	described	 brand	 authenticity	 through	 four	 dimensions:	 continuity	 (stability	 of	 values
across	 time),	 credibility	 (truthfulness	 in	 communication),	 integrity	 (sincere	 concern	 for
consumers),	and	symbolism	(expression	of	personally	meaningful	values).	These	four	dimensions
are	used	as	the	primary	analytical	framework	for	this	study.	Gilmore	and	Pine	(2007)	and	Smith
and	Du	(2026)	added	a	macro	perspective	that	in	the	experience	economy	era,	consumers	make
authenticity	a	primary	consideration	in	purchase	decisions,	often	more	determining	than	price	or
technical	 product	 quality.	 This	 view	 becomes	 increasingly	 relevant	 for	 artisan	 businesses	 that
must	 differentiate	 themselves	 from	mass	 or	 conventional	 products	 through	 brand	 value	 and
authenticity.

Solomon	and	Mathias	 (2020)	and	Hasanah	 et	 al.	 (2023)	identified	 that	 in	 artisan
businesses,	 the	 brand	 and	 its	 founder	 are	 almost	 inseparable;	 brand	 authenticity	 is	 the
authenticity	of	the	person.	This	creates	unique	conditions	where	customers	have	direct	access	to
verify	authenticity	claims	through	interaction	with	the	founder,	something	impossible	with	mass-
market	brands.
Cinelli	and	LeBoeuf	(2020)	and	Zhang	and	Han	(2025)	showed	that	perceived	authenticity
in	artisan	products	is	significantly	influenced	by	perceptions	of	the	production	process	and	the
motivations	behind	starting	the	business.	Joo	and	Guèvremont	(2024)	extended	this	finding	by
demonstrating	 that	 brand	 authenticity	 formation	 is	 dynamic,	 evolving	 as	 the	 brand	 faces	 new

Oliver	(1999)	and	Dey	(2022)	characterizes	customer	loyalty	as	an	enduring	intention	to
continue	repurchasing	a	product	or	service	over	time.	In	the	artisan	business	context,	loyalty	has
an	 additional	 dimension:	 relational	 attachment	 to	 the	 person	 behind	 the	 brand,	 not	 just	 the
product.	Ofosu-Boateng	and	Jose	(2025)	found	that	brand	storytelling	is	perceived	as	authentic
when	aligned	with	personal	values	and	life	contexts,	and	this	alignment	directly	predicts	loyalty.
Halwani	and	Cherry	 (2024)	likewise	 identify	 loyalty	 as	 a	 recurring	 favorable	 consequence
associated	 with	 brand	 authenticity;	 in	 the	 present	 study,	 this	 pattern	 is	 reflected	 in	 the
approximately	7.0-year	average	relationship	reported	across	the	loyal-customer	informants.
Flanagan	(1954)	and	Sadeghi	et	al.	(2025)	Critical	Incident	Technique	(CIT)	is	designed	to
elicit	concrete	episodes	that	exert	substantial	influence	on	how	people	interpret	an	experience.
Gremler	(2004)	and	Sadeghi	et	al.	(2025)	confirmed	that	CIT	effectively	reveals	mechanisms	of
customer	perception	formation	that	cannot	be	found	through	ordinary	Likert-scale	surveys.	In	the
context	of	brand	authenticity,	CIT	is	relevant	because	authenticity	is	not	formed	all	at	once	but
through	the	accumulation	of	critical	moments	where	the	brand	proves	or	neglects	its	values.
Across	 this	 literature,	 consumers'	 assessments	 of	 authenticity	 are	 generally	 organized
around	four	dimensions	-	credibility,	integrity,	symbolism,	and	continuity	(Bulmer	et	al.,	2024;
Morhart	 et	 al.,	 2015).	 However,	 most	 research	 remains	 focused	 on	 identifying	 and	 measuring
these	 dimensions	 as	 static	 constructs	 through	 cross-sectional	 approaches.	 Consequently,	 the
mechanism	 of	 how	 these	 dimensions	 develop	over	 the	 course	 of	 customer-brand	 relationships
remains	underexplained.	Yet,	recent	research	indicates	that	brand	authenticity	perceptions	are
dynamic	 and	 continuously	 evolve	 through	 repeated	 customer	 experiences	(Joo	 &	 Guèvremont,
2024).	Therefore,	 room	 remains	 to	 explain	 how	 each	 dimension	 of	 brand	 authenticity
progressively	 forms	 in	 the	 context	 of	 long-term	 relationships	 between	 customers	 and	 artisan
businesses.
Prior	scholar	has	proposed	multiple	dimensions	of	brand	authenticity	(Halwani	&	Cherry,
2024;	Morhart	et	al.,	2015;	Napoli	et	al.,	2014)	and	examined	the	factors	that	precede	it	as	well	as
the	 consequences	 that	 follow.	 Much	 of	 this	 work,	 however,	 conceptualizes	 authenticity	 as	 a
relatively	fixed	construct	and	captures	it	through	cross-sectional	measurement	at	one	moment	in
the	customer-brand	relationship.	Such	approaches	have	not	been	able	to	explain	how	authenticity
perceptions	develop	gradually	through	customer	experiences	that	accumulate	over	the	course	of
their	relationship	with	the	brand.	Thus,	there	remains	a	knowledge	gap	regarding	the	mechanism
of	 brand	 authenticity	 formation	 as	 a	 dynamic	 and	 temporal	 process,	 particularly	 in	 the	 artisan
business	context	characterized	by	close	personal	relationships	between	founders	and	customers.
Alazkalogy,	an	artisan	brand	producing	fermented	products,	gluten-free	foods,	handmade
soaps,	and	artisan	teas	from	Ponorogo	that	has	been	operating	since	2015,	offers	an	ideal	context
to	 address	 this	 gap.	 With	 an	 average	 customer	 loyalty	 duration	 of	 approximately	 seven	 years
among	the	ten	informants	studied,	sufficient	temporal	depth	is	available	to	analyze	how	brand
authenticity	perceptions	develop	through	the	accumulation	of	long-term	experiences.	This	study
addresses	 the	 question:	How	 is	 brand	 authenticity	 at	 the	 artisan	 business	 Alazkalogy	 built	 and
perceived	by	loyal	customers	over	more	than	a	decade?

METHOD
Critical	 Incident	 Technique	 (CIT)	 was	operationalized	 by	 asking	 each	 loyal	 customer	 to
reconstruct	specific	episodes	that	changed,	confirmed,	or	weakened	perceptions	of	authenticity.
Each	 episode	 was	 coded	 through	 a	 common	 sequence	 of	 incident	 context,	 customer	 appraisal,
authenticity	 cue,	 and	subsequent	 behavioral	 response.	 Cross-informant	 comparison	 was	 then
used	to	identify	recurrent	patterns	that	formed	the	Three-Layer	Model.
A	descriptive-exploratory	single-case	approach	was	used	in	this	study	(Creswell	&	Poth,
2018;	“Qualitative	Research	Designs,”	2025).	This	single-case	approach	was	chosen	purposively
because	 Alazkalogy	 meets	 the	 criteria	 as	 a	 unique	 case,	 rich	 in	 information	 for	 constructing	 a
phenomenon	that	has	not	been	extensively	explored	(Yin,	2018,	2024).	Alazkalogy	was	selected
as	 the	 focal	 case	 for	 three	 reasons:	 (1)	 more	 than	 ten	 years	 of	 operations,	 which	 permits
examination	over	an	extended	period;	(2)	organic	expansion	without	external	investment,	making
authenticity	 a	 particularly	 salient	 competitive	 resource;	 and	 (3)	 a	 stable	 base	 of	 customers

In	 February	 2026,	 each	 informant	 participated	 in	 a	 45-90-minute	 semi-structured
interview	guided	by	CIT.	Each	informant	was	asked	to	identify,	among	others,	the	moment	they
first	 believed	 in	 the	 brand's	 authenticity,	 the	 moment	 that	 most	 strengthened	 their	belief,	 and
whether	there	was	ever	a	moment	that	shook	their	belief.
Trustworthiness	 was	strengthened	 through	 inter-informant	 triangulation	 across
recurring	incident	patterns,	member	checking	of	the	interpreted	meaning	of	key	incidents,	and	an
iterative	 saturation	 assessment.	 Recruitment	 stopped	 when	 additional	 interviews	 no	 longer
produced	substantively	new	authenticity	cues	or	layer	transitions.
Interview	material	was	analyzed	using	the	three-stage	framework	proposed	by	Miles	&
Huberman	(1994)	and	Miles	(2023).	Open	coding	was	conducted	to	identify	all	units	of	meaning
related	to	authenticity	perceptions.	During	axial	coding,	these	units	were	organized	in	relation	to
the	 four	 authenticity	 dimensions	 identified	 by	Morhart	 et	 al.	 (2015)	and		Bulmer	 et	 al.	 (2024),
together	 with	 the	 critical	 incidents	 evidencing	 each	 dimension.	 Selective	 coding	 constructed
patterns	of	authenticity	perception	formation	across	informants	and	across	time.	Data	credibility
was	ensured	through	inter-informant	triangulation	Lincoln	&	Guba	(1985)	and	Beuving	&	de	Vries
(2025)	member	 checking	 with	 representative	 informants.	 Interviewing	 concluded	 once	 later
conversations	yielded	no	additional	themes,	providing	evidence	of	saturation	(Guest	et	al.,	2006;
Rahimi	&	khatooni,	2024;	Saunders	et	al.,	2018).

RESULTS	AND	DISCUSSION
Result
The	 results	 are	 presented	 as	 a	 critical-incident	analysis:	 reported	 incidents	 were	 first
interpreted	within	their	temporal	sequence	and	then	compared	across	customers.	This	procedure
distinguishes	 the	 trigger,	 perceived	 authenticity	 cue,	 customer	 evaluation,	 and	 behavioral
consequence	 associated	 with	 each	 layer	 rather	 than	 treating	 interview	 statements	 as
decontextualized	themes.

Three	Layers	of	Brand	Authenticity	Formation	from	the	Loyal	Customer	Perspective
Analysis	 of	 10	 interview	 transcripts	 identified	 that	 Alazkalogy's	 loyal	 customers	 do	 not
build	brand	authenticity	perceptions	all	at	once	but	through	three	layers	that	develop	gradually
over	the	subscription	duration.	This	finding	extends	Beverland	(2005)	and	Ranfagni	&	Salvietti
(2023)		framework,	which	analyzed	authenticity	from	the	producer's	side;	this	research	shows
that	from	the	consumer's	side,	authenticity	also	has	a	layered	and	temporal	structure.

First	Layer:	Experience-Based	Authenticity
Analysis	shows	that	the	first	layer	is	the	initial	stage	when	customers	begin	building	brand
authenticity	perceptions	based	on	the	founder's	narrative	before	having	deep	product	experience.
This	first	layer	is	formed	from	the	founder's	real-life	experiences,	particularly	health	experiences
that	motivated	 Alazkalogy's	 establishment	 in	 2015.	 From	 the	 customer's	 perspective,	 this
narrative	functions	as	a	legitimacy	signal,	among	others,	evidence	that	the	brand	was	not	born
from	 business	 calculation	 but	 from	 identifiable	 and	 relevant	 health	 needs.	This	 aligns	 with
Beverland	(2005)	and	Ranfagni	and	Salvietti	(2023)		finding	that	sincerity—authenticity	proven
through	 passion	 behind	 the	 product—is	 a	 key	 component	 of	 brand	 authenticity	 in	 artisan
businesses.
PL-02	(8	years,	Ponorogo):	"I	knew	it	not	from	the	brand's	story,	but	because	my	husband
was	 diagnosed	 with	 anxiety	 and	 consumed	 kefir	 from	 Alazkalogy,	 and	 we	 saw	 the	 benefits
firsthand."	PL-10	(8	years,	state-owned	enterprise	employee,	Ponorogo):	"Hearing	the	founder's
struggle	story	of	building	a	business	based	on	personal	experience	makes	me	feel	closer	to	the
brand	and	appreciate	the	process	behind	every	product."
The	authenticity	dimensions	most	active	in	this	layer	are	credibility	and	integrity	(Bulmer
et	al.,	2024;	Morhart	et	al.,	2015).	Both	are	formed	even	before	customers	try	the	product,	merely
from	hearing	the	origin	narrative.	This	confirms	Beverland	and	Farrelly	(2010)	and	Mann	(2023)
finding	that	consumers	actively	select	authentic	cues	aligned	with	their	personal	goals,	and	Kumar
and	Kaushik	 (2022)	finding	 that	 value	 congruence	 between	 consumers	 and	 brands	 can	 form
before	the	first	transaction.

Second	Layer:	Process-Based	Authenticity
The	 second	 layer	 forms	 after	 customers	 have	 direct	 experience	 with	 the	 product	 and
interaction	with	the	founder.	At	this	stage,	customers	actively	verify	the	authenticity	claims	from
the	first	layer	through	observation	of	the	production	process,	honesty	about	product	effects	and
limitations,	and	the	founder's	responsiveness	in	addressing	questions.
PL-06	(8	years,	bank	employee,	Ponorogo):	"The	founder	is	kind	and	patiently	explains
product	benefits,	even	the	initial	effects	when	first	trying	water	kefir,	so	as	a	customer	I	feel	safe
trying	 new	 Alazka	 product	 variants."	 Honesty	 about	 potentially	 uncomfortable	 initial	 effects—
something	that	could	be	hidden	to	boost	sales—becomes	the	strongest	evidence	that	the	brand	is
authentic.	Halwani	&	Cherry	(2024)	identify	transparency	and	credibility	as	primary	drivers	of
brand	authenticity	perceptions	from	the	consumer	side.
PL-08	(8	years,	teacher,	Ponorogo,	ovarian	cancer	survivor):	"Alazkalogy	understands	that
and	 tries	 to	 produce	 all	 the	 food	 or	 drinks	 needed	 for	 ovarian	 cancer	 patients."	 A	 brand	 that
understands	 and	 responds	 to	 specific	 medical	 conditions	 proves	 its	 care	 is	genuine,	 not
performative.	This	is	the	strongest	manifestation	of	the	integrity	dimension	in	Bulmer	et	al.	(2024)
framework.

Third	Layer:	Decision-Based	Authenticity
The	 third	 layer	 is	 the	 deepest,	 available	 only	 to	 customers	 who	 have	 interacted	 long
enough	to	observe	the	consistency	of	the	founder's	decisions	when	facing	external	pressures.	At
this	layer,	customers	build	authenticity	perceptions	not	from	what	the	brand	says	but	from	what
the	brand	chooses	when	there	is	pressure	to	compromise.
PL-02	(8	years,	Ponorogo):	"The	longer	I	follow	its	journey,	I	feel	this	brand	is	consistent,
honest,	and	truly	cares	about	consumer	health.	That's	what	makes	me	comfortable	and	stay	as	a
customer."	The	phrase	"the	longer"	is	key;	this	perception	does	not	form	in	the	first	year	but	from
years	 of	 accumulation.	Joo	 &	 Guèvremont	 (2024)	confirms	 that	 authenticity	 is	 dynamic	 and
requires	active	maintenance	through	consistent	decisions.
PL-05	(6	years,	homemaker,	Ponorogo):	"Alazkalogy	never	stops	innovating,	from	kefir	to
kombucha,	kimchi,	gluten-free.	In	Ponorogo,	no	one	is	as	consistently	focused	on	fermentation	as
this."	 PL-09	 (8	 years,	 self-employed,	 Madiun):	 "Alazka	 doesn't	 just	 sell	 but	 also	 educates,"	 a
perception	of	the	motivation	behind	the	brand's	actions	formed	from	long-term	observation.
The	 authenticity	 dimensions	 most	 active	 in	 this	 layer	 are	 continuity	 and	 symbolism
(Bulmer	et	al.,	2024;	Morhart	et	al.,	2015).	Both	require	sufficient	observation	duration,	aligning
with	Gilmore	and	Pine	(2007)	and	Smith	and	Du	(2026)	argument	that	in	the	experience	economy
era,	consumers	evaluate	authenticity	from	accumulated	real	interactions,	not	from	single	claims.

Discussion
These	 findings	 build	 on	 the	 Perceived	 Brand	 Authenticity	 framework	 introduced	 by
(Bulmer	et	al.,	2024;	Morhart	et	al.,	2015).	Rather	than	treating	continuity,	credibility,	integrity,
and	symbolism	as	dimensions	that	are	equally	observable	at	the	same	stage,	the	present	analysis
indicates	that	their	salience	emerges	in	a	sequence.	The	dimensions	of	credibility	and	integrity
appear	 at	 the	 early	 stage	 because	 they	 can	 be	 evaluated	 immediately	 through	 the	 founder's
narrative	 and	 first	 product	 experience.	 Conversely,	 continuity	 and	 symbolism	 can	 only	 be
evaluated	after	customers	have	accumulated	long-term	experience	that	allows	them	to	observe
business	 decision	 consistency.	 This	 finding	 also	 complements	Joo	and	Guèvremont	 (2024)
argument	that	brand	authenticity	is	dynamic	by	explaining	the	concrete	mechanism	of	how	this
dynamic	occurs	in	the	artisan	business	context.
The	 mechanism	 of	 brand	 authenticity	 formation	 identified	 in	 this	 study	 appears	 to	 be
influenced	by	the	distinctive	characteristics	of	artisan	businesses	that	position	the	founder	as	the
primary	brand	representative.	The	personal	relationship	between	founder	and	customers	allows
customers	 to	 directly	 observe	 production	 processes,	 communication,	 and	 business	 decisions
made.	 This	 condition	 differs	 from	 corporate	 brands	 where	 most	 interactions	 are	 mediated	 by
organizational	 systems	 and	 marketing	 communications.	 Therefore,	 the	 Three-Layer	 Model
resulting	 from	 this	 research	 is	 more	 appropriately	 positioned	 as	 an	 explanation	 of	 brand
authenticity	formation	in	founder-driven	artisan	businesses	rather	than	as	a	universal	model	for
all	brand	types.
Based	 on	 these	 findings,	 this	 research	 provides	 three	 theoretical	 contributions.	 First,	 it
shifts	 the	 perspective	 on	 brand	 authenticity	 from	 a	 static	 construct	 to	 a	 temporally	 evolving
process.	 Second,	 it	 explains	 the	 mechanism	 of	 brand	 authenticity	 formation	 through	 three
sequential	layers	of	customer	experience.	Third,	the	analysis	suggests	that	how	long	customers
have	 been	 connected	 to	 the	 brand	 conditions	 how	 deeply	 they	 can	 evaluate	 its	 authenticity.
Moreover,	this	relationship	can	become	an	essential	lens	for	understanding	how	consumer	trust
evolves,	 not	 merely	 forms.	 The	 implication	 is	 that	 future	 research	 using	 cross-sectional	 survey
approaches	needs	to	consider	the	length	of	the	customer-brand	relationship	because	customers
at	different	relationship	stages	may	produce	different	levels	of	authenticity	perception	even	when
evaluating	the	same	brand.

CONCLUSION
Taken	together,	the	study	shows	that	brand	authenticity	in	an	artisan	business	develops
temporally	rather	 than	 appearing	 as	 a	 static	 perception.	 The	 Three-Layer	 Model	 links	 initial
narrative	cues,	verified	production	practices,	and	repeated	purchase	decisions	into	a	sequential
process	 that	 explains	 why	 loyal	 customers	 continue	 to	 regard	 the	 brand	 as	 authentic.	 This
temporal	 contribution	 extends	 established	 authenticity	 dimensions	 by	 showing	 how	 they	 are

experienced	and	confirmed	over	time.
In	the	artisan	setting	examined	here,	authenticity	should	not	be	viewed	as	four	dimensions
that	 become	 fully	 visible	at	 the	 same	 moment.	 Instead,	 it	 develops	 through	 an	 incremental,
relationship-based	 process.	 As	 founder-customer	 interactions	 accumulate,	 credibility	 and
integrity	 can	 be	 assessed	 earlier,	 whereas	 continuity	 and	 symbolism	 become	 clearer	 through
longer	observation.	Accordingly,	the	study	broadens	the	application	of	brand-authenticity	theory
by	 adding	 a	 temporal	 explanation	 of	 how	 customers'	 judgments	 change	 across	 stages	 of	 the
customer-brand	relationship.
This	research	shows	that	Alazkalogy's	brand	authenticity	is	built	and	perceived	by	loyal
customers	 through	 three	 cumulative	 layers	 that	 develop	 with	 subscription	 duration:	 first,
experience-based	 authenticity—legitimacy	 from	 the	 founder's	 real-life	 experience	 narrative
resonant	 with	 customer	 health	conditions,	 formed	 even	 before	 the	 first	 transaction;	 second,
process-based	authenticity—verification	through	production	process	transparency	and	honesty
about	product	effects,	activating	credibility	and	integrity	dimensions;	and	third,	decision-based
authenticity—confirmation	 from	 observing	 business	 decision	 consistency	 over	 years	 reflecting
brand	values.	These	three	layers	complement	each	other	in	building	long-term	relationships	and
customer	loyalty.
The	study's	principal	theoretical	contribution	is	to	introduce	time	as	an	explicit	element
in	 understanding	 brand	 authenticity,	 which	 prior	 work	 has	 commonly	 assessed	 as	 a	 static
construct.	 The	 resulting	 Three-Layer	 Model	 explains	 how	 authenticity	 perceptions	 develop
through	 accumulated	 experience,	 a	mechanism	 that	 differs	 between	 early-stage	 customers
(accessing	only	the	first	layer)	and	long-term	customers	(accessing	all	three	layers	cumulatively).
Practical	 implications	 for	 artisan	 business	 practitioners:	 investment	 in	 the	 first	 layer	 (honest
origin	narrative)	 has	 the	 broadest	 reach	 and	 forms	 most	 quickly.	 The	 second	 layer	 requires
consistency	in	every	interaction	between	founder	and	customer	that	cannot	be	fully	delegated	to
systems.	The	third	layer	requires	patience	and	value	commitment	over	years	but	produces	the
most	enduring	loyalty	and	is	the	most	difficult	for	competitors	to	imitate.
This	research	has	limitations	due	to	the	single-case	study	approach,	so	findings	cannot	be
generalized	 statistically.	 Future	 studies	 should	 therefore	 examine	 the	 model	 in	 other	 artisan
categories,	 develop	 quantitative	 measures	 for	 each	 layer	 and	 investigate	 whether	 comparable
temporal	sequences	occur	in	artisan	settings	beyond	Indonesia.
