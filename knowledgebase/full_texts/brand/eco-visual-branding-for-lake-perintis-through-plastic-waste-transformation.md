# Eco-Visual Branding for Lake Perintis Through Plastic Waste Transformation

INTRODUCTION
Tourism destinations are perceived not only through physical landscapes and services but also
through sign systems that construct visitor memories, emotional associations, and interpretations of
place. Visual identity, local storytelling, merchandise or souvenir products, and digital content operate
synergistically to guide how tourists recognise, interpret, and internalise a destination, making visual
communication increasingly inseparable from the construction of destination meaning. Destination
brand experiences established across official online platforms can influence perceived authenticity and
subsequent behavioural responses because digital representations shape destination expectations before,
during, and after the visit (Jiménez -Barreto et al., 2020 ). Recent research further conceptualises
destination branding as a co-created gestalt in which multiple visual, experiential, social, and symbolic
elements are assembled into a holistic perception of place rather than communicated through an isolated
brand artefact (Wang et al., 2025). Visual branding therefore functions not merely as an aesthetic
mechanism but as an interface through which landscapes, local meanings, visitor experiences, and
market interactions become connected. Evidence from Indonesian t ourism destinations also indicates
that visual branding can influence tourists' perceptions, although its effectiveness depends on how visual
elements are connected to destination characteristics and visitor interpretation (Prawira et al., 2023).
The conte mporary challenge is consequently to develop destination identities that remain visually
recognisable while simultaneously communicating authenticity, sustainability, and a meaningful
relationship between visitors and the places they encounter.

natural landscape and site planning that has attracted domestic visitors, yet its existing tourism

Abstract
Danau Perintis faces interconnected challenges involving plastic waste management and the
limited availability of distinctive tourism merchandise that represents the destination. This study
develops an eco -visual branding approach by transforming discarded  plastic bottle caps into
functional keychains that combine local visual identity, ecological narratives, and practical utility.
An exploratory research and development design was implemented through the Double Diamond
stages of Discover, Define, Develop, and Deliver, supported by field observation, informal manager
interviews, material experimentation, prototyping, documentation, simulated market testing, and
descriptive evaluation. The development produced twelve keychains under the brand identity
Perintis by Aruna with the tagline “From Waste to Worth.” All twelve units were sold at IDR 20,000
per unit during a campus-based exhibition, generating IDR 240,000 in gross revenue and providing
initial evidence of product acceptance within the tested environmen t. The findings further
demonstrate that material transformation can function as part of destination storytelling by
connecting waste origin, local visual elements, product utility, and consumer interaction. The study
formulates the Eco -Visual Branding Loo p, integrating ecological input, material transformation,
local visual identity, digital storytelling, AISAS activation, and market feedback. The model
provides a practical framework for developing circular tourism merchandise while requiring
further validation through larger-scale production, consumer profiling, material safety testing, and
longitudinal digital communication measurement.

discarded material can be redirected into a productive system that creates environmental, cultural, and
communicative value. Reframing plastic bottle caps as design resources opens an applied innovation
pathway in which waste management can become directly connected to destination identity and visitor
engagement.
Plastic waste management at tourism sites needs to be addressed within the circular economy
framework because conventional collection and disposal retain the destination within a predominantly
linear material system. The circular economy shifts the take -make-dispose paradigm towards
maintaining material value through reuse, recycling, recovery, and design innovation, although tourism
applications remain comparatively emergent and fragmented across different destination contexts
(Rodríguez et al., 2020). Within tourism and hospitality, circular practices can generate environmental
and business benefits, but their effectiveness depends on stakeholder participation and their integration
into broader destination value chains rather than on isolated recycling activities (Jones & Wynn, 2022).
Indonesian initiatives around Borobudur similarly illustrate how plastic upcycling can be embedded in
tourism areas to transform waste management into participatory creative activity rather than treating
discarded material exc lusively as an environmental burden (Khaerunnisa & Cininta, 2023). The
relevance of this approach to Danau Perintis lies in the possibility of converting discarded bottle caps
into functional and symbolic products whose material history remains connected t o the destination.
Such transformation requires design decisions concerning form, usability, visual identity, narrative, and
perceived value rather than simple material substitution. Design thinking provides a useful foundation
for this process because it frames innovation as an iterative response to human needs, contextual
problems, and opportunities for creating meaningful solutions (Brown, 2008).
Developing souvenirs from upcycled materials demonstrates strong potential to link
environmental management, creativity, and regional identity because the resulting product can
simultaneously function as a material recovery outcome and a communication medi um. Research on
Surakarta demonstrates that recycled plastic can be transformed into souvenir products associated with
city-specific identity, showing how waste materials can acquire cultural and economic value through
intentional design (Wahida & Fuad, 20 22). A comparable study using waste -derived biocomposite
materials positions sustainable tourism souvenirs as instruments of green innovation and place
branding, indicating that the material composition of a product can become part of its destination
narrative when design and branding are developed together (Syaputra et al., 2024). Yet these approaches
leave an important conceptual space between the physical transformation of waste and the subsequent
circulation of the product through visitors' digital expe riences. Souvenirs are not simply commercial
objects because their locality can be constructed through representations of place, people, and
remembered experiences, enabling them to extend destination meaning beyond the temporal boundaries
of the trip (Liu et al., 2025). Their branding potential is also strengthened when authenticity and visitor
co-creation contribute to destination brand experience and souvenir repurchase intention (Jog &
Gumparthi, 2025). The unresolved issue is how an upcycled souvenir c an operate simultaneously as a
circular-economy product, a visual representation of a specific destination, and a trigger for continued
digital interaction after visitors leave the site.

products do not automatically generate positive consumer responses merely by displaying

tension between the increasing prominence of environmental discourse and the difficulty of translating
sustainability messages into memorable and persuasive visi tor experiences (Tölkes, 2018). Tourist
responses can become more meaningful when sustainability is embedded within the experience rather
than communicated as an overly explicit promotional message, suggesting that ecological value should

create tensions around cultura l ownership and representation (Freire et al., 2022). This concern
reinforces the need for an eco-visual identity in which local visual elements are selected and transformed
through contextual design rather than being applied as superficial decoration. The product consequently
needs to communicate three interconnected meanings: the environmental transformation of discarded
plastic, the visual identity of Danau Perintis, and the visitor's continuing relationship with the
destination.
On the promotional front, the management of Danau Perintis relies predominantly on Facebook,
which provides a relevant communication channel for local audiences but does not yet constitute a
structured content ecosystem connecting product identity, informa tion search, purchase action, and
post-purchase sharing. Social media marketing enables organisations to develop interactive
relationships, electronic word-of-mouth, consumer engagement, and brand -related responses, making
digital platforms important envir onments for extending destination communication beyond physical
encounters (Alalwan et al., 2017). The AISAS model—Attention, Interest, Search, Action, and Share—
is particularly relevant because it conceptualises digital consumer behaviour as a sequence in  which
audiences can move from exposure and interest towards information seeking, action, and subsequent
sharing (Abdurrahim et al., 2019). Research on video branding in ecotourism demonstrates that AISAS
can connect visual content with tourism communicati on by encouraging audiences to process, search,
respond to, and disseminate destination information through digital media (Fadlurrahman et al., 2023).
Similar evidence from TikTok shows the relevance of AISAS to contemporary short-form social media
communication, where attention is rapidly converted into search, interaction, action, and sharing
behaviours (Sasmita & Achmadi, 2022). These findings indicate that the communication value of an
eco-souvenir should not terminate at the point of purchase because t he product itself can become a
visual trigger for digital storytelling, user -generated content, and post -visit destination remembrance.
The research gap therefore concerns the absence of an integrated framework that connects plastic waste
transformation, l ocal visual branding, souvenir design, AISAS -based digital communication, and
market feedback within one circular destination communication cycle. This study addresses that gap by
developing the concept of eco-visual branding, positioning the upcycled product as a tangible message
carrier that connects the physical destination, environmental action, local identity, digital storytelling,
and post-visit engagement. The study aims to explain the development process of tourism merchandise
crafted from plastic bottle caps, evaluate its initial market acceptance through production and sales data
within a simulated market environment, and formulate the Eco -Visual Branding Loop as an initial
framework for circular destination branding. Its theoretical contribution l ies in extending destination
branding and circular tourism perspectives by conceptualising waste -derived merchandise as a
simultaneous material, visual, and communicative asset, while its methodological contribution lies in
integrating design development, product-market feedback, and AISAS-oriented communication into an
applied framework that can be examined and refined in subsequent destination studies.

RESEARCH METHODS
This study employed an empirical exploratory Research and Development (R&D) design
guided by the Double Diamond framework, which consists of the Discover, Define, Develop, and
Deliver stages and provides a structured process for moving from problem identif ication toward
iterative solution development (Brown, 2008). The study was conducted at Lake Perintis, Bone Bolango
Regency, Gorontalo Province, with the development process integrated into Research-Based Learning
involving undergraduate communication students in field observation, concept formulation, prototype
production, promotion, simulated sales, and evaluation. The Discover stage involved direct observation
of plastic bottle -cap waste, existing waste -handling practices, destination promotional media, visitor
characteristics, and potential consumer preferences, supported by informal interviews with destination
managers. Findings from this stage were translated during the Define stage into the positioning, visual
identity, naming, tagline, colour palette, product form, and narrative concept of Perintis by Aruna. The
Develop stage experimentally transformed collected High -Density Polyethylene (HDPE) and
Polypropylene (PP) bottle caps through sorting, washing, drying, heat melting, moulding, trimming,
hardware assembly, and packaging, following a circular approach  that seeks to retain material value
through reuse and recovery within tourism activities (Rodríguez et al., 2020). The Deliver stage
consisted of prototype presentation, promotional documentation, simulated market testing, sales
activities, and evaluation , enabling the developed merchandise to be examined as both an upcycled
material product and a destination-branding medium.

Validation combined field -observation records, manager information, visual documentation,
production logs, sales records, and informal consumer feedback to establish evidence triangulation
across the development process. Prototype quality was evaluated according to identity legibility,
aesthetic appeal, functional usability, durability, narrative alignment, material transformation, and
production replicability, recognising that the emotional  and experiential qualities of designed objects
contribute to how users perceive and value products (Norman, 2004). The environmental dimension
was assessed by examining the extent to which discarded bottle caps could be transformed into
functional merchandise while minimising secondary production residues and maintaining a coherent
sustainability narrative, reflecting the importance of communicating tourism sustainability through
meaningful experiences rather than through environmental claims alone (Tölkes, 2018). Initial market
acceptance was evaluated descriptively using production quantity, available inventory, number of units
sold, sales completion, observed purchasing interactions, and informal consumer responses concerning
product form, visual appearance, usefulness, price, and destination association. The AISAS framework
was used as an interpretive evaluation lens to examine the potential progression from Attention and
Interest to Search, Action, and Share, particularly in relation to the capacity of visual product content
and promotional materials to stimulate digital engagement and tourism-related responses (Abdurrahim
et al., 2019). The methodological distinctiveness of the study lies in integrating physical waste
transformation, iterative visual-design development, simulated market validation, and AISAS-oriented
communication analysis within a single empirical development cycle, allowing the proposed Eco -
Visual Branding Loop to be grounded in observable production and market evidence rather than
formulated solely as a conceptual proposition.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Problem and Needs Identification (Discover Stage)
The Discover stage identified plastic bottle caps as one of the most accessible forms of waste
around Lake Perintis and as a material with potential for small -scale creative transformation. Direct
observation showed that discarded bottle caps were relative ly easy to collect, separate, and prepare
compared with larger or more contaminated plastic waste. Informal discussions with destination
managers indicated that plastic waste was commonly gathered and, in some cases, burned as a practical
way to reduce visible accumulation. Although burning can temporarily improve the visual cleanliness
of a destination, it eliminates the possibility of recovering material value and creates additional
environmental concerns. Suryawan et al. (2024) note that waste management  in Indonesian tourist
attractions continues to face practical and policy -related challenges, particularly in relation to plastic
waste. The condition at Lake Perintis therefore presented not only a waste -management problem but
also an opportunity to recon sider discarded plastic as a resource for destination -related creative
production.
The material assessment further indicated that bottle caps were suitable for a micro -scale
upcycling process because of their relatively compact form, manageable sorting requirements, and
potential to produce varied visual effects after thermal transformat ion. The material needed to be
sufficiently clean, workable, and durable to support repeated experimentation without creating
excessive production complexity. These requirements were important because the project was designed
around student-based production rather than industrial manufacturing with specialised equipment. The
circular economy perspective positions waste recovery as a means of retaining material value while
reducing dependence on newly extracted resources (Rodríguez et al., 2020). Such an app roach shifts
the role of waste from an endpoint of consumption toward an input for new forms of economic and
cultural value. For Lake Perintis, the transformation of bottle caps could connect environmental
management with creative production without requir ing a large -scale technological system. The
feasibility of this material choice consequently became an important foundation for developing a
souvenir concept that could be replicated through relatively accessible production practices.
The Discover stage also revealed a gap in the tangible representation of Lake Perintis within
the visitor experience. The destination provides opportunities for scenic appreciation, photography, and
recreational activities, yet these experiences do not automatically produce a physical object that visitors
can retain after leaving the site. Souvenirs can function as material representations of locality because
they allow visitors to carry aspects of a destination into their post -trip environment (Liu et al.,  2025).
The absence of a distinctive product therefore represented a branding issue as well as an opportunity

for extending destination experiences beyond the physical location. A souvenir derived from local waste
could address both dimensions by linking the identity of the destination with an environmental
narrative. The product would not merely reproduce a visual symbol of Lake Perintis but would also
communicate a transformation process associated with the place. This combination created a basis for
developing an eco -visual branding approach in which mater ial origin, visual identity, and visitor

The target audience analysis then focused on users who were likely to value products that
combine visual appeal, practical function, and accessible pricing. University students, young adults,
and domestic visitors were considered relevant audiences because  compact accessories can be
incorporated into everyday activities without requiring substantial financial commitment. The product
therefore needed to be lightweight, portable, visually distinctive, and sufficiently versatile to function
as an accessory for keys, bags, or other personal items. A keychain was selected because its small size
could reduce material consumption while allowing multiple design iterations during the prototype stage.
Its everyday utility also created opportunities for the destination  identity to remain visible after the
visitor had left Lake Perintis. Norman (2004) argues that the emotional dimension of everyday objects
can influence how users perceive and interact with designed products, making functional objects
relevant carriers of  symbolic and experiential meaning. The selection of a keychain consequently
connected production feasibility with the need for an object capable of carrying both aesthetic and place-
related meanings.
The identified problems and needs established three interconnected design requirements:
environmental relevance, destination distinctiveness, and consumer usability. Environmental relevance
required the product to demonstrate a credible transformation of d iscarded plastic rather than simply
using sustainability as a promotional claim. Destination distinctiveness required the visual language to
establish a recognisable relationship with Lake Perintis without reducing local identity to decorative
imagery. Consumer usability required the resulting object to be practical enough to encourage actual
use rather than function only as a symbolic environmental artefact. Brown (2008) describes design
thinking as an iterative process in which user needs and contextual p roblems are translated into
opportunities for experimentation and solution development. Applied to Lake Perintis, this orientation
meant that waste availability alone was insufficient to determine the product concept. The material,
audience, place identity, and intended use had to be considered as a connected design problem before
visual identity development could begin.
The Discover findings ultimately positioned plastic waste as both an environmental liability
and a potential branding resource for Lake Perintis. The most relevant opportunity was not simply to
produce a souvenir from recycled material, but to develop an o bject in which material transformation
itself contributed to the meaning of the destination. Such an approach could strengthen the relationship
between ecological practice and destination representation while maintaining attention to affordability
and everyday utility. Kusumaningrum (2024) shows that sustainability can contribute to destination
marketing value when it is connected to tourist perceptions and experiences rather than communicated
as an isolated environmental message. The Lake Perintis context suggests that this connection can be
pursued through a product that combines local visual references, practical function, and visible material
transformation. The findings from the Discover stage therefore provided the conceptual and practical
basis for de fining the identity of Perintis by Aruna  and determining how its visual language would
communicate both locality and ecological value.

Formulating the Identity of Perintis by Aruna (Define Stage)
The Define stage translated the needs identified during the Discover stage into a specific
identity for the proposed souvenir product. The main design direction positioned the product as an eco-
visual representation of Lake Perintis rather than as a generi c recycled craft. The brand name Perintis
by Aruna was selected to connect the destination name with a symbolic idea of renewal and
transformation. Aruna, derived from Sanskrit and associated with dawn or reddish light, represents the
emergence of new value from material previously regarded as waste. Brown (2008) explains that design
thinking involves reframing contextual problems into opportunities for de veloping solutions that
respond to user and environmental conditions. The naming process therefore established a conceptual
connection between the destination, material transformation, and the intended value of the product.

The tagline “From Waste to Worth” was formulated as the central message supporting the
identity of Perintis by Aruna . The phrase directly communicates the conversion of discarded plastic
bottle caps into products with functional, aesthetic, and economic value. Its concise wording also allows
the message to be consistently incorporated into product presentation and promotional materials.
Sustainability communication in tourism can become more meaningful when environmental concerns
are incorporated into memorable experiences rather than communicated only as abstract environmental
claims (Hanna et al., 2018). The product was consequently designed so that the sustainability message
would operate together with its visual appearance and practical function. Kusumaningrum (2024) shows
that sustainabili ty can contribute to destination marketing value when it is connected with tourist
perceptions and experiences. The tagline thus functioned as a concise narrative device that connected
the physical transformation of plastic with the broader experience offered by the destination.
The visual identity was developed through selected representations of the environmental
characteristics of Lake Perintis. The design incorporated the lake landscape, an iconic ship structure,
and aquatic biota such as fish, octopus, and seahorse forms. These elements were simplified into a clean
contemporary cartoon aesthetic to maintain recognizability while accommodating the relatively small
dimensions of the keychain. Prawira et al. (2023) indicate that visual branding can shape tourists’
responses to de stination representations when visual elements create meaningful associations with
place. The selected motifs were therefore treated as visual interpretations of the destination rather than
literal reproductions of its physical environment. Freire et al. ( 2022) emphasise the need for careful
consideration of cultural representation in destination branding. The visual system maintained this
consideration by using local environmental references as design inspiration while avoiding claims that
the selected motifs represented the entirety of local cultural identity.
The relationship between the selected motifs and the physical product can be observed in the
prototype variations presented in Figure 2. The different forms demonstrate how lake -related imagery
was translated into compact objects while retaining the distinctive characteristics of the recycled plastic
material. The organic colour patterns generated through the transformation process also contributed
variation between individual pieces, preventing the prototypes from appearing completely uniform.
Such variation can strengthen the perception that each product contains a material history rather than
functioning as an industrially identical commodity. Norman (2004) associates everyday product
experiences with emotional responses generated through form, appearance , and interaction. In this
context, the visual character of the keychain supported both functional usability and emotional
associations with the destination. The prototype forms therefore became an important physical
expression of the Perintis by Aruna identity.

The Define stage established Perintis by Aruna as an integrated identity comprising the brand
name, tagline, visual motifs, colour palette, material characteristics, packaging, and ecological
narrative. These elements were deliberately connected so that the product could communicate locality
through b oth its appearance and the origin of its material. The approach differs from merchandise
concepts that rely only on destination images because the recycled bottle cap itself becomes part of the
narrative being communicated. Wang et al. (2025) conceptualise destination branding as an increasingly
interconnected process involving multiple elements and forms of participation. The Lake Perintis
concept similarly connects environmental material, visual representation, product utility, and
storytelling within a single identity system. The defined identity provided the design parameters for the
next stage, in which the selected concepts were further developed through material experimentation,
moulding, finishing, and prototype refinement.

Material Transformation and Prototype Development (Develop Stage)
The Develop stage translated the visual and conceptual identity established during the Define
stage into physical products through direct experimentation with discarded plastic bottle caps. Material
collection was conducted from 19 to 28 October 2025, foll owed by the fabrication process from 15
November to 16 December 2025. The collected bottle caps were first sorted according to colour and
material characteristics to reduce inconsistencies during subsequent processing. Each selected piece
was then washed and dried thoroughly to remove dirt and residual substances that could interfere with
melting and moulding. The preparation process was particularly important because the quality of
recycled material directly influenced the appearance and workability of the  resulting prototypes.
Wahida and Fuad (2022) demonstrate that plastic waste can be transformed into souvenir products when
material preparation is integrated with appropriate creative production techniques. At Lake Perintis, this
initial preparation estab lished the material condition required to move from waste collection toward
controlled product experimentation.
The transformation process involved heat melting followed by manual compression and
moulding to produce the desired keychain forms. After the softened plastic reached a workable
condition, it was compressed into moulds and allowed to form according to the predetermined product
dimensions and visual concepts. The resulting pieces were subsequently trimmed along their edges to
remove excess material and improve their physical finish. Hardware components were then attached so
that the transformed plastic could  function as an actual keychain rather than remaining a decorative
plastic sample. Surface finishing was conducted to improve the overall appearance while retaining
visible characteristics generated during the recycling process. Syaputra et al. (2024) demo nstrate the

potential of waste -based materials to support sustainable souvenir development when material
transformation is connected with tourism branding objectives. The process at Lake Perintis extended
this principle by combining recycled material processing with a destination -specific visual identity
developed in the preceding stage.
One notable result of the experimentation was the emergence of organic colour swirls and
irregular visual patterns within individual pieces. These patterns were not treated as defects requiring
complete standardisation because their variation reflected the  physical characteristics of the recycled
bottle-cap material. Each prototype consequently possessed a slightly different visual composition even
when similar mould forms were used. Such variation provided an additional aesthetic characteristic that
distinguished the products from conventionally manufactured souvenirs with highly uniform surfaces.
Norman (2004) argues that the appearance and sensory character of everyday objects can contribute to
users’ emotional responses and perceptions of value. In this context, material irregularity became part
of the product narrative because it visually signalled that the object originated from transformed waste.
The design process therefore demonstrated that controlled imperfection could support the authenticity
of an upcycled product while remaining compatible with the intended visual identity.
The prototyping process also exposed several technical and operational constraints that
influenced the feasibility of small -scale production. Manual thermal management required careful
handling because differences in heating conditions could affect the plastic's consistency, surface quality,
and final form. Mould capacity limited the number of pieces that could be processed simultaneously,
while sorting and preparation increased the labour required for each individual product. These
conditions indicate that the prototype process cannot yet be interpreted as equivalent to standardised
industrial manufacturing. The circular economy literature emphasises that waste valorisation in tourism
requires attention not only to environmental benefits but also to the practical organisation of production
and resource use (Rodríguez et al., 2020). The current production process therefore functioned primarily
as an experimental demonstration of material feasibility and design adaptability. Its limitations
simultaneously identified the technical aspects that would require further standardisation if the product
were developed beyond the prototype scale.
The development stage also demonstrated that material transformation could function as part
of the communication value of the product rather than merely as a production technique. The recycled
bottle caps retained visible traces of their previous material form while being reorganised into objects
associated with Lake Perintis through colour, shape, and visual motifs. This transformation created a
direct relationship between the environmental problem identified in the Discover stage and the
destination ident ity formulated in the Define stage. The resulting keychain therefore carried two
interconnected meanings: it represented a physical response to plastic waste and served as a portable
representation of the destination. Jones and Wynn (2022) emphasise the relevance of circular economy
principles to tourism through approaches that retain resource value while connecting environmental
management with tourism activities. The Lake Perintis prototypes operationalised this relationship at a
micro scale by converting locally collected waste into a product that could circulate within the visitor
economy. Material origin consequently became an active component of product meaning rather than
remaining an invisible background to the branding process.
From a development perspective, the prototypes provided sufficient evidence that discarded
bottle caps could be transformed into functional and aesthetically distinctive souvenirs within an
accessible small-scale production setting. The process also showed that product development required
continuous adjustment between material behaviour, mould dimensions, visual composition, finishing
quality, and intended use. Rather than treating the first production attempt as a final product
specification, the prototypes served as physical iterations through which design assumptions could be
tested and refined. This iterative character corresponds with the experimental orientation of design
thinking, where tangible prototypes help reveal practical problems that cannot a lways be identified
during conceptual planning (Brown, 2008). At the same time, the absence of formal thermal safety
assessment, tensile-strength testing, and long -term durability testing means that the products should
remain classified as functional design prototypes rather than fully validated commercial goods. Future
development would benefit from documented temperature parameters, personal protective equipment

Market Testing, Dissemination, and Eco-Visual Branding Loop (Deliver Stage)
The Deliver stage introduced the completed prototypes into a controlled simulated market
environment through a campus exhibition involving university students, academic staff, and visitors as
a purposively selected audience. The exhibition provided an oppo rtunity to observe how the product
was received when presented together with other tourism and cultural visual communication outputs.
Student creators participated directly in dissemination by providing rotation-based oral explanations of
the material transformation process, visual concept, ecological rationale, and relationship between the
product and Lake Perintis. This arrangement allowed the prototype to be evaluated not only as a physical
souvenir but also as a communication object whose meaning depend ed partly on how its production
story was conveyed. The exhibition setting also created a relatively accessible environment for
observing audience attention, inquiries, purchasing interactions, and spontaneous responses without
claiming to represent the wi der tourist market. Such an approach is consistent with the role of
destination-oriented social media and marketing communication in connecting visual representations
with audience responses and subsequent actions (Abdurrahim et al., 2019). The Deliver stage therefore
functioned as an initial market and communication test rather than as a comprehensive assessment of
commercial demand.
The observed interactions could be interpreted through the AISAS sequence of Attention,
Interest, Search, Action, and Share. Attention was initially generated by the vibrant colour
combinations, distinctive organic patterns, and compact forms of the keycha ins, while Interest
developed as visitors examined the products and asked about their unusual material composition.
Search-related behaviour appeared through questions concerning how bottle caps were processed, why
the material was selected, and how the products related to the environmental condition of Lake Perintis.
Action was observable through direct purchasing interactions, whereas Share emerged when buyers
documented their purchases through photographs or short videos and connected them with the proje ct
or destination through social media. Sasmita and Achmadi (2022) identify AISAS as a relevant
framework for understanding marketing communication in digital environments where consumer
behaviour can extend from attention and information seeking toward sh aring. Fadlurrahman et al.
(2023) similarly demonstrate the relevance of AISAS for tourism-oriented digital branding, particularly
when visual content and visitor engagement are connected. The observed sequence suggests that the
physical prototype could in itiate a communication process that extended beyond the immediate
exhibition encounter.

characteristics, narrative, and pri ce. The framework therefore conceptualises branding as a cyclical

CONCLUSION
The study developed an upcycled tourism merchandise model from plastic bottle caps collected
in the Lake Perintis context and transformed the material into functional keychains under the Perintis
by Aruna brand. The development process demonstrated that discarded plastic could be converted into
aesthetically distinctive products that combine local visual references, ecological meaning, practical
utility, and potential economic value. The Double Diamond pro cess connected waste identification,
destination-oriented identity formulation, material experimentation, prototype development, and
simulated market testing within an integrated design framework. Under the tagline “From Waste to
Worth,” twelve prototype units were offered at IDR 20,000 per unit during a campus-based exhibition,
and all twelve units were sold, generating IDR 240,000 in gross revenue. The complete sell-out provides
an initial indication of product acceptance within the specific simulated mar ket environment and
demonstrates that the transformed material could attract purchase interest when combined with
functional and visual value. However, the result should be interpreted as an initial proof -of-concept
because the production volume was limite d, the testing period was short, and the audience did not
represent a statistically defined tourism market. The findings indicate that plastic waste at a tourism
destination can be repositioned from an environmental burden into a tangible component of destination-
related creative production.
The principal conceptual contribution is the Eco -Visual Branding Loop, which integrates
ecological waste inputs, material transformation, local visual identity, digital storytelling, AISAS -
oriented consumer journeys, and iterative market feedback into a cyclical branding process. The model
positions upcycled merchandise not simply as a recycled product but as a tangible communication
medium through which environmental action and destination identity can be experienced
simultaneously. Material origin becomes part of the brand narrative because the physical object retains
a visible connection with the waste problem from which it was created. The product can also extend
destination communication beyond the exhibition context when consumers use, photograph, or s hare
the souvenir through digital platforms. Future development should expand production capacity while
maintaining material quality, integrate community co -creation, and establish clearer procedures for
thermal safety, durability, and quality control. Further research should employ buyer profiling, formal
material testing, longitudinal destination -brand awareness measures, and social media conversion
analytics to examine the sustainability and communication performance of the proposed model over
time. Thes e developments would allow the Eco -Visual Branding Loop to be evaluated beyond its
current micro -scale prototype context and potentially refined as a replicable approach to circular
tourism branding.
