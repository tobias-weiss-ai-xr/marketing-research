Abstract
Place branding has evolved into a complex, interdisciplinary domain central to governance, tourism,
economic development, and public diplomacy, yet the ﬁeld has lacked dedicated academic provision to
prepare its practitioners. This paper examines the professionalisation of place branding through the
design and delivery of the MA in Place Branding, one of the ﬁrst dedicated postgraduate degrees in the
discipline, developed in 2022 and delivered in 2023. Drawing on the author's dual position as academic
and practitioner, it argues that the ﬁeld's persistent legitimacy deﬁcit stems in part from the absence of
formal educational pathways through which practitioners can acquire shared knowledge, a common
analytical language, and an ethical grounding for their work. The paper sets out the theoretical
foundations that structured the programme, namely cultural intermediation, stakeholder theory, and
systems thinking, and derives from them a ﬁve-part professional competency framework spanning
theoretical literacy, stakeholder systems management, strategic brand design, evidence-based practice,
and ethical stewardship. It proposes a future model for place branding education that is interdisciplinary,
practice-informed, globally accessible, and benchmarked against explicit professional standards. The
paper contributes to ongoing debate about how place branding can mature into a recognised profession
supported by rigorous and sustainable educational infrastructure.
1. Introduction
Place branding has matured considerably over the past two decades. It has evolved from a marketing
subdiscipline into a complex, interdisciplinary domain that engages questions of identity, governance,
and civic strategy (Kavaratzis and Hatch 2013; Lucarelli 2018; Zenker and Erfgen 2014). The ﬁeld now
operates across policy areas that include tourism, urban planning, economic development, cultural
policy, and public diplomacy. Despite this expansion in scope and signiﬁcance, its formal educational
infrastructure remains underdeveloped. Practitioners enter the profession from diverse disciplinary
backgrounds such as urban planning, communications, marketing, and public administration, and they
often do so without training speciﬁc to the ethical, strategic, and interdisciplinary demands of place-
based identity work.
This paper addresses that gap. It documents the conception, design, delivery, and institutional trajectory
of the MA in Place Branding, delivered at a post-1992 university in London, one of the ﬁrst dedicated
postgraduate degrees in the discipline. Developed by the author in 2022 and delivered in 2023, the
programme was a deliberate response to what had long been identiﬁed as a persistent training deﬁcit in
the ﬁeld (Warren, 2025). The paper offers an original contribution to the scholarship of place branding
and to the scholarship of teaching and learning (SoTL) by giving a theoretically grounded account of how
postgraduate education in an emergent, interdisciplinary professional ﬁeld can be designed, what it can
achieve, and why its institutional conditions matter.
Beyond these educational outcomes, this paper signals a vital shift in the professionalization of place
branding. By establishing a rigorous academic home for the discipline, a post-graduate programme in
Page 2/26

place branding can provide a necessary scaffold for practice, including standardizing shared knowledge,
developing a common analytical language, and pivoting the ﬁeld from ad-hoc promotion toward a more
cohesive model of strategic governance. For practitioners, these implications are signiﬁcant: they offer a
pathway to bridge the legitimacy gap that has long characterized the profession, enabling them to
operate not merely as message managers, but as reﬂexive, values-led cultural intermediaries capable of
navigating complex multi-stakeholder landscapes with both critical insight and practical authority.
This paper ﬁrst situates the programme within the professional and intellectual landscape of place
branding, drawing on practitioner research and ﬁeld-level analysis to establish the case for dedicated
postgraduate provision. It then sets out the theoretical framework that underpinned the programme's
design before examining its curriculum architecture, pedagogical principles, and assessment strategy.
The analysis further explores the programme's stakeholder relevance and civic signiﬁcance, followed by
a critical reﬂection on the structural conditions that constrained its sustainability. Finally, the paper
considers implications for the future of place branding education and concludes by positioning the
programme's legacy within a broader argument for professionalising the ﬁeld.
Positionality and Methodological Frame
This paper adopts an autoethnographic case study approach (Chang 2008; Denzin 2014), drawing on the
author's experience as designer and lead academic of the MA in Place Branding. The author occupies a
dual position, as a practitioner-scholar with more than a decade of experience in place branding
education, consultancy, and research, and as the programme leader who conceptualised, validated, and
delivered the programme under examination. This positioning is not incidental to the analysis. It is
constitutive of it. As Bochner and Ellis (2016) argue, autoethnographic inquiry is well suited to the study
of professional practice in emergent ﬁelds, where the researcher's situatedness within the phenomenon
yields insights that detached observation cannot reach. The method also carries a responsibility for
critical reﬂexivity. The author's institutional investment in the programme's success is acknowledged
here, and the analysis that follows attends deliberately to structural and systemic factors, including
those beyond the author's control, that shaped the programme's outcomes. The paper does not claim
generalisability in the conventional sense. It offers instead a theoretically grounded and critically
reﬂective case study intended to generate transferable insights for educators, programme designers, and
scholars working to advance place branding as a discipline and a profession.
2. Context: The Professional and Educational Landscape of Place
Branding
In a policy context, place branding has been deﬁned as an ongoing, participatory, and evidence-informed
process through which governments and stakeholders articulate a shared identity and value proposition
for a place, and use that identity to guide strategic decisions, coordinate action, and communicate
coherently across audiences (Boisen et al. 2018; Kavaratzis and Hatch 2013). This deﬁnition reﬂects a
signiﬁcant conceptual shift away from the ﬁeld's earlier, more promotional framing. Contemporary
Page 3/26

scholarship increasingly positions place branding as a governance mechanism, one that operates not
only at the level of communication but also upstream in decision-making, where it helps shape
investment priorities, partnerships, and policy frameworks (Warren et al., 2021; Eshuis and Edwards
2013; Lucarelli 2018).
This governance turn has been accompanied by growing professional demand. Over the past decade,
place branding functions have expanded within local and national government, regional development
agencies, national tourism organisations, and private-sector consultancies. Practitioners in these roles
are routinely expected to navigate complex stakeholder environments, manage competing narratives,
and translate civic values into strategic communications, all while operating across political cycles and
institutional cultures that are frequently resistant to brand logic (Warren and Dinnie 2018; Warren and
Grigaliūnaitė 2023).
Those practitioners have rarely had access to formal education tailored to these demands. Doctoral
research in the ﬁeld, drawing on dozens of in-depth interviews with place branding professionals across
several national and municipal contexts, consistently surfaced a shared experience of professional
isolation and a deﬁcit of legitimacy, sometimes described by participants as ‘imposter syndrome’.
Practitioners characterised their work as institutionally valued but intellectually under-recognised, and
they frequently cited the absence of a shared disciplinary home, a professional community with common
knowledge structures, standards, and language (Warren 2021). This experience aligns with what Maguire
and Matthews (2012) identify as a deﬁning feature of the cultural intermediary condition: the
performance of inﬂuential symbolic work without the institutional recognition it warrants.
The absence of dedicated educational provision contributed directly to this legitimacy gap. By the early
2020s, place branding had established a credible academic publication record, including dedicated
journals such as this one, several Routledge handbooks, and a growing citation network. No
postgraduate degree programme existed, however, through which practitioners could gain formal
disciplinary grounding. Short courses, conference workshops, and practitioner-led webinars had
proliferated, but they operated outside the quality assurance frameworks, the critical pedagogical rigour,
and the sustained intellectual development that a structured academic programme provides.
Further evidence of unmet demand came from the author's experience as programme leader for an
undergraduate degree in advertising, public relations and branding at the same university. That degree
included a transnational ﬁnal-year undergraduate module entitled Place Branding and International
Diplomacy, delivered across the university's international campuses. Students consistently described the
module as the most intellectually engaging and professionally relevant component of their degree and
frequently requested pathways for postgraduate specialisation. These observations were reinforced by
encouragement from senior industry ﬁgures and practitioners aﬃliated with leading place-branding
consultancies and professional networks. Together they conﬁrmed that both professional demand and
academic precedent existed for a dedicated MA.

3. Theoretical Framework
3.1 Cultural Intermediation and the Ethics of
Representation
The concept of cultural intermediation provided the primary conceptual anchor for the programme. First
theorised by Bourdieu (1984) and later developed for media and branding contexts by Moor (2008) and
Maguire and Matthews (2012), the concept describes professionals who mediate between production
and consumption. Cultural intermediaries select, translate, and legitimise symbolic content, and in doing
so they shape how identities are constructed, circulated, and received. In place branding, this
intermediation is particularly complex. It involves navigating multi-sector stakeholder environments,
managing the intersection of commercial and civic logics, and exercising what Warren and Dinnie (2018)
describe as an invisible yet consequential form of inﬂuence that operates across political, cultural, and
bureaucratic domains simultaneously.
The programme positioned this concept not as a theoretical backdrop but as a guiding professional
ethic. Students were encouraged to understand themselves as narrative stewards, professionals with an
obligation to the inclusivity, authenticity, and long-term resonance of the place-based stories they help to
construct. This framing drew explicitly on the critical place branding literature, which has challenged the
ﬁeld to move beyond corporate marketing logic towards a more participatory and politically accountable
model of practice (Aronczyk 2013; Kavaratzis 2009; Lucarelli 2018).
3.2 Stakeholder Theory and Participatory Governance
Stakeholder theory, developed by Freeman (1984) and reﬁned by Mitchell, Agle, and Wood (1997),
provided a second framework. In place branding, the stakeholders of a place are not limited to external
target audiences. They include residents, civil servants, cultural institutions, community organisations,
diasporas, and many others whose claims on identity and representation must be actively negotiated.
Kavaratzis and Kalandides (2015) and Zenker and Erfgen (2014) have each advanced participatory
frameworks that locate legitimacy not in top-down brand strategy but in dialogue, co-creation, and
transparency, principles now central to the ﬁeld's governance turn.
These ideas were operationalised throughout the curriculum. Students were trained to map stakeholder
networks, design inclusive engagement processes, and anticipate the ethical tensions that arise when
identity-making occurs across unequal distributions of power and voice. The concept of the network
constellation, the dense and often misaligned web of actors whose competing interests shape place
brand outcomes, was introduced as both an analytical tool and an ethical reference point (Warren and
Grigaliūnaitė 2023).
3.3 Systems Thinking and Place Complexity
The third pillar of the framework drew on systems thinking, a paradigm rooted in complexity theory and
organisational studies (Meadows 2008; Senge 1990). Places are dynamic systems composed of
Page 5/26

interconnected social, economic, environmental, and symbolic elements. Branding interventions
therefore operate within and upon those systems rather than in isolation from them. This systemic
perspective resisted the reductive tendencies of promotional thinking. It encouraged students to analyse
unintended consequences, feedback loops, and the longer temporal arcs through which place identities
form and reform.
Systems thinking also informed the programme's approach to wicked problems, the complex and
multidimensional challenges such as urban inequality, postcolonial identity, and climate-related
regeneration that increasingly deﬁne the work of place branding practitioners. Students were equipped
with tools to map place systems, identify leverage points, and develop adaptive strategies that can
evolve as governance priorities shift. This approach connected to Anholt's (2020) argument for a more
collaborative and responsibility-oriented framework for understanding how places relate to each other
and to global challenges.
3.4 Integrating Theory and Practice
Across all three frameworks, the programme maintained a commitment to integrated and practical
application. Theoretical content was never delivered as an abstract backdrop. It was mobilised as a lens
through which professional situations could be analysed, critiqued, and acted upon. This commitment
aligned with Schön's (1987) concept of the reﬂective practitioner, a professional who does not simply
apply technical knowledge but thinks in action, drawing on conceptual frameworks while remaining
attentive to the speciﬁc complexity of each situation. For a ﬁeld as contextually varied and politically
contingent as place branding, this capacity for reﬂective integration is a prerequisite for competent and
ethical practice rather than an optional supplement to practical skill.
4. Programme Design and Pedagogical Principles
4.1 Constructive Alignment as Curriculum Architecture
The programme was designed in accordance with the principle of constructive alignment articulated by
Biggs and Tang (2011). This approach holds that effective curriculum design begins with clearly
speciﬁed intended learning outcomes (ILOs) and ensures that all teaching and learning activities and all
assessment tasks are coherently aligned with those outcomes. Rather than assembling content inputs
and attaching assessment as a separate mechanism, constructive alignment treats the assessment task
as the destination and works backwards, so that students are scaffolded towards it through a sequence
of meaningful learning activities.
In the MA in Place Branding, this principle was applied at both module and programme level. Each of the
ﬁve core modules, namely Place Branding Theories and Concepts, Place Brand Strategy and
Implementation, Stakeholders, Sustainability and Social Cohesion, Competitive Advantage, Creativity and
the Central Idea, and Research Methods and Major Project, was designed with distinct but mutually
reinforcing learning outcomes. Each set of outcomes corresponded to a phase in the professional
Page 6/26

lifecycle of place branding practice, moving from conceptual foundations through participatory
governance to creative strategy and evidence-based inquiry.
Assessment tasks were constructed to simulate authentic professional outputs, including brand strategy
documents, stakeholder engagement frameworks, campaign pitches, and independent research. These
outputs are not analogues to professional practice. They are instantiations of it, developed within a
critically supervised academic context. This alignment between academic rigour and professional
relevance was central to the programme's identity and to its appeal across the cohort's diverse career
stages.

4.2 Scaffolded Authentic Assessment
Page 9/26

Authentic assessment, as theorised by Wiggins and McTighe (2005) and developed in the higher
education literature by Ashford-Rowe, Herrington, and Brown (2014), requires assessment tasks to
reﬂect real-world problems and professional expectations, to demand transfer of learning across
contexts, and to generate products that practitioners and stakeholders in the ﬁeld would recognise as
meaningful. In practice this means moving beyond the traditional essay or examination towards tasks
that mirror the complexity, ambiguity, and multi-audience demands of actual professional work.
The programme's most developed application of this principle was in the module Competitive Advantage,
Creativity and the Central Idea, which used a three-stage scaffolded assessment sequence. The ﬁrst task,
a brand position and vision statement of 500 to 1,000 words, required students to conduct substantive
background research into a speciﬁc place's existing brand landscape, competitive context, and
stakeholder dynamics. This foundation informed the second task, a persuasive pitch presentation of a
central brand idea delivered in the persona of a municipal brand strategist addressing internal
stakeholders. The ﬁnal task was a comprehensive brand strategy and visual identity document, in which
students formalised their brand architecture, articulated creative direction, identiﬁed public-facing
touchpoints, and developed a phased implementation plan.
This sequence exempliﬁes what Ashford-Rowe, Herrington, and Brown (2014) identify as the critical
elements of authentic assessment, namely challenge, performance, transfer, metacognition, and a
product recognisable as authentic to external stakeholders. It also aligns with Knowles's (1980)
principles of andragogy, which describe adult learning as problem-centred, self-directed, and grounded in
immediate relevance to professional contexts. For mid-career students, the ﬁnal strategy document
frequently constituted a directly transferable work product. For recent graduates, it served as a portfolio
artefact that demonstrated industry-readiness.

4.3 Community of Practice and Cross-Generational
Learning
The inaugural cohort of eleven students formed an intentionally designed cross-generational learning
community (Lave and Wenger 1991; Wenger 1998). Roughly half were mid- or senior-level professionals
working in government, cultural institutions, or place-based consultancies across several continents. The
other half were recent graduates from the undergraduate advertising, public relations and branding
degree at the same university, many of whom had taken the undergraduate place branding module and
had chosen postgraduate specialisation.
This diversity generated a productive dynamic that a more homogeneous cohort could not have
produced. Professionals brought immediate contextual application. Week by week, they described how
the module's frameworks, including stakeholder auditing, brand narrative construction, and systems
mapping, were being deployed in their day-to-day professional contexts. Recent graduates brought
inquiry, theoretical engagement, and creative energy. The mutual challenge and accountability that
emerged constituted the kind of community of practice that Wenger (1998) describes: a group united by
Page 18/26

a shared domain, mutual engagement in practice, and a developing repertoire of knowledge, tools, and
analytical language.
The programme's online structure supported global participation through weekly live sessions
supplemented by asynchronous pre- and post-class content, expert lectures, and collaborative
workshops. Students joined from Canada, the United Kingdom, continental Europe, Vietnam, Ghana,
South Africa, and other contexts, contributing a pluralistic perspective on how place branding principles
operate and are resisted across different governance cultures. Nearly twenty guest speakers from
industry and academia contributed to the inaugural syllabus, among them representatives from leading
global branding consultancies, municipal brand strategists, destination-management practitioners
specialising in Indigenous tourism and inclusive storytelling, and policy oﬃcials from city governments
across several regions. This integration of practitioner knowledge with academic content
operationalised the situated cognition model (Brown, Collins, and Duguid 1989), in which learning is
most powerful when it occurs in context and through application to meaningful problems.
5. Stakeholder Relevance and the Civic Value of Place Branding
Education
The MA in Place Branding was never conceived as a narrowly disciplinary academic programme. From
the outset it was designed to generate demonstrable value for the sectors in which its graduates would
work, including government, tourism, cultural policy, and economic development. The distinction the
programme sought to make was between upskilling as a functional outcome and education as a civic
investment. Consistent with the ﬁeld's governance turn, the argument was that skilled, reﬂective, and
ethically grounded place branders contribute directly to the quality of public decision-making,
stakeholder engagement, and place identity formation.
This argument was borne out in the cohort's engagement. In reﬂective learning journals and live
sessions, professional students described how programme content had enabled them to articulate the
strategic signiﬁcance of place branding to senior decision-makers who had previously understood it only
as promotional activity. Several described using the frameworks developed in the programme to reframe
internal policy discussions, shifting conversations from logomarks and slogans towards stakeholder
alignment, values-based narrative, and long-term identity governance. This outcome speaks to a
recognised need in the ﬁeld. As place branding has taken on greater governance responsibility, the
practitioners delivering it require a depth of conceptual understanding that informal professional
development cannot reliably provide (Eshuis and Edwards 2013; Warren and Dinnie 2018).
The programme also modelled a distinctive form of sector bridging. The cohort's geographical and
professional diversity spanned urban planning, marketing, media, diplomacy, and municipal government
across several continents, creating a cross-sectoral learning environment. Students interrogated how
place branding principles were implemented, adapted, and contested in varying governance cultures,
drawing comparative insights that neither a purely academic nor a purely practitioner-based programme
Page 19/26

could have generated. This cross-fertilisation of sector knowledge and lived professional experience is
itself a model for how place branding education can contribute to the ﬁeld's maturation as both a
scholarly discipline and a professional practice.
While this study offers an account of the programme’s design and outcomes, it is important to
acknowledge certain limitations. First, the inaugural cohort of 11 students, while rich in cross-sectoral
experience, constitutes a modest evidence base from which to draw broad conclusions. Second, the
autoethnographic nature of the inquiry, conducted by the programme’s designer, introduces the risk of
self-justiﬁcation, necessitating the critical reﬂexivity employed throughout this analysis. Acknowledging
these tensions is crucial for validating the ﬁndings and invites further longitudinal research into the long-
term impact of such educational interventions.
6. Structural Challenges: UK Higher Education and the Conditions for
Innovation
6.1 Systemic Pressures on Postgraduate Provision
The MA in Place Branding launched into a UK higher education sector experiencing acute and
compounding structural pressures. The formal withdrawal from the Erasmus+ mobility programme
following Brexit signiﬁcantly reduced inbound student ﬂows from European Union member states. This
particularly affected post-1992 universities such as the one in question, which had previously attracted
strong cohorts of EU students under mobility agreements. At the same time, changes to the immigration
rules for international students, including the removal of dependent visa rights that had been a
signiﬁcant factor in recruitment from markets such as Nigeria and India, redirected prospective
postgraduate students towards competitor destinations with more accessible entry conditions, among
them Canada, Ireland, Germany, and the Netherlands (McGettigan 2023; Universities UK 2024).
The domestic funding context compounded these pressures. UK universities had operated under a
capped undergraduate tuition fee structure since 2012, and after a decade of inﬂation, those fees
represented a declining real-terms income. Postgraduate provision is not subject to the same regulatory
cap, but its ﬁnancial logic is similar. In a context of squeezed institutional margins, the short-term return-
on-investment calculation for new programmes favours established, high-demand subjects over
interdisciplinary innovations with longer market-building timelines, and it does so most sharply for
emerging, low-search-volume ﬁelds.
The removal of student-number controls at selective universities after 2015 had a further indirect effect.
As research-intensive institutions expanded undergraduate intake, post-1992 universities faced
intensiﬁed competition for domestic students across subject areas. In this environment, the institutional
risk appetite for new programme development declined, and programmes came to be evaluated
increasingly by near-term enrolment metrics rather than by longer-term academic or societal impact.

6.2 Marketing a New Discipline Without Institutional
Support
The programme's ﬁrst year saw an enrolment of 11 students, a ﬁgure that, in any established disciplinary
ﬁeld, would represent a creditable inaugural cohort. Place branding, however, lacks the search identity
and institutional recognition of established academic subjects. Prospective students cannot easily ﬁnd it
through conventional disciplinary channels, and many who would beneﬁt from such a programme do not
yet identify themselves as potential place branding postgraduates. Effective recruitment for a
programme of this kind requires sustained investment in content marketing, professional network
engagement, and partnership with industry associations, investment that was not provided.
All promotional activity was conducted by the programme leader alone, through organic professional
networking, conference presentations, and partnership with sector media outlets and professional
networks. These channels reached motivated and well-qualiﬁed applicants, but they could not substitute
for the digital marketing infrastructure, enrolment agent relationships, and institutional brand support
that are standard in competitive postgraduate recruitment. This resourcing gap is not unique to this
case. It reﬂects a systemic problem in the way UK universities incubate new programmes in emerging
ﬁelds, which require more than one cycle to establish market visibility and a word-of-mouth reputation.
6.3 Lessons for Interdisciplinary Programme Development
Three structural lessons emerge from this experience with implications beyond this speciﬁc case. First,
programmes in emergent interdisciplinary ﬁelds cannot be assessed against the enrolment benchmarks
of established disciplines. The legitimate peripheral participation that characterises entry into any new
professional ﬁeld (Lave and Wenger 1991) applies equally to prospective students entering a ﬁeld they
are still learning to name and value. Building a market for such a programme requires multi-year
investment and institutional patience.
Second, professionalising a ﬁeld, by developing shared knowledge frameworks, ethical standards, and
recognised educational pathways, requires active institutional advocacy rather than academic validation
alone. Place branding, like other hybrid governance domains such as cultural diplomacy and urban
innovation, sits between established disciplinary categories, and it is therefore vulnerable to
deprioritisation precisely because it resists easy classiﬁcation.
Third, the intellectual and pedagogical capital developed in programmes such as this one does not
disappear when the programme closes. The frameworks, assessment designs, and community
structures created for the MA in Place Branding have continued to inform practice through ongoing
academic leadership in the ﬁeld, through the development of a practitioner-focused CPD platform, and
through the professional trajectories of the graduates themselves. The discontinuation of the
programme is evidence not of its failure but of a structural environment insuﬃciently equipped to
support slow-growth, high-impact educational ventures in emerging ﬁelds.

7. The Future of Place Branding Education
7.1 Towards a Professional Competency Framework
For place branding education to develop with the rigour the ﬁeld requires, it needs a shared foundation: a
clearly articulated competency framework that identiﬁes the knowledge, skills, and ethical commitments
a qualiﬁed place branding practitioner should possess. Such a framework would serve as both a
curriculum design reference and a standard against which professional development offerings, including
short courses, micro-credentials, and continuing professional development pathways, could be
benchmarked.
The MA in Place Branding proposed one model for this foundation, based on ﬁve core competencies that
are essential for the professional practitioner:
1. Theoretical Literacy: A deep understanding of place branding scholarship and its allied
interdisciplinary ﬁelds.
2. Stakeholder Systems Management: The capacity to map, navigate, and engage complex multi-
sector stakeholder environments.
3. Strategic Brand Design: Fluency in developing brand strategy, visual identity, and creative narrative.
4. Evidence-Based Practice: A grounded approach to research, data analysis, and strategy
implementation.
5. Ethical Stewardship: An enduring commitment to inclusivity, representation, and the long-term
governance of place identity.
These competencies align with the ﬁeld's governance turn and the broader demands that public-sector
employers place on strategic communications professionals (Eshuis and Edwards 2013; Kavaratzis,
Zenker, and Warnaby 2020).
Developing such a framework would require cross-sector collaboration among universities, professional
bodies such as the International Place Branding Association, consultancies, and public-sector
employers. No single institution can complete the task alone, but a dedicated postgraduate programme
is well positioned to initiate it and to test it through live pedagogical practice.
7.2 Modular and Hybrid Provision
The structural conditions of UK higher education, combined with the dispersed professional geography
of potential place branding students, who are located across sectors and continents, point to the value
of more ﬂexible, modular models of provision. Micro-credentials and stackable learning pathways, in
which short and targeted courses accumulate towards a larger qualiﬁcation, have attracted signiﬁcant
interest across the sector as a mechanism for extending higher education's reach to working
professionals (Universities UK 2024).
Page 22/26

For place branding, this model has particular appeal. The ﬁeld's practitioners are rarely in a position to
pause their careers for full-time study, and the global distribution of relevant professional experience
makes synchronous cohort-based programmes diﬃcult to sustain without online infrastructure. Some
private offerings already attempt to meet demand through this modular approach, using accredited short
courses or small online cohorts that embed the reﬂective, community-based learning principles of the
university environment in a format accessible to working professionals across time zones and career
stages.
Private provision of this kind cannot replace the epistemic authority, critical rigour, and quality assurance
that formal university programmes offer. The most effective future model for place branding education is
likely to be a hybrid ecosystem in which university-validated programmes, whether MA degrees,
executive programmes, or credit-bearing certiﬁcates, sit at the apex of a broader learning infrastructure
that also includes industry-delivered continuing professional development, peer networks, and
practitioner-led knowledge exchange. Each component serves a distinct function, and none can
substitute for the others.
7.3 Global Relevance and Equitable Access
Any future model for place branding education must take the global distribution of the ﬁeld's most
pressing challenges and its most engaged practitioners seriously. Post-industrial regeneration,
postcolonial rebranding, climate resilience, and social cohesion are not uniquely Western problems. They
are the deﬁning place challenges of the era, experienced with particular urgency in the Global South, in
rapidly urbanising economies, and in communities navigating transitions that no established branding
template adequately addresses. An education ecosystem accessible only to those who can afford UK or
European tuition fees, or who can take extended leave from professional roles, will systematically
exclude the practitioners and places that most need its insights.
The online and globally distributed model piloted by the MA in Place Branding, which brought students
from six continents into a common online learning community, demonstrated that this accessibility is
achievable without sacriﬁcing pedagogical depth. What it requires is institutional commitment to ﬂexible
delivery, authentic assessment designs that are portable across professional contexts, and genuine
investment in the community-building infrastructure through which cross-cultural learning occurs.
8. Conclusion
The MA in Place Branding was an ambitious and timely educational intervention. As the ﬁrst
postgraduate programme in the ﬁeld, it demonstrated that place branding can sustain rigorous academic
study at postgraduate level, that its theoretical foundations in cultural intermediation, stakeholder theory,
and systems thinking are rich enough to structure a coherent and demanding curriculum, and that the
practitioners and emerging professionals who enrolled were able to integrate academic rigour with
professional application in ways that generated immediate and lasting value.
Page 23/26

The programme also demonstrated a less comfortable truth: that educational innovation in emerging
ﬁelds is structurally precarious in the current UK higher education environment. The conditions that
constrained its sustainability, including demographic and policy shifts in student recruitment, under-
resourced programme marketing, and the absence of multi-year institutional commitment, are not
idiosyncratic to this case. They are symptomatic of a funding model that has reduced universities'
capacity to make the patient, high-conviction commitments that ﬁeld-building educational innovation
requires.
The argument of this paper is that professionalising place branding, as a discipline, a practice, and a
civic function, depends on the development of dedicated, rigorous, and ethically grounded educational
provision. Practitioners equipped to understand the conceptual complexity of place identity, to navigate
multi-stakeholder governance environments, to design inclusive and evidence-informed strategies, and
to account critically for the symbolic power they exercise are not only better professionals. They are
better civic actors, and the places they serve are better served.
The implications for practice follow directly. Universities and their partners should treat competency
frameworks, portable authentic assessment, and multi-year incubation not as optional reﬁnements but
as the operating conditions for the viable provision of education in emerging professional ﬁelds. The MA
in Place Branding was a prototype. Its intellectual and pedagogical legacy continues, and this paper has
sought to document it not as an institutional achievement but as a contribution to an ongoing
conversation about what it means to educate for the complex, contested, and consequential work of
branding places. The ﬁeld is ready for that provision. Whether the institutions that could support it are
prepared to make the commitment remains an open question.
