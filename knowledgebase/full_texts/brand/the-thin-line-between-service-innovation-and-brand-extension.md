1. INTRODUCTION

During 2002, the Council for Medical
Schemes (“the Council”) commissioned the
University of Pretoria to conduct a survey of
governance practices among trustees of
medical schemes, with a view to better
understanding governance practices and
providing information to  allow trustees to
benchmark their governance practices
against best industry practice.  The study
reinforced the view that sound governance
of medical schemes was critical to their
successful operation and to the best
interests of members.  The study found
many aspects of board function that were
working well.  At the same time, there were
many gaps in board performance against
reasonable expectations, some widespread
and serious.  Specific recommendations
w e r e  m a d e  a s  t o  h o w  i m p r o v e m e n t s  c o u l d
be effected, which were widely
communicated to trustees.

Since then, there have continued to be
unacceptable levels of governance failure
within medical schemes.  Governance
failure, for purposes of this report, is defined
as the violation of the principles of good
governance. This often results in the total
collapse of the medical scheme or severe
difficulties encountered by the scheme.
However, the effects of governance failure
need not be immediat ely apparent in the
operations and performance of a medical
scheme.  Left unchecked, though, such
failures are likely to lead to tangible adverse
effects on the scheme.

Governance failures undermine the statutory
objective of the Council to protect the
interests of beneficiaries of medical
schemes.  It was therefore considered
necessary to follow the 2002 survey with a
more limited review of key aspects of
current governance practices within medical
schemes, and to focus more specifically on
key determinants of governance failure.
Key objectives of the project were:

a. to identify causes of governance
failure;

b. to assess the extent to which
the existing governance model,
and/or misapplication of the
model, contribute to the
identified causes; and

c. to recommend additional
strategies to improve medical
scheme governance and
mitigate the risk of governance
failure.

2. THEORETICAL, LEGAL AND POLICY
FRAMEWORK

21. The concept of corporate
        governance

Corporate governance is the system by
which business corporations are directed
and controlled. The corporate governance
structure specifies the distribution of rights
and responsibilities among different
participants in the corporation, such as the
board, managers, shareholders and other
stakeholders, and spells out the rules and
procedures for making decisions on
corporate affairs. By so doing, it also
provides the structure through which the
company’s objectives are set, and the
means of attaining those objectives and
monitoring performance.

In the context of medical schemes,
corporate governance is concerned with
promoting independence, transparency,
accountability and discipline within the
management and operation of the schemes.
It covers the structures and systems that a
medical scheme has in place to oversee its
affairs. This involves a number of elements,
including a clear understanding by board
members of the scheme's strategic
objectives, systems to ensure the effective
management of risks, and mechanisms to
ensure that the scheme's obligations are
identified and discharged effectively.

Sound corporate governance in medical
schemes is essential not only to the
wellbeing of the individual medical scheme,

 4
but also as a critical ingredient in
maintaining a financially sound and robust
private health care industry that ensures the
protection of beneficiaries.

Responsibility for maintaining sound
corporate governance within medical
schemes rests with the board of trustees.
Trustees are the representatives of the
medical scheme members and their
dependants, and are legally responsible for
the direction of the scheme on their behalf.

These responsibilities can be best met
through a sound medical scheme
governance philosophy and through policies
and practices that maximize the board’s
overall focus, effectiveness, and efficiency.
Board policy and practice should in
particular promote good decision making,
proper and timely execution, clear
accountability and regular performance
review.

2.2 Overview of the medical
scheme governance model

Figure 1 provides a diagrammatic outline of
the relationships between a medical scheme
and other entities which directly or indirectly
affect, or are involved in, the governance of
a medical scheme.

In terms of the governance model
contemplated by the statutory framework,
the board of trustees of a medical scheme
governs, and is responsible for, the business
of the medical scheme.

The trustees exercise their powers of
oversight of the affairs of a medical scheme
in terms of a mandate given to them by the
members of a scheme.  The scope and
exercise of the powers which trustees are
mandated to perform are to some extent
prescribed by law, although the scope of
these powers may be extended in the rules
of a medical scheme provided that this is
not inconsistent with the legal parameters.

Having received a mandate from the
members of a scheme to exercise powers of
governance, the trustees are accountable to
those members for the manner in which
those powers have been exercised.  The
manners in which trustees are, and could
be, held accountable are dealt with
elsewhere in this report.

The board of trustees appoints an executive
to manage the day-to-day affairs of the
medical scheme, and to report to it on
issues relating to the performance of the
scheme.  The principal officer is the chief
executive officer of the medical scheme,
possibly supported by a more substantive
staffing infrastructure.  In many schemes,
the executive structure is not suited to
performing all the administrative functions
relating to the business of a medical
scheme.  As a result, a portion of these
functions is typically outsourced.

In third party administered schemes,
virtually the entire administrative function is
outsourced to a third party administrator.  A
variety of other functions are outsourced to
other third party intermediaries, including
managed care organisations and brokers.
The activities of these entities should be
overseen by the scheme executive, in terms
of a service level agreement between the
scheme and the third party.

 5
The governance model is predicated on the
board of trustees being independent from
these third party contractors.  Governance
decisions should be taken in the interests of
the members of a medical scheme, rather
than being influenced by the commercial
interests of a third party.

The legal parameters of the powers of
trustees derive primarily from two sources:
the common law, in particular as it has been
amplified through the King Report; and
statute, in this case the Medical Schemes
Act, 131 of 1998.

2.2 Common Law

As persons who exercise fiduciary
responsibilities over the financial affairs of
others, at common law they are expected to
meet certain duties:

a. First, they have a duty of care.
In other words, they should
exercise the same level of care,
diligence, and skill over the
affairs of others that an
ordinarily prudent person would
exhibit over her or his own
affairs.  This requires that
decisions should be taken with
honesty and integrity and with
due consideration to all relevant
facts.

b. Secondly, they have a duty of
loyalty.  In other words, they
must always ensure that the
interests of members of their
medical scheme remain
paramount in their decisions
and dealings, and that they do
not use their positions for
personal gain. Loyalty to
members also entails scrupulous
protection of the confidentiality
of individual member
information.

c. Thirdly, they have a duty of
obedience.  They are required
to act in accordance with the
relevant legislation and with the
rules of the medical scheme.

The common law has been expanded and
amplified by the King Committee, which
produced a code of conduct and guidelines
for good corporate governance for use by
South African industries. The guidelines
covered a variety of issues including: the
role and functions of boards and directors;
risk management and internal audit;
accounting and auditing; and legal
compliance.

Of particular relevance to medical schemes
is the King report’s description of the
respective roles of the board, chairperson,
executive and non executive directors and
the chief executive officer.  It also details
particular conditions for good corporate
governance in relation to: director selection
and development; appraisal of boards and
directors; disqualification of directors; and
board committees.

Central to all of these issues are seven
principles of good corporate governance
identified by King, namely: discipline;
transparency; independence; accountability;
responsibility; fairness; and social
responsibility.

2.3 Medical Schemes Act, 131 of 1998

In terms of section 24(2) of the Medical
Schemes Act (“the Act”), no medical scheme
shall be registered unless the Council is
satisfied that members of the board of
trustees and the principal officer of the
proposed medical scheme are fit and proper
persons to hold the office concerned.  The
statutory duties of the board of trustees of
medical scheme, however, derive primarily
from the provisions of section 57 of the Act.

In terms of that section, all medical schemes
shall have a board of trustees who shall be
fit and proper to manage the business of the
scheme.  At least half the members of the
board shall be elected from amongst
members of the medical scheme.

 6
To reduce opportunity for conflict of
interest, certain categories of person may
not serve as trustees of a medical scheme.
These include (a) brokers; and (b)
employees, directors, consultants or
contractors of the administrator of the
medical scheme concerned, or of the
holding company, subsidiary, joint venture
or associate of that administrator.1

The Act imposes certai n general obligations
on trustees, arising from their common law
obligations.  These duties are: to protect the
interest of members at all times; to act with
due care diligence, skill and good faith; and
to act with impartiality in respect of all
beneficiaries.

Section 57 also lists a number of specific
duties of boards of trustees.  These include:
appointment of the principal officer;
accountability for operations of the scheme
and resolutions passed by the board;
ensuring that proper control systems are in
place; communication to members on rights,
benefits, contributions, and duties in terms
of rules of the scheme; ensuring timeous
payment of contributions to the scheme;
procuring professional indemnity insurance
and fidelity guarantee insurance; obtaining
expert advice on legal, accounting, and
business matters as required; ensuring
compliance with the Act; and protecting the
confidentiality of member information.

Further obligations on trustees arise from
the rules of medical schemes, which set the
legal parameters of the relationship between
members and medical schemes.  Section 29
of the Act sets out certain minimum
requirements to be contained in the rules of
a medical scheme, with a view to protecting
the interests of members and providing a
framework for good governance.  For
example, rules are required to include
provisions relating to: the appointment,
removal from office, powers and
remuneration of officers of a medical
scheme; processes for the execution of

custody of securities, books, documents and
other effects of medical scheme; and the
appointment of auditors of a medical

3. CONCEPTUALISING THE
POTENTIAL FOR GOVERNANCE
FAILURE

Having described how the medical schemes
governance model should work in theory, it
is relatively easy to identify potential sources
of failure in application of the model at a
conceptual level.  We will illustrate these
potential sources of failure in a series of
diagrammatic departures from the model in
Figure 1.

Figure 2 illustrates the scenario in which
there is no effective participation by
members in the affairs of the medical
scheme.

If members lack a voice in the affairs of the
medical scheme, the notion of trustees
acting in terms of a mandate given by their
members becomes meaningless.

 7

Similarly, in the absence of a participative
membership, the notion of trustee
accountability to members of the scheme
becomes equally senseless.

The absence of both a meaningful mandate
and a process for keeping trustees
accountable for the exercise of a mandate
leaves a medical scheme vulnerable to the
probability that the interests of members will
not be optimally served by the board of
trustees.  It also creates the potential for
trustees to serve their own interests on the
board or to derive their mandate from, and
establish lines of a ccountability to, other
persons or entities which may have a vested
financial interest in the affairs of the scheme
which may not necessarily accord with the
interests of members.

Figure 3 illustrates the scenario where a
medical scheme lacks an effective executive.
management structure.

In those instances where the administration
of the medical scheme is basically
performed in-house, the removal or
breakdown of an effective management
structure will obviously lead to complete
dysfunctionality of the administrative
processes of the medical scheme.

Where the administrative and other business
processes of a medical scheme are largely
outsourced to third party contractors, the
absence of an effective executive
management structure within the medical
scheme calls into question the effectiveness
of any delegation of powers to the executive
(and the reciprocal reporting responsibilities
which arise from delegation), as well as the
extent to which adequate monitoring takes
place of the performance of third party
contractors in terms of their service level
agreements with the medical scheme.

In fact, the potential is created for
governance responsibilities within the
medical scheme to be effectively delegated
to the third party administrator or other
third party contractor.

Under these conditions, any notion of
governance of medical schemes occurring
independently from the commercial interests
of third parties is seriously jeopardised.

Compare this scenario to the one presented
in Figure 4.

In that scenario, a strong executive
management structure remains in place
within the medical scheme, but, there is an
effective removal or breakdown of the third

 8
party administrators or other third party
contractors.

In this scenario, the governance structure of
the medical scheme is not affected.  There
may be a temporary administrative lapse in
the medical scheme, but this situation can
be managed by the executive of the medical
scheme.

The executive has the option of creating
internal capacity to self-administer the
medical scheme (or to take on its own
functions relating to managed care,
marketing etc).  Alternatively, if the scheme
preference is to remain administered by a
third party or to have another third party
performing other business functions for the
scheme, the executive of the scheme is in a
position to enter into a contract with
another third party  to perform these
functions.

A further scenario arises when the board of
trustees is itself dysfunctional (see Figure
5).

The possible factors giving rise to
dysfunctionality of the board are discussed
elsewhere in this report.

If the board is not operating effectively, the
governance of the medical scheme is
seriously compromised in almost every
respect.  There can be no question of a
mandate provided by members being
effectively exercised, nor of an
accountability relationship between
members and the board of trustees.

To the extent that there is an executive
management operating within the medical
scheme, the absence of an effective board
of trustees means that the executive cannot
be managing within a well-defined set of
delegations, and cannot be held responsible
for non-performance of those delegations.

Under these circumstances, any question of
effective governance of the scheme
becomes nonsensical.

In the worst case scenario (see Figure 6),
there is no effective member voice or
participation in the affairs of the medical
scheme, an absence of effective executive
management of the scheme, and no
functional board of trustees.  Under these
conditions, the scheme operates without any
semblance of a governance structure, and
with no mandate, no internal accountability,
and no effective delegations.

In this kind of scenario, the only parties
which exercise any significant control over

 9
the affairs of the medical scheme are the
third party administrators and other
intermediaries.  There are also no effective
checks and balances to this control.  It is the
interests of these parties, rather than the
interests of members, which becomes
paramount.

Under these conditions, the medical scheme
would effectively be a “front” for the
commercial third parties, and would be a
not-for-profit mutual entity only in form and
not in substance.

Nevertheless, it has been demonstrated in
the above discussion of the various
scenarios, that it is not necessary for this
worst case scenario to occur before
governance failure may result.  Application
of the governance model illustrated in
Figure 1 may fail as a result of absence of
dysfunctionality of any of the governance-
related components of the model: strong
member voice and participation, a working
executive management structure, or a
functional board of trustees.

From a conceptual understanding of the
critical success factors relating to application
of the governance model, the governance
theme project sought to review the practical
working of the model in some more detail
and then to focus on applying the theory of
governance failure to actual experience of
governance failure in the environment.

4. PROJECT METHODOLOGY

There were essentially three components to
the project which have been drawn upon for
completion of this report.

First, a survey was conducted in a cross-
section of medical schemes to develop an
understanding of generally prevailing
governance practices in the industry.
Twenty four registered medical schemes
participated in the survey.  A non-probability
convenience sampling was used to select
schemes.  The schemes were stratified by
type (open or restricted) and included a
cross-section of schemes in terms of
governance and financial performance.  Six
administrator and 24 medical scheme
representatives were interviewed using a
semi-structured questionnaire on a range of
governance related issues. Quantitative data
analysis was limited, and was intended for
exploratory purposes.  Qualitative data
analysis allowed for an in-depth
interpretation of the data and thematic
coding of the results.

Secondly, a more focused case study
methodology was employed to identify
causes of governance failure in medical
schemes which had experienced significant
governance problems within the preceding
two years.   An explanatory multiple case
study approach was followed.  This
approach allowed for in-depth investigation
of issues, as well as for the assessment of
causal factors of governance failure from
more than one scheme using data from
several sources.  Eight medical schemes
which had experienced some form of
governance failure in the preceding two
years were selected for the study.2
Data sources included: court papers;
inspection document s; audit reports;
statutory returns; medical scheme rules;
financial reports and correspondence.  The
advantage of using multiple sources of
information was that it allowed for the
evaluation of a broad range of historical,
attitudinal and behavioural issues.
Quantitative indicators included
membership, board size and financial
soundness.  Qualitative indicators included:
managerial culture; board effectiveness
(composition, committees, and
compensation); influence of the
chairperson; internal control systems; key
strategic issues, such as choice, clarity and
execution of strategy, and ability to adjust
to regulatory or market changes.  They also
included extent of strategic oversight in
relation to, evaluation of the scheme and
board, use of performance measurement

5.1 Background
Reporting on project findings begins with a
discussion of member voice and
participation.  This is  because, as described
in the model above, the origin of the
mandate to the trustees to govern the
affairs of the scheme should be in the
membership of the scheme.  The whole
notion of accountability, which is discussed
in greater detail in the next section, is

members who give the mandate, and it is to
the members that trustees should be
accountable in terms of that mandate.  And
it is through this mandate and accountability
that members can satisfy themselves that
the scheme is being governed in their
interests.  If members do not have an
effective voice, decision-making by trustees
may at best be guided by a benign sense of
what members want and need (which might
not correspond particularly well in fact with
the actual wants and needs of members).
At worst, schemes are rendered vulnerable
to being used for sectoral or external

the interests of members overall.

5.2 Findings

Most schemes reported that members had
easy access to the principal officer and
trustees, although they might need to
contact trustees through the principal
officer.  In some instances, members are
allowed to write to the trustees or make
appointments to see them. To facilitate such
a process, schemes would provide members
with all trustee names and contact details

such as telephone numbers and emails.  In
employer-based restricted schemes, the
trustees would be well known within the
company and would also be accessible to
members.  Some schemes also include
provision in their rules for member access to
trustees.

There are schemes where access to trustees
is less institutionalised and is usually
relegated to trustee contact with members
at the AGM, although even in these cases
some trustees have reported having been
approached informally by members outside
the AGM.

More generally, most schemes reported
having comprehensive, objective and
understandable communi cation processes,
which allowed them to effectively
communicate all relevant changes to
members.  Typical communication processes
are listed in Table One.

Communication to members could be done
by the administrator, executive
management, brokers, or by using the
infrastructure of the employer, depending
on the scheme’s contractual arrangements.

Restricted schemes sometimes communicate
with members through the human resources
departments and internal billboards of
employers.  Responses were mixed with
regard to attitude to brokers. Various
respondents regarded them as “a necessary
evil” to the industry; one that nonetheless
had to be tolerated.

A particularly important opportunity for
scheme interaction and communication with
members is provided by the Annual General
Meeting (AGM).  Procedural requirements
for AGMs are typically set out in the rules of
schemes.  Theoretically, AGMs provide
opportunity for direct member involvement
in scheme governance through election of
trustees.  In most ca ses, members who do
not attend the AGM are not eligible for
nomination to the Board. The AGM is also a
forum through which schemes engage
members directly on issues pertaining to
benefits and contributions.  In most
schemes, the AGM or special general
meetings provided the only effective
opportunity for members to participate in
discussion on changes in benefits and
contributions.

Typical issues which form part of AGM
agendas included: declaration of a quorum;
the annual report; election of trustees;
honoraria; concerns pertaining to the
scheme; members’ issues; regulatory issues;
financial statements; remuneration of
trustees; the chairperson’s report; the
auditor’s report; and tabling of motions.
Provision also exists in the rules of most
schemes for the removal of trustees at the
AGM (and sometimes outside the AGM too),
as well as for the passing of a motion of no
confidence on the board at a meeting.

with members relates to resolution of
complaints, although all the schemes
interviewed reported that the number of
complaints was generally small and
manageable. Complaints  received generally
related to: inadequate benefits; affordability

A clear indicator of low levels of member

generally very low, despite initiatives by
some schemes to actively encourage
members to participate. The participation

In some schemes, members who attended

specific concerns.  Some trustees described
member participation in AGMs as “passive.”

Trustees pointed out that, while attendance
at AGMs is actively encouraged, a
contributory factor to poor attendance is the
location of venues, which constrains
member participation because of distances,
time and costs involved.  At the consultative
seminar, various other logistical factors were
identified which impeded member
attendance at AGMs.  These included the
timing of AGMs, with time slots both in and
out of working hours presenting various
difficulties for members.  The logistical
constraints on member participation in
AGMs are clearly very real.
At the same time, survey and seminar
participants raised an alternative explanation
to the issue of low member participation in
AGMs.  This explanation suggests that
member “apathy” can be attributed to a
significant extent to a perception that their
participation will make no difference to
decision-making in medical schemes, at
least in so far as these decisions affect
issues of member interest.  If members are
unable to take decisions at AGMs which bind
the trustees in so far as the determination of
benefits and contributions, there could be
little incentive for members to participate
(particularly given the logistical constraints
to participation described above).  It was
also pointed out that the opportunity to
review and comment on annual financial
statements in AGMs offered little or no
additional incentive for participation because
that was “history”.  It was suggested that
the perception that members could not
influence outcomes in AGMs is reinforced by
the fact that AGMs are typically not
structured as fora where members can

participate, debate and make decisions
regarding trustee mandates.

A further comment suggested that members
may be disincentivised to participate in
scheme governance due to a perception that
many issues affecting determination of
benefits and contributions are in any event
outside the control of medical schemes –
particularly in the context of uncontrolled
escalation of the costs of healthcare on the
supply side.

Another interesting observation was made in
relation to the fact that members may feel
disempowered at AGMs by subversion of
voting processes.  As a key example of this,
a participant at the seminar related how
some members come to AGMs armed with
up to 30 000 proxy forms – which effectively
makes a mockery of voting processes in
AGMs.

Some delegates at the consultative seminar
expressed the view that the very low levels
of member participation in AGMs raised
fundamental questions about the legitimacy
of decisions taken by boards of trustees.
Questions were asked about whether
trustees elected at AGMs with negligible
attendance could legitimately be regarded
as holding a mandate from members to
govern the affairs of medical schemes.
Furthermore, questions were asked about
the basis for decision-making by boards – if
member interests were not adequately
represented on boards, what interests were
driving decision-making?

participants at the consultative seminar get
to the heart of the problem of inadequate

5.3 Potential Solutions

Survey and seminar participants identified
the need for further consultation and
research around mechanisms to incentivise
member participation in medical schemes.
In this regard, it was suggested that
consideration should be given to
participative models used in the trade union
movement.

While this is undoubtedly supported, it was
clear from the survey that more can already
be done from a regulatory and industry
perspective to encourage member
participation based on our current
understanding of the issues.   There is
clearly room for improvement on methods
that are already been used to facilitate
member participation, but there are also
ideas for new innovations which need
serious consideration.
a. Communication

There was a widespread perception among
respondents that both the “how” and the
“what” of communication could be
improved.

In relation to “how” communications are
carried out, participants believed that
schemes, administrators and the Council for
Medical Schemes needed to intensify efforts
to educate members about the value of their
participation in medical schemes.  This
needed to be accompanied by a “serious
campaign of jargon bust ing,” to ensure that
members are not disempowered by
complexity in communications, scheme rules
and so on.

Some participants suggested more effective
use of broker networks for enhancing
member voice and participation.  This is
regarded as a useful suggestion given the
extensive distribution network represented
by brokers, and the fact that many brokers

 14
are already been paid to provide ongoing
services to members.  However, it needs to
be borne in mind that broker interests may
not necessarily be aligned to member
interests in cases where financial
incentivisation of brokers may favour the
interests of a third party rather than the
independent interests of a member.

To reduce the potential for brokers to be
partisan towards medical schemes or
administrators, rather than acting as
independent agents of members, the
recommendation was made to remove the
current regulatory requirement that brokers
must enter into a written agreement with a
medical scheme as a precondition for
payment of commission by that medical
scheme.

In relation to “what” communications are
made, it was suggested that a sense of
ownership of scheme affairs could be
engendered if trustees not only
communicated decisions, but also found
ways of more effectively communicating the
process of decision-making and the factors
that informed those decisions – for example
in explaining how decisions relating to
contributions and benefits were arrived at.

It was further recommended that member
understanding of medical scheme issues
would be enhanced by greater industry-wide
standardization of communications on issues
such as prescribed minimum benefits,
designated service providers, preferred
provider networks and benefit option design.
In this regard, both the Council for Medical
Schemes and the Board of Healthcare
Funders of Southern Africa (BHF) were
believed to have a role.

It was also suggested that every scheme
should be encouraged to specifically report
on levels of member participation annually,
including in reports presented at AGMs.

b. AGMs

Participation constraints regarding venues
and timing of AGMs may differ from the
membership of one medical scheme to
another.  It was recommended that medical
schemes should try to better understand the
constraints and preferences of their
membership through conducting member
surveys (including telephonic surveys where
return on postal surveys is likely to be
small).

Schemes should more actively mobilise
membership for attendance at AGMs.  This
could include: negotiating with employers to
release employees for purposes of
attendance; requiring brokers, as part of
their ongoing service obligations, to notify,
remind and encourage members to
participate in AGMs; and where schemes
have regional structures, to require them to
engage specifically with members in their
regions.

It was also suggested that constraints on
location could in some cases be overcome
by holding the AGM in the area most
populated by scheme membership, and then
linking in venues in other areas through the
use of teleconferencing facilities.

c. Elections

In the discussions which took place at the
consultative seminar, there was
considerable support for proposals for fairly
far-reaching reform of election processes in
medical schemes.  The objectives of these
measures would be to contribute to the
greater democratization of governance of
medical schemes, the enhancement of
member involvement in scheme affairs, and
greater prioritization of member protection
by boards.

Specifically, it was proposed that a single
date should be selected for election of
trustees across all medical schemes as is
done, for example, in Belgium.  The election
date and venues for elections would be
widely publicised both in scheme
communications to members and by the
Council for Medical Schemes.

The revised election processes would lend
themselves to greater standardization,

 15
which will facilitate member education and
simplify participation.  This would include,
for example, greater uniformity of voting
forms and standardized periods for
nomination of trustees.

Scheme-specific venues for the casting of
ballots would need to be timeously
published on medical scheme websites,
together with the names and CVs of all
nominees for the elections.  The Council for
Medical Schemes could include on its
website a consolidated list of ballot venues –
and potentially also the names and details of
trustees – provided by every medical
scheme.  Other mechanisms for casting
ballots, including postal votes (and even the
use of cellphones), could be developed –
provided that adequate security measures
were put in place to ensure verification of
the identity of voters.  Counting of votes
would need to be overseen by scheme
auditors.

A consequence of the above proposals
would be that election processes would not
necessarily have to take place at scheme
AGMs.  Participation in elections for trustees
would not be dependent on ability to attend
AGMs, and would not be susceptible to
manipulation of attendance at AGMs or the
abuse of proxies.  To the extent that proxies
would still need to be accommodated in
election processes, it was recommended
that a limit to the number of proxies which
may be held by one person should be
regulated.  It was also suggested that there
should be a prohibition on photocopying of
proxy voting forms, and adequate measures
should be in place to ensure verification of
the identity of proxies.

It is also proposed that there should be
some form of independent oversight of
election processes.  A requirement could be
established for a list of nominations to be
sent to the Council within a prescribed
period preceding the elections, accompanied
by notice of acceptance by the nominees
and affidavits concerning their fit and proper
status.  The names and details of
successfully elected trustees would be
entered into a Trustee Registry, maintained
by the Registrar, which would be made
publicly accessible.  In addition, it is
proposed that the Registrar of Medical
Schemes should be able to nullify elections
where there is substantial evidence that
they were not “free and fair.”

d. Disputes

One of the common interfaces between
members and their medical schemes is
currently when a complaint is lodged with
the scheme by a member.  If members do
not feel that they are adequately “heard” or
“listened to” on the occasions when they
have a problem, they may be far less
enthusiastic about participating in the
routine affairs relating to governance of the
scheme.  On the other hand, positive
experiences in relation to the manner in
which grievances are handled may
encourage a far more positive outlook by
members which may encourage greater
participation in scheme affairs.

Where members are aggrieved by the
conduct of their schemes, they should
therefore have confidence in the
effectiveness, fairness and independence of
dispute resolution processes.  It would
appear that ad hoc  internal processes of
certain schemes are inadequate to provide
these assurances.  It is therefore
recommended that all schemes should have
an independent dispute resolution
committee, and that minimum standards for
dispute resolution processes at scheme level
need to be prescribed.  These standards
would cover issues such as: time frames for
resolution of disputes; composition of
dispute resolution committees; and appeal
processes.

e. Member ownership

Another set of suggestions which arose in
this discussion at the seminar related to
changing the ownership structure of scheme
assets to additionally financially incentivise
members to take an interest in the business
affairs of their medical schemes.  These
discussions are picked up on in the section
on the governance model, below.

6. TRUSTEE ACCOUNTABILITY

6.1. Background

The centrality of trustee accountability to
membership of the scheme has been
discussed in the description of the
governance model.  There are three
fundamental components to accountability:

a. trustees must perform in
accordance with the mandate given
to them by the members of the
scheme;

b. trustees must effectively report back
to the membership on their
performance in terms of the
mandate; and

c. To the extent that trustees fail to
meet the mandate, sanctions may
accrue.

Accordingly, there is likely to be a
breakdown in accountability if, for some or
other reason, trustees are unwilling or
unable to perform according to the
mandate, trustees fail to evaluate their own
performance, trustees fail to effectively
report back to scheme membership on their
performance, or trustees believe that they
will not be held responsible for failure to
meet the terms of their mandate.

6.2. Findings

a. Fulfilment of the mandate

The case studies on instances of governance
failure revealed serious instances of trustees
willfully or negligently failing to fulfil their
mandates to governing the affairs of their
schemes in compliance with the law and
while exercising an appropriate level of
fiduciary care.  In various instances, the
failure to act in terms of the mandate
constituted corruption.

Schemes which experienced governance
failure typically demonstrated poor
compliance with legislative provisions.  In
some cases, unregistered benefit options
were run, and there were allegations of
unauthorised deductions from members.  In
the case studies, non-compliance was also
identified with the following provisions of
the Act or regulations: section 26(5),
prohibiting payment of dividends, rebates or
bonuses; section 57(4), concerning timeous
payment of contributions; section 57(6)(c),
relating to conflicts of interest; regulation
15(1), regarding managed health care
arrangements; and regulation 18(3),
concerning agreements between schemes
and administrators.

In some of the schemes which experienced
governance failure, there was evidence of
poor financial oversight by trustees and
weak internal financial controls.  This
resulted in:  misuse of members’ money;
double payment of claims; poor assessment
of claims; poor internal controls; and
inadequate reconciliations of billings and
contributions. Poor financial oversight by
trustees sometimes resulted in excessive
contribution increases.

Some boards were found to be guilty of
irregularities and mismanagement of the
resources of the scheme. There were
instances where such boards were
subsequently replaced by newly elected
ones, although ensuing legal challenges had
the propensity to render the scheme
dysfunctional.

In many of the identified instances of
governance failure within medical schemes,
seemingly inappropriate financial
incentivisation of scheme office-bearers was
a concern.  Some trustees were paid
excessively given their responsibilities within
the scheme, the performance of the scheme
and the time they engaged in scheme
activities.  In other instances, the
chairperson was paid consultancy fees in
addition to normal trustee remuneration.
There were also instances where trustees
were paid an honorarium or received other
benefits in contravention of the scheme
rules.

Some trustees used scheme monies for their
personal and social benefit, including
subscription fees for social and sporting
clubs. There was also inappropriate use of
the financial resources of the scheme for
activities that were of no benefit to
members, or which were to the detriment of
the scheme. These included: obtaining
prime property in prim e locations; purchase

In one instance, an administration company

In another instance, a special general

An analysis of these instances of governance
failure seemed to indicate a number of
critical success factors which must exist if a
board of trustees will effectively meet their
mandate.

i. Fit and proper status of trustees

A key critical success factor is that the
trustees must be fit and proper persons to
govern the affairs of the medical scheme.
In the survey, schemes saw it as their
responsibility to ensure that trustees were fit
and proper. In some schemes, a full
assessment is made to ensure that new
trustees are fit and proper to serve on the
board. Typical tools used by schemes to
assess whether trustees are fit and proper
include: a trustee charter; curriculum vitae;
regulatory provisions for the various
professions; reference checks; relevant
provisions in the Companies Act; credit
checks; and checks for criminal history.
There were however, schemes that had no
policies for screening new trustees in terms
of fit and proper requirements.

In the absence of a statutory definition to
the term “fit and proper”, medical schemes
clearly differed in their approaches to
evaluation of the fit and proper status of
trustees.  The analysis of governance
failures nevertheless showed a number of
considerations which may impact on the fit
and proper status of trustees.

The first such consideration is whether or
not there exists a material conflict of
interests between the interests of the
trustee and that of the scheme.  While some
respondents had little or no understanding
of the concept, most trustees and principal
officers interviewed had a reasonable
understanding of what would constitute
conflict of interest in a medical scheme
setting. They gave several of the examples
of conflict of interest situations where
trustees would be unable to take an
impartial view on a matter in which they had
vested interest.

Certain schemes had no formal policy on
conflict of interest, sometimes arguing that
it was not considered to pose a significant
threat to scheme business.  There were
nevertheless implicit policies and processes
which they considered adequate to deal with
the issue, including: terms of reference of
various subcommittees; recusal of interested
members from specific deliberations of the
board; general legal and business principles;
and relationships of trust.  In at least one
case, the scheme had a conflict of interest
policy that was never signed.  Certain
schemes undertook to develop a formal
policy in response to the survey.

of interest, some were explicitly based on

ii. Scheme management

A second critical success factor related to
whether or not trustees can adequately fulfil
their mandate is that the trustees must be
supported by adequate executive
management capacity.  The importance of
this to the governance model is evident
from the scenarios sketched in section 3
above.

Self-administered schemes generally had a
fully-fledged executive management
structure and a full staff complement.
Business divisions typically included: human
resources and finance; business
development; operations; managed health
care; and information technology.

Only two participating third party
administered schemes had a formal
executive management structure, which
took charge of all the operational activities
of the scheme. These management
structures included a principal officer,
company secretary and executives
responsible for finance, risk management,
client liaison and product development.
Restricted schemes typically used the
employer’s infrastructure for financial,
procurement, human relations and labour
relations issues.

In some schemes, the executive
management committee served in an
advisory capacity to the board of trustees
and attended all board meetings.

Trustees generally demonstrated
understanding of the role of the principal
officer, which were typically described as:
ensuring compliance with Acts and rules;
managing the scheme’s office;
administration; managing trustee business;
implementing board resolutions;
communicating with members; making
recommendations to the board; managing
subcommittees; managing staff; and
ensuring appropriate investment of funds.

In most third party administered schemes,

executive responsibilities and together with

recruitment, remuneration, retrenchment,
disciplinary, leave, and labour relations.
Financial policies tended to cover such
issues as debt collection, investment,
travelling, fixed assets, and so on.
Procurement policies typically entailed a
schedule of authority and sign off.  Not all
schemes had a formal policy on
procurement, though.  Some of these
schemes commented that “certain
consistent selection criteria are used to
identify appropriate persons or service

identifying procurement agencies which
would then be taken to the board for

A key concern arising from the study of
governance failures was the relinquishment
of management responsibility from the
scheme to third party contractors such as
administrators.  Whereas administrators
should perform specific operational activities
for which they are contracted, management
oversight and decision-making should
always remain within the control of the
scheme for the best interests of its
membership.  It is not appropriate that the
huge budgets of medical schemes are
managed by a part-time board, a single
principal officer and one or two clerical staff.
Weaknesses in this regard appear to have
been a significant contributor to lack of
effective internal controls, inadequate
performance management, lapses in
administration and undue influence of third

b. Reporting on the mandate

The second component of accountability, as
discussed above, is that trustees must
effectively report back to the membership
on their performance in terms of the
mandate. Meeting this requirement requires
three conditions.  First, trustees need to
regularly and adequately assess their own
performance and the performance of the
scheme.  Secondly, trustees need to
effectively communicate these assessments
to members.  In relation to this
requirement, there has already been
discussion about the challenges of
effectively communicating with members
through AGMs and other media – and some
potential solutions to these problems have
been raised.  The third requirement for
effective reporting on the mandate is to
revert to members where the mandate is
insufficiently broad to cover intended action
or where there is not clarity on the
mandate.

In relation to the first requirement, there
was quite a lot of variation between medical
schemes on the extent to which
performance evaluations of both the scheme
and the trustees were done.

Monitoring of the execution of strategies

or six monthly reviews of budgets, business

according to the service level agreement

Certain schemes conducted formal

6.3. Potential Solutions

In response to the various factors identified
above which contribute to a breakdown in
accountability of governance structures
within medical schemes, various potential
solutions have been identified to address
these problems.

A number of suggestions have been made
to ensure that trustees are fit and proper to
serve in this position:

a. Respondents suggested that there
needs to be greater regulatory
clarity on the criteria that need to
be met for a trustee to be regarded
as “fit and proper”, to increase the
likelihood that trustees will act
independently, with integrity and
with accountability toward medical
scheme members.  The Council for
Medical Schemes already has an
ongoing project in this regard,
which is drawing to a conclusion.
T h i s  m a y  g i v e  r i s e  t o  d e f i n i t i o n  o f
the concept through regulatory
amendment.

b. A set of core competencies for
trustees also needs to be
developed, taking into account the
diversity of expertise required for
the efficient running of schemes.
Ideally, there needs to be a balance
of skills, knowledge and experience
among trustees for the scheme to
function effectively.  It is
nevertheless acknowledged that
skills required to oversee the affairs
of medical schemes will sometimes
only be acquired in the course of
trusteeship, and this underscores
the importance of effective
induction programmes and optimal
opportunities for in-service growth
and development.  In this regard,
many of the informal learning
processes employed by some
schemes at present are considered
ineffective.  Formal training
programmes for trustees are
therefore encouraged.

c. Participants in the consultative
seminar believed that each medical
scheme should adopt its own
individual code of conduct for
trustees and staff, if they do not
already have one, although this
process could be facilitated by the
publication of generic corporate
governance guidelines.  These
corporate governance guidelines
should be focused not only on
compliance with legislative
provisions and the rules of the
scheme, but also about ensuring
optimal strategic direction, financial
soundness and sustainability of the
scheme. Guidelines on performance
measures for trustees should also
be developed by the Council in
consultation with industry, to
provide a tool whereby trustees can
measure their individual
performance as well as performance
of the entire board.

d. Given the significant role which
conflict of interest appeared to play
in scheme governance failures, it
was recommended that a more
substantive regulatory framework is
developed for the management of
conflict of interest in medical
schemes, and the institution of
appropriate checks and balances.
This should be accompanied by
clearer legislative definitions of
“conflict of interest” and “related
parties” in the context of medical
schemes.  Protections against
conflict of interest should be applied
in the same manner for trustees and
for the executive management
structure of the scheme.  In the
absence of a sufficiently exhaustive
definition of conflict of interest,
participants at the seminar

 26
suggested that trustees should be
guided by the following rule of
thumb:  “if there is a possibility that
there is potential conflict of interest,
then there probably is, so declare
it.”

e. It was further recommended by
participants at the seminar that
each medical scheme should be
required to hold a register of
trustees’ interests.  In the event of
any potential for conflict of interest,
the disclosure of the interest and
the decision of the Board in that
regard (for example, in respect of
recusal of the trustee) should be
properly minuted.

f. Another suggestion was made at
the seminar that an Institute of
Trustees should be established,
which could offer training and
guidance to trustees, and offer
“accreditation” to potential trustees
based upon background checks
and/or assessments of competence
or capability.  The Council would not
be averse to such an Institute being
created in the market in response to
market demand for such services,
although it would have no
regulatory status and use of its
services would have to operate on a
purely voluntary basis.  It could not
assume the function of a quasi-
regulatory authority, given the legal
implications involved.

g. A further suggestion was that the
Council for Medical Schemes should
consider implementation of a
national toll-free whistleblowing
hotline, where confidentiality of
whistleblowers is guaranteed.
Statutory protection for
whistleblowers should also be
considered for inclusion in the
Medical Schemes Act.

Checks and balances need to be in place to
ensure that proper internal controls are
adhered to at all times within medical
schemes.  Effective controls can minimise
opportunities for abuse and increase the
likelihood that trustees and their executive
management will perform effectively.  In
this regard, certain recommendations were
made.

a. The role of the audit committees of
medical schemes needs to be
recognised and strengthened, in
both third party administered
medical schemes and self-
administered schemes.  These
committees have an important role
to play in: monitoring internal
controls; checking implementation
of scheme decisions and identifying
potential areas of non-compliance
with legislation, scheme rules or a
scheme’s code of conduct;
monitoring of risk; and development
of fraud prevention plans.  It was
suggested that clear terms of
reference need to be developed for
audit committees.
b. Schemes’ disputes committees were
also important for identifying breaches
and problems with performance within
schemes.  In this regard, the
importance of independence of
members of the dispute committee was
emphasised.  Decisions of this
committee should be binding on the
scheme.

c. Greater attention should be given to
effective internal control systems within
schemes.  Participants emphasised the
need for development of proactive
measures to ensure proper
implementation of these systems, and
the importance of appropriate
segregation of duties.

Internal controls, informed decision-making
and enhanced monitoring of the
performance of schemes and contracted
parties all require adequate management
capacity within medical schemes.  We have
also seen that one of the key concerns
arising from the project was relinquishment
of management responsibility by medical
schemes to third parties – with concomitant

 27
weakness in independent governance
capacity by trustees.

It is therefore recommended that all
schemes should have an executive
management structure adequate to execute
the decisions of the board, and to oversee
the day-to-day management of its affairs.
This would not necessarily require
considerable legislative rigidity, but could
instead be effected through a condition that
ongoing registration of schemes is made
subject to schemes demonstrating sufficient
internal capacity to manage the affairs of
the scheme.

Supported by such an executive structure,
the board could remain non-executive and
would focus on strategic issues pertaining to
the scheme as well as monitoring
performance and conformance to sound
governance principles.  Boards nevertheless
need to be reminded that they ultimately
remain accountable for the performance and
management of the entity under their
control, and that delegation of responsibility
to board committees or management
structures does not relieve boards of their
responsibilities for the delegated functions.
Accordingly, delegations should all be clearly
and carefully defined and documented to
ensure proper and effective monitoring.

Given the frequency of problems with
remuneration practices of medical schemes
which experienced governance failures, it
has also been recommended that criteria for
the remuneration of trustees and employees
of schemes should be developed by the
Council, in consultation with industry.  At
the same time, the powers of the Registrar
should be strengthened to enable effective
intervention when remuneration is found not
to be in the best interest of members.
Remuneration should be performance
related, taking into account the performance
of the scheme, the performance of the
individual, and any other criteria deemed
appropriate.  To avoid conflict of interest, it
is also recommended that trustees should
not be contracted to perform paid work for
their schemes, and should not be
remunerated for undertaking executive
duties for the scheme.

Finally, respondents sugge sted that rules of
medical schemes should make explicit
provision for removal of trustees in the
event of codes of conduct being breached.
This recommendation is supported because
it reinforces the third component of
accountability, namely that adverse
consequences will accrue for wilful or
negligent failure to adhere to the mandate
given by members.  In those instances
where fraudulent or grossly negligent
conduct persists within a board, it is
recommended that the penalties for the
individual should be immediate and more
severe.  Unless this is done, the perception
will remain that the “good guys always
come second.”  Where a scheme incurs
losses due to the fraudulent or grossly
negligent conduct of a trustee, it is
recommended that the trustee should be
held personally liable for those losses.
Consideration could be given to increasing
legislative sanctions in this regard.

7. THE GOVERNANCE MODEL

7.1. Background

Earlier in the report it was noted that
consideration needed to be given to whether
governance failures occur as a result of the
model per se being fundamentally flawed, or
whether governance failures occur in spite
of a basically sound model which is either
abused or misapplied.  Depending on the
answer to this question, alternative
approaches could include: shelving the
model in its entirety and starting with
something else; improving the model where
deficiencies exist; or introducing a greater
level of flexibility to governance design
provided that interests of members are
adequately protected.

7.2. Findings

Structures of the boards of schemes
surveyed generally varied in terms of the
number of elected versus appointed

trustees.  The number of trustees ranged
from three to sixteen, although there was
no statistically significant correlation

In some cases, elected trustees represented
60% or more of the total membership of the
board.

Board composition differed in other respects
too.  Some schemes made provision for a
pensioner representative  on the board, as
they considered pensioners to have special
needs.   Certain boards also included
regional representation.  In these cases,
regional representatives typically formed
part of the board, resulting in a larger
board. Other schemes used alternate
trustees to replace those who might not be
able to attend meetings.

Board committees also varied in terms of
number and focus. Typical committees
included: disputes; audit; executive
management; marketing; product
development; finance; risk management;
medical; remuneration; investment; ex
gratia; and rules committees.  In most
schemes, trustees were required to
participate in at least one of the
committees, although not all schemes had a
formal policy of participation in committees.
Committees are delegated specific tasks by
the board, which they then discharge on
behalf of the board. The committees can
only report findings and make
recommendations to the board. The board
has the power to make the final decisions.
All committees are chaired by trustees who
would have been selected from among
committee members.

The maximum term of office for a trustee in
all schemes was 3 years, which could be
extended depending on whether the trustee
was re-elected or re-appointed.

There was no consensus among trustees or
other participants in the survey regarding
whether or not the governance model as
appropriate.  Some trustees stated that it
was possible to apply the model in its
entirety, and that some of the problems
being experienced in schemes were due to
the fact that the current governance model
was not being applied appropriately.

Others felt that schemes cannot fully
implement the governance model of the Act
without negatively impacting on the scheme,
arguing that it was impossible to attract the
right skills mix with a minimum requirement
of 50% of trustees elected from among
members.  Trustees reported that in open
schemes, members are sometimes elected
on the basis of popularity, with no requisite
skills or knowledge of the industry.  In
restricted schemes, on the other hand, rank
and file employees are reportedly reluctant
to participate, resulting in management
filling most if not all of the board positions.
The resultant effect is reportedly that the
balance of skills is sometimes better.

Various other governance challenges were
experienced by schemes and attributed to
the current governance model. First, the
governance model was considered to unduly
add to non-healthcare expenditure and
lessen efficiency.  Committee structures cost
money and could increase non-healthcare
expenditure.  Some schemes were reluctant
to introduce a clinical governance
committee, as proposed by the Council,
because this would require costly
recruitment of external people with
appropriate skills.  The reporting
requirements of the Council were also
perceived by some to unduly add to cost, as
they were considered to be too detailed.

Secondly, some respondents considered the
governance model to militate against
entities with vested financial interest, such
as administrators, resulting in them being
reluctant to engage in high capital
investment for entities in which they
exercised no executive power.  This created
a challenge to the development of an
effective relationship between the board and
third party contractors like administrators.

the current governance model was

guidance from the scheme’s principal officer
or chairperson of the scheme, while others
relied on the principles of governance as
practised in their respective professions.

Schemes also reported that they conformed
to King 2 principles, although not everyone
was familiar with the principles enshrined in
the King 2 report.  Certain schemes

understanding of governance and had, on

presentations to their board. Others had
organised seminars on good governance for
trustees.  There were, however, certain
trustees who felt that King’s governance
principles were being forced on them.

There were respondents who were of the

Some respondents suggested that investors,
including consumers, should be given
opportunity to access a guaranteed return
from scheme, with appropriate statutory
protection for consumers against abuse and
profiteering by industry players.  It was
argued that this model could allow for
innovation without a complete loss of
accountability.  Regulatory attention could
focus on providing assistance and guidance
to boards, with more direct intervention only
in circumstances where consumers stand to
be severely prejudiced.

Various other suggestions were made in
relation to improvement of scheme

which would supplement the board of

voting or decision-making powers.  Other

trustees. It was also felt that principal

the affairs of the scheme.  Some trustees
stated that, whatever changes were made,
the model should ensure that people within
the system have a more active interest in
the success of the scheme.

7.3. Potential Solutions

Although there were detractors from the
medical scheme governance model, there
was no convincing evidence that governance
failures could be attributed to an overall
failure of the model.  Instead, there are
clearly problems in implementation (to
which remedial measures discussed in this
document could be applied) and abuse of
the model including corruption (which
requires more definitive response, but is not
only symptomatic of this model).

Nevertheless, there are areas in which it
would appear that the model itself can be
improved.  Some of these areas have
already been discussed, including the
strengthening of executive management
structures of schemes.  Additional

 30
recommendations in this regard are
discussed below.  A case may also be made
to investigate the po ssibility of allowing
some innovation and flexibility in
governance models, where it can clearly be
demonstrated that the interests of members
are adequately protected.  Some of these
issues are also explored below.

a. Administrator representation on
Boards

Third party administrators in particular have
argued that, given their investment in the
operation of medical schemes, they should
be allowed to participate in the governance
structures of those schemes.  Given the
potential for conflict between the interests
of members and the financial interests of
third party administrators, however, it is
recommended that there should be no
amendment to the governance model which
precludes administrator participation in the
governance structures of medical schemes.

At the same time, it is acknowledged that
third party administrators have concerns
regarding the stability of investments in the
context of potentially short-term contracts
with medical schemes.  Whereas
administrators of medical schemes incur
significant expenses in pursuit of quality
service to schemes, the security of these
investments is said to be compromised by
the provisions of regulation 18(d), which
requires administration contracts to allow for
termination at the instance of either party
after a period of not more than twelve
calendar months.  This requirement is said
to undermine efficiency in service delivery
by these administrators.  It is accordingly
recommended that this termination period
should be removed and left to contractual
negotiations between the parties.

b. Elected versus appointed trustees

The study also found that few boards
comprised more than the statutory minimum
of 50% of elected trustees.  In the context
of concerns of conflict of interest leading to
governance failure, as well as the reported
disproportionate influence of appointed
trustees, the appointment of trustees by
mechanisms outside the control of scheme
membership becomes an issue of potential
concern. These trustees may, for instance,
be incentivised to represent the interests of
employers rather than the interests of
members, where these interests diverge.  It
is important to ensure that board members
are not representative of specific
constituencies, but that member interests
always dominate.

There are other difficulties with the
appointment rather than the election of
trustees too.  There are no requirements in
the Act regarding who may appoint trustees,
how they are appointed and for how long
their tenure may be.  Elected members are
elected on the basis that their trusteeship
will be limited mostly to a maximum period
of three years where-after they could stand
for re-election. Appoin ted trustees are in
certain instances, not subjected to the same
set of rules as they are able to stay on the
boards for a prolonged period of time. There
is no mechanism of changing them unless
they resign, retire or are expelled. They
tend to have undue influence on the
activities of the board and sometimes, they
represent the interests of the employer,
particularly in restricted schemes. This could
unduly prejudice elected members who
incidentally represent member interests.

At the same time, it is acknowledged that
there may not necessarily be the required
skills, expertise and experience among
scheme members for all trustees to be
elected from among scheme membership.

It was originally proposed that consideration
be given to requiring 100% of trustees to be
elected by members, although incumbents
would not necessarily themselves need to be
members of the scheme. The election of all
the trustees by members of the scheme
could also reduce th e disproportionate
influence of appointed trustees of the
decisions of the board.

At the consultative seminar, the breakaway
group charged with looking at the
governance model proposed a compromise

 31
which would significantly reduce the
problems with dominance of appointed
members on Boards, while still ensuring that
some of the benefits of appointment are not
lost.

It was recommended that the legislation
should be amended to provide that two-
thirds of members of the board should be
elected by membership and should
themselves be members of the scheme.  In
open schemes, the remaining one-third
should be appointed by the already elected
members of the board, and need not
necessarily be members of the scheme.
This would ensure that additional expertise
could be brought on board, but without
skewing the balance of power on the board
to subvert the will of members.  It was,
however, stipulated that in open schemes it
should be legislated th at no person should
serve on the board of more than one
medical scheme and that the principal
officer of the scheme should not be
employed by more than one scheme.  In the
absence of these protections, it was felt that
there would be potential for significant
conflict of interest.

In the case of restricted membership
schemes, it was the view of the group that
the legislation should not be prescriptive as
to who may appoint the remaining third,
and that it may well be appropriate for
employers who contribute to the scheme via
employee subsidies to have some role in
appointment of these members.  In the
context of an increasing trend for employers
to do away with medical scheme subsidies,
it was considered impo rtant not to alienate
employers who still provide subsidies from
participation in governance of schemes.
This recommendation was not unanimously
supported by participants in the seminar,
with some believing that even a minority of
employer representatives could sway
decisions of the Board in favour of the
employer rather than the needs to the
membership.

In both open and restricted medical
schemes, the potential for a third of trustees
to be appointed create s the opportunity for
any deficiencies in skills mix among elected
trustees to be rect ified.  It was also
considered to create an important
opportunity for schemes to effect greater
transformation of the boards by promoting
greater demographic representivity on
boards.  It was proposed that the one-third
of trustees who could be appointed should
be used to ensure adequate representivity
on boards.  This sh ould supplement other
efforts by medical schemes to increase
representivity of their governance
structures, including for example allowing
repetition of invitations for nominations in
cases where initial nominations to not
provide adequate opportunities for
demographic representivity.

c. Constituency interests on Boards

Legislation contemplated a situation where
members of a medical scheme would choose
trustees to represent them as members of
that medical scheme. However, there are
schemes that have opted for representation
of constituencies in the election of board of
trustees. Typical representation is usually
done on the basis of union membership,
continuation members and pensioners.
There are also medical schemes that use
regional boards based largely on provinces,
in the election of trustees. The practice is
that the chairpersons of the regional
structures are ex-officio members of the
board.  The effect of this is that technically,
members in one region or province are only
able to elect one member based in their
region, to the board.

In both of these practices, serious questions
may be raised on the ability of trustees to
represent the interests of members as a
whole.  Constituency representation
suggests that a trustee may favour the
interests of a particular constituency over
those of general membership, and regional
representation may also preclude members
from exercising choice in the choice of
trustees to represent their interests.  It is
therefore our view that schemes should
refrain from any form of constituency
representation on boards and ensure that,

 32
even if elections are regionally organised
from logistical point of view, all members of
the scheme should have opportunity to vote
in respect of all nominees for trusteeship.

At the same time, the practice of
appointment of alternates by individual
trustees is also regarded as potentially
subversive of the power of members to
choose who will represent their interests,
and reduces individual accountability.  It is
recommended that this practice should be
eliminated.

d. Size of Boards

Leadership weaknesses identified in
schemes which had experienced governance
failures may be exacerbated by
dysfunctionally small boards, which do not
allow adequate deliberations to occur within
the board and which could impede effective
member access to trustees.  On the other
hand, costs of board expenditure may be
inflated by unnecessarily large boards.  It is
therefore recommended that the size of
boards should be based on some
predetermined criteria, most likely linked to
size of scheme.

e. Role of Principal Officer

At the consultative seminar, the question
was raised of whether principal officers
should become voting members of boards of
trustees.  Whereas there was some
disagreement on this issue, the general
opinion appeared to be that this would not
be appropriate.  Participants said that they
did not see a problem with existing models
where principal officers did not have voting
rights, and suggested that giving them
voting rights would create conflicts of
interest in relation to their performance
management and remuneration.

There was however consensus that the Act
provides insufficient direction on the role of
principal officers.  This was raised as a
problem in the context of historically
dominant third-party administrators which
retained significant influence in the
governance of the scheme.  It was
suggested that the Council could assist in
this regard by developing a model set of
delegations to principal officers and that the
Act should be developed to more explicitly
define their powers and duties.
In addition, the independence of principal
officers should be supported by: potential
restraint of trade provisions prohibiting the
principal officer from being employed or
contracted by third party administrators or
other contracted parties for a specified
period after leaving the medical scheme
(within the constraints of constitutionality);
a prohibition on principal officers being
seconded by brokerages; greater detail
being provided in legislation on how
principal officers may be appointed,
remunerated and removed from
employment of the medical scheme; and the
specification of fit and proper requirements
for principal officers.  It was also considered
important to specify the principal officer as
the accounting officer of the medical
scheme.

f. Tenure of trustees

There was some discussion at the
consultative seminar regarding whether or
not the tenure of trustees should be limited,
to avoid a trustee developing an undue level
of influence on a board.  It was
acknowledged in the discussion that
accumulated experience can also be of
benefit to the board, and that members who
were dissatisfied with the performance of
trustees could simply vote them out at the
end of a term of office.  The
recommendation of the group was that the
tenure of trustees should not be legislated,
but that the Act should limit the duration in
which any member of the board can hold
the office of chairperson.  This was regarded
as a sufficient safeguard against the
problems of undue influence which were
identified as contributors to governance
failure.

g. Alternative models of governance

While certain participants in the survey and
seminar vociferously argued for retention of
the not-for-profit model, others argued that

 33
some scope should be given to the
development of for-profit or limited not-for-
profit models.

Some participants argued for at least
allowing for distribution of dividends to
members in the event of a scheme
generating particular levels of surplus.

A major argument in this regard was that if
members were given an additional
investment interest in their schemes – with
a dividend share in surpluses generated by
the scheme, they would be more likely to
feel a sense of “ownership” of the scheme
and take a far more keen interest in
participating in the governance of the
scheme and holding governance structures
accountable for their performance in terms
of the member mandate.  Trustees would
also gain a vested interest in the success of
the scheme, and would be less likely to sign
away scheme responsibilities (and attendant
profits) to third parties.

It was suggested that there would also be
additional incentives for members to remain
with their medical schemes, which would
decrease the likelihood of member churning
in the environment, with all the costs
associated with this.  It was suggested by
one seminar participant that member
retention could be further enhanced by a
model which vested profits back into
apportionment account for members which
could only be accessed at a particular age,
as is done in the insurance industry, which
could then be used by the member to fund
post-retirement benefits.

It was also argued that notwithstanding the
existing legislative model, profits are being
made at the moment by industry players but
that these profits are not accruing to the
benefit of members.  An alternative
legislative framework could facilitate those
profits vesting in the membership of the
scheme.

A further argument in favour of this model is
that it would deal with the problem of
certain schemes having, for historical
reasons, accumulated significant “surplus”
reserves which are not being put to optimal
use.  Furthermore, it would remove the
possibility that a scheme with significant
reserves, which lost the bulk of its
membership for some or other reason, could
voluntarily liquidate itself and unfairly
distribute those reserves among a small
residual membership.

Another model which was proposed was for
a limited shareholding to be permitted by
external investors, but where members of
the scheme would still numerically dominate
the Board.  One of the advantages of this
variation, it was argued, is that it would
promote increased financial investment in
the scheme, balance the legitimate interests
of the members with the commercial
interests of the scheme and ensure that the
commercial focus centres on the scheme
and not contracted parties.

These recommendations would entail a
significant modification of the existing policy
and legislative framework, and accordingly
will have to be put to the Department and
Ministry of Health for consideration.  In the
meantime, the Council cannot comment on
their desirability or otherwise, save to say
that if flexibility were granted for such
models to be considered , it is our view that
they could not replace the existing not-for-
profit model but would provide an
alternative in certain defined circumstances.

Furthermore, if there was to be principled
endorsement for this approach at a policy
level, the preconditions for implementation
of such models would have to be very
clearly worked out.  These preconditions
would at minimum need to ensure that the
governance structure was ultimately to the
benefit of members, that the structure did
not result in indirect advantage to members
with better risk profiles than others, that the
financial stability of the scheme was not
compromised, and that it did not create the
potential for conflict of interest by third
parties with vested financial interests in the
affairs of the scheme.  Careful plans for
migration to the new governance structures
would need to be worked out, ensuring that
members are fully involved every step of the

 34
way and that there is proper regulatory
oversight to ensure member protection.  It
may also be appropriate to limit the
potential use of limited for-profit models to
open schemes, rather than restricted
membership schemes.

Finally, when considering these possibilities,
a word of caution sounded by a participant
at the consultative seminar is appropriate.
He indicated that a for-profit or limited-for-
profit should not be seen as a panacea to
governance problems in the industry, when
in a commercial environment like the
insurance industry, there is also evidence of
significant governance failures and abuse.
Further, even the participants proposing
these models acknowledged that the details
were complex, and that careful analysis of
precedent in the pension fund industry, for
example, would need to be made to ensure
that avoidable pitfalls were not replicated.

8. RECOMMENDATIONS

This report has detailed a number of
suggestions which have been advanced by
stakeholders in the course of the
governance theme project.  Some of them
entail enhancements to the way in which
medical schemes implement the existing
governance model.  Others suggest
improvements to the model itself.  Still
others suggest that the environment would
benefit from a greater degree of flexibility to
governance structures in the environment.
Certain of the proposals can be
implemented immediately, without the need
for regulatory development.  Other
proposals require further evaluation from a
policy perspective, and may require
legislative change before they can be
implemented.  In this concluding section of
the report, the intention is not to repeat all
the suggestions made in the report, but
merely to highlight some of the key areas
which we believe should be taken forward in
one form or another.

From the perspective of improving member
voice and participation in medical schemes,
the suggestions advanced in section 5.3 of
this report suggest a lot that can already be
done by medical schemes in this regard.
These include taking on board the
suggestions regarding methods to improve
communications to members, encourage
greater participation in AGMs, streamline
election processes and review dispute
resolution procedures.  The Council for
Medical Schemes will similarly review its
consumer education efforts to focus on the
key priority areas identified in the report,
including a strengthening of the emphasis
on the benefits of member participation in
the governance of schemes.

From a perspective of regulatory
development to promote optimal member
voice and participation, the Council will
recommend to the Minister of Health some
fairly far-reaching amendments to the
legislative and regulatory framework
regarding the process of trustee elections, in
line with the recommendations made in
section 5.3 of the report.  These
recommendations will include, amongst
others:
• institution of a common election
date for trustees of all medical
schemes;
• standardization of trustee
nomination processes;
• requirements for publication of
details of nominees, venues for
elections and so on;
• independent oversight of election
processes;
• separation of election processes
from AGM’s; and
• The potential for elections to be
declared invalid in the event of
substantive evidence of irregularity.
In addition, the Council supports the
recommendation recorded in section 5.3 to
the effect that the requirement for brokers
to contract with medical schemes as a
precondition for payment of commission

In relation to the governance model itself,

governance best practices and to explain the
differences where a scheme’s governance
system differed from the published
guidelines.

The Council will also present to the

limited for-profit models in the medical
schemes environment, with a view to
evaluation of these issues from a policy
