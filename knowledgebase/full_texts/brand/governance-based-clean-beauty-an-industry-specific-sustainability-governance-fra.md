Abstract
Clean beauty has emerged as a prominent category in the cosmetics industry, often associated with
sustainability, ethical consumption, and environmental responsibility. However, its definition remains
fragmented and is largely based on ingredient -focused claims, sustainability narrativ es, and consumer
perceptions rather than systematic sustainability governance. As a result, the governance mechanisms
responsible for producing and maintaining sustainability outcomes across the cosmetics value chain remain
insufficiently theorized. This study aims to reconceptualize clean beauty as an industry-specific sustainability
governance framework. Drawing on literature on sustainability governance, transparency, lifecycle
accountability, sustainability assessment, and related governance concepts, t his conceptual study synthesizes
existing literature and develops a governance-oriented conceptual framework consisting of six interconnected
dimensions: Environmental Impact, Ingredients Integrity, Supply Chain Governance, Transparency and
Accountability, End-of-Life Environmental Governance and Circularity, and Human, Ecological, and Ethical
Impact. This study proposes a Governance -Based Clean Beauty Framework that shifts analytical attention
from product attributes and symbolic sustainability claims to the governance systems through which
environmental, social, ethical, and lifecycle outcomes are addressed, monitored, and maintained across the
cosmetics value chain. By integrating sustainability considerations from raw material sourcing to product use
and end-of-life management, the study further proposes that governance -based clean beauty may offer a
conceptual basis for the future development of an industry-specific sustainability governance framework. The
framework further illustrates how sustainability governance can be conceptually organized through six
interrelated governance dimensions, rather than constituting a validated assessment approach or providing a
basis for comparative performance evaluation. The study further proposes that governance-based clean beauty
may inform the future development of an industry -specific sustainability governance framework that
complements broader sustainability approaches such as ESG by addressing governance challenges unique to
cosmetics value chains. Rather than replacing ESG, the framework conceptually organizes sustainability
governance through industry-specific dimensions that reflect the distinctive characteristics of the cosmetics
sector. In addition, the framework may be applicable to other consumer -facing industries characterized by
complex supply chains, sustainability -oriented product narratives, and lifecycle -related environmental and
social impacts. This study concludes that clean beauty may be understood not merely as a marketing concept
or product category, but as  a proposed conceptual structure for examining transparency, accountability,
lifecycle responsibility, and governance-oriented examination of sustainability. The study contributes to clean
beauty research by providing a governance-oriented conceptual foundation for future sustainability evaluation
research and offers a  basis for future research on indicator development, governance benchmarking, brand -
level sustainability assessment, and sector-specific sustainability assessment systems.

1. Introduction
1.1. The Rise of Clean Beauty
The global clean beauty market has expanded rapidly over the past decade, driven by growing consumer
concerns regarding environmental sustainability, human health, ethical sourcing, and corporate responsibility
(Bom et al., 2019; Dini & Laneri, 2021). Terms such as “natural,” “green,” “non -toxic,” “ethical,” and
“sustainable” have become increasingly prominent in the cosmetics industry, shaping both product
development and brand communication (Bom et al., 2019; Dini & Laneri, 2021).
As clean beauty has evolved from a niche market segment into a mainstream industry trend, it has become
an influential framework through which consumers evaluate the sustainability performance of cosmetic
products and companies. Consequently, clean beauty is no longer merely a marketing category but an emerging
sustainability discourse that influences corporate strategies, consumer expectations, and industry practices.
1.2. The Governance Problem Behind Clean Beauty
Despite its growing prominence, clean beauty remains conceptually ambiguous and inconsistently defined
(Bom et al., 2019; Dini & Laneri, 2021). Existing interpretations often focus on ingredient safety, product
attributes, or sustainability -related narratives, while providing limited attention to the governance systems
through which environmental and social outcomes are generated, monitored, and verified.
As a result, sustainability claims are frequently communicated through symbolic narratives rather than
supported by transparent governance mechanisms, measurable accountability structures, or lifecycle -based
management practices. This disconnect has contributed to growing concerns regarding greenwashing (Delmas
& Burbano, 2011; Bowen & Aragon-Correa, 2014), sustainability decoupling (Bromley & Powell, 2012), and
the divergence between corporate sustainability claims and actual implementation.
These limitations raise a fundamental question: what constitutes sustainability in clean beauty, and what
governance mechanisms are required to evaluate and support sustainability performance across the cosmetics
value chain?
This study suggests that sustainability in clean beauty may not be adequately understood through
ingredient-based claims, symbolic sustainability narratives, or product -level attributes alone. Instead,
sustainability should be evaluated through the governance systems that are associated with the management of
environmental, social, ethical, and lifecycle outcomes across the cosmetics value chain. Accordingly, the study
reconceptualizes clean beauty as an industry -specific sustainability governance framework and proposes six
governance dimensions through which sustainability performance may be examined and interpreted.
1.3. Research Gap
Existing research on clean beauty has primarily focused on ingredient safety, natural and sustainable
formulations, consumer perception, ethical consumption, branding strategies, and sustainability
communication (Fonseca-Santos et al., 2015; Bom et al., 20 19; Dini & Laneri, 2021). These studies have
significantly advanced understanding of how clean beauty is defined, marketed, and interpreted by consumers.
Consequently, clean beauty has largely been conceptualized through product attributes, sustainability
narratives, and consumer-facing claims.
In parallel, broader sustainability research has increasingly emphasized the importance of governance,
transparency, accountability, and sustainability assessment in ensuring that sustainability commitments are
effectively implemented and monitored (Delmas & Burbano, 2011; Bromley & Powell, 2012; Sala et al., 2015;
Eccles et al., 2020). Within ESG and sustainability governance research, governance is generally discussed in
relation to corporate oversight, compliance, organizational management, and stakeholder accountability (Lock
& Seele, 2017; OECD, 2023). However, relatively limited attention has been paid to how governance
mechanisms can be  conceptualized as an industry -specific framework for understanding sustainability
governance in the cosmetics sector.
Consequently, key governance dimensions of clean beauty , including traceability, supply chain
accountability, transparency, lifecycle responsibility, and implementation mechanisms , remain insufficiently

In response to these limitations, this study aims to reconceptualize clean beauty as an industry -specific
sustainability governance framework rather than as a narrative- or ingredient-based category. The study argues
that sustainability in clean beauty should be evaluated not only through visible sustainability claims or product
characteristics, but also through the governance systems that shape environmental, social, ethical, and lifecycle
outcomes across the cosmetics value chain.
To achieve this objective, the study makes two principal contributions.
First, it develops a conceptual foundation for understanding clean beauty as an industry -specific
sustainability governance framework, thereby providing a structured basis for examining how sustainability is

Second, the study proposes a Governance -Based Clean Beauty Framework consisting of six interrelated

research highlighted the importance of governance in addressing sustainability challenges in clean beauty, the
present study advances this discussion by proposing a comprehensive six -dimensional framework for
examining sustainability implementation, accountability, and related governance practices.

2. Literature Reviews
2.1. Existing Conceptualizations of Clean Beauty
The concept of clean beauty has emerged as a rapidly expanding category in the global cosmetics industry and
is increasingly associated with health, environmental responsibility, ethical consumption, and sustainability -
related concerns (Fonseca -Santos et a l., 2015; Bom et al., 2019; Dini & Laneri, 2021). In both academic
literature and industry practice, clean beauty is commonly discussed through concepts such as natural
ingredients, product safety, ethical sourcing, cruelty-free practices, environmental responsibility, and consumer
wellbeing (Bom et al., 2019; Dini & Laneri, 2021).
Among these themes, ingredient-related characteristics have received particular attention (Fonseca-Santos
et al., 2015). At the same time, ethical considerations such as vegan formulations, cruelty -free commitments,
fair trade sourcing, and responsible bus iness practices have become important elements of clean beauty

positioning (Bom et al., 2019). These characteristics have contributed to the growing popularity of clean beauty
among consumers seeking products that align with personal health, environmental values, and ethical
preferences (Dini & Laneri, 2021).
Despite its increasing prominence, clean beauty remains conceptually fragmented. Existing definitions vary
considerably across firms, certification schemes, industry initiatives, and consumer interpretations (Bom et al.,
2019). In many cases, clean beauty is defined through product attributes, ingredient exclusion lists, packaging

sustainability outcomes are implemented, monitored, and maintained throughout the cosmetics value chain.
This limitation provides the basis for the governance-oriented perspective developed in the present study.
2.2. Sustainability Governance and ESG
Sustainability governance has become an increasingly important framework for understanding how firms
manage environmental, social, and ethical responsibilities in contemporary business systems (OECD, 2023).
In recent years, the rise of Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) frameworks has expanded corporate
sustainability discussions beyond environmental performance alone, emphasizing accountability, risk
management, stakeholder responsibility, and long -term organizational resilience (OECD, 2023). In t his
context, governance has emerged as a critical mechanism through which sustainability objectives are translated
into operational practices, measurable indicators, and institutional accountability (UNEP, 2021).
Sustainability governance has increasingly expanded beyond internal corporate management to encompass
broader value-chain and lifecycle considerations (Sala et al., 2015; European Union, 2024). Contemporary
sustainability challenges often involve environme ntal, social, and ethical impacts occurring across sourcing,
production, distribution, consumption, and end-of-life stages. Consequently, governance mechanisms such as
traceability (European Union, 2024), transparency, accountability, supplier management ( European Union,
2024), and lifecycle assessment (ISO, 2024) have become increasingly important for evaluating sustainability
performance (Sala et al., 2015; OECD, 2023).
Transparency also plays a central role in sustainable governance systems. Increasing stakeholder
expectations, including those of consumers, regulators, investors, and civil society organizations, have
intensified demands for sustainability disclosure (Eur opean Union, 2022), measurable reporting (European
Union, 2022), and verifiable environmental and social claims (OECD, 2023). Consequently, sustainability
governance increasingly requires firms not only to communicate positive sustainability narratives, but also to
provide evidence -based explanations (UNEP, 2021) regarding how sustainability outcomes are produced,
monitored, and evaluated across organizational and supply chain structures (UNEP, 2021).
However, despite the expansion of ESG and sustainability governance frameworks, existing discussions in
the cosmetics and clean beauty sectors often remain focused on branding, ingredient selection, or symbolic
sustainability communication (Bom et al., 2019; Dini & Laneri, 2021). Comparatively less attention has been
paid to the governance mechanisms required to operationalize and verify sustainability claims throughout the
product lifecycle. This gap suggests the need for a more governance-centric understanding of sustainability in
clean beauty systems, particularly regarding supply chain coordination, transparency, accountability, and
implementation structures.
2.3. Greenwashing and Governance Decoupling
As sustainability has become increasingly integrated into corporate branding and consumer communication,
concerns regarding greenwashing have also intensified across multiple industries, including cosmetics and
clean beauty (Delmas & Burbano, 2011; Lyon & Montgomery, 2015). Greenwashing generally refers to
situations in which firms communicate environmentally or ethically responsible images without sufficient
substantive implementation to support such claims (Delmas & Burbano, 2011). In the clean beauty sec tor,
sustainability-related narratives frequently emphasize positive symbolic attributes such as “natural,” “eco -

friendly,” “ethical,” or “clean,” while providing limited evidence regarding measurable environmental
outcomes, governance systems, or lifecycle accountability (Bom et al., 2019; Dini & Laneri, 2021).
This phenomenon can be understood through the concept of organizational decoupling, which describes the
separation between symbolic commitments and operational practices (Bromley & Powell, 2012). In
sustainability contexts, firms may publicly adopt sustain ability-oriented narratives, certifications, or ethical
positioning while underlying governance structures, supply chain systems, and accountability mechanisms
remain underdeveloped or only partially implemented (Bromley & Powell, 2012; Delmas & Burbano, 2011).
As a result, sustainability communication may become disconnected from actual sustainability performance.
In the clean beauty sector, this decoupling is particularly significant because sustainability is often
communicated through consumer-facing narratives rather than through transparent governance mechanisms.
For example, brands may emphasize ingredient excl usion lists, botanical imagery, or ethical storytelling
without sufficiently disclosing Scope 3 emissions, sourcing conditions, traceability systems, or measurable
environmental indicators (OECD, 2023). Consequently, sustainability becomes associated with symbolic
perception rather than verifiable implementation.
Accordingly, the persistence of greenwashing risks in clean beauty may be understood not only as a
communication problem, but also as a governance problem arising from the structural divergence between
sustainability narratives and implementation mechanism s. This governance perspective provides the
theoretical foundation for the Governance-Based Clean Beauty Framework proposed in this study, which seeks
to evaluate sustainability through governance structures rather than through symbolic product claims alone.
3. Toward a Governance-Based Framework for Clean Beauty
The framework consists of six interrelated governance dimensions that represent interconnected areas relevant
to examining sustainability performance of clean beauty products and firms.
3.1. Limitations of Existing Definitions
Existing definitions of clean beauty remain fragmented and largely focus on ingredients, narratives, or
consumer perceptions (Bom et al., 2019; Dini & Laneri, 2021). Terms such as “natural,” “clean,” “ethical,”
and “non-toxic” are widely used in the clean beauty sector, yet their meanings often remain ambiguous and
inconsistently defined (Fonseca -Santos et al., 2015; Bom et al., 2019). While these approaches have
contributed to the popularization of “sustainable cosmetics”, they often fail to adequately exp lain how
environmental and social outcomes are operationally produced, monitored, and verified throughout the product
lifecycle. Consequently, existing definitions provide limited scope for examining sustainability performance
and suggest the value of further conceptualization through a governance-based perspective.
Therefore, a reconceptualization of clean beauty is necessary. Rather than relying primarily on ingredients,
product attributes, or sustainability narratives, a governance -based perspective provides a way to examine
sustainability through the systems and mechanisms that support implementation, accountability, transparency,
and lifecycle responsibility. Therefore, the present study proposes a Governance -Based Clean Beauty
Framework that shifts the analytical focus from symbolic product characteristics to gov ernance mechanisms
through which sustainability outcomes can be examined across the cosmetics value chain.
3.2. From Product Attributes to Governance Systems
Existing clean beauty definitions focus on what products are rather than how sustainability is governed (Bom
et al., 2019; Dini & Laneri, 2021). Its limitation suggests the need for a conceptual shift from product attribute-
and narrative -based understandings of sustainability to governance -based approaches. While conventional
definitions frequently emphasize ingredients, safety claims, naturalness, and ethical branding, these attributes
alone provide limited insight into the governance systems through which sustainability outcomes are managed,
monitored, and verified across the cosmetics value chain.

3.3. Conceptual Foundation of Governance-Based Clean Beauty
In this study, the term “framework” refers to a proposed conceptual structure and analytical lens for organizing
and examining sustainability governance issues in the cosmetics sector. It is not intended to constitute a
validated assessment tool, governance system, or causal model.
Based on the preceding discussions, this study defines governance -based clean beauty as an industry-
specific sustainability governance framework in which environmental, social, ethical, and lifecycle
considerations are systematically addressed, monitored, verified, and maintained through governance
mechanisms operating across the cosmetics value chain.
Unlike conventional clean beauty definitions that primarily emphasize ingredients, product attributes,
sustainability narratives, or consumer perceptions (Fonseca -Santos et al., 2015; Bom et al., 2019; Dini &
Laneri, 2021), governance -based clean beauty fo cuses on the institutional arrangements through which
sustainability is implemented and evaluated. In this perspective, sustainability may be considered not only as
a product characteristic or marketing claim, but as an ongoing governance process involving  accountability,
transparency, traceability, lifecycle responsibility, and continuous performance improvement (OECD, 2023).
Accordingly, the evaluation of clean beauty extends beyond whether products appear “natural,” “safe,” or
“ethical.” Instead, it places greater analytical emphasis on examining the governance systems that are

3.4. Framework Development Approach
This study adopts a conceptual framework-building approach rather than a systematic literature review or an
empirical framework -development methodology. The framework was developed through an iterative,
interpretive synthesis of literature relevant to clean beauty and sustainability governance.
The identification of relevant literature was initially informed by the author's longstanding professional
experience in the cosmetics industry and subsequent work on sustainability and environmental governance in
the sector. Based on this industry -specific knowledge, a set of preliminary governance -related themes and
search concepts was identified, including sustainable sourcing, circularity, biodiversity, supply chain
governance, transparency, lifecycle responsibility, and environmental and social impacts. These themes were
used to guide the identification of relevant academic and policy-oriented literature. AI-assisted literature search
was used as a supporting tool for identifying potentially relevant publications and sources. The identified
literature w as subsequently reviewed and verified by the author, and sources considered relevant to
sustainability governance in the cosmetics value chain were retained for conceptual analysis.
The literature was examined iteratively to identify recurring and complementary governance concerns
across the cosmetics lifecycle. Particular attention was given to issues relating to environmental impact,
ingredient integrity, supply chain governance, tr ansparency and accountability, circularity and lifecycle
responsibility, and human, ecological, and ethical considerations. The identified themes were then compared
and refined through repeated examination of the literature and their relevance to the cosmetics industry context.
The six dimensions were not derived through a predetermined coding protocol, bibliometric analysis, or
statistical procedure. Rather, themes addressing substantially overlapping governance concerns were
consolidated, while themes representing analytically distinct governance functions were retained as separate
dimensions. Through this iterative process of literature review, verification, comparison, and conceptual
consolidation, six dimensions were established: Environmental Impact; Ingredients Integrity; S upply Chain
Governance; Transparency and Accountability; End -of-Life and Circularity Governance; and Human,
Ecological, and Ethical Impact.

4. The Six Dimensions of Governance-Based Clean Beauty
4.1. Environmental Impact
Environmental Impact represents one of the core dimensions of governance -based clean beauty because the
cosmetics industry generates environmental pressures throughout the product lifecycle  (Sala et al., 2015;
OECD, 2023). These pressures arise not only from product formulation but also from activities associated with
sourcing, manufacturing, transportation, consumption, and end -of-life management. Consequently,
sustainability performance depends on how environmental impacts are governed across interconnected stages
of production and use.
This dimension encompasses multiple environmental concerns, including climate change, biodiversity
impact, water use, resource consumption, and pollution generation (United Nations Environment Programme
[UNEP], 2021; OECD, 2023) . Rather than treating sustainability as a product attribute or marketing claim,
governance-based clean beauty places analytical emphasis on the identification, measurement, management,
and disclosure of environmental impacts throughout the cosmetics value chain.
In the cosmetics sector, environmental performance is influenced by a wide range of operational decisions,
including ingredient sourcing practices, manufacturing efficiency, packaging design, logistics, and waste
management strategies (Bom et al., 2019) . While many clean beauty initiatives emphasize environmentally
friendly ingredients or packaging materials, these visible attributes often represent only a portion of the overall
environmental footprint (Sala et al., 2015). Therefore, a governance-based perspective may be considered in
terms of  lifecycle impacts and systemic environmental risks that may not be immediately apparent to
consumers.
From a governance perspective, environmental responsibility involves the establishment of mechanisms
for measurement, monitoring, disclosure, and continuous improvement  (OECD, 2023) . Governance -based
clean beauty places emphasis on integrating environmental considerations into decision-making processes and
accountability structures rather than relying solely on sustainability narratives or marketing claims.
Accordingly, this study conceptualizes environmental impact as a governance challenge  involving lifecycle
accountability, transparent reporting, and long-term environmental stewardship throughout the cosmetics value
chain.
4.2. Ingredients Integrity
Ingredients integrity encompasses ingredient traceability, authenticity, formulation integrity, and ingredient
transparency. This dimension represents a core dimension of governance -based clean beauty because
ingredients constitute the primary interface through which sustainability, safety, and ethical claims are
communicated to consumers  (Bom et al., 2019; Dini & Laneri, 2021). Traditionally, clean beauty has been
strongly associated with ingredient-focused narratives, including claims related to naturalness, non-toxicity, or
the exclusion of specific substances (Fonseca-Santos et al., 2015). However, such claims often provide limited
insight into the governance systems that ensure the quality, authenticity, and sustainability of ingredients
throughout the product lifecycle.
In the cosmetics industry, ingredient integrity extends beyond formulation decisions and encompasses
issues such as ingredient traceability, sourcing practices, authenticity verification, supplier oversight, and
compliance with environmental and ethical sta ndards (OECD, 2023). The sustainability implications of
cosmetic ingredients are influenced not only by their chemical composition but also by how they are produced,

sourced, processed, and monitored across supply chains. Consequently, ingredient-related sustainability cannot
be adequately assessed through ingredient lists or marketing claims alone.
From a governance perspective, ingredient integrity requires mechanisms that ensure transparency,
verification, accountability, and continuous monitoring throughout the value chain (OECD, 2023). Therefore,
governance-based clean beauty evaluates ingredients not merely as symbolic sustainability signifiers but as
governed resources whose environmental, social, and ethical implications must be systematically managed and
disclosed. Accordingly, this study concep tualizes ingredient integrity as a critical gover nance dimension
linking product formulation with broader sustainability outcomes.
4.3. Supply Chain Governance
Supply chain governance  includes ethical sourcing, labor and human rights protection, environmental
responsibility across supply chains, traceability systems, and supplier governance mechanisms. This dimension
constitutes a critical dimension of governance -based clean beauty because sustainability outcomes in the
cosmetics sector are largely shaped through interconnected sourcing, production, manufacturing,
transportation, and distribution processes (OECD, 2023; European Union, 2024). While ingredients integrity
focuses primarily on what materials are contained in products, supply chain governance addresses how those
materials are sourced, processed, managed, and circulated throughout the value chain. In this sense, supply
chain governance shifts analytical attention from ingredient composition itself to the governance systems
through which sustainability is operationalized through networks within and between firms.
One of the most significant dimensions of supply chain governance involves labour and human rights.
Cosmetic supply chains frequently depend on globally dispersed production systems covered with agricultural
producers, extractive industries, manufacturing facilities, logistics networks, and subcontracted suppliers
operating across diverse regulatory environments (OECD, 2023). Because cosmetic production relies heavily
on agricultural raw materials, farming practices involving pesticides, fertilizers, and other chemical inputs may
contribute not only to soil and water pollution but also to occupational health risks for workers  (FAO, 2024).
Consequently, sustainability governance requires firms to address issues such as labour conditions, fair wages,
occupational safety, forced labour risks, gender inequality, and broader human rights protections embedded in
supply chain structures  (OECD, 2023) . Therefore, g overnance-based clean beauty extends beyond
environmental narratives to include the social governance systems necessary to ensure ethical production
conditions throughout the value chain.
Ethical sourcing of raw materials also represents a critical governance concern. Many clean beauty brands
emphasize naturally derived, botanical, marine, or ethically sourced ingredients; however, sustainability cannot
be evaluated solely through the symbolic identity of ingredients themselves. Governance-based sustainability
instead may examine sourcing practices, extraction methods, harvesting systems, supplier relationships, and
ecological management structures associated with raw material procurement  (OECD, 2023) . Accordingly,
ethical sourcing involves not only selecting “sustainable” ingredients, but also governing the institutional
processes through which such resources are accessed and managed.
Environmental responsibility in supply chains reinforces the importance of governance -centric
sustainability. Significant environmental impacts , including greenhouse gas emissions, biodiversity
degradation, land use change, marine ecosystem pressure, water consumption, and waste generation , often
occur in upstream supply chain activities rather than in final consumer -facing products alone (UNEP, 2021;
FAO, 2024) . As a result, governance -based clean beauty places analytical emphasis on  integrating
environmental accountability throughout procurement, manufacturing, logistics, and distribution systems
rather than limiting sustainability efforts to product-level marketing claims.
Traceability and supplier governance become essential mechanisms for connecting sustainability narratives
to operational accountability  (OECD, 2023) . While ingredients integrity focuses on what ingredients are
included in formulations, supply chain governance addresses how th ese ingredients were produced, sourced,
transported, and managed prior to product formulation. Traceability systems enable firms to identify supplier
relationships, sourcing origins, environmental risks, and labour conditions embedded in supply chain activities,
while supplier governance mechanisms establish standards, monitoring systems, auditing processes, and
accountability structures across organizational networks. In this sense, traceability functions not merely as a
disclosure tool , but as a governance infrastructure linking sustainability claims to measurable operational
practices.

4.4. Transparency and Accountability
Transparency and accountability include disclosure completeness, verifiability, consistency and comparability,
and Communication integrity. Transparency and accountability constitute essential dimensions of governance-
based clean beauty because sustainability performance cannot be effectively assessed without mechanisms that
disclose, verify, and communicate governance practices  (OECD, 2023; European Commission, 2022). As
sustainability claims become increasingly prominent in the cosmetics sector, stakeholders require reliable
information that allows them to distinguish between symbolic sustainability narratives and demonstrable
governance performance.  While sustainability communication often emphasizes ethical or environmental
values, governance-based sustainability requires information that enables stakeholders to assess whether such
claims are supported by actual governance practices and measurable outcomes (Delmas & Burbano, 2011).
Disclosure completeness refers to the provision of information regarding ingredients, sourcing, supply
chains, environmental impacts, and sustainability commitments. Verifiability requires sustainability claims to
be supported by evidence, traceability systems, certifications, audits, or other forms of independent validation.
Comparability enables stakeholders to evaluate sustainability performance across firms through consistent
reporting frameworks and governance indicators  (European Commission, 2022). Co mmunication integrity
concerns the alignment between sustainability narratives and operational realities, helping reduce the risk of
exaggerated, selective, or misleading sustainability claims (Lyon & Montgomery, 2015).
Importantly, transparency and accountability are distinct from ingredients integrity. Whereas ingredients
integrity concerns the governance of ingredients themselves, transparency and accountability address how
governance practices are disclosed, communica ted, and externally evaluated. Responsible sourcing or
formulation alone is insufficient if related governance information remains inaccessible or unverifiable.
Accordingly, transparency functions as a foundational condition for effective sustainability governance. By
enabling external scrutiny, stakeholder engagement, and organizational accountability, transparency helps
connect sustainability narratives to verif iable governance practices  (OECD, 2023). Without transparent
disclosure and accountability mechanisms, sustainability claims risk becoming symbolic narratives detached
from measurable implementation and governance performance , which could lead to a risk of  greenwashing
and “cleanwashing”.
Importantly, transparency and accountability function as a cross -cutting governance dimension that
supports all other dimensions in the framework. Whereas Dimensions 1, 2, 3, and 5 address specific areas of
sustainability governance across the product lifecycle, transparency and accountability provide the disclosure,
verification, traceability, and reporting mechanisms necessary to monitor and evaluate their implementation.
Accordingly, this dimension serves as the governance infrastructure through which sustainability performance
can be communicated, assessed, and held accountable across the cosmetics value chain.
Accordingly, this study conceptualizes Transparency and Accountability as the governance infrastructure
through which sustainability performance can be communicated, assessed, verified, and held accountable
across the cosmetics value chain.

4.6. Human, Ecological, and Ethical Impact
Human, Ecological, and Ethical Impact constitutes the outcome-oriented dimension of governance-based clean

sustainability is governed, but also of the impacts associated with governance systems for people, ecosystems,
and animals.
Human impact concerns the effects of cosmetic systems on consumers, workers, and surrounding
communities. Relevant considerations include product safety, occupational health, exposure to potentially
harmful substances, and broader health implications associated with sourcing, manufacturing, and product use
(OECD, 2023; FAO, 2024). Therefore, governance-based clean beauty may examine human wellbeing through
measurable and evidence-based criteria rather than relying solely on generalized claims such as “safe” or “non-
toxic.”
Ecological impact refers to the environmental consequences of cosmetic production and consumption,
including biodiversity loss, water pollution, habitat degradation, resource depletion, and ecosystem
disturbance, in terms of ecosystem services - and water- & soil- health (Millennium Ecosystem Assessment,
2005; UNEP, 2021). These impacts often represent the cumulative outcomes of governance decisions made
across sourcing, manufacturing, distribution, and waste management systems. Consequently, ecological
performance provides an important indicator of the effectiveness of sustainability governance throughout the
product lifecycle.
Ethical impact addresses animal welfare, cruelty -free practices, alternative testing methods, and broader
ethical responsibilities embedded in cosmetic value chains. In recent years, considerable attention has been
devoted to eliminating animal testing and promoting cruelty-free and vegan cosmetics (European Commission,
2013). However, ethical responsibility extends beyond animal testing. It also encompasses the governance of
biological resource procurement, including compliance with regulations governing t he collection and
harvesting of wildlife and other protected natural resources. Therefore, Governance -Based Clean Beauty
places analytical emphasis on the identification, measurement, management, and disclosure of ethical
commitments to be supported by verifiable governance mechanisms, supplier standards, traceability systems,
and accountability structures rather than functioning solely as consumer-facing sustainability narratives.
The interdependent nature of the six governance dimensions is particularly evident in the way sustainability
impacts emerge across the cosmetics lifecycle. For example, environmental risks associated with UV filters,
microplastics, and other substances rel eased during product use, as discussed in Section 4.5, may ultimately
affect aquatic ecosystems and biodiversity (Takai & Nagai, 2023). Similarly, environmental and social impacts
associated with unsustainable agricultural practices discussed in Section 4.2 may influence ecosystem health,
resource availability, and community wellbeing  (FAO, 2024). Accordingly, Human, Ecological, and Ethical
Impact represents the cumulative outcome dimension through which the effectiveness of governance
arrangements across the preceding dimensions can ultimately be evaluated.
Importantly, this dimension functions as the outcome -oriented layer conceptually linked to the preceding
governance dimensions. Whereas Dimensions 1–5 and Use (consumer phase: see Figure 2) address governance
structures, management systems, and implementation processes, Human, Ecological, and Ethical Impact
evaluates the sustainability outcomes associated with  those governance arrangements. Improvements in
environmental management, ingredient integrity, supply chain governance, transparency, circularity , an d
consumer use may be associated with sustainability-related outcomes concerning human wellbeing, ecosystem
health, and animal welfare. Accordingly, this dimension serves as the primary outcome-oriented layer through
which sustainability-related implications of the governance dimensions can be conceptually examined.

conceptual structure through which governance concepts can be translated into an industry-specific analytical

commitments may be actually implemented, monitored, verified, and continuously improved. Accordingly,
sustainability communication should be understood as an outcome of effective governance rather than as  a
substitute for governance itself.

based approach developed in this study offers a conceptual foundation for future research exploring industry-
specific sustainability governance models across a wider range of sectors.  Rather than proposing a universal
sustainability framework, this study suggests how governance principles can be translated into industry -
specific conceptual governance frameworks that reflect the characteristics of individual sectors.
Finally, this study contributes to the growing discussion on governance-oriented sustainability by providing
a conceptual basis for future empirical research, indicator development, governance benchmarking, and sector-
specific sustainability assessment sys tems. Future studies may further operationalize the six dimensions
through measurable indicators and comparative evaluation models, thereby supporting more systematic
assessment of sustainability governance and implementation in the cosmetics industry and beyond. Future
empirical research should also validate the proposed framework across a broader range of cosmetics brands
and organizational contexts to further examine its practical applicability and robustness.
