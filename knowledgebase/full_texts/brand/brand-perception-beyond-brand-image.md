# Brand Perception Beyond Brand Image

SCIENCE INTERNATIONAL EMPIRICAL REVIEW
Volume 2 - Issue 1 - 2026
Brand Perception Beyond Brand Image
Toward an Integrative Conceptualisation of Brand Perception
Jens Schröder, brajns establishment, Liechtenstein
Abstract
Branding research offers a rich set of constructs for explaining how brands are
represented, evaluated, experienced, and socially understood. Brand Image, Brand
Associations, Brand Attitude, Brand Knowledge, Brand Experience, Brand Personality,
Brand Meaning, and Brand Reputation each capture important but different aspects of
this external brand reality. The theoretical challenge is therefore not the absence of
perception-related concepts but their fragmentation. This conceptual article clarifies the
position of Brand Perception in relation to established branding constructs and
proposes it as an integrative analytical perspective rather than a new superconstruct.
Brand Perception is defined as the dynamic configuration through which relevant
stakeholders experience, interpret, associate, evaluate, and make sense of a brand. The
article differentiates Brand Perception from adjacent constructs according to primary
focus, unit of analysis, and temporal character, and develops a compact architecture
linking brand encounters with experience, interpretation, association, evaluation, sense-
making, and stakeholder-specific perceptual configurations. The framework emphasises
that Brand Perception is dynamic, stakeholder-sensitive, and broader than any single
measure, while preserving the theoretical integrity of established constructs. It provides
a conceptual foundation for research that seeks to use Current Brand Perception as
strategic evidence in Brand Management and Perception Brand Positioning.
Keywords: brand perception, brand image, brand knowledge, brand experience, brand
meaning, brand reputation, brand associations, strategic brand management
JEL codes: M31
1. Introduction
Brands do not exist solely through what organisations intend them to represent. They
also exist through what people perceive, remember, associate, experience, evaluate,
interpret, and communicate about them. Branding research has studied this external
dimension extensively through constructs including Brand Image, Brand Associations,
Brand Attitude, Brand Knowledge, Brand Experience, Brand Personality, Brand Meaning,
and Brand Reputation (Keller, 1993; Aaker, 1997; Brakus et al., 2009).
The richness of this conceptual landscape is valuable but also creates ambiguity. Some
constructs emphasise cognition, others evaluation, experience, symbolic meaning,
identity, or collective judgement. Some are primarily consumer-level constructs, while

others accommodate multiple stakeholder groups. Some capture relatively immediate
responses, whereas others imply accumulation over time. As a result, the broader term
Brand Perception is intuitively understandable but often theoretically underspecified.
This ambiguity matters when Brand Perception is expected to perform a strategic role.
If Current Brand Perception is to provide evidence for Brand Management or Brand
Positioning, it must first be clear what the concept includes, how it differs from adjacent
constructs, and where its boundaries lie. The relevant question is therefore not whether
branding research already contains perception-related constructs. It clearly does. The
question is how Brand Perception can be conceptualised without simply duplicating
them.
Existing constructs set a demanding boundary. Keller’s (1993) Brand Knowledge
framework already integrates Brand Awareness and Brand Image, while Brakus et al.
(2009) conceptualise Brand Experience through sensory, affective, intellectual, and
behavioural responses. Research on Brand Meaning further extends the field toward
symbolic, cultural, social, and identity-related interpretations (Escalas and Bettman,
2005; Batra, 2019). Brand Perception therefore needs to contribute integration rather
than terminological substitution.
This article proposes the following working definition:
Brand Perception refers to the dynamic configuration through which relevant
stakeholders experience, interpret, associate, evaluate, and make sense of a brand.
The definition is deliberately broad but not unlimited. “Dynamic” recognises that
perception can change through repeated encounters and new contexts. “Configuration”
indicates that Brand Perception is not assumed to be one score. “Relevant stakeholders”
broadens the perspective beyond consumers alone. The verbs experience, interpret,
associate, evaluate, and make sense connect the concept to established research
traditions without erasing their distinctions.
The central research question is:
How can Brand Perception be conceptually distinguished from, and integrated
with, established constructs such as Brand Image, Brand Associations, Brand
Attitude, Brand Knowledge, Brand Experience, Brand Meaning, Brand
Personality, and Brand Reputation?
The article contributes by mapping the fragmented conceptual landscape, defining
explicit boundaries, and proposing an integrative Brand Perception architecture. It does
not claim that Brand Perception should become a master construct that subsumes
established theories. Its purpose is to provide a coherent analytical perspective for
questions that require an understanding of how a brand presently exists within
stakeholder perception.
2. The Landscape of Brand-Related Perception Constructs
Branding research provides multiple constructs that explain how brands are
represented and interpreted beyond managerial intention. Their overlap is substantial,
but their theoretical emphasis differs. Clarifying these differences is therefore a
prerequisite for an integrative conceptualisation of Brand Perception.

2.1. Brand Associations and Brand Image
Brand Associations are central to understanding how brands are represented in
memory. Keller (1993) conceptualises Brand Image as perceptions reflected by
associations held in consumer memory. These associations can relate to attributes,
benefits, attitudes, users, usage situations, or other meanings and vary in strength,
favourability, and uniqueness. Brand Associations can therefore be understood as
individual or interconnected elements of mental representation, while Brand Image
describes the broader configuration of such associations.
Brand Image is consequently the construct closest to Brand Perception. Systematic
review research also demonstrates the breadth of Brand Image measurement (Plumeyer
et al., 2019). The distinction proposed here is not categorical. Brand Image remains an
established core representation of how a brand is held in memory. Brand Perception
places additional emphasis on how such representations emerge, are interpreted,
evaluated, experienced, and dynamically reconstructed across stakeholders and
contexts.
2.2. Brand Attitude and Brand Knowledge
Brand Attitude is primarily evaluative. Whereas Brand Image may contain many
descriptive and symbolic associations, Brand Attitude addresses how favourably or
unfavourably the brand is evaluated. Attitude can therefore be considered an evaluative
component of a broader perceptual configuration. A brand may be perceived as
innovative, distant, prestigious, reliable, and expensive, while the overall attitude
remains positive or negative depending on the relevance of those meanings.
Brand Knowledge is broader than Brand Image because Keller (1993) combines
Brand Awareness and Brand Image within the construct. Its principal orientation,
however, remains cognitive and memory-based: what is known, recalled, recognised,
and associated with the brand. Brand Perception, as developed here, is more process-
and stakeholder-oriented. It asks not only what is represented in memory but how the
brand becomes meaningful through experience, interpretation, evaluation, and social
sense-making.
2.3. Brand Experience and Brand Personality
Brand Experience describes subjective internal responses evoked by brand-related
stimuli. Brakus et al. (2009) distinguish sensory, affective, intellectual, and behavioural
dimensions. Experience is therefore an important input into perception because brands
are encountered through products, services, communication, environments, interfaces,
and employees. Motta-Filho (2021) similarly argues for a closer integration of brand
strategy and customer experience, underlining that planned brand meaning and lived
brand encounters cannot be treated as separate managerial domains. Brand Perception
extends this logic by focusing on how experiences and other encounters are interpreted
and integrated into an evolving representation of the brand.
Brand Personality is narrower. Aaker (1997) shows that consumers attribute human
characteristics to brands, such as sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, and
ruggedness. Brand Personality therefore represents one specific symbolic attribution
domain. A brand can possess a clear personality while simultaneously being understood
through functional qualities, values, experiences, reputation, and other meanings.

2.4. Brand Meaning and Brand Reputation
Brand Meaning comes especially close to the present perspective because brands can
acquire symbolic, cultural, social, and identity-related significance beyond functional
performance. Such meaning develops through communication, experience,
consumption, reference groups, cultural narratives, and interaction (Batra, 2019;
Escalas and Bettman, 2005). More recent reviews reinforce the shift from a firm-
controlled view of branding toward co-creation among multiple actors: Sarasvuo et al.
(2022) synthesise 148 studies on co-creation in branding, while Siano et al. (2022)
describe a broader movement from brand control toward co-creation. Brand Meaning
and Brand Perception therefore overlap, but their emphasis differs: Brand Meaning asks
what the brand signifies; Brand Perception addresses the wider configuration through
which it is experienced, interpreted, associated, evaluated, and made meaningful.
Brand Reputation introduces a more collective and longitudinal emphasis. Reputation
develops through accumulated evaluations across time and stakeholder groups and
therefore extends beyond an individual’s immediate perception (Walsh et al., 2009). An
individual stakeholder can hold a Brand Perception; stakeholder groups can display
recurring Brand Perception Patterns; reputation represents a more aggregated and
socially accumulated manifestation of judgement. The constructs are related but not
synonymous.
3. Conceptual Boundaries of Brand Perception
The preceding comparison suggests that Brand Perception should not be introduced as
another competing construct. Its conceptual value lies in integration. Three boundary
criteria are especially important: primary focus, unit of analysis, and temporal character.

The comparison shows why Brand Perception occupies a different conceptual level. It
does not replace the adjacent constructs; rather, it provides a perspective through which
their relationships can be interpreted when the research interest is the broader
perceptual state of the brand.

3.2. Unit of Analysis
Several established constructs were originally developed primarily from a consumer
perspective. Contemporary branding research increasingly recognises that employees,
business partners, investors, communities, intermediaries, and other actors can
participate in the construction of Brand Meaning (Merz et al., 2009; Gyrd-Jones and
Kornum, 2013; Sarasvuo et al., 2022; Siano et al., 2022). The proposed Brand Perception
perspective therefore uses relevant stakeholders rather than consumers alone as its
analytical frame. This does not imply that all stakeholder groups are equally important.
Their relevance remains strategically and contextually determined. The implication is
that a brand may possess several simultaneous perceptual configurations rather than
one homogeneous Brand Perception.
3.3. Temporal Character and Configuration
Brand Perception is dynamic. It exists at a particular point in time but is shaped by
previous experiences and remains open to change through new products,
communication, organisational behaviour, social events, competitive developments, and
stakeholder discourse. This produces both a current state and a developmental
character.
The term configuration is equally important. Brand Perception is not proposed as a
latent superconstruct statistically composed of Brand Image, Experience, Attitude,
Reputation, and other variables. Such a claim would require substantial empirical
validation and risk conceptual inflation. Configuration instead refers to the pattern
through which different perceptual phenomena become relevant in a particular context.
A Brand Perception configuration may include associations, evaluations, experiential
memories, value relationships, symbolic meanings, identity relationships, or reputation-
related judgements, without requiring every component to be equally relevant for every
brand.
4. Toward an Integrative Conceptualisation
The proposed definition identifies five interrelated processes: experience,
interpretation, association, evaluation, and sense-making. These processes clarify how
established branding constructs relate to a broader perceptual configuration.
4.1. Experience, Interpretation, and Association
Stakeholders encounter brands through products, services, communication,
environments, employees, interfaces, conversations, and other touchpoints. These
encounters create sensory, emotional, cognitive, and behavioural responses. Experience
therefore provides important input into Brand Perception. Yet brand-related stimuli do
not carry fixed meaning. Stakeholders interpret them within personal, social, cultural,
competitive, and situational contexts. The same signal may therefore be understood
differently across individuals or stakeholder groups.
Encounters and interpretations also become linked with existing knowledge,
memories, categories, values, people, situations, emotions, and competing brands. This

associative process connects Brand Perception closely with Brand Image and Brand
Knowledge, while interpretation explains why the resulting configuration cannot be
reduced to stored associations alone.
4.2. Evaluation and Sense-Making
Stakeholders evaluate what they perceive. Brand characteristics and meanings may be
regarded as positive, negative, relevant, irrelevant, desirable, problematic, trustworthy,
attractive, or contradictory. Evaluation links Brand Perception with Brand Attitude and
reputation-related judgement. Stakeholders then integrate experiences, associations,
and evaluations into broader meaning structures: what the brand represents, what kind
of organisation stands behind it, what values it symbolises, or what using, rejecting, or
recommending it communicates.
The five processes should not be interpreted as a mandatory sequence. Stakeholders
may associate before they consciously evaluate, interpret new encounters through
existing associations, or reinterpret previous experiences after receiving new
information. The relationship is recursive.

Stakeholder-Specific Manifestations → Reinforcement, Contradiction, and Change
The architecture highlights three principles. First, Brand Perception is not created by
communication alone; products, services, behaviour, third-party information,
employees, competitors, cultural narratives, and previous experience all contribute.
Second, Brand Perception is stakeholder-specific. Employees, customers, partners,
investors, and communities can encounter different aspects of the same brand, so one
brand does not necessarily produce one homogeneous perception. Third, Brand
Perception is dynamic. Some associations may become strongly established and
resistant to change, whereas others remain context-dependent.
6. Implications for Research and Brand Management
For research, the framework implies that Brand Perception should not become a vague
substitute for more precise terminology. If a study measures Brand Image, Brand
Attitude, or Brand Reputation, it should use the established construct name. The broader
term becomes useful when the research question explicitly requires integration across
several perceptual phenomena. Researchers should also distinguish the constructs used
for measurement from the wider configuration being interpreted.
The stakeholder perspective further implies that aggregation can conceal strategically
relevant differences. An overall Brand Image may mask divergent perceptions between
employees, customers, partners, or other groups. Longitudinal work is equally
important because a one-time diagnosis describes a current state but cannot explain
perceptual development.

For managers, Brand Perception encourages a pattern-based rather than metric-based
view. Brand Awareness may be high while Brand Meaning is weak; Brand Attitude may
be positive while differentiation is low; Brand Experience may be strong while
reputation is problematic; internal and external stakeholders may interpret the brand
differently. Such findings are not necessarily contradictions. They may represent
different components of the same broader perceptual configuration. The managerial
question therefore becomes: how does the brand presently exist in the perceptions of
the stakeholder groups that matter strategically?
6.1. Three Principles of the Brand Perception Architecture
The architecture developed above can be condensed into three principles that clarify its
strategic meaning. First, Brand Perception is not created by communication alone.
Products, services, behaviour, previous experience, employees, third-party information,
digital discourse, competitors, and cultural narratives can all contribute to how a brand
is interpreted. Managerial communication is therefore one input into a broader
perceptual environment rather than the exclusive author of Brand Meaning.
Second, Brand Perception is stakeholder-specific. Different stakeholders encounter
different aspects of the same brand and may therefore construct different but
simultaneously valid perceptual configurations. Employees experience organisational
culture and leadership; customers experience products and services; partners
experience collaboration; investors may interpret strategic credibility; communities
may respond to social or environmental behaviour. The implication is not that every
stakeholder perspective must be equally weighted, but that a single aggregated Brand
Image can conceal differences that matter strategically.
Third, Brand Perception is dynamic. Some perceptions become deeply established
through repeated experience and social reinforcement, while others remain context-
sensitive and open to rapid change. A current diagnosis therefore provides a state
description rather than a permanent essence of the brand. This distinction is important
for any later attempt to compare Current and Desired Brand Perception or to assess
Brand Perception Change over time.
6.2. Research Implications
The integrative perspective has several consequences for research design. Studies
should first specify whether they are examining a particular established construct or a
broader Brand Perception configuration. If the research question concerns Brand
Attitude, Brand Image, or Reputation, the established construct should be named and
measured directly. The umbrella term Brand Perception becomes useful when the
research question explicitly spans several forms of experience, cognition, evaluation,
meaning, or stakeholder interpretation.
Second, researchers should distinguish measurement from configuration. A study can
use association measures, experience scales, value measures, qualitative interpretation,
or stakeholder interviews without claiming that any one instrument captures Brand
Perception in its entirety. The broader configuration is an analytical synthesis across
relevant evidence, not a single universal metric. Third, future work should examine
perceptual heterogeneity explicitly rather than treating between-group differences as
noise. Shared and divergent patterns may have different strategic implications.

Fourth, longitudinal research is needed. If Brand Perception is dynamic, cross-
sectional designs capture only a current configuration. Repeated measurement could
clarify which meanings are stable, which are context-dependent, and how perceptions
respond to strategic interventions. Such work would also help distinguish short-lived
reactions from more durable Brand Perception Change.
6.3. Managerial Implications and Conceptual Limits
For managers, the framework argues against reliance on one summary metric as a
complete representation of the brand. High awareness can coexist with weak meaning;
positive attitude with low differentiation; strong experience with problematic
reputation; and favourable customer perception with internal uncertainty. These are not
necessarily inconsistencies in the data. They may describe different facets of the same
perceptual configuration.
The framework also reinforces the distinction between Brand Identity and Brand
Perception. Brand Identity concerns what the organisation considers the brand to
represent or intends it to become. Brand Perception concerns how relevant
stakeholders currently experience and interpret it. Neither automatically determines the
other. Strategic Brand Management requires interpretation of the relationship between
them.
At the same time, Brand Perception should not become conceptually unlimited. Brand
Trust, Attachment, Love, Authenticity, Engagement, and Identification may relate to
perception but are not automatically components of it. The present architecture
therefore should be treated as a bounded integrative perspective whose usefulness
depends on conceptual discipline. Its purpose is to connect established perceptual
phenomena when a broader strategic diagnosis is required, not to absorb every
consumer-brand construct into one label.
7. Discussion and Conclusions
The theoretical problem addressed in this article is one of fragmentation and integration
rather than conceptual absence. Branding research already provides sophisticated
constructs concerning associative representation, evaluation, experience, personality,
meaning, knowledge, and reputation. Brand Perception is useful only if it preserves
those distinctions while providing a broader analytical frame.
The principal contribution is therefore to conceptualise Brand Perception as a
dynamic, stakeholder-specific configuration rather than a replacement construct. This
perspective shifts the unit of analysis from a single consumer representation toward a
potentially heterogeneous stakeholder configuration and adds a temporal dimension by
recognising both current state and change. At the same time, it deliberately avoids
claiming that Brand Perception is an empirically validated superconstruct.
Several limitations remain. The boundaries among experience, interpretation,
association, evaluation, and sense-making are analytically useful but not necessarily
empirically discrete. Other constructs, including Brand Trust, Attachment, Love,
Authenticity, Engagement, and Identification, may also relate to Brand Perception and
warrant future integration. Stakeholder heterogeneity and longitudinal change require
empirical development across different brand contexts.

In conclusion, Brand Perception can be defined as the dynamic configuration through
which relevant stakeholders experience, interpret, associate, evaluate, and make sense
of a brand. The purpose of the definition is not to replace Brand Image, Brand
Knowledge, Brand Experience, Brand Meaning, or Reputation, but to connect them
where a broader understanding of the brand’s perceptual state is required. This
conceptual clarity provides the foundation for subsequent research on the strategic role
of Current Brand Perception and Perception Brand Positioning.
