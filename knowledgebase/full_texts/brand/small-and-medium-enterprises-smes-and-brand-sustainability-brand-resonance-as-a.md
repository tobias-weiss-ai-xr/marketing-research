ABSTRACT
This study investigates the role of brand resonance, conceptualized through the Consumer–Brand Relationship
(CBR) theory, as a source of competitiv e advantage fo r sustainin g Small and Medium Enterprise (SME) brands.
Brand resonance represents the deep, enduring emotional and behavioral bond formed between consumers and
brands. Grounded in this perspective, the research examines the influence of CBR on sustainabl e brand equity
and consumers’ perceptions of SMEs’ social media branding activities on Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter. A
quantitative cross-sectional design was employed across Peninsular Malaysia using an electronic survey distributed
via email and WhatsApp. Of the 500 SME consumers invited t o participate, 300 responses were obtained and
analyzed. The results indicate that CBR significantly affects sustainable brand equity and positively shapes consumer
perceptions of SMEs’ social media branding initiatives . These findings demonstrate that CBR functions as a critical
competitive advantage strategy for SMEs by strengthening consumer attachment and long-term brand value. The
study extends CBR theory by validating its relevance within the SME context, traditionall y examined among larger
firms, and contributes to the growing body of literature on sustainabl e brand equity. Despite the theoretical and
managerial implications, this research is limited to the food and beverage SME sector in Malaysia. Future studies
should consider examining CBR across broader SME industries and geographical regions.

1. INTRODUCTION
Small and medium enterprises (SMEs) are widely
recognized as essential contributors to the global
economy and are often referred to as the backbone
of economic development (Zutshi et al., 2021; Berniak-
Woźny et al., 2023). In Malaysia, SMEs continue to play
a significant role as key drivers of national economic
growth, contributing substantially to the country’s Gross
Domestic Product and providing important sources
of employment (Department of Statistics Malaysia,
2020; Lim et al., 2021). In recent years, the Malaysian
government has strengthened its support for SMEs,
particularly in efforts to restore economic resilience
in the post-COVID-19 period (Sit, 2022). As a result,
the question of how SMEs can sustain their growth,
maintain competitiveness, and ensure long-term brand
sustainability has become increasingly relevant.
The COVID-19 pandemic had a severe impact on SMEs
worldwide, and Malaysia was no exception. Since 2020,
more than 150,000 Malaysian SMEs have reportedly
ceased operations, resulting in over 1.2 million job losses
(Bernama, 2020; Zutshi et al., 2021). These circumstances
demonstrate that inadequate business sustainability
increases the vulnerability of SMEs to environmental,
social, and economic uncertainties. Consequently,
the crisis prompted many SMEs to reassess their brand
equity from the consumer perspective to strengthen
resilience and ensure continuity during uncertain times
(AbdGhani et al., 2021; Zutshi et al., 2021). A strong
brand with high equity is regarded as a strategic asset,
reflecting a company’s competitive differentiation and
long-term market viability. Therefore, the continuous
development of sustainable brand equity is essential
for businesses, including SMEs, to maintain long-term
relevance in the marketplace (AbdGhani et al., 2021;
Kumar & Kaushik, 2022; Sit, 2022).
Previous studies have shown that SMEs frequently

2. LITERATURE REVIEW
The influence of CBR on brand equity has been widely
examined across various market settings. Sharma and
Singh (2023) highlighted that sustainable brand equity
substantially enhances SMEs’ competitiveness by
fostering trust, loyalty, and long-term customer retention.
When SMEs can maintain strong brand equity, they
differentiate themselves more effectively in competitive
markets, thereby increasing customer recognition and
perceived credibility (Anim et al., 2020). Recent research
continues to reinforce the significance of SMEs’ brand
equity (Kumar & Kaushik, 2022; Chao et al., 2023; Phua
et al., 2023; Indrasari et al., 2024)
Previous studies have commonly measured CBR using
Fournier’s (1998) multidimensional relationship quality
model or Hudson et al.’s (2016) digital adaptation.
Most of these studies, however, focus on large
corporations operating in developed economies.
Research examining SMEs remains comparatively
limited, especially in contexts where digital platforms
intersect with brand equity formation. This study extends
these earlier models by applying the CBR scale to
Malaysian SMEs, thereby situating the construct within
a developing-economy environment and a social
media–driven branding landscape.
In Malaysia, SMEs are recognized for their significant
contribution to economic and social development.
Nonetheless, many SMEs continue to face challenges
in sustaining long-term brand competitiveness.
Commentators such as Dr. KK Johan, President of The
BrandLaureate, have emphasized the need for SMEs
to strengthen and modernize their branding efforts to
remain competitive in a rapidly evolving marketplace
(Bernama, 2020). The argument underscores the
importance of strategic branding in ensuring resilience
and long-term sustainability (Zutshi et al., 2021).
Furthermore, SMEs must remain adaptive and forward-
looking in response to shifting market conditions to
ensure continued brand relevance (Bernama, 2020;
Zutshi et al., 2021).
2.1 Sustainable Brand Equity
Sustainability refers to a product’s ability to function
effectively throughout its lifecycle while minimizing
adverse environmental and social impacts. In the
context of sustainable brand equity, it reflects the long-
term value added to a product because of its brand
name. This concept highlights a brand’s capacity
to maintain consumer trust and loyalty through
sustainable practices (Farquhar et al., 1989; Srivastava
& Shocker, 1991; Kumar & Kaushik, 2022). Previous
research has shown that sustainable brand equity in the
telecommunications industry is closely associated with
customer trust (Kumar & Kaushik, 2022). However, fewer
studies have examined CBR as a competitive strategy
for developing sustainable brand equity, particularly
among SMEs.
Aaker (1991) and Keller (1993) introduced foundational
perspectives on brand equity, arguing that consumer
perceptions determine the strength of a brand through
elements such as brand awareness, perceived quality,
brand associations, and brand loyalty. Building upon
these theories, sustainable brand equity encompasses
a brand’s ability to remain resilient and relevant amid
market changes. Such resilience strengthens perceived
loyalty, enhances profitability, and ensures long-term
success. Over time, focusing on these brand equity
dimensions enables companies to sustain their market
presence.
Specifically for SMEs, Ajour El Zein et al. (2019) emphasized
that sustainable brand equity plays a vital role in
helping them differentiate themselves in competitive
markets. Ishaq and Di Maria (2020) further noted that
the effectiveness of brand management is critical
in developing sustainable brand equity within SMEs.
Their study of Indonesian SMEs revealed that well-
implemented brand management is important for
achieving sustainable, long-term brand equity. Strategic
brand management practices help create positive
brand awareness and associations that strengthen
brand image and foster brand trust as key elements of

2.2 Brand Resonance as Competitive Advantage towards
Sustainable Brand Equity and Social Media Marketing
Brand resonance, often examined through the lens
of the CBR, refers to the emotional, behavioral, and
cognitive connections consumers develop with brands.
While CBR reflects the quality of the relational bond
between consumers and brands (Fournier, 1998),
brand resonance specifically captures the highest
level of brand equity, where consumers exhibit loyalty,
commitment, and active engagement (Keller, 2001).
This study adopts CBR as the primary construct, as it
encapsulates the multidimensional emotional and
behavioral ties that reflect long-term consumer–brand
connections.
Prior research demonstrates that high levels of CBR
serve as a strategic source of competitive advantage,
particularly for SMEs, by fostering customer retention,
advocacy, and long-term loyalty (Adhikari & Panda,
2020; Tanveer et al., 2021). CBR comprises elements
such as emotional attachment, self-connection,
interdependence, commitment, intimacy, and
perceptions of partner quality. These components
strengthen the psychological and behavioral
foundations of brand relationships, leading to improved
business outcomes. Easmon et al. (2019) found that
SMEs that focus on cultivating CBR enhance customer
loyalty and performance, illustrating its importance
as a competitive strategy. Accordingly, effective
management of CBR is central to developing sustainable
brand equity (Fetscherin et al., 2014; Adhikari & Panda,
2020; Jeon & Yoo, 2021).
Although CBR plays a critical role across competitive
markets, SMEs must adopt deliberate strategies to
strengthen these long-term relational bonds. Empirical
findings consistently show that CBR significantly
contributes to sustainable brand equity (Fournier &
Avery, 2011; Ramaseshan & Stein, 2014; Abd Ghani &
Tuhin, 2016; Abd Ghani & Tuhin, 2018; Adhikari & Panda,
2020; Kumar & Kaushik, 2020; Gupta & Kumar, 2022;
Wang et al., 2022; Choi & Lee, 2023). In the SMEs context,
CBR not only enhances brand equity but also translates
trust, satisfaction, and emotional engagement into
sustained competitive advantage. These outcomes
align with the view that relationship-driven assets are
central to long-term competitiveness in resource-
constrained firms (Adhikari & Panda, 2020; Jeon & Yoo,
2021). Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H1: CBR has a significant effect on sustainable brand
equity.
Strong CBR influences consumer engagement and
loyalty on Facebook. Studies by Wang et al. (2022),
Lee and Eastin (2021), and Casaló et al. (2020)
show that interactive content and emotional brand
communication enhance user engagement, brand
experience, and satisfaction. Higher interdependence
and intimacy between consumers and brands have
been found to strengthen users’ engagement and brand
loyalty on Facebook (Jin & Ryu, 2019; Phua et al., 2023).
Additional evidence from Chao et al. (2023) indicates
that higher consumer–brand interdependence on
social media encourages repeat engagement and
positive brand attitudes. Research by Hollebeek and
Macky (2019), Moisescu (2022), and Zhou et al. (2022)
also supports the idea that CBR-driven content-sharing
and responsiveness reinforce positive brand associations
and enhance online brand presence. Therefore, the
following hypothesis is proposed:
H2: CBR has a significant effect on consumers’
perceptions of SMEs’ brand-building activities on
Facebook.
CBR significantly improves brand engagement and
loyalty on Instagram. Phua et al. (2023) and Lee
and Eastin (2021) found that visual storytelling and
interactive features strengthen brand experience, trust,
and satisfaction. Interdependence and emotional
attachment on Instagram contribute to increased
user engagement and brand loyalty (Jin & Ryu, 2019;
Lou & Xie, 2021). Other studies further confirm that
strong CBR enhances users’ brand attitudes and
purchase intentions on Instagram (Lou & Yuan, 2019).
Chao et al. (2023) reported that higher consumer–
brand interdependence among SMEs fosters greater
engagement and repeat interactions. Phua et al. (2023)
similarly highlighted that personalized communication
helps brands cultivate deeper relationships and higher
engagement. Therefore, the following hypothesis is
proposed:
H3: CBR has a significant effect on consumers’
perceptions of SMEs’ brand-building activities on
Instagram.

2.3 Research Model
Figure 1 presents the research model used in this study.
The model is grounded in CBR theory and supported
by brand equity and social media marketing theory.
It focuses on the SME context and examines the role
of CBR as a competitive advantage that contributes
to sustainable brand equity, as well as customers’
perceptions of SMEs’ social media branding activities
on platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter.
Drawing on CBR pioneers such as Fournier (1998), the
model reflects the view that sustainable brand equity
is developed through anthropomorphism, in which
consumers attribute human-like qualities, emotions, and
behaviours to brands. This perspective explains how
consumers form deep and enduring relational bonds
with brands. The application of CBR to SMEs expands this
theoretical foundation, as the concept has traditionally
been examined among larger firms. Prior research has
consistently shown that CBR influences key brand equity
assets such as loyalty, engagement, and commitment
(Algesheimer et al., 2005; Hollebeek, 2011; Fetscherin
et al., 2014; Veloutsou, 2015; Abd Ghani & Tuhin, 2016;
Abd Ghani & Tuhin, 2018). Yoo et al. (2000) also argued
that other marketing strategies, such as CBR, should be
examined in addition to price, store image, distribution
intensity, advertising, and price deals to provide deeper
insights into sustainable brand equity development.
The continuous growth and accessibility of social media
technologies have prompted the examination of the
CBR within digital contexts. The role of social media
platforms (such as Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter) is
discussed to enable user-generated content and virtual
communities, which facilitate deeper engagement
between consumers and brands (Rios & Riquelme,
2008; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Algharabat et al., 2019).
Despite earlier discussions, recent researchers have
increasingly highlighted the important role of CBR as
a predictor of the role of social media marketing, such
as Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter. For example,
Algharabat et al. (2022) found that the CBR element
of emotional attachment and strong trust strengthen
FB consumer–brand engagement intentions. The CBR
dimension of love/commitment further enhances
FB relational behaviour by motivating users to stay
connected to the brand community over time and
support the brand through consistent engagement (de
Vries & Carlson, 2014).
Shan et al. (2020) found that strong CBR elements such
as emotional attachment and influencer–follower
connection predict higher engagement through
likes, comments, and story interactions in Instagram.
Otherwise, Islam and Rahman (2017) showed that
the CBR dimension of love/commitment increases
users’ intention to engage with brand content on
microblogging platforms such as Twitter. Therefore,
these studies confirm that CBR dimensions serve as
strong antecedents or predictors in influencing users’
emotional bonding, behavioral participation, and
advocacy in the social media context.

3. METHODS
3.1 Research Construct and Scale Measurement
In this study, sustainable brand equity is defined as
the long-term added value attributed to the focal
brand compared with an unbranded product, thereby
supporting continuous business growth and stability. This
definition is adapted from the theoretical arguments
presented by Yoo et al. (2000), Aaker (1991), and Keller
(1993). Yoo et al. (2000) conceptualized sustainable
brand equity as the difference in consumer choice
between a branded product and an unbranded
alternative when product attributes are comparable.
For example, consumers who purchase Radiq coffee
do so with established perceptions of the brand,
whereas an unbranded coffee lacks such recognition.
Yoo et al. (2000) further argued that effective brand
management enables firms to cultivate and sustain
brand equity over time, which supports long-term
performance. The food and beverages SME sector
was selected for this study due to its dynamic market
environment in Malaysia, where brand differentiation
and social media engagement are crucial for business
sustainability and competitive advantage.
In addition, Aaker (1991) and Keller (1993) argued that
a business that properly manages the core aspects
of brand equity assets (e.g., brand awareness, brand
association, perceived quality, and brand loyalty) will
be able to create sustainable brand equity and achieve
long-term market success. Therefore, to represent SMEs’
sustainable brand equity, this study adapts the four-item
scale measurement of overall brand equity developed
by Yoo et al. (2000), which capitalizes on the core
aspects of brand equity assets. The findings by Yoo et
al. (2000) demonstrated that the proper management
of brand equity assets enables businesses to develop
enduring or sustainable brand equity.
Meanwhile, the measurement for the social media
marketing construct was adapted from Yazdanparast
et al. (2016) and refers to consumers’ perceptions of
SMEs’ brand-building activities on Facebook, Instagram,
and Twitter. The construct includes a total of 24 items,
comprising eight items each for Facebook, Instagram,
and Twitter.
The CBR construct is measured as a six-dimensional
relationship quality framework consisting of love and
commitment, self-connection, nostalgic attachment,
interdependence, partner quality, and intimacy. These
dimensions collectively reflect the strength and durability
of consumer–brand relationships (Fournier, 1998; Hudson
et al., 2016). The measurement scale proposed by
Hudson et al. (2016) is applied in this study, consisting of
thirty items: seven items for love and commitment, six for
self-connection, three for nostalgic attachment, three
for interdependence, five for partner quality, and six for
intimacy. These items represent both consumer-to-brand
and brand-to-consumer relationship dimensions.
Fundamentally, Hudson et al.’s (2016) scale builds upon
Fournier’s (1998) conceptualization, which highlights the
importance of maintaining long-term emotional and
behavioral bonds between consumers and brands.
Prior studies have shown that strong CBR provides firms
with a competitive advantage by cultivating durable
emotional connections between consumers and
brands. This construct has received growing interest
in both academic and managerial contexts due to
its relevance for sustainable brand equity and brand
performance.
All responses related to sustainable brand equity, social
media marketing, and CBR were measured using a five-
point Likert scale ranging from one (strongly disagree) to
five (strongly agree). In addition, all research constructs
and their corresponding scale measurements are
presented in the Appendix.
3.2 Data Collection and Sampling Procedure
A quantitative research design was adopted for this
study, employing a cross-sectional survey administered
electronically across Peninsular Malaysia. The unit
of analysis consisted of consumers of SMEs’ food
and beverage brands who were also exposed to
the social media marketing activities used by these
SMEs to promote and sell their products. The food
and beverages SME sector was selected because it
represents the largest SME category in Malaysia.
Before data collection began, a list of potential
participants, including their names, email addresses,
and phone numbers was obtained after receiving their
consent to participate in the study. During participant
recruitment, the intercept mall-intercept approach was
implemented, and respondents were selected through
systematic random sampling. All participant information

4. RESULTS
The study used partial least squares structural equation
modeling (PLS-SEM) for data analysis. According to
Hair et al. (2019), PLS-SEM is an effective technique
for examining direct relationships between constructs
in research models. The analysis was conducted
following the recommendations of Hair et al. (2019),
which emphasize both measurement model evaluation
and structural path analysis. Additionally, the R² values
were interpreted to assess the model’s explanatory
power, where values of 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 are typically
considered weak, moderate, and strong, respectively
(Hair et al., 2019).

data distribution. The evaluation considered p-values,
confidence intervals, and effect sizes as complementary
evidence of relationship strength and practical
relevance (Hahn & Ang, 2017). The model tested CBR
as the primary exogenous construct and examined its
effects on sustainable brand equity and on social media
marketing, operationalized through platform indicators
(Facebook, Instagram, Twitter).

relationship with sustainable brand equity and with the
three social-media indicators, although the effect for
Twitter is comparatively weaker.

= 0.038) but weaker than the effects observed for
Facebook and Instagram. This indicates that while
CBR influences consumer responses on Twitter, the
magnitude of that influence is lower, possibly because
platform affordances (brevity, immediacy) moderate
the translation of deep relational bonds into measurable
engagement behaviours.

5. DISCUSSION
The findings of this study provide important insights into
the role of the CBR in strengthening sustainable brand
equity and enhancing consumers’ responses to social
media marketing activities on Facebook, Instagram,
and Twitter. The results confirm that CBR significantly
influences BE, indicating that strong relational bonds
between consumers and brands serve as a strategic
resource that enhances brand value. This aligns with
previous studies asserting that emotional attachment,
interdependence, and brand intimacy contribute
to stronger relational outcomes and, consequently,
sustainable brand equity (Aaker, 1991; Fournier, 1998;
Hudson et al., 2016).
The results also reinforce past evidence that CBR
supports SMEs in cultivating long-term brand loyalty and
brand competitiveness. Specifically, this study confirms
that CBR functions as a key competitive advantage
enabling SMEs to strengthen brand differentiation,
foster customer trust, and accelerate brand loyalty
(Adhikari & Panda, 2020; Dwivedi et al., 2020). The
positive impact of CBR on sustainable brand equity
demonstrates that emotional and behavioral brand
relationships provide SMEs with durable brand strength,
a crucial asset in industries where product offerings are
easily substitutable.
The role of CBR as a competitive advantage is further
highlighted through its impact on social media marketing
activities across major platforms. On Facebook, CBR
significantly enhances consumer engagement and
brand perceptions through emotionally resonant and
interactive content. On Instagram, CBR strengthens
visually driven engagement and brand connection,
making Instagram a favorable platform for SMEs seeking
to communicate brand personality and cultivate loyalty.
Similarly, the significant yet comparatively weaker effect
of CBR on Twitter suggests that although relational ties
influence user engagement, the platform’s fast-paced,
text-based environment may moderate the depth of
emotional interaction.
These findings emphasize the importance for SMEs
to strengthen CBR-driven strategies across major
social media platforms to enhance sustainable
brand equity and engagement. Despite the valuable
insights offered by this study, the results are based
solely on the Malaysian SME context and may not fully
represent other industries or geographical regions.
Future research should therefore replicate this model
across various sectors and locations to validate and
extend its theoretical relevance. From a managerial
perspective, SMEs are encouraged to actively integrate
customer feedback, personalize digital interactions,
and maintain relational consistency across platforms
to reinforce emotional connections. Overall, CBR is not
merely a branding concept but a strategic imperative
for SMEs seeking long-term competitive advantage and
sustained brand success.
6. CONCLUSION
This study examined the influence of the CBR as a
strategic asset for enhancing sustainable brand equity
and improving consumers’ perceptions of social
media marketing activities on Facebook, Instagram,
and Twitter. The findings demonstrate that CBR is a
significant predictor of sustainable brand equity and
social media engagement, highlighting its importance
as a competitive advantage for SMEs. Strong relational
bonds between consumers and brands translate into
improved brand loyalty, differentiation, and long-term
growth.
The study contributes to the literature by validating
the role of CBR in the SME context, an area previously
underexplored compared to larger firms. It extends
existing theories by confirming that relational constructs
such as emotional attachment, interdependence, and
perceived partner quality are central to developing
sustainable brand equity in digitally competitive
environments. The research model also demonstrates
that platform-specific social media marketing responses
vary according to the strength of consumer–brand ties,
reflecting differences in platform affordances.
Despite these contributions, the study is limited to
Malaysian SMEs within the food and beverages sector.
Future research should explore the applicability of
CBR across different industries, cultural contexts, and
social media environments. Additionally, incorporating
moderating or mediating variables such as brand
authenticity, brand trust, or customer experience may
enrich understanding of the mechanisms linking CBR to
sustainable brand outcomes.
Overall, this study underscores CBR as a strategic
branding resource for SMEs, offering theoretical and
practical implications that support effective digital
branding, customer loyalty, and sustainable business
development.
