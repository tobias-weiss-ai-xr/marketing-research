How can conscientious brands best influence consumers’ sustainable behaviours? The role of
sustainable brand equity in consumer responses to carbon offsetting messages

Abstract
Conscientious brands are emerging as key drivers of sustainable consumption. With
limited research on effectively communicating conscientious brands and nudging
consumers to adopt sustainable behaviours, this research explores how conscientious
brands can best influence consumers' sustainable actions by analysing the impact of
sustainable brand  equity in consumer responses to carbon offsetting  messages. Two
online experiments were conducted. Study 1 (n=202) examined the interaction effect of
message depth and sustainable brand equity on outcome expectations and subsequent
carbon offset  purchase. Results indicated that deep claims are significantly more
effective in raising consumer outcome expectation  and subsequent purchase intention.
Sustainable brand  equity strengthens the impact of deep and shallow claims while
decreasing the effectiveness gap between the two claim types . Study 2 (n= 390)
examined the moderating effect of mean -end visuals on the relationship between
message depth and outcome expectation. Results confirmed the interaction effects that
mean-visual enhances the impact of deep claim , whereas end-visual strengthens the
effect of shallow claim.  This paper expands the existing literature on conscientious
brands, focusing specifically on articulating conscientious value proposition that can
effectively motivate consumers to engage in sustainable behaviours.

Keywords: conscientious brands, brand equity, sustainable consumer behaviour,
voluntary carbon offsetting, air travel VCO

Introduction
With the increasing complexity of the business world, companies intentionally or unintentionally
behave in irresponsible business practices, product scandals, and corporate misconduct to seek profit
maximisation that leads to unfavourable consequences and negative impacts on different stakeholders
(Habersang et al., 2018; Iborra & Riera, 2022) . In particular, consumers increasingly pay attention to
organisations' ethical standards and corporate marketing activities (Balmer et al., 2011). In response to
the potential challenges, scholars have proposed corporate marketing, which has an explicit ethical,
corporate social responsibility (CSR), and stakeholder management (Balmer et al., 2007; Balmer et al.,
2011). There has been a growing trend to examine corporate marketing over the past decades (Balmer
et al., 2011; Powell, 2011) . This research field has mainly focused on ethical or socially responsible
marketing on consumer choice (Carrigan & Attalla, 2001; Carrington et al., 2021; Shaw & Shiu, 2002),
green brand image (Bashir et al., 2020; Kang & Hur, 2012), and green purchase intentions (Luchs et al.,
2010; Majeed et al., 2022).
Another related stream of corporate marketing literature is corporate branding (Brunk, 2010a;
Palazzo & Basu, 2007). The research on ethics in brand management is diverse, including theoretical
(Brunk, 2010b; Fan, 2005) and empirical studies (Singh et al., 2012; Story & Hess, 2010). In particular,
conscientious brands embed a moral stance and are emerging as key drivers of corporate marketing,
sustainable consumption, and CSR as deliberative acts.  Companies integrate CSR as a proactive
approach to develop conscientious brands with several benefits, such as sustainable brand equity (Ishaq
& Di Maria, 2020), while other companies adopt CSR as a passive reaction in response to stakeholder
pressure to mitigate potential risks and adverse outcomes (Walsh & Beatty, 2007) . This remains
sceptical of the legitimacy of CSR. Some critical scholars argue that CSR is impression management,
greenwashing, and camouflage (Kurpierz & Smith, 2020; Leung & Snell, 2021; Merkl‐Davies et al.,
2011). This has led to concerns about the motivations and rationales of CSR practices, which may be
manipulative and insincere (Pope & Wæraas, 2016).
Thus, conscientious brands need to go beyond CSR, integrate this as an essential element of
corporate brand identity, and have ongoing stakeholder dialogue to create brand co-creation as the core

branding ingredient, position, and strategy (Iglesias et al., 2019; Ind & Horlings, 2016). Conscientious
brands stem from the company’s core values and corporate culture and are driven by an internal moral
compass (Iglesias & Ind, 2020) . Conscientious brand is a growing field in branding management
research. Most previous studies on conscientious brand tend to be conceptual (Abratt & Kleyn, 2023;
Biedenbach & Biedenbach, 2022; Iglesias & Ind, 2020; Ind & Ryder, 2011a; Vallaster & Lechner, 2022)
and qualitative studies examining the role of business partners in expanding conscientious brand in
value chain, brand activism, and developing new innovative capabilities (Iglesias et al., 2023; Kapitan
et al., 2022; Olsen & Peretz, 2011) . For quantitative studies, scholars find that conscientious brands
indicate those brands whose ethical values are integrated in the business strategy and extend to the
supply chain (Rindell et al., 2011) , while other scholars find that conscientious brand is an integral
dimension in the manufacturer-supplier relationship (Hutchinson et al., 2013). In addition, most extant
conscientious brand studies focus on B2B conscientious brands (Abratt & Kleyn, 2023; Iglesias & Ind,
2020; Iglesias et al., 2023; Kapitan et al., 2022; Vallaster & Lechner, 2022) . However, there is still a
limited empirical study examining B2C conscientious brands.
Conscientious brands could strengthen their brand image and have a positive indirect influence
on their sustainable brand equity (Iglesias et al., 2019; Sierra et al., 2017) . This indicates a brand’s
commitment to ethical, social, and environmental sustainability and its ability to positively influence
consumer perceptions and preferences (Ishaq & Di Maria, 2020). Thus, sustainable brand equity is an
important construct in brand management which is a relational market -based asset via ongoing
interactions and long -term relationships between corporate brands and brand credibility with their
customers (Davcik et al., 2015; Hur et al., 2014). Brand image is an antecedent of brand equity (Kim et
al., 2012). Thus, brands perceived as sustainable have a high possibility of enforcing their sustainable
brand equity (Iglesias et al., 2019). Several studies have widely investigated the relationship between
CSR and brand equity (Fatma et al., 2015; Guzman & Davis, 2017; Hur et al., 2014; Torres et al., 2012).
However, there is a paucity of previous studies t hat examine the relationship between conscientious
brand and sustainable brand equity to enhance the effectiveness of brand communication (Iglesias et al.,
2019; Ind & Iglesias, 2022c) . Considering the limited empirical research on the communication
strategies of conscientious brands, the role of sustainable brand  equity, and the effectiveness of

encouraging consumers to enhance sustainable purchase intention, particularly in the service sector
(Alwi et al., 2017; Balmer et al., 2007; Balmer et al., 2011; Iglesias et al., 2023; Ind & Iglesias, 2022b).
Within this research context, it is essential to study B2C conscientious brands within the service
sector for three main reasons. First, the service sector accounts for over 66 percent of global GDP and
50 percent of global employment. This sector includes young workers, females, and entrepreneurs in
micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises (MSMEs) (WB & WTO, 2023). This sector significantly
impacts responsible consumption behaviour more  than other sectors. Second, given the nature of
services (i.e., heterogeneous, inseparable and perishable), service brands interact more directly with
consumers daily (e.g., clothing, food, housing, and transportation) than other sectors to shape their
sustainable behaviours and lifestyles (Iglesias et al., 2019) . Carbon emissions from the service sector
increased twofold when imported inputs were counted on a consumption basis (Roberts et al., 2021) .
The service sector contributes the most to pollution in high -income countries, while entrepreneurship
in pollution is much higher in middle - and low -income countries (Omri, 2018) . Thus, economic
activities have increasingly challenged environmental sustainability in the service sectors (Mulder et
al., 2014). Third, growing demands and opportunities on brand equity reflect consumers’ increasing
concerns for ethical, social, and environmental issues (Baalbaki & Guzmán, 2016) . Previous studies
show that the service sector relies heavily on brand reputation and trust rather than ethical corporate
marketing and conscientious brands (Balmer et al., 2011) . Thus, conscientious branding offers an
opportunity to reinforce this trust by providing products and services with ethical and sustainable
practices.
To address the above research gaps, this study investigates how conscientious brands can
influence consumers' sustainable actions by analysing the impact of sustainable brand  equity in
consumer responses to carbon offsetting messages. Studying carbon offsetting is particularly important
in that governments and regulatory bodies are increasingly pressuring businesses to implement carbon
emissions reduction strategies, such as c arbon offsetting, in their business operation to meet climate
change targets due t o the Paris Agreement in 2015. In addition, one of the SDGs (goal 12) is to
encourage individuals to adopt more sustainable lifestyles, choose products with lower negative

5

environmental impacts, and reduce their carbon footprint (The United Nations, 2024) . In particular,
China is one of the countries with the highest carbon emissions, and its target to mitigate carbon
emissions aligns with China’s dual carbon goals of reaching the peak carbon emission by 2030 and
carbon neutrality by 2060 (Yang et al., 2024) . Thus, companies have increasingly placed a greater
importance on carbon -neutral or carbon -offsetting strategies. Consumers are increasingly becoming
aware of not only sustainable consumption and ethical consumerism but also consider the impact of
their c onsumption choices concerning environmental and social aspects (Carrington et al., 2021;
Caruana et al., 2016; Shaw & Shiu, 2002). Thus, this study shows a timely and crucial research topic.
This study extends both conscientious brands and business ethics literature in the service sector
in two ways (Markovic et al., 2018; Sierra et al., 2017). First, this study advances the extant literature
by offering two empirical experiments from the service sector to examine how brands articulate their
conscientious values and carbon offsetting initiatives to encourage consumers to purchase carbon
offsets for air travel voluntarily (Abratt & Kleyn, 2023; Hutchinson et al., 2013; Rindell et al., 2011) .
Second, this study provides empirical evidence of a company that shows genuine efforts in articulating
conscientious value propositions and the best practices to effectively use different communication
strategies of conscientious brands, the role of sustainable brand  equity, and the effectiveness in
encouraging consumers to enhance sustainable purchase intention, particularly in the service sector
(Alwi et al., 2017; Balmer et al., 2007; Balmer et al., 2011; Iglesias et al., 2023; Ind & Iglesias, 2022b).
Understanding how customers perceive conscientious brands provides deeper insights into their
responses to carbon offsetting purchase intention, which makes a difference in improving environmental
sustainability.

Theoretical background and hypothesis development
Conscientious brands
Ind and Ryder (2011b) introduced the concept of conscientious brands as part of a developing trend in
brand management, transitioning from corporate social responsibility (CSR) to a more conscientious
approach. The idea of conscientious brands emphasises the importance of considering all stakeholders

6

(e.g., business partners, employees, consumers, and local communities) and their long -term benefits
rather than  just the short -term gains of the companies and  shareholders. The rising awareness of
environmental and social issues has led to a growing demand for businesses to operate ethically and
sustainably, fuelling  the emergence of conscientious brands  (Bhagwat et al., 2020; Ind & Iglesias,
2022b). This concept draws from the psychological trait of conscientiousness, which involves self -
discipline and deliberation, translating it into organisational behaviour that prioritises long-term societal
well-being alongside business goals (Abratt & Kleyn, 2023; Vallaster & Lechner, 2022). While terms
such as "ethical," "moral," and "responsible" brands have overlapping meanings, "conscientious brands"
extend further by focusing on proactive measures, inclusivity of stakeholders, and purpose -driven
actions aimed at fostering positive societ al and environmental change (Iglesias & Ind, 2020) .
Conscientious brands are distinguished by their proactive and systematic commitment to incorporating
ethical, social, and environmental considerations into all aspects of their operations. This approach is
driven by diligent self -regulation and a focus on continuous improvement. The stakeholder-inclusive
approach asserts that brands should operate as cooperative enterprises where employees, customers,
communities, and the environment are interdependent, rather than merely being ethical or responsible
to other stakeholders (Abratt & Kleyn, 2023; Biedenbach & Biedenbach, 2022; Hutchinson et al., 2013;
Iglesias & Ind, 2020; Ind & Iglesias, 2022c; Kapitan et al., 2022; Vallaster & Lechner, 2022). Expanding
on the conventional sustainability concept in branding, which suggests that brands operate ethically,
morally, or responsibly, Iglesias and Ind (2020) proposed that brands, similar to individuals, can possess
moral agency and opt to act in ways that positively impact the world.  Conscientiousness refers to a
brand's commitment to integrating ethical principles, responsibility, and morality into its operations
through proactive vigilance, broadening the focus towards value  co-creation. Ethical brands focus on
fair practices and avoiding harm, while conscientious brands go further by embedding ethical reflection
into daily processes (e.g., transparent supply  chain audits) and anticipating unintended consequences
(Carroll, 2016; Golob et al., 2022) . Moral brands are characteri sed by their commitment to a set of
ethical principles grounded in cultural, religious, or philosophical beliefs that inform their business
decisions. In contrast, conscientious brands integrate a profound sense of moral responsibility into the
strategic alignment with business partners, rather than adhering to static moral codes  (France et al.,

7

2024; Iglesias et al., 2023). Responsible brands are frequently associated with CSR initiatives, however,
conscientious brands go beyond isolated CSR activities by integrating responsibility into their
governance structures (Ind, 2023).
While organisations have expanded in size and influence , corporate brands are expected to
balance the needs of people, the planet, and profit to respond to t he sustainability agenda. Therefore,
conscientious brands are expected to  look beyond short -term operational viability and profitability,
committing instead to integrating conscience into their actions and adopting a broader approach to value
creation with various stakeholders to achieve long -term transformative change and benefits for all
(Iglesias & Ind, 2020). In doing so, brands should possess morality, autonomy, the commitment to make
ethical choices, and the capability to engage in actions that positively impact the world  (Ind & Iglesias,
2022c). To provide a guiding framework for genuine conscientious brands, Abratt and Kleyn (2023)
posited to identify and evaluate five dimensions: purpose, ethics, stakeholder co-creation, sustainability,
and corporate social responsibility (CSR). First, the purpose  of a conscientious brand  should be
explicitly defined and communicated.  The purpose should be aligned with  the company's strateg ic
objectives and serve as a guiding principle for its decision -making processes. Thus, a conscientious
brand must embrace social and environmental responsibilities that extend beyond mere compliance with
legal and ethical standards, thereby adopting a higher level of vo luntary accountability in addressing
societal and environmental challenges (Olsen & Peretz, 2011). Second, ethical leadership is pivotal in
fostering a culture of ethical conduct within the organisation. The company should seek to integrate
ethical practices throughout the organisation. Third, developing and managing  conscientious brands
should occur through close collaboration and co-creation with stakeholders. It is vital to reconcile and
embed stakeholder perspectives to establish and communicate sustainable and responsible behaviours,
thereby achieving a valuable, significant, and lasting positive impact on those stakeholders (Iglesias &
Ind, 2020). Fourth, sustainable business practices must be central to  the corporate brand, proactively
shaping best practices in environmental, social, and governance. Last, CSR initiatives should transcend
mere philanthropy  and be firmly integrated into the company's operations and communications in
alignment with the corporate brand purpose.

 As brands work towards being conscientious in their management, operations, and
collaborations, it is equally essential for them to enhance their understanding of communicating a
conscientious value proposition and motivate consumers to embrace more responsible and sustainable
behaviours. Considering the limited research on the communication strategies of conscientious brands
and their effectiveness in encouraging consumers to adopt responsible and sustainable behaviours (Ind
& Iglesias, 2022b), this paper investigates how brands articulate their conscientious values and carbon
offsetting initiatives to encourage consumers to purchase carbon offsets for air travel voluntarily.

Voluntary carbon offsetting
V oluntary carbon offsetting (VCO) purchase for air travel consumers refers to the option for them to
pay an additional fee to offset the carbon emissions generated by their flight (Gössling et al., 2007). By
purchasing carbon credits, individuals compensate for their emissions from air travel through schemes
that reduce or eliminate greenhouse gas emissions. Systematic literature reviews (Cordes et al., 2024;
de Mello, 2024)  revealed that e xtensive studies have identif ied factors influencing voluntary carbon
offset purchases among consumers . Some factors are closely related to consumers' characteristics,
including demographics, travel habits, and values and beliefs about climate change (Cordes et al., 2024).
Some factors are closely related to the practices of airline brands, including communication,
information, awareness, pricing, transparency, and perceived effectiveness of the VCO scheme (Cordes
et al., 2024; de Mello, 2024).
 Although prior studies have identified the roles airline brands can play in motivating consumers
to participate in carbon offsetting, Guix et al. (2022)  found that the low awareness of VCO and a
significant portion of the aviation industry's misleading claims raise critical questions about the true
impact on environmental sustainability.  While consumers suspect that airline brands might be using
VCO schemes as a marketing tactic , essentially greenwashing their image  rather than genuinely
committing to sustainable practices , consumers who are genuinely interested in environmental
preservation will hesitate to participate in VCO (de Mello, 2024; Gössling et al., 2007) . However,
limited studies have explored how airline brands can influence consumers' sustainable behaviours by
effectively communicating their brand s’ conscientiousness and VCO scheme effectiveness in

9

addressing climate issues (de Mello, 2024; Ind & Iglesias, 2022a). Many airline brands offer VCO as a
sustainable and responsible option to offset the greenhouse gas emissions of consumers’ air travel when
they purchase flight tickets. However, there is a lack of research exploring how to communicate brand
authenticity in promoting sustainability, particularly regarding the VCO scheme, and how this
communication affects consumers' adoption of similar values and behaviours by emphasising the
brand’s commitment to sustainability.

Carbon offsetting communication and outcome expectation
Conscientious brands emphasise the importance of educating consumers about sustainability because
well-informed consumers are more inclined to make sustainable choices (Golob et al., 2022). Carbon
offsetting messages communicate the possibility for air travellers to neutralise the environmental impact
of their flight travel by investing in projects that reduce greenhouse gas emissions elsewhere. The
message claims are central to forming outcome expectation, which refers to an individual's belief
regarding the probable consequences of their sustainable actions associated with a brand’s messages
(Lee et al., 2011) . Carbon offset communication  affects consumers' perceptions  of how effectively
purchasing offsets reduces their environmental impact. When carbon offset promotions articulate clear
and compelling claims about positive outcomes, consumers are more likely to develop a favourable
outcome expectation.  Expectancy-Value Theory (EVT) suggests that individuals are driven to
participate in behaviours they anticipate will result in favourable outcomes (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000).
The Expectancy-Value Theory suggests that an individual's motivation to engage in pro-environmental
behaviour is influenced by both expectancy and value (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000): expectancy refers to
the belief that one can successfully perform the behaviour (self-efficacy) and that the behaviour will
result in a desired outcome (outcome expectancy); value pertains to the perceived importance, relevance,
or attractiveness of the outcome associated with the behaviour. This theory is frequently employed in
research on consumer green behaviour, as it presumes that consumer actions are based on rational
decision-making (Chen, 2010; Rozenkowska, 2023) . In this study examining how carbon offsetting
communication influences consumers’ outcome expectations, EVT provides a robust theoretical lens to
analyse how the VCO messages and visuals shape beliefs about carbon offsetting’s expectancy  and

10

resonate with consumers’ personal values to drive behavioural intent. Despite that, according to EVT,
consumers who hold high outcome expectation regarding the effectiveness of carbon offsetting are more
inclined to engage in this practice.  Rozenkowska (2023)  observed that a notable limitation in the
application of Expectancy-Value Theory (EVT) is perceived behavioural control, or self-efficacy, which
has shown varied effects on outcome behaviours across multiple studies.  In this study aimed at
understanding the interaction effects of VCO messages and visuals on consumers' VCO purchases,
limitations of EVT can be excluded from the empirical model since consumers merely engage in
clicking actions on digital purchase platforms when purchasing air tickets.
Recent studies examining the message strategies employed to enhance VCO purchases have
demonstrated that shorter messages are more effective in capturing attention and increasing scheme
awareness compared to longer, more complex ones (Babakhani et al., 2019). Furthermore, loss-framed
messages prove to be more impactful than gain-framed ones (Ropret Homar & Knežević Cvelbar, 2023).
Utilising psychological distance framing (both spatial and temporal) can enhance the persuasiveness of
messages (Zhang et al., 2019) . However, while framing that invokes feelings of guilt and shame can
elicit strong emotional responses from consumers, it does not necessarily translate into behavioural
change (Mkono & Hughes, 2020). Regardless of framing or appeals used, prior research focused mainly
on message content that highlights the environmental benefits and positive impact on climate change
mitigation or consumers' social responsibility and ethical duty to offset their carbon footprint. There is
a lack of focus on how incorporating conscientious brand values in the communication strategy can
enhance consumer engagement with carbon offsets.
 Meanwhile, g reen advertising research found that deep and substantive claims are more
effective than shallow environmental claims in fostering green purchase intentions (Shen et al., 2024).
Deep claims demonstrate a brand’s genuine commitment and actions toward environmental protection
or harm reduction (Chan, 2000). These substantive claims provide more detailed information about a
brand’s pro-environment attributes or initiatives (e.g., how the carbon offsetting amount is calculated,
where the offsetting payment will be invested, and who will be responsible for the implementation of
carbon offsetting). In contrast, shallow claims refer to superficial pro-environmental messages used to

11

create an impression of having lesser harmful or even beneficial ecological impacts without providing
any specific, tangible, or substantiated information to support the claims (Chan, 2000).
Although providing comprehensive explanations of carbon offset projects (such as their
operational mechanisms, benefits, and underlying scientific principles) along with clear communication
regarding fund allocation and effectiveness is beneficial, lengthy  messages that attempt to convey
excessive information are often ineffective (Babakhani et al., 2019). Moreover, online ticket purchasing
has become prevalent in the digital age. Communication  regarding VCO is integrated as an optional
add-on during the flight ticket purchasing process, alongside other optional items such as priority check-
in, additional luggage, or seat selection . Thus, many VCO promotional messages are short and
straightforward (e.g., add $5 to offset the CO 2 emissions of your trip ) but ineffective in influencing
consumer actions. When conscientious airline brands encourage consumers to invest in carbon offsets
alongside other profit -driven options, brands must find a balance between achieving short -term
profitability and promoting long -term sustainability advocacy. Hence, this study investigates the
message depth needed to effectively communicate the VCO scheme, as informed consumers are more
likely to participate. Such engagement strengthens brand loyalty and motivates consumers to embrace
sustainable behaviours that align with the brand's mission (Biedenbach & Biedenbach, 2022). Therefore,
we hypothesise that
H1: A deep VCO claim is significantly more effective than a shallow claim in affecting
outcome expectation.
H2: Outcome expectation mediates the relationship between an effective message claim and
consumers’ VCO purchase.

Sustainable brand equity
Sustainability has recently emerged as a critical dimension of brand equity, reflecting
consumers' increasing concern for environmental and social issues (Baalbaki & Guzmán, 2016) .
Sustainable brand equity is the value derived from a brand’s adherence to sustainable practices, which
include environmental conservation, ethical labour practices, and social responsibility. It represents a
brand’s commitment to sustainable development and ability to posi tively influence consumer

12

perceptions and preferences (Ishaq & Di Maria, 2020). Conscientious brands prioritise the transparent
sharing of sustainable practices and actively involve stakeholders in co-creating sustainable business
initiatives, enhancing consumers’ awareness and positively influencing sustainable behaviours (Golob
et al., 2022). Thus, given the increasing importance of sustainability in consumer decision-making, it is
plausible that sustainable brand  equity could negatively moderate the relationship between VCO
messages and outcome expectation. Specifically, when a brand is perceived as emphasising its value
proposition, its commitment to sustainability and its outreach efforts to engage with stakeholders , the
effectiveness of VCO messages in shaping positive outcome expectation may be enhanced (Baalbaki &
Guzmán, 2016). Hence, the differentiated impact of deep versus shallow claims will be diminished as
consumers demonstrate greater trust in the brand's conscientiousness. This is because consumers are
more likely to trust and value messages from brands that they perceive as genuinely committed to
sustainability. Brand conscientiousness, ethicality, and sustainability perceptions can significantly
enhance sustainable brand equity, leading to better consumer evaluations and expectations regarding
the brand's sustainable practices  (Iglesias et al., 2019; Ind & Iglesias, 2022c) . This relationship
underscores the importance of integrating sustainability into brand equity frameworks and highlights
the potential for conscientious brands to enhance the effectiveness of brand communications. Customers
tend to trust messages delivered by brand s with substantial sustainability equity (Kang & Hur, 2012)
and the influence of message depth would become less critical. Hence, we hypothesise that:
H3: Sustainable brand  equity negatively moderates the relationship between the VCO
message and the outcome expectation of the VCO scheme.

In addition to the perceived equity of a known brand, the visual content in brand communication
also contributes to conveying the brand's sustainability equity to consumers. Empirical research
indicates that incorporating visuals with textual environmental claims leads to more positive advertising
responses and a more favourable perception of the brand's environmental initiatives compared to using
solely textual content (Babakhani et al., 2019; Xue & Muralidharan, 2015) . The execution of visual
content plays a crucial role in shaping audiences' attitudes and behaviours. However, research indicates
that misusing visual cues in green communication risks  being perceived as greenwashing rather than

13

providing consumers with authentic sustainable options (Schmuck, Matthes, & Naderer, 2018) .
Henceforth, t he strategic use of visual content in VCO communication can be a powerful tool for
conscientious brands to convey their sustainability equity and influence consumer perceptions and
behaviours.
Research revealed that c omplex information benefits from visual aids that facilitate
understanding (Miniard et al., 1991; Shen et al., 2024; Xue & Muralidharan, 2015) . Mean-focused
visuals depict the process or actions involved in carbon offset projects, such as images of tree planting,
renewable energy installations, or community engagement activities, illustrating the tangible steps taken
to achieve carbon offsets. Hence, mean-focused visuals can “visualise” deep VCO claims to enhance
consumers' understanding of the specific actions involved in carbon offset projects, potentially
increasing their outcome expectation. On the other hand, simple information may not require the same
level of processing support (Miniard et al., 1991; Shen et al., 2024; Xue & Muralidharan, 2015) . End-
focused visuals showcase the outcomes or benefits of carbon offset projects, such as images of forests,
healthy communities, or wildlife thriving in protected areas. These visuals aim to convey the positive
end results of carbon offset purchases.  By aligning visual elements with environmental values and
transparency conveyed in the textual claims, we assume that brands can effectively communicate their
commitment to sustainability and foster consumer engagement in VCO schemes. Thus, we hypothesise
that there will be a significant interaction effect between message depth and visual placement on
outcome expectation, specifically:
H4a: Mean-visual will be more effective than end-visual at enhancing outcome expectation for
deep messages.
H4b: End-visual will be more effective than mean-visual at enhancing outcome expectation for
shallow messages.

Methods and results
Overview of studies
Two experimental studies were conducted to examine the impact of message depth, visual content and
sustainable brand equity on outcome expectations and subsequent carbon offset purchases. Study 1

14

focused on the interaction between message depth (shallow vs. deep) and sustainable brand equity. It
investigated how these factors influence consumers’ expectations of carbon offset outcomes and their
willingness to pay for carbon offsets. Study 2 replicated study 1 and extended th e framework by
exploring the moderating role of mean -end visuals in shaping consumers ’ outcome expectations and
VCO purchase. Replicating Study 1 in the same context using similar scales allows us to confirm the
consistency of th e results across two different  samples and verify the  validity of the relationships
observed. Both studies utilised a panel service provided by a Chinese marketing research agency which
covers 300 million Chinese customers. The agency’s sample is diverse, representative, and comparable
to national benchmarks (Del Ponte et al., 2024). Table 1 presents a sample composition of Studies 1 and
2.
We compared our samples’ composition with the recent statistics published by the Tourism
Research Centre of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences (Song et al., 2024). The gender distribution
in both Study 1 and Study 2 closely aligns with national travel statistics, which report 44.9% male and
55.1% female travellers. The age distribution of our participants also reflects the broader trend of a
younger travel market in China. In both studies, most respondents were between 18 and 40 years old .
This is consistent with national data showing that younger generations, particularly those born after
1995 (approximately under 30 years old), have the highest travel frequency, fol lowed by those born
between 1981 and 1994 (approximately aged 30 to 45). In contrast, older generations (aged 45 and
above) travel less frequently. The higher representation of younger respondents in our sample further
supports the external validity of our  study because it aligns with the demographic profile of active
travellers in China.
As this research aims to examine the effects of message depth, visual contents, and sustainable
brand equity, four control variables  (i.e. environmental self -identity, biospheric value, ascribed
responsibility, and brand attitude ) were included as covariates  in our analys es. Environmental self-
identity, biospheric value, and ascribed responsibility capture travellers’ pre-existing psychological
tendencies toward pro-environmental behaviour (Han, 2015; Ribeiro et al., 2023). Controlling for these
factors ensures that any observed differences in participants' responses are driven by the experimental
manipulations rather than their inherent environmental motivations. Furthermore, brand attitude was

Study 1
Design, participants, stimuli, and procedures
Study 1 aimed to examine the interaction effect of message depth and sustainable brand equity on
outcome expectation and how these expectations subsequently influence the purchase of carbon offsets.
As described above, d ata was collected through the research agency which randomly selected
participants from its nationwide panel. A total of 202 responses were collected (46% male, 54% Female;
age ranged from 18 to 60). Participants were randomly assigned to one of the two experimental
conditions (i.e., shallow and deep messages)  as illustrated in Figure 1 . Samples for each of the two
conditions were 100 and 102 respectively. After reading the assigned scenario, participants were asked
whether they would be willing to add an additional payment for carbon offset when purchasing a flight
ticket. They then completed a questionnaire that included items related to sustainable brand equity,
outcome expectation, carbon offset purchase, demographic questions,  and manipulation checks. They
were also asked to answer questions regarding the four control variables: enviro nmental self-identity,
biospheric value, ascribed responsibility, and brand attitude.

Measures
The measurement items for all variables were ad opted from validated scales used in previous studies.
Table 2 shows the measurement items used in the questionnaire and their scale reliabilities. Outcome
expectation  (Allison et al., 2021) was measured by three items. An example of the measurement items
is, “It makes a difference to the environment whether I pay for carbon offsetting for my air travel ”.
Sustainable brand equity, adopted from the brand equity scale from Baalbaki and Guzmán (2016) and
perceived brand ethicality from Iglesias et al. (2019), was measured by five items. An example of the

16

items is, “The brand is a socially responsible brand”. Carbon offset purchase intention was measured
by three items adopted from Ajzen (1991). An example of the items is, “I am willing to pay for carbon
offsetting when travelling by air”. The control variables, environmental self-identity (Van der Werff et
al., 2014), biospheric value (Steg et al., 2012), ascribed responsibility (Verma et al., 2019), and brand
attitude (MacKenzie & Lutz, 1989) were measured using established scales. All variables except brand
attitude were measured using a 7 -point Likert scale (1 —totally disagree to 7 —totally agree). Brand
attitude toward brand posts was measured using a semantic differential scale.

Results
Manipulation Checks
One-way ANOV A was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the experimental manipulations.
Respondents in the shallow-message condition rated the information provided in the scenario as more
superficial compared to those in the deep-message condition (MShallow = 5.74, MDeep = 2.20, F(1, 200) =
1160.26, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.853). On the other hand, respondents in the deep -message condition rated
the information as more specific and detailed in addressing environmental issues compared to those in
the shallow-message condition (M Shallow = 2.03, M Deep = 5.98, F(1, 200) = 1474.67 , p < 0.001, η2 =
0.881). Overall, the manipulations were successful.

Hypothesis Testing
A one-way ANCOV A was conducted to test the impact of shallow vs. deep messages on outcome
expectations, with the four control variables (i.e., environmental identity, environmental value, ascribed
responsibility and brand attitudes) included as covariates in the analysis. The results showed that the
impact of message depth on outcome expectations was significant in which deep message elicited a
higher level of outcome expectations (MShallow = 4.974, MDeep = 5.680, F(1,196) = 21.900, p < 0.001, ηp2
= 0.101) (Figure 2).
To test hypotheses H1 to H3 presented in the conceptual framework, we conducted a moderated
mediation analysis using PROCESS MACRO v4.2 Model 7 (Hayes, 2018), with a 95% confidence
interval and 10,000 bootstrap samples. In the analysis, message depth (0 = shallow, 1 = deep) was

17

included as an independent variable, outcome expectations as mediators, carbon offset purchase as the
dependent variable and sustainable brand  equity as the moderator . The four control variables, i.e.
environmental self-identity, biospheric value, ascribed responsibility, and brand attitude, were included
as covariates in our analyses. The index of moderated mediation was significant (Index = -.0710, SE
= .0397, CI = [-.1601, -.0054]), indicating that the effect of message depth on carbon offset purchases
through outcome expectations depends on sustainable brand equity.
Specifically, the results showed a significant positive relationship between message depth and
outcome expectations ( β = 3.4755, SE = .9734, p < 0.001, CI = [1.5558, 5.3953]) and the positive
coefficient indicated that the deep message elicited a higher level of outcome expectations than the
shallow message. Thus , H1 was supported. The relationship between sustainable brand  equity and
outcome expectations was also significant ( β = .9404, SE = .1398, p < 0.0001, CI = [.6646, 1.2163]).
The interaction effect of message depth and sustainable brand equity on outcome expectations was

supported. As shown in Figure 3, the impact of message depth attenuated at high levels of sustainable
brand equity. The effect of outcome expectations on carbon offset purchase was also significant ( β
= .1436, SE = .0518, p < 0.01, CI = [.0414, .2458]) and this supported H2.

Discussion
This study examined the impact of message depth on outcome expectations and , subsequently,
customers’ willingness to purchase VCO. The findings from both studies provided convergent empirical
evidence that a deep VCO claim is more effective in enhancing customers’ expectations of the positive
outcomes generated by VCO and, therefore, increasing willingness to pay for VCO. Deep claim is often
found to be more effective in fostering green purchase behaviours (Shen et al., 2024) . This can be
explained by the customers’ need for more detailed information about the brands’ pro -environmental
initiatives (Chan, 2000) . Substantive and factual claims also play important roles in providing
information that serves the best interests of customers (Polonsky et al., 2024).
Furthermore, both studies examined the interaction effect of message depth and sustainable
brand equity on outcome expectations. The findings demonstrated that sustainable brand  equity
negatively moderates the relationship between message depth and outcome expectations. In other words,
the importance of message depth diminishes when customers perceive a high level of sustainable brand
equity. Customers tend to trust messages delivered by brands with strong sustainability equity (Bashir
et al., 2020; Kang & Hur, 2012). Therefore, they are more likely to commit to brands with a strong and
positive green image (Majeed et al., 2022), regardless of whether the brand uses a deep or shallow VCO
claim.
In Study 2, the findings revealed the role of mean-end visual presentation in shaping consumer
perceptions of the VCO outcomes. Specifically, the interaction effect of message depth and mean-end
visual showed that deep-mean and shallow-end combinations effectively enhance outcome expectations
and subsequently increase  VCO purchases.  This suggests that mean-visuals depicting the carbon
offsetting process can strengthen the impact of deep messages . In contrast, end -visuals highlighting
environmental outcomes are more effective when paired with shallow messages. This provides novel
insights that deep and shallow VCO claims can also effectively encourage  sustainable behaviours
(Polonsky et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024) . Specifically, customers require detailed and substantive

21

information on how their VCO spending is utilised (mean-visual), but they respond well to more
straightforward messages regarding the positive environmental outcomes of their purchase (end-visual).

Theoretical implications
This research contributes to the theoretical understanding of how conscientious brands influence
consumer behaviour, particularly in the context of sustainable actions such as purchasing carbon offsets.
Firstly, while most extant conscientious brand studies focus on corporate brand or B2B brand
conscientiousness (Abratt & Kleyn, 2023; Iglesias & Ind, 2020; Iglesias et al., 2023; Kapitan et al.,
2022; Vallaster & Lechner, 2022) , this study extends the literature on brand conscientiousness by
demonstrating its significant impact on consumer willingness to engage in sustainable behaviours.
Consumers view conscientious brands as actively pursuing ethical, socially responsible, and
environmentally sustainable practices, often exceeding legal requirements and showing a genuine
commitment to stakeholders and the planet. By integrating the Expectancy -V alue Theory (EVT), this
study highlights the nuanced roles of brand conscientiousness in raising consumers’ outcome
expectations on sustainable actions and subsequent behaviours. The study reveals that conscientious
brands, which actively pursue ethical, socially responsible, and environmentally sustainable practices,
can significantly influence consumer behaviour by raising their outcome expectations. This aligns with
EVT, which suggests that individuals are motivated to engage in behaviours they anticipate will result
in favourable outcomes. Furthermore, the study demonstrates how effective EVT can be used to explain
consumers’ engagement in sustainable behaviours in the context of conscientious branding. It suggests
that the importance of message depth diminishes when consumers perceive a high level of sustainable
brand equity. This indicates that consumers tend to trust messages delivered by brands with strong
sustainability equity, regardless of whether the brand uses deep or shallow claims. This finding suggests
that outcome expectations, central to EVT in shaping consumer motivations and behaviours, become
particularly powerful in the influence of brand conscientiousness . The findings  highlight that
conscientious brands can effectively leverage their sustainability equity to influence consumer decisions,
aligning with findings from existing research (Iglesias et al., 2019; Ishaq & Di Maria, 2020). Secondly,
the study underscores the importance of message depth in sustainability communications. Deep

22

messages, which provide comprehensive and detailed information, are more effective in driving
consumer action than shallow messages. This is consistent with the research findings of Chan (2000),
which suggest that providing detailed information enhances cognitive processing and persuasive
effectiveness. Meanwhile, this study advances the understanding of the topic and demonstrates that
strong sustainable brand equity can amplify the persuasion effectiveness of shallow claims. The findings
of this study contribute to conscientious brand research by examining how brand conscientiousness can
enhance the effectiveness of green advertising and influence consumers’ sustainable behaviours .
Additionally, the study examines the role of visual communication, showing that visual highlighting
means (processes) are more effective than those concentrating on ends (outcomes) when deep claims
are utilised. This suggests that consumers are more responsive to sustainability claims with matching
visuals. This insight adds depth to visual persuasion theories examined in prior green advertising studies
(Schmuck, Matthes, & Naderer, 2018; Shen et al., 2024; Xue & Muralidharan, 2015) , offering a new
perspective on how visuals can be leveraged in brand sustainability communications.

Managerial implications
From a managerial perspective, this research offers practical insights for conscientious brands seeking
to effectively convey a conscientious value proposition and motivate consumers to embrace sustainable
behaviours. Firstly, brands should emphasise their conscientious attributes and commitment to
sustainability in their marketing strategies. By building strong sustainable brand equity, companies can
foster consumer trust and motivate sustainable actions, such as purchasing carbon offsets. Secondly, the
research suggests marketers should prioritise deep messages that offer detailed and substantive
information about the brand’s sustainability efforts. Such messages can enhance consumer engagement
and drive action by enhancing consumers' outcome expectation of their actions and efforts. Lastly, the
findings regarding visual communication suggest that brands should ensure that their visuals are
consistent with the conveyed messages rather than relying on suggestive imagery that may lead to
accusations of greenwashing. Overall, this research equips manage rs with a deeper understanding of
how to effectively communicate their sustainability efforts, thereby fostering consumer -driven
sustainable actions with strong sustainable brand equity.

Conclusion
The main objective of this study is to explore how conscientious brands can best influence consumers'
sustainable actions by analysing the impact of sustainable brand equity in consumer responses to carbon
offsetting messages. Two online experiments were conducted in this study. Study 1 showed that deep
claims are significantly more effective than shallow claims in raising consumer outcome expectations
and subsequent carbon offset purchase intention. Sustainable brand equity strengthens the impact of
deep and shallow claims while decreasing the effectiveness gap between the two claim types. The results
of Study 2 confirmed the interaction effects that mean -visual enhances the impact of deep claim,
whereas end -visual strengthens the effect of shallow claim. Insights from this study could provide
supporting examples and empirical evidence to advocate the development of conscientious brands via
corporate culture, a balanced multi -stakeholder perspective , and long -term strategic management
(Iglesias & Ind, 2020).

Limitations and future research
This study involves four main research limitations and identifies avenues for future research. First, this
study has primarily focused on how conscientious brands can best influence consumers' sustainable
actions through carbon offsetting messages. Future research could broaden  internal and external
stakeholder responses to examine the role of different internal stakeholders (senior management,
investors, and employees) in promoting conscientious brands and how conscientious brands foster a
balanced external stakeholder perspective (Iglesias & Ind, 2020). Second, this study adopted two online
experiments by using surveys. Future research could support the results and insights with a qualitative
approach, such as semi -structured interviews or focus groups , to understand the viewpoints from
customers’ perceptions and interpretations on how conscientious brands can influence their sustainable
actions and behaviours. Third, this study mainly focused on individuals in the Chinese context. Future
research could replicate the research design in this study in othe r regions and countries with similar
political, institutional, and social settings. This study also calls for future research direction in different
cultural settings with different levels of environmental consciousness to affect consumers' sustainable

24

actions and behaviour s. Fourth, this study conducted a single observation period for two online
experiments. Future research can conduct a longitudinal study to assess whether consumers' sustainable
actions and behaviour will remain consistent or change over time.
