ABSTRACT
This study examines whether destination branding strategies that produced a globally
recognized resort brand in Thailand can be replicated within China’s tourism system.
Phuket has, over four decades, become one of the world’s most recognized beach
destinations, maintaining strong international arrivals and foreign-exchange revenue even
as Thailand’s national tourism numbers softened in 2025. In contrast, China’s Hainan
Island, particularly Sanya, has been the focus of accelerated state -driven development.
Key reforms include the implementation of island -wide Free Trade Port customs
operations in December 2025, expansion of visa-free access to 86 countries, and increased
offshore duty-free allowances. These policies have generated measurable growth: Hainan
recorded 106 million visitors in 2025, a 9.1 percent increase, with international overnight
arrivals exceeding 1.5 million, up 35.2 percent year -on-year. Duty-free sales also rose
sharply in early 2026. However, Sanya’s international visitor base remains limited
compared to both its domestic tourism volume and Phuket’s international arrivals, which
recovered to near pre-pandemic levels by late 2025. Using a qualitative comparative case
study based on secondary data from major tourism and economic sources, this paper
analyzes structural, regulatory, and perceptual differences between the two destinations.
The findings suggest that visa policy design, private -sector involvement, accumulated
brand equity, and geopolitical and currency factors play a more decisive role than natural
resources or state investment. The study concludes that while recent policy acceleration
strengthens Sanya’s position, long -term international brand recognition depends on
gradual, path-dependent processes that cannot be rapidly replicated.

1 INTRODUCTION

Resort-destination branding occupies a distinct position within the broader field
of tourism marketing. Unlike national campaigns, which must reflect the diversity of a
country’s tourism offer, resort branding focuses on a single, geographically bounded
place-image such as a beach, island, or coastline. When effective, this focus generates
disproportionate returns, allowing a resort to achieve global recognition more easily than
a nation as a whole. Phuket exemplifies this dynamic in Southeast Asia. Despite its
relatively small size, the island functions as an independently recognizable tourism brand
and is often cited by international travelers as the primary reason for visiting Thailand.
Since the establishment of the Hainan Free Trade Port in 2018, Chinese authorities
have sought to replicate this model on Hainan Island, positioning the city of Sanya as its
flagship resort destination. Marketed as “China’s Hawaii,” Sanya has been promoted
through expanded duty -free incentives, a broader visa -free regime, luxury resort
development, and significant infrastructure investment in sectors such as yachti ng, golf,
and wellness tourism. This effort accelerated at the end of 2025, when Hainan
implemented island-wide special customs operations, eliminating tariffs on a large share
of imports and expanding duty -free product categories. At the same time, visa -free
eligibility increased to 86 countries, contributing to a sharp rise in inbound flight
bookings.

Despite this policy acceleration, a significant gap remains between Sanya’s
ambitions and its international tourism performance. In 2025, Hainan recorded 10 6
million visitors, yet only 1.5 million were inbound international travelers, representing a
small fraction of total arrivals. In contrast, Phuket welcomed over five million
international visitors within a total of approximately fourteen million, resultin g in a far
higher proportion of foreign tourists. This disparity highlights structural differences that
extend beyond policy reforms.
This paper addresses whether destination branding can effectively replicate resort-
tourism success across differing institutional and geopolitical contexts, or whether
structural constraints limit its impact. Building on comparative research on destination
branding at the national level, this study focuses on the subnational scale, using Phuket
and Sanya as paired cases . It draws on secondary data from tourism authorities,
international organizations, and industry sources through early 2026 to evaluate the roles
of visa policy, market accessibility, private-sector participation, geopolitical and currency
dynamics, and accumulated brand equity.
The analysis suggests that these factors are more decisive than natural resources
or levels of investment in explaining the persistent divergence between the two
destinations. Furthermore, while the December 2025 reforms represent t he most
significant liberalization undertaken in Hainan, they coincide with a period in which
Thailand’s tourism sector faces an unusual slowdown. This creates a rare comparative
moment in which both destinations are simultaneously adjusting their market positioning.
The paper concludes by considering what a realistic and effective branding strategy for
Sanya might entail under these conditions, emphasizing the importance of long -term,
path-dependent processes that cannot be rapidly reproduced through polic y intervention
alone.

2 LITERATURE REVIEW

2.1 DESTINATION BRANDING AND THE FORMATION OF PLACE IMAGE

The theoretical foundation for sub -national resort branding builds on broader
destination branding literature while focusing on a more specific unit of analysis. Govers
and Go (2016) argue that both tangible elements, such as landscape, climate, and
infrastructure, and intangible associations like leisure and escape shape a destination’s
place image. In resort contexts, these elements can be marketed with relative
independence from the national brand. Kotler, Bowen, and Makens (2017) further
emphasize that a clear destination value proposition stimulates investment in hospitality
and related industries, a pattern evident in Phuket’s long -term development and Sanya’s
more recent resort expansion.
Morgan, Pritchard, and Pride (2011) introduce a critical temporal dimension,
noting that destination image develops gradually through sustained exposure across
media, word-of-mouth, and industry networks. This process is inherently path-dependent,
giving early entrants into international tourism markets a lasting advantage. Aaker (1996)
reinforces this argument by showing that brand equity—comprising awareness, perceived
quality, and associations —accumulates over time  and cannot be rapidly constructed
through marketing alone. Together, these perspectives highlight the limits of accelerated
branding strategies in newer resort destinations.

2.2 SUB -NATIONAL BRANDING: BRANDED HOUSE VERSUS HOUSE OF
BRANDS

Zenker and Braun’s (2017) distinction between a “branded house” and a “house
of brands” is directly relevant to this comparison. Thailand follows a house -of-brands
approach: while “Amazing Thailand” serves as a national umbrella, destinations like
Phuket and Chiang Mai have developed distinct, largely independent identities supported
by private-sector activity and long-term international exposure.

6 Revista Acadêmica On-line, Brazil, v.12, n.64, p. 01-23, 2026.

China, by contrast, applies a more centralized branded -house model in Hainan,
where Sanya remains closely tied to nationa l and provincial branding initiatives such as
the Hainan Free Trade Port. As a result, it has not developed a fully independent
international identity. This difference is significant, as branded -house strategies are
effective for domestic or familiar marke ts, whereas house -of-brands models are better
suited to attracting diverse international audiences—an ongoing challenge for Sanya.

2.3 TOURISM AREA LIFE CYCLE AND PATH DEPENDENCY IN RESORT
EVOLUTION

Butler's (1980) tourism area life cycle model — moving from exploration, through
involvement, development, consolidation, and stagnation, toward either decline or
rejuvenation — offers a useful diagnostic frame for comparing destinations at different
structural stages. Phuket's 2025 performance, marked by record hotel supply, intensifying
competition, and shortening average length of stay even as arrivals hit new highs, is
consistent with a mature destination navigating the boundary between consolidation and
stagnation, requiring active rejuvenation strategies (event tourism, art festivals, quality -
over-volume positioning) to sustain momentum. Sanya, by contrast, remains firmly in the
involvement-to-development transition with respect to its international segment
specifically, even though its domestic tourism fu nction is comparatively mature.
Applying the life -cycle lens across two different market segments within the same
destination — as this paper does for Sanya — is itself a contribution to the branding
literature, since most life-cycle analyses treat a destination's audience as undifferentiated.

2.4 VISA POLICY AND MARKET ACCESS AS BRANDING INFRASTRUCTURE

A substantial empirical literature has examined visa policy as a determinant of
tourism demand, largely independent of marketing activity. Neumayer (2010)
demonstrates that the imposition of a visa restriction by a destination country measurably
deters bilateral travel, while Czaika and Neumayer's (2017) subsequent analysis of a
bilateral visa-policy database spanning 1995 –2013 finds that new visa restricti ons deter
inbound tourism by more than twenty percent, with the effect concentrated among

travelers from lower-income origin markets. Lawson and Roychoudhury (2016) similarly
confirm that visa requirements measurably impede tourist travel using cross -national
data, while Bangwayo -Skeete and Skeete (2017) show that the extension of visa -free
privileges is itself a politically and institutionally mediated decision, not simply a function
of a destination's tourism ambitions.
Taken together, this literature imp lies that visa architecture functions
simultaneously as market-access infrastructure and as an implicit signal of openness that
shapes destination perception independent of any marketing layered on top of it — a
dynamic with direct bearing on the Phuket–Sanya comparison, given the two destinations'
materially different visa regimes even after Hainan's late-2025 liberalization.

2.5 FREE TRADE ZONES AND STATE -LED TOURISM DEVELOPMENT IN
CHINA

Recent Chinese- and English-language scholarship examines Hainan’s Free Trade
Port as a model of state -led tourism development. Cao (2023) describes its strategy as
industrial “agglomeration,” integrating tourism with modern services and high -tech
sectors rather than treating it as a standalone industry. Harsiwi et al. (2025) highlight key
policy tools, including duty-free expansion, simplified visa procedures, and infrastructure
investment, while noting that these measures have produced limited growth in truly
international visitation prior to the late-2025 reforms.
Overall, this literature aligns with Zenker and Braun’s (2017) “branded house”
framework, showing that Hainan’s tourism development is driven primarily by top-down
institutional design, in contrast to the more market-driven evolution seen in Phuket.
Finally, literature on destination image management during periods of instability
(Avraham, 2015) is newly relevant to this comparison in light of 2025 developments.
Thailand's national arrivals fell for the first time since the pandemic in 2025, driven
substantially by a sharp contraction in Chinese arrivals linked to safety perceptions and
regional security incidents, even as Phuket specifically continued to grow on the strength
of Russian, Indian, and European demand. This divergence between a national brand
under perceptual strain and a resort sub -brand that continued to perform illustrates
precisely the kind of semi -autonomous brand resilience that house -of-brands structures

are theorized to provide, and offers an unusually current empirical test of the branding
theory reviewed above.

3 METHODOLOGY

3.1 RESEARCH DESIGN

This study adopts a qualitative comparative case study design, consistent with the
approach used in prior research examining destination branding's economic impact across
Thailand, Dubai, and China. The unit of comparison here is narrowed to the sub-national
resort level, pairing Phuket (Thailand) with Sanya (Hainan Island, China) as the most
structurally comparable resort destinations within each national tourism system, while
incorporating provin cial-level Hainan data where city -specific figures for 2025 –2026
were not yet publicly available at the time of writing.

3.2 CASE SELECTION RATIONALE

Phuket and Sanya were selected because both are officially promoted flagship
beach-resort destinations w ithin their respective national tourism strategies; both have
received sustained state and private infrastructure investment; both are positioned as the
primary vehicle for attracting high -value international leisure travelers; and both offer
comparable natural endowments — tropical climate, coastline, and proximity to major
regional aviation hubs. Both destinations, moreover, experienced significant disruption
in their traditional Chinese -outbound and Chinese -domestic demand base during 2025,
making this an analytically useful moment to examine how each system absorbs source -
market volatility.
This comparability isolates policy, branding, and market -access variables as the
primary explanatory factors for divergent outcomes, rather than differences in underlying
tourism resources.

3.3 DATA COLLECTION

Data were drawn exclusively from secondary sources: the Tourism Authority of
Thailand (TAT), the Phuket Tourist Association and Tourism Council of Phuket, the
World Tourism Organization (UNWTO), the World Travel & Tourism Council (WTTC),
Statista, CEIC, the Hainan Free Trade Port administration, Sanya municipal and Hainan
provincial tourism releases, C9 Hotelworks and CBRE Thailand market research, and
KPMG's Hainan travel-retail analysis. Data collection was extended through the first half
of 2026 specific ally to capture the effects of Hainan's 18 December 2025 customs
transition and the associated visa -free expansion, both of which post -date most existing
academic literature on the Hainan Free Trade Port. Where figures diverged across sources
— a recurring issue with Chinese provincial tourism statistics in particular — the most
recent official or officially cited figure was used, and discrepancies are flagged in the
limitations discussion below.

3.4 DATA ANALYSIS APPROACH

Consistent with the qualitative content-analysis framework used in the author's
prior comparative research, the analysis proceeds in three stages: first, a within -case
review of each destination's branding architecture, policy history, and current
performance; second, a structured cross -case comparison organized around visa access,
marketing reach, private-sector dynamism, and perception; and third, a synthesis stage in
which Butler's (1980) life -cycle framework and Zenker and Braun's (2017) branded -
house/house-of-brands typology are appl ied to interpret the observed divergence and to
generate policy-relevant recommendations.

3.5 LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY

As with the prior study on which this paper builds, exclusive reliance on secondary
data limits the ability to capture nuanced visitor perception directly. Chinese sub -
provincial tourism statistics are reported with varying methodologies across years and
sometimes conflate overnight, same -day, and total -visit counts, which affects the

precision of trend comparisons; where this occurred, t he analysis defaults to the most
conservative and most recently confirmed figure. Because Sanya-specific 2025 data were
not uniformly available at the time of writing, several 2025 figures for Sanya are

city-level baseline, and are labeled as estimates throughout. Finally, the very recency of
Hainan's December 2025 customs and visa reforms means that this paper captures only
the first two quarters of their effect; longer-run conclusions about their impact on Sanya's
international brand position would require data collected over a longer subsequent period.

4 CASE STUDY: PHUKET

4.1 HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT AND BRAND FORMATION

Phuket's emergence as an internationally recog nized resort brand predates any
single coordinated marketing campaign. Its brand equity developed cumulatively from
the 1980s onward, propelled by early European charter tourism, subsequent
diversification into Russian, Chinese, Australian, and Indian mark ets, and a private
hospitality sector that scaled rapidly and independently of state direction. The island now
operates as a semi-autonomous brand beneath the national "Amazing Thailand" umbrella,
benefiting from name recognition that in many markets excee ds general awareness of
Thailand as a country. This trajectory illustrates the house -of-brands dynamic discussed
in Section 2.2: Phuket's identity was permitted to develop organically, shaped more by
international tour operators, airline route decisions, a nd hotel investment than by any
centrally authored campaign.

4.2 PERFORMANCE THROUGH 2025–2026

Phuket’s post-pandemic recovery accelerated through 2024 and 2025, reaching
13.1 million visitors and 497 billion baht in revenue in 2024, the highest of any T hai
province. Growth continued into 2025, with 7.6 million visitors and 290 billion baht
recorded by mid-year. Full-year revenue was projected at 550 billion baht, with over 80
percent driven by international tourism. By late 2025, international arrivals had recovered

to 97.5 percent of pre -pandemic levels, and the island experienced its strongest high
season in five years. Total international arrivals exceeded 5.4 million, surpassing pre -
pandemic benchmarks, with further growth expected in 2026.
However, this expansion has shifted in composition. Chinese arrivals declined by
over 40 percent, while Russian visitors became the largest source market, supported by
strong growth from India and long -haul markets such as Europe, the United States, and
Australia. At the same time, average length of stay fell from around 11 to 8–9 nights, and
revenue growth lagged behind arrivals, reflecting increased competition from expanding
hotel supply, including unlicensed accommodations.

4.3 STRUCTURAL FEATURES OF THE PHUKET MODEL

Several structural features distinguish Phuket from Sanya. First, Phuket’s visitor
base is highly diversified across multiple international markets, reducing reliance on any
single country despite the sharp decline in Chinese arrivals in 2025. Second, its tourism
infrastructure has developed largely through private and foreign investment rather than
centralized planning, enabling faster adaptation to changing demand, as seen in the rapid
expansion of villa and condominium markets. Third, Thailan d’s broad visa -exemption
policy, covering 93 countries, minimizes barriers to international travel.
Finally, Phuket has shifted toward a “quality over quantity” strategy for 2025 –
2026, focusing on higher -spending visitors, event tourism, and infrastructure  readiness
rather than sheer volume. This approach aligns with Butler’s (1980) life-cycle model for
mature destinations.

5 CASE STUDY: SANYA

5.1 INSTITUTIONAL STRATEGY : THE FREE TRADE PORT MODEL

Sanya’s development follows a fundamentally different model from Phuket’s.
Instead of building brand equity through long -term private -sector growth, it has been
shaped by a top-down national strategy centered on Hainan’s designation as a Free Trade
Port in 2018. This approach combines infrastructure investment, expanded duty -free
allowances, and a progressively liberalized visa regime, including the extension to
independent travel in 2023.
The strategy intensified at the end of 2025, when Hainan became a separate
customs territory, exempting most imports from major taxes within the island. Duty-free
categories were expanded, purchase limits eased, and visa -free access reportedly
increased to 86 countries. A 144-hour visa-free transit policy also remains in place. These
measures mark a significant acceleration of Sanya’s state -led t ourism development
model.

5.2 PERFORMANCE THROUGH 2025–2026

The results of this accelerated strategy are significant. Hainan recorded 106
million visitors in 2025, up 9.1 percent, with tourism revenue reaching 225.4 billion yuan,

a 10.5 percent increase.  Inbound overnight international arrivals exceeded 1.5 million,
growing 35.2 percent year-on-year. Growth continued into early 2026, with strong Spring
Festival performance and rising international arrivals, particularly from regional markets.
Duty-free sa les also surged, including sharp increases following the December 2025
customs reforms, alongside a major rise in inbound flight bookings.
Sanya remains central to this expansion. The city’s GDP reached about 103 billion
yuan in 2025, driven largely by tourism, while duty-free sales surpassed 20 billion yuan.
Although international visitors still represent a small share of total arrivals, their growth
has been strong, reflecting Sanya’s role as Hainan’s primary international tourism hub.

5.3 PERSISTENT STRUCTURAL GAP

Despite this acceleration, the composition of Hainan's growth reveals the core
structural gap with Phuket: it remains overwhelmingly domestic. At 1.5 million inbound
overnight international arrivals against 106 million total visitors, internat ional travelers
account for roughly 1.4 percent of Hainan's visitor base — even after the fastest year of
international growth the province has recorded. Sanya's visa -free policy, though now
covering 86 countries, continues to impose procedural conditions that Thailand's regime
does not: registration requirements through a Hainan -based travel agency, confinement
of stay to Hainan province alone, a 30 -day cap on stay (against Thailand's 60), and
exclusion of onward travel to mainland China without a separate  visa. These frictions,
layered on top of a still -developing base of international brand awareness, help explain
why even a historic single-year policy liberalization produced strong relative growth from
a very small base rather than a fundamental change in visitor composition.

6.2 INTERNATIONAL MARKETING REACH AND DISTRIBUTION

Thailand’s tourism authorities have built extensive international trade
relationships over decades, supported by global fairs, targeted roadshows, and strong
organic media and influencer presence. In contrast, Hainan’s marketing remains largely
state-driven and focused on niche segments rather than mass leisure markets, although
recent reforms aim to expand its appeal to higher-spending international visitors.

6.3 PRIVATE-SECTOR DYNAMISM VERSUS STATE-LED DEVELOPMENT

Phuket's hospitality landscape has been built substantially through private and
foreign investment, producing a diverse, internationally branded hotel and nightlife
economy that responds quickly to shifting source-market demand — evident in the rapid
2022–2025 buildout catering to Russian long -stay visitors and digital nomads. Sanya's
luxury resort sector, while comparably high -end, has developed within a more centrall y
coordinated planning framework tied to Free Trade Port objectives, prioritizing duty-free
retail and high-end domestic consumption. The December 2025 customs transition itself
illustrates this contrast: it was implemented as a single, nationally coordina ted policy

event rather than an incremental, market -driven evolution, generating an immediate and
measurable spike in duty -free consumption but a comparatively slower corresponding
rise in international overnight visitation.

6.4 CURRENCY, GEOPOLITICS, AND SOURCE-MARKET VOLATILITY

A notable and previously underexplored parallel emerged in 2025: both
destinations experienced a simultaneous surge in Russian arrivals, while both saw their
traditional China-linked demand soften — Phuket through an outright decline in Chinese
arrivals to Thailand, and Sanya through the persistent gap between domestic Chinese
demand (still the overwhelming majority of its visitor base) and the international
diversification the Free Trade Port strategy is explicitly designed to ac hieve. This
suggests that some 2025 shifts affecting both destinations are driven by factors exogenous
to either government's branding strategy — currency movements, shifting Russian travel
patterns following years of Western travel restrictions, and chang ing regional safety
perceptions — rather than by the comparative success or failure of branding execution
alone. Isolating the branding -attributable share of these shifts from macro -driven
volatility remains difficult and is flagged as a limitation of this and similar studies.

6.5 VISITOR COMPOSITION AND PERCEPTION GAPS

Global travel perception of China more broadly continues to affect Sanya's
positioning independent of the destination's own attr ibutes: language accessibility,
digital-payment friction for foreign visitors (despite recent improvements via UnionPay-
linked e-wallets), and general unfamiliarity with Hainan as a leisure destination among
Western and non-regional Asian travelers all constrain the conversion of awareness into
visitation. Phuket carries none of these perception burdens, having been fully integrated
into global leisure travel consciousness for decades. The scale of the resulting gap is
visible even after Hainan's most liberal year on record: international travelers made up an
estimated 75 percent of Phuket's visitor base in 2025, against roughly 1.4 percent for
Hainan Province as a whole.

6.6 TOURISM AREA LIFE CYCLE POSITIONING

Read through Butler's (1980) life -cycle framework, Phuket's 2025 indicators —
record arrivals alongside flat revenue, compressed length of stay, and rising supply -side
competition — are consistent with a destination approaching the boundary between
consolidation and stagnation, prompting the rejuvenation strategies (event tourism,

18 Revista Acadêmica On-line, Brazil, v.12, n.64, p. 01-23, 2026.

infrastructure investment, quality -over-volume positioning) that Phuket's tourism
authorities have explicitly adopted for 2026. Sanya's international segment, by contrast,
sits at the involvement-to-development transition, characterized by rapid but small -base
growth, expanding but still-limited infrastructure for non-Chinese-speaking visitors, and
a policy environment still being actively constructed. This asymmetry implies that direct
imitation of Phuket's current strategy would be a poor fit for Sanya: Phuket's 2026
priorities (managing overcapacity, extending stay length, protecting yield) address
problems Sanya's international segment does not yet have, while Sanya's priorities
(building basic international awareness, simplifying access, establishing distribution
relationships) are problems Phuket resolved decades ago.

7 STRATEGIES AND RECOMMENDATIONS

If China intends to convert the momentum generated by the December 2025
customs and visa reforms into a durable international resort brand for San ya, rather than
a temporary consumption spike, several policy directions follow directly from the
comparative analysis above.
First, further simplification of Hainan's visa -free regime — removing the
mandatory travel-agency pre-registration requirement, or at minimum digitizing it into a
traveler-initiated online process comparable to an e -visa — would materially reduce the
remaining friction that distinguishes it from Thailand's exemption model. Extending the
maximum stay closer to Thailand's 60 -day standa rd would better accommodate the
longer-haul travel patterns typical of the European and North American markets Sanya is
now actively courting.
Second, sustained investment in international travel -trade relationships —
participation in major global tourism fairs, coordinated marketing partnerships with
international airlines to expand direct long -haul routes to Sanya Phoenix International
Airport, and engagement with international travel media and content creators, particularly

20 Revista Acadêmica On-line, Brazil, v.12, n.64, p. 01-23, 2026.

in the Russian, Southeast Asian, and European markets already showing organic growth
— would help build the kind of durable global awareness that currently distinguishes
Phuket. This is a multi-year undertaking rather than a single campaign, and expectations
should be calibrated to the  fact that even a historic single -year liberalization moved
Hainan's international share from a negligible base to a still-small one.
Third, addressing remaining friction points for international visitors — digital
payment accessibility, English -language service availability, and simplified onward
travel arrangements for those wishing to visit mainland China after Hainan — would
improve the on -the-ground visitor experience that ultimately shapes word -of-mouth
reputation, the single most durable driver of d estination brand equity identified in the
literature reviewed in Section 2.1.
Fourth, rather than attempting to directly replicate Phuket's mass -market
international leisure model, Sanya may derive more value from consolidating its
comparative advantages i n niche high -value segments where China already holds
structural strengths: wellness and traditional medicine tourism, golf, yachting, cruise
tourism, and MICE (meetings, incentives, conferences, exhibitions) tourism tied to events
such as the Boao Forum f or Asia. The early evidence from the December 2025 reforms
— with Russian, Malaysian, Singaporean, and Thai travelers leading the new
international growth — suggests that a regionally focused, duty -free-shopping-led
positioning may be more immediately achi evable than a head -on challenge to Phuket's
broad Western leisure-market appeal.
Fifth, Sanya’s authorities should treat the December 2025 customs launch as a
branding opportunity, not just a policy reform. The sharp rise in duty-free sales and flight
bookings suggests strong latent international demand that could be converted into repeat
visits through coordinated global marketing.
At the same time, Phuket’s recent experience offers a caution: rapid growth
without matching infrastructure can reduce visitor  spending and satisfaction. Sanya’s
lower baseline allows it to plan ahead —particularly in multilingual services and long -
haul connectivity—if managed strategically.
Finally, continued domestic tourism strength should not be treated as a weakness
to be corrected, but as a genuine structural asset. Hainan's ability to mobilize 225.4 billion
yuan in tourist spending and 106 million total visits in 2025 represents an economic model

21 Revista Acadêmica On-line, Brazil, v.12, n.64, p. 01-23, 2026.

with its own logic, less exposed to the currency and geopolitical volatility th at
periodically disrupts Phuket's international -dependent revenue base, as illustrated by
Thailand's own first annual arrivals decline in 2025. A realistic strategy for Sanya
combines continued domestic strength with steady, well -sequenced international
liberalization, rather than pursuing international volume as an end in itself.

8 CONCLUSION

The comparison between Phuket and Sanya suggests that destination branding, on
its own, cannot fully substitute for the structural, regulatory, and historical conditions that
produced Phuket's international resort brand — even under conditions of the kind of rapid,
well-resourced policy liberalization Hainan undertook at the end of 2025. Sanya's tourism
authorities have, within a matter of weeks, closed much of the v isible policy gap with
Thailand's model: a near -doubling of visa -free country coverage since 2023, a fully
separate island-wide customs regime, and duty -free sales growth exceeding 40 percent
year-on-year. Yet the underlying visitor composition remains overwhelmingly domestic,
a gap rooted less in the pace of policy reform than in the remaining visa friction, the
difference between state -led and private -sector-led development, and decades of
accumulated international brand equity that Phuket holds and Haina n does not yet
possess.
This does not mean Sanya's ambitions are unachievable; rather, it suggests that
fully closing the gap will require sustained policy consistency and international market -
building over a multi -year horizon, alongside a realistic ackno wledgment that some of
Phuket's advantages are path-dependent and not readily compressed through investment
or reform intensity alone. The events of 2025 — Thailand's first annual arrivals decline
in years, driven by a sharp fall in Chinese demand and offs et by Russian and long -haul
growth, alongside Hainan's most aggressive liberalization to date — suggest that both
systems are being reshaped by forces larger than either government's branding strategy
alone. A differentiated strategy for Sanya — leveraging China's genuine strengths in
wellness, MICE, duty-free retail, and niche high-value tourism, while continuing to build
on its substantial and resilient domestic tourism base, and converting the early momentum
of the December 2025 reforms into durable rather than transitory international interest —

may ultimately offer a more attainable and economically sound path than direct
replication of the Phuket model.
