Abstract
As destinations compete in an Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI) driven global economy, place branding research
must continue to strive and unlock AI's potential for authentic, competitive, and sustainable place brand
development. This study maps existing literature on AI applications in place branding research.
Speciﬁcally, it aims to: (a) contextualize AI-related place branding research by study type, temporal
development, and geographical coverage; (b) determine the scope by identifying research goals and
objects of study; and (c) examine methodological approaches, including AI technologies used and data
sources. Following JBI and PRISMA-ScR protocols, a systematic scoping review analyzed 56 studies
(2010–2025), identifying ﬁve research gaps. In a second phase, we complement these gaps by
identifying advances in the adjacent domains of public diplomacy and branding. While AI provides the
technical capacity and urgent impetus for comprehensive studies, ﬁndings reveal that research remains
constrained by a case-centric orientation, a discrepancy that underscores the urgent need to adopt a
holistic lens. Although some systemic challenges appear to be shared with adjacent ﬁelds of knowledge,
insights from branding and public diplomacy are highlighted to provide guidance to address their
knowledge gaps. We conclude by providing a ten-point research agenda advancing AI integration in
place branding research.
Introduction
The rapid adoption of artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) across management and branding represents one of the
most consequential shifts reshaping both practice and scholarship in the contemporary era. Driven by
eﬃciency gains, predictive capabilities, and creative augmentation, AI is transforming how organizations
understand and engage their audiences (Cubric 2020). Place branding is no exception; AI technologies
are emerging as transformative tools for place branding research and practice (Keegan 2021), enabling
the analysis of massive data volumes, insights into public perceptions, and the detection of emerging
trends with unprecedented speed and scale, to name a few.
However, despite growing AI adoption, the interdisciplinary nature of place branding has produced
fragmented knowledge across multiple domains spanning tourism studies, marketing, urban planning,
computer science, and communication studies creating challenges in identifying methodological
patterns (Grandi and Neri 2014). While individual studies demonstrate AI's potential, there remains
limited understanding of the overall research landscape (Mathayomchan et al. 2023). As different
academic domains bring varying methodological approaches and theoretical frameworks (Kasapoğlu et
al. 2023), mapping the current state of knowledge is essential to identify patterns, trends, and gaps. A
scoping review is therefore necessary to chart diverse AI applications, identify methodological
contributions, highlight ethical and operational challenges, and guide future research directions
(Chotisarn and Phuthong 2025).
This scoping review aims to map the existing literature on AI applications in place branding research,
identify key concepts and methodological approaches, and chart the research landscape to inform future
Page 2/27

scholarly endeavors. The overarching research question is: What is the current practice of research on
artiﬁcial intelligence applications in place branding? This was operationalized through three goals: a) to
contextualize AI-related place branding research by study type, temporal development, and geographical
coverage; (b) to determine the scope of the ﬁeld by mapping research goals and objects of study; and (c)
to examine methodological approaches, including AI technologies employed and data sources utilized.
Ultimately, the study aims to identify existing scholarly gaps and to draw on advances in adjacent
domains ‘general branding and public diplomacy’, establishing future research directions that move
place branding forward.
BACKGROUND AND LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Place Branding and Digital Transformation
The place branding domain has evolved considerably from early city marketing approaches toward more
complex frameworks encompassing identity formation, experience co-creation and, more recently,
stakeholder engagement through bottom-up approaches (Kavaratzis 2004; Ripoll González et al. 2025).
Central to this evolution is the understanding that place brands are continuously shaped through
stakeholder interactions across conceived, perceived, and lived dimensions, making them inherently
open-ended rather than ﬁxed managerial outputs (Kavaratzis and Hatch 2021). In this process, digital
transformation has fundamentally reshaped the place branding landscape, altering the relationship
between place management organizations and their publics (Sevin 2016) by expanding communication
tools and embracing multi-channel digital approaches (Hanna and Rowley 2014).
For instance, digital communication has made an extensive array of tools available to destinations of
varying sizes and resource levels (Correia et al. 2024, Fernández-Cavia et al. 2018). For instance, the rise
of social media platforms, review sites, and user-generated content has democratized and decentralized
place image formation (Lund et al. 2018). Place brands are now co-created through interactions between
oﬃcial destination management messaging and multiple public stakeholders, with understanding the
complex communication scheme beyond touristic promotion marking a pivotal moment in the
understanding of place image formation (Govers et al. 2007). This shift led contemporary place branding
to recognize places as complex socio-cultural constructs with multiple representations across diverse
digital platforms (Florek 2011).
In the current digital age, place brands are collectively shaped through decentralized narratives produced
by multiple authors rather than controlled by a single dominant oﬃcial organization (Björner and
Aronsson 2022). This evolution has contributed to a shift from top-down branding approaches to
participatory models, presenting both opportunities and challenges for place brand managers in
engaging with large volumes of online data (Oliveira and Panyik 2015). Increasingly, AI-powered
technologies are enabling DMOs to interpret these data volumes for real-time, context-aware decision-
making (Koo et al. 2025) marking a new frontier in the digital transformation of place branding.
2.2 Artiﬁcial Intelligence Applications in Place Branding Research
Page 3/27

Building on this digital evolution, the rapid adoption of AI technologies represents the next signiﬁcant
transformation in place branding research, driven by advances in algorithm sophistication and data
availability (Truong and Ta 2025). With AI's dynamic nature, place perception research requires real-time
monitoring and trend identiﬁcation capabilities that AI technologies make possible (Liu et al. 2021). For
instance, Natural Language Processing (NLP) enables researchers to analyze sentiment, extract themes,
and identify patterns in large datasets from social media, reviews, and online content (Nixon 2023).
Machine Learning (ML) algorithms support predictive modeling, classiﬁcation, and clustering of large
datasets, while computer vision techniques enable analysis of visual content and imagery associated
with destinations (Wang et al. 2024; Taecharungroj and Mathayomchan 2020; Koltringer and Dickinger
2015).
The shift from centralized to decentralized digital content networks involving travel content creators,
digital inﬂuencers, tourists, travelers, and algorithmic platforms has expanded exponentially. AI
applications now extend to destination brand experience analysis through data mining and machine
learning (Calderón-Fajardo et al. 2024), visual branding analysis on social media (Nixon 2023), and
municipal slogan creation with Generative AI (Hakala 2025).
Just as social media reshaped place image formation by decentralizing content creation and enabling
real-time stakeholder engagement (Lund et al. 2018), AI now extends these capabilities through
automated large-scale data processing, predictive modelling, and sophisticated content analysis beyond
the reach of most destination management organizations (Koo et al. 2025). Despite this growing body of
work, research on AI applications in place branding remains scattered across disciplines, fragmented in
focus, and uneven in methodological robustness. Mapping this knowledge, identifying what has been
achieved, what remains unexplored, and where the most promising opportunities lie, is therefore
essential for advancing place branding scholarship at the forefront of methodological and theoretical
innovation.
Methodology
This study employed a two-phase scoping review design. Phase 1 consists of a scoping review based on
the nine-step Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) framework (Peters et al. 2020), providing a systematic
process for mapping a total of 56 publications. Phase 2 presents a complementary analysis comparing
Phase 1 ﬁndings with advances in AI integration in the adjacent domains of branding and public
diplomacy. In the second phase we analyzed 12 journal papers. Figure 1 represents the ﬂow diagram of
the two-phased methodological strategy of the study.
3.1 Phase 1: Scoping Review of AI in Place Branding
This study followed the JBI nine-step scoping review framework (Peters et al. 2020) with PRISMA-ScR
reporting guidelines (Tricco et al. 2018). Inclusion criteria comprised peer-reviewed empirical,
theoretical, or methodological studies explicitly addressing place branding while employing, discussing,
or proposing AI technologies. Studies were published in English and retrieved from the SCOPUS
Page 4/27

database from inception through March 2025. Non-peer-reviewed publications, studies employing AI
outside place branding contexts, full book manuscripts, and articles unavailable through institutional
database subscriptions were excluded.
 The ﬁnal sample was analyzed based on ten variables: author and publication year, publication source
and country, study type (Empirical/Theoretical/Methodological), AI technologies and tools employed,
place branding dimensions addressed, research aims and application domains, case study objects and
geographical focus, online data sources and digital platforms analyzed, and key ﬁndings.
Manual dual-coding achieved an overall agreement rate of 87%. To further enhance methodological
reliability, ChatGPT (GPT-5, August 2025) was used as an independent coder to analyze all 56 articles
based on the authors' guidelines and analysis prompt, validating manual coding results. The comparative
analysis between manual dual-coding and GPT-5 demonstrated a convergence rate of 76.8%. In all cases
of divergence, manual dual-coding by the authors prevailed as the authoritative classiﬁcation. Both
authors independently reviewed each ﬂagged case against the original article, and consensus was
reached through discussion. GPT-5 outputs thus served as a triangulation mechanism to identify
potential oversights in manual coding rather than as a replacement for human interpretive judgment.
Evidence was analyzed through descriptive quantitative analysis of contextual information
complemented by thematic qualitative analysis of research aims, methodological approaches, and case
study focus. Results and implications were formulated following Phase 2, post-complementary domains
analysis, mapping gaps and opportunities.
3.2 Phase 2: Complementary AI in Branding and Public Diplomacy Domains Literature
Phase 2 compared AI's integration in place branding with advancements in the ﬁelds of branding and
public diplomacy. Sources were identiﬁed through purposive sampling via manual search on the
SCOPUS database, using the Boolean keywords "Artiﬁcial Intelligence" AND "Branding" OR "Public
Diplomacy," limited to institutionally accessible publications. Inclusion criteria required sources to be
peer-reviewed review articles published in English between 2020 and 2025, addressing AI integration
within branding and public diplomacy.
Three review articles were identiﬁed for branding and another three for public diplomacy. Given that
public diplomacy and place branding are not merely adjacent but convergent ﬁelds sharing the same
scholarly community and publication venue (Anholt 2006; Florek and Pamment 2024), six additional
articles were included from the special issue "The End of Diplomacy? ChatGPT, Generative AI and the
Future of Digital Diplomacy" (Place Branding and Public Diplomacy, Vol. 21, No. 3). Three publications
from the special issue were excluded due to restricted institutional access. Altogether, the analytical
corpus comprised 68 articles, reﬂected in Table 1 below.
Data extraction was conducted by the ﬁrst author against ﬁve dimensions derived directly from the
critical gaps identiﬁed in Phase 1: (1) advanced AI technologies and capabilities reported, (2)

Results
4.1 Research Contextual Characteristics
The 56 studies reviewed in Phase 1 demonstrate a strong empirical orientation: 75.0% (N=42) are
empirical, 17.9% (N=10) are theoretical, and 7.1% (N=4) are methodological, focusing on developing AI-
related methodologies for the domain.
Regarding temporal evolution, publication output across the years, accelerated markedly from 2019,
peaking at 10 studies in 2022 (Figure 2). It is important to highlight an acceleration of publication output
from 2019 onwards, peaking in 2022 (N=10, 17.9%). During the early years (2010–2018), output
remained scattered at one to two studies annually, whereas the period 2019–2024 demonstrates
consistent production of ﬁve to ten studies per year, indicating progressive consolidation of the ﬁeld.

4.3 TECHNOLOGY AND DATA SOURCES
Text mining and information extraction are the most frequently employed techniques across the 42
empirical studies (23.2%, N=13), followed by sentiment analysis (16.1%, N=9) and natural language
processing (10.7%, N=6).
Three methodological milestones in AI adoption emerge from the temporal analysis of these
technologies, as represented in ﬁgure 4: early years (2013–2018), AI adoption (2019–2022), and
advanced integration (2023–present). During the early years phase, the literature was predominantly
characterized by sentiment analysis and text mining, with sparse and irregular output. In the AI Adoption
phase, these foundational techniques consolidated while NLP, machine learning, and multimodal
analysis began to emerge. Most recently, the Advanced Integration phase is marked by the adoption of
more complex machine learning models, large language models (LLMs), and more sophisticated AI
frameworks.
 Regarding data sources, Social Media constitutes the dominant primary source (50.0%, N=21), followed
by Travel Review Platforms (21.4%, N=9) and Online Media including Web Content and News Sites
(11.9%, N=5). Surveys account for 7.1% (N=3), with the remaining studies drawing on News Articles,
Travel Blogs, Book Text, and Mobile Applications (2.4%, N=1 each). Together these account for all 42
empirical studies. Within the 21 Social Media Studies, Twitter/X is the most frequently used platform
(42.9%, N=9), followed by Instagram (28.6%, N=6), YouTube and Facebook equally (14.3%, N=3 each),
and Sina Weibo (9.5%, N=2).
4.4 Gap identiﬁcation and adjacent research ﬁelds insights analysis
Page 14/27

Based on Phase 1 analysis, we identiﬁed ﬁve critical gap domains: Technological, methodological,
geographical, theoretical, and ethical. Phase 2 then examined how neighboring domains address these
gaps. Table 2 synthesizes the integrated ﬁndings.
At a technological level, there is a clear text-based dominance; sentiment analysis, text mining, and NLP
account for the majority of AI implementations. Only 1.8% of studies (N=1) engage with generative AI
through LLMs, indicating near-absent adoption of LLMs for content creation or scenario modelling.
Furthermore, Computer vision, multimodal AI, and predictive analytics remain virtually absent, limiting
analysis of increasingly visual digital environments. Similarly, methodological narrowness also exists.
Research designs are dominantly single-case and single data-platform concentrated (i.e., a single social
media platform). Furthermore, no longitudinal studies were found so far.
Regarding geographical limitations, on the one hand, 57.2% of studies focus on Western and East Asian
contexts. Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East remain largely unexamined; showing a Western/East
Asian concentration. On the other hand, the AI systems training on culturally homogeneous datasets risk
producing biased outputs that misrepresent or marginalise underrepresented cultural narratives, which
has not been challenged in the current research.
At a theoretical level, 39.3% of studies are situated within tourism and destination marketing, resulting in
a narrow disciplinary lens for understanding AI's potential across the full spectrum of place branding
applications. Furthermore, AI is predominantly employed instrumentally for data extraction and
classiﬁcation without engagement with theories from the other dimensions of place branding.
Finally, the literature so far adopts managerial perspectives prioritising eﬃciency, targeting, and
optimisation over social responsibility, cultural representation, or community impact. Critical ethical
thinking is currently absent; algorithmic bias, data privacy, representational consequences of AI-
generated narratives, and AI's potential to amplify power asymmetries in place brand discourse remain
unaddressed.

The cross-domain comparison reveals that the gaps identiﬁed in Phase 1 reﬂect broader challenges

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
This study provides a comprehensive mapping of artiﬁcial intelligence applications in place branding
research. This study makes three interconnected contributions to place branding scholarship. First, it
establishes the ﬁrst empirical baseline of artiﬁcial intelligence adoption in the domain setting the state
of the art. Second, it identiﬁes not merely that capability gaps exist in the domain but potential
expansion directions through comparative analysis with branding and public diplomacy literature. Finally,
it translates these ﬁndings into a ten-point research agenda for future research in place branding.
First, the results of this investigation compel a critical change of lens by the researchers in the ﬁeld: from
a focused to a holistic and integrative perspective. The complexity of the place branding domain (e.g.,
the diverse reality of places’ activity and landscape, the participation of the different stakeholders, etc.)
has long been recognized as a primary source of research challenges or this unique category of brands
(Zenker et al. 2017;  Kavaratzis and Kalandides 2015). Due to this complexity, the theory's development
has faced methodological constraints and has often been contingent on evidence from speciﬁc cases,
while remaining bold in aims and ambition (Lucarelli and Berg 2011; Swain et al. 2024). In other words,
the ﬁeld’s theoretical core has evolved in an inductive way; from speciﬁc, single-source data to broader,
more ambitious paradigms.
The evidence presented from the Place Branding literature indicates that a case-centric orientation
persists in shaping AI in Place Branding research, even as these AI technologies transcend the empirical
limitations traditionally associated with the domain (Liu et al. 2021). Adopting a holistic lens when
addressing AI-related place branding challenges is crucial to ensuring continued relevance in a rapidly
Page 17/27

shifting ﬁeld. Beyond the long-standing need for comprehensive research, AI provides both the urgent
impetus and the technical capacity (i.e., solutions) to conduct such studies at scale.
Furthermore, the cross-domain evidence shows that several critical challenges posed by AI are shared
with adjacent ﬁelds. The dominance of Western and North-Asia cases (Ilcic et al. 2025), the algorithmic
bias intrinsic to AI training datasets (Couldry and Mejias 2019), the constraints of fundamental theories
preceding AI (Varsha et al. 2021), or the little ethical reﬂection about AI impact and integration (Ilcic et al.
2025; Aizenberg and Van den Hoven 2020; Huang 2025) are challenges shared with public diplomacy
and branding ﬁelds.
Nevertheless, valuable insights can be learned from adjacent disciplines, particularly regarding.
Multimodal integration, generative content creation, predictive modelling, and real-time tracking are
already being implemented (Tuo et al. 2025; Musaiqer and Hamdan 2023; Fredheim and Pamment
2025). Similarly, advanced architectures supporting complex decision-making, stakeholder network
analysis, and scenario simulation (Mostafaei et al. 2025), and updated frameworks that capture
stakeholder complexity (Di Martino and Ford 2025;   Ilcic et al. 2025; Bano et al. 2025) can inspire
pathways for future place branding research
What is ultimately at stake are not incidental outcomes of technological systems, but structural changes
and a broad range of methodological possibilities that AI technologies bring along and can be leveraged
to address historical challenges of the place branding research.
5.7 Limitations
Several constraints should be acknowledged. Reliance on SCOPUS as the sole database and restriction
to English-language publications risks excluding non-Western scholarship, a limitation especially
noteworthy given the geographic bias the study itself identiﬁes. Phase 2's purposive sampling of three AI
and branding reviews, three AI and public diplomacy reviews, and six special issue articles provides
illustrative rather than exhaustive cross-domain coverage. Finally, technological developments in
generative AI and large language models advance faster than publication cycles; the ﬁeld's practical
adoption is likely more advanced than the published literature reviewed here reﬂects.
FUTURE RESEARCH AGENDA
Drawing on the cross-domain gap analysis, synthesized in (Table 2), the study presents a ten-point
research agenda for advancing AI integration in place branding research.
1. Multimodal AI Integration
Developing uniﬁed frameworks integrating natural language processing, computer vision, and video
analysis would unlock richer place understanding. Furthermore, generative AI, multimodal AI, and
predictive analytics remain absent in the research.
Page 18/27

2. Cross-Platform Intelligence Systems
Research concentrates narrowly on two platforms (i.e., Twitter and Trip Advisor), overlooking the
diversiﬁed digital ecosystem shaping brand discovery. Cross-platform frameworks would reveal how
place narratives are constructed and contested across distinct audiences.
3. Longitudinal AI Tracking Systems
No longitudinal studies exist in the ﬁeld. Without temporally extended designs, analysis produces static
snapshots unable to explain how place reputations form, stabilize, or recover over time.
4. Privacy-Conscious Personalisation Frameworks
Hyper-personalisation capabilities from broader branding remain untranslated to place contexts.
Community identities and public data raise distinct ethical stakes requiring dedicated governance.
5. Generative AI for Culturally Authentic Brands
Generative AI tools can produce place narratives at scale but risk homogenising place identities due to
geographical and training biases. Research must examine whether culturally embedded model ﬁne-
tuning can preserve local uniqueness.
6. Human–AI Collaboration Models
Place branding requires cultural sensitivity and democratic accountability that automation cannot
replicate alone. Productive human–AI partnerships demand dedicated frameworks calibrated to multi-
stakeholder governance.
7. Multi-Stakeholder AI Methodologies
Reducing place branding to tourist behaviour eliminates the co-creative roles other stakeholders play.
Multi-stakeholder methods are needed to expose power dynamics and narrative competition invisible to
current approaches.
8. Algorithmic Bias Detection and Mitigation
Systems trained on Western and East Asian data risk marginalising destinations across other regions.
Equitable AI requires representative training data.
Page 19/27

9. Ethical AI Implementation Standards
Place branding shapes how cities and nations are perceived globally with consequences for investment,
migration, and community identity. Algorithmic bias, opacity, and eﬃciency-ﬁrst priorities pose risks the
ﬁeld has yet to govern.
10. Interdisciplinary Theoretical Integration
AI is deployed instrumentally rather than theoretically, applied without engaging what it reveals about
place brand formation. Bridging and examining AI’s role across communication studies, urban
governance, cultural geography, and AI ethics would build a comprehensive understanding of AI’s role in
place branding.
