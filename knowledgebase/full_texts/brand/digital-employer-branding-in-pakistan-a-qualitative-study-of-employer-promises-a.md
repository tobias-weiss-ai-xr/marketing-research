Employer branding has traditionally emphasized the employment value proposition offered
to permanent, on -site employees. Yet the expansion of gig work, contract -based
employment and remote work, together with the reputational consequences of public digital
communication and organizational crises, challenges the adequacy of this conventional
model. This article examines how firms in Pakistan can adapt employer branding across
nontraditional work ar rangements, crisis conditions, digital communication environments,
and expectations concerning diversity, inclusion and corporate social responsibility (CSR).
The study is framed as a qualitative multiple -case inquiry involving organizations from
technology, manufacturing and service sectors, including large firms and small and medium-
sized enterprises. The proposed evidence base comprises semi -structured interviews with
human-resource professionals, organizational leaders and relevant employees,
complemented by publicly available employer -brand material and digital content.
Reflexive thematic analysis is used to organize the inquiry around four themes: (1) adapting
employer branding for nontraditional work arrangements; (2) digital media’s influence on
employer-brand perception; (3) resilience in employer branding during crises; and (4)
societal responsibility and ethical employer branding. Recent research indicates that digital
employer branding increasingly depends on credibility, employee -generated conten t and
platform-sensitive communication, while CSR signals are unlikely to compensate for
inconsistent employee treatment. Research on gig workers further indicates the importance
of psychological -contract fulfilment, identification and job satisfaction. Th e article brings
these developments together in the Pakistani context and outlines implications for a more
inclusive, evidence -based employer -brand strategy. The four themes constitute the
analytical structure of the manuscript and were validated against t he final interview
transcripts.

Introduction
Employer branding refers to the meanings and employment -related associations that an
organization creates and that shape how it is perceived as a place to work. It connects human -
resource management, organizational identity, and external reputation, which is the communication
of the employment experience that an organization provides to current or prospective employees
(Backhaus & Tikoo, 2004). Employer branding is not just recruitment advertising; the values that
are communicated outwardly must also be mir rored in work design, management practices,
employee treatment, and development opportunities.
The nature of the employment relationship has changed. In addition to full -time and permanent
positions, companies are now increasingly using independent contractors, temporary staff, platform-
mediated workers and remote or hybrid workers. These arrangements can create new opportunities
for skill development and flexibility, but can also reduce the daily relationships that create an
employee's sense of belonging t o an organization. The question now becomes not only how
organizations secure labour, but how they are able to convey a legitimate job offer to those whose
contract, location, length of service and access to the organization’s resources can vary greatly.
Meanwhile, employer -brand information is no longer controlled solely through organizational
websites and recruitment materials. A history of organizational activity, maintained by employees,
applicants, former employees, online communities and third-party review sites, is a part of the public
record. According to Kaur, Lal, and Verma (2026), some of the key characteristics of current
employment branding in social media platforms are authenticity, transparency, and employee -
generated content and platform -specific communication. In Pakistan, Shahid and Ayyaz (2023)
discovered that perceived employer attractiveness and corporate reputation were correlated with
intention to apply and that the relationship between perceived corporate reputation and
application intention was moderated by social media use. While their study provides valuable local
data, it may not be directly applicable when considering the interpretation of the employer brand
by incumbent staff, gig workers and remote contractors.
This communication challenge becomes more apparent during a crisis. When a disruption occurs, job
seekers and employees might be more focused on an employer's communication about uncertainty
than its stated values, and more focused on how an employer protects workers, communicates reliable
information and treats workers with less bargaining power. The study on employer communication
before and after COVID -19 outbreak revealed that during the crisis the relationships between
internal communication and employer attractiveness have shifted – changed the significance of the
climate of communication and availability of information about the organization (Tallac Ver čič &
Špoljarić, 2023). There is also some evidence that warm and competent crisis responses can convey
organizational trustworthiness and influence applicant attraction (Kanwal et al., 2022).
This article focuses on the dynamic relationship between the employment experience and the signals
(e.g. the ways in which the employment experience is represented). It focuses on P akistani firms
operating in the technology, manufacturing, and service sectors. The context is important since there

Problem statement and research motivation
Research on employer branding has provided insights into organizational attractiveness, employer
image, and recruitment, However, the literature gives uneven attention to workers in nontraditional
employment arrangements and employer -brand claims and shifting workplace realities. Tran,
Collin-Lachaud and Pham (2024) chart the development of employer -brand scholarship and
identify opportunities for further research to cover topics and geography not yet explored. There is a
specific need to link digital reputation, nontraditional employment, crisis responses, and social
responsibility in the same contextual analysis.
The present inquiry is motivated by three issues. First, the benefits and career paths  developed for
employer branding could be different from those of contractors, gig workers, or remote workers.
Second, social media and other third -party websites give workers the opportunity to compare
corporate assertions with informal word of mouth and public discussions of organizational practices,
diminishing the organization's ability to control how it is understood. Third, CSR and diversity
statements can be seen as hollow when they are not paired with equitable working practices and
open and honest communication within the organization. Current studies, however, challenge the
universality of the effect of CSR as an employer -brand signal as applicant responses may differ
according to career orientation and educational level and instrumental aspects of the job (Buzzao &
Rizzi, 2024).
 These issues inform the context -sensitive analysis of the way firms build, share and adapt their
employer brand as circumstances at the workplace, in the wider community and in relation to their
reputation change.

Literature review and conceptual framing
 Employer branding as an employment promise
Employer branding offers an opportunity to express the distinct functional, economic, developmental,
and symbolic values that an employer can offer workers. According to Backhaus and Tikoo (2004),
the concept of employer branding is related to the development of an external employer image and
its internal organizational effects. To be believable, a brand proposition must be lived by the workers
who experience it. Mismatches between job postings and workplace experience can decrease trust
and lead to negative reviews, especially when existing and past employe es are able to post reviews
that are accessible to prospective workers.
It is useful to distinguish between employer -brand claims and employer -brand enactment. Claims
consist of statements about flexibility, career advancement, inclusion, well -being and pu rpose;
enactment refers to the scheduling, compensation, management, development and support that
workers experience. This distinction is particularly important when employer branding extends
beyond permanent employees. When contractors or platform workers participate in the organization
but their involvement is not acknowledged or they receive insufficient information or organizational
support the public brand may not reflect their involvement with the company.
Adapting employer branding for nontraditional work arrangements
 Nontraditional jobs are diverse in nature. Gig workers may be recruited through digital platforms or
work for specific projects; temporary and independent workers can be required to be part of an
organization but away from the physical workplace; or remote workers can be formally employed
but away from the physical workplace. The level of autonomy, security, access to benefits,
managerial visibility, and social connection vary among these arrangements. An employer -
branding strategy that treats these workers as a single group will likely miss some nuance in its
expectations and experiences.

The importance of the employment relationship in a task-oriented economy is better explained with
recent evidence on gig work. Based on studies from ri de-hailing and food delivery, Yu and Abdul
Hamid (2024) found that transactional psychological-contract fulfilment was associated with lower
turnover intention, with organizational identification and job satisfaction as intervening variables.
While this is not evidence from Pakistan, it does indicate that clarity around payment, expectations
around the task, treatment, communication and recognition may be relevant to the relationship
between gig workers and organizations. This is evidence of a broader persp ective on employer
branding – the employment promise for nontraditional workers should reflect actual employment
conditions and relational quality of engagement, and not mimic the value proposition developed for
permanent workers.
Implementation may involv e the following for Pakistani companies: Clearly defining contract
duration, workload, payment schedules, evaluation criteria, flexibility, access to training, and
avenues for addressing concerns. Digital onboarding, regular supervisory check -ins, clear
communication and recognition from the contractors themselves regarding the project's work can
clarify the organization's values without signaling that all forms of work are the same. Firms should
avoid promotional claims that imply equal treatment of all wo rkers when contingent employees do
not receive the same essential safety information or are treated inconsistently.
Based on this, the following sensitizing proposition can be drawn from the literature: When the
employment proposition that is communicated is specific to the work relationship and aligns with
the received treatment, it is more likely to be meaningful for nontraditional workers. This is an
interpretive proposition, rather than a testable hypothesis in the present design, for qualitative
inquiry.
Theme 2: Digital media’s influence on employer-brand perception
 Digital communication has transformed applicants' experiences and perceptions of employers.
Company websites and recruitment notices remain relevant, but they now coexist with reviews,
employee posts, professional sites and networks, discussion boards, and third -party rankings. This is
not a one-way communication process; rather, it is a continuous relationship-building process called
employer-brand management. In a systematic review of soc ial media and employer branding, Kaur
et al. (2026) found that the main practices are currently focused on platform -specific recruitment,
employees' own content, authenticity and transparency. The review also notes that despite efforts to
“move beyond” theory, it remains fragmented, and that the literature is not as diverse as it should be
with respect to context, underscoring the need for further research on these processes in settings not
well represented in the literature.
In Pakistan, Shahid and Ayyaz ( 2023) found that prospective applicants associate employer
attractiveness and corporate reputation with intention to apply and that the use of social media
could influence the relationship between employer reputation and intention to apply. These results
do not indicate that a high number of positive posts will automatically create a positive employer

brand. Instead, these findings highlight the importance of the information applicants encounter, its
perceived trustworthiness, and its relevance to their employment priorities.
Consistency, responsiveness and employee voice, impression management, should then be the focus
of a digital strategy if credibility is to be maintained. When responding to employer information,
employers can use accessible information to describe arrangements for work, development
opportunities, inclusion practices and CSR commitments, but must also respond in a way that is
constructive when questions are asked and accept that improvements are necessary in areas that are
not satisfactor y. Different platforms serve different audiences and functions. Therefore, the same
message should not be expected on a professional-network post as one would expect on a job-board
description and employee-review site. A consistently polished organizational narrative may appear
less authentic when it conflicts with workers' accounts.
The proposition that follows is that digital communication as part of em ployer branding is more
likely to help with recruitment if it is transparent, platform -specific and aligned to the experience
employees have when working.
Theme 3: Resilience in employer branding during crises
Crises—whether public -health emergencies, economic shocks, security incidents, technological
disruptions or supply -chain interruptions —can expose inconsistencies between organizational
values and actions. Crises also change the information that workers and applicants need. During
periods of uncert ainty, messages regarding safety, continuity, workload, compensation, remote
arrangements, and support can be more influential than standard promotional messages.
The relationship between internal communication and employer attractiveness was found to change
over time around the first outbreak of COVID -19 by Tkalac Ver čič and Špoljarić (2023). Their
findings highlight the significance of communication climate and the availability of information in
the organization during a crisis. Similarly, Kanwal et al. ( 2022) reported that those who found
competent responses to be more attractive than no response found warm crisis responses to be
particularly attractive, as these signals were perceived as warm, competent and trustworthy. All
these studies suggest that the re is an informational aspect of crisis communication, as well as a
relational aspect.
Employer-brand resilience should therefore involve more than maintaining a positive public image.
Employer-brand resilience requires timely communication, acknowledgment  of uncertainty,
expressions of concern, explanations of decisions, and consistency between public statements and
internal actions. Organizations should also share lessons learned from previous disruptions and how
they will safeguard workers in the event of future shocks, if possible. The COVID-19 pandemic is an
appropriate reference point, but an assessment of organizational resilience should consider overall
crisis preparedness, not just the pandemic.

Theme 4: Societal responsibility and ethical employer branding
 CSR and sustainability are now being seen as part of employer-brand narratives. Organizations can
share their environmental vision, community involvement, ethical behavior and diversity and
inclusion as part of a larger organization's mission. While these signals may be important to
applicants, recent evidence suggests not all CSR activities have the same influence on every
applicant. Gintal ė et al. (2024) uncovered a positive relationship between environmental and
philanthropic CSR and employer attractiveness, whereas the relationship between ethical CSR and
employers' attractiveness was less pronounced, while their study also cast doubt on the assumption
that age groups differ in terms of interest in CSR. Buzzao and Rizzi (2024) demonstrate that social
identities, educational levels and career orientations affect the attractiveness of CSR -related and
other symbolic attributes.
Therefore, the relationship between CSR and employer branding is not straightforward. Employee
treatment, development opportunities, and internal s upport may be perceived negatively when it
comes to external community or environmental initiatives. Other studies on CSR and organizational
attractiveness have focused on how organizational trust and perceived organizational support
function as linkages between perceived CSR and organizational attractiveness (Francis et al., 2024).
In a case study of an international corporation, the relationship between internal and external CSR
and sustainable development practices with employer branding is also explored (Andruszkiewicz et
al., 2024).
Responsible employer branding can involve actions taken externally through social and
environmental initiatives and actions taken internally through employment practices in the case of
Pakistani organizations. The latter can  encompass fair recruitment practices, accessible grievance
procedures, equitable advancement and a safe environment, as well as consistency between
commitments to inclusion and the opportunities actually provided. The workforce should not be the
target of CSR campaigns simply because they can be reached, but also because they can support an
organization's claims while also identifying its shortcomings. The corresponding proposition is that
when the external commitments are congruent with fair internal practices, and can be substantiated
with open evidence, CSR further enhances the credibility of the employer's brand.
Integrative perspective and contribution
 The four themes are interdependent. The type of promises that an employer makes matters as much
as t he type of work arrangement, especially when the work is done through digital platforms;
pressure during the crisis calls the promises into question; and the promises of CSR and inclusion are
substantively credible only when visible in the work. The articl e thus understands employer
branding as a process of a dynamic alignment between (a) the employment proposition, (b) the lived

Research methodology
Research design
 Employer-branding practices vary across organizations, making a qualitative multiple -case study
design appropriate. Both the structure and the design allow for comparisons between organizations,
while maintaining focus on the impact of organization size, sector and work arrangement on
communication and employee experience. Organizations in the Pakistani study setting include
technology, manufacturing an d services companies, including large companies and small and
medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). The digital readiness, remote and contract work, and the
transparency of CSR work practices can vary among these sectors.
The study targeted 20 manufacturing org anizations, with three managers interviewed in each
organization, resulting in 60 interviews. One human-resource manager, one supply-chain manager,
and one marketing manager were interviewed in each organization, to capture a variety of functional
perspectives. This allowed for the phenomenon to be explored from complementary managerial
perspectives and for similarities and differences between the organizations to be identified. The
selection of participants was based on their professional roles or relevanc e of their experience to the
research goals. The sample adequacy in qualitative studies was discussed in terms of the relevance of
participants, specificity of the research aims and depth of the data, similar to Malterud et al. (2016),
rather than quantity of samples.
Sampling and participants
Purposive sampling is proposed to recruit participants with direct knowledge of employer -brand
strategy or first -hand experience of how the employer brand is communicated and enacted.
Potential participants include human -resource managers, organizational l eaders, marketing or
communications managers, line managers and employees. Supply-chain managers may be included
where their responsibilities intersect with employment communication, distributed teams or
contractor management.

Data collection
 The primary data sources were structured and semi -structured interviews. The interview guide
focused on specific examples rather than policy statements alone. Questions cover what the
organization says about its employee value proposition, the differences between permanent, contract
and remote employees, channels through which applicants and employees communicate, how the
organization reacted in the event of a major crisis, how concerns are addressed by the organization
and how the organization’s commitments to CSR, diversity and inclusion are realized in practice.
The evidence collected through interviews was confirmed and supplemented by recruitment pages,
job advertisements, corporate websites, CSR or sustainability reports, public employer -brand posts,
publicly acce ssible employee reviews and other relevant organizational documents. Digital data
were collected ethically and in accordance with platform terms, while the collection of personal
information was minimized.
Informed consent was obtained, and interviews were transcribed accurately. Interviews were held in
English and/or Urdu (or other language) and the researcher recorded the translation process and
maintained the original text where relevant to the interpretation of the interview. Researchers are
able to id entify direct quotations from paraphrases and were able to keep a clear connection
between the final themes and the source data.
Data analysis: reflexive thematic analysis
The analysis is conducted based on reflexive thematic analysis, which is based on th e experience-
sharing of Byrne (2022) and Braun and Clarke (2022). This way, themes are created by continuous
interaction with the data and the researcher's interpretation rather than being identified as “common
categories” or “agreed results” with inter -coder consensus. As these researchers found, t he analytic
procedure should be reported clearly, and should be tailored to the specific study and not simply said
to be finished before the data is analyzed.
1. Familiarization: reading and rereading transcripts and documents to take initial impressions, record
any contradictions and look for any noteworthy patterns.
2. Initial coding: allot short codes to segments that relate to the research questions, such as expected
topics as well as unexpected aspects that come up in the participants' accounts.
3. Developing candidate themes: grouping codes into patterns that convey an analytic claim, rather
than just a topic label.
4. Reviewing themes: compare each candidate theme with the coded extracts and the wider dataset;
look at overlaps, exceptions and counter-examples.
5. Identifying and specifying themes and naming themes: identify and explain the main organizing
idea of each theme and how it links to the research question(s) and other themes.
6. Writing the an alysis: construct a coherent interpretative analysis linking claims to strategically
selected, anonymized extracts from the transcripts and relevant literature with clear indications of
how findings confirm, extend or challenge previous research.

 Thematic Analysis: Four-Theme Synthesis
This section brings together the four the mes identified in the study and considers their relationship
with recent research. The discussion maintains the original thematic focus while examining the
connections between employer -brand communication, organizational practices, and workers'
experiences. Accounts of organizational and participant experiences are considered in relation to the
final dataset.
Theme 1: Adapting Employer Branding for Nontraditional Work Arrangements
The first theme discusses the limitations of using a single employer -brand pr oposition for all
employee groups. Flexibility, work -life balance and organizational belonging are all critical in
remote work, temporary employment, and the gig economy. These workers may have limited access
to information, benefits, development opportuni ties, and informal workplace relationships,
compared to permanent employees who work in a shared workplace. This means that a commitment
by an employer to include or support a worker may mean different things to those who are employed
under different conditions. Research on gig workers' psychological contracts provides further insight
into this issue. Yu and Abdul Hamid (2024) concluded that lower turnover intention could be
explained by organizational identification and job satisfaction as a result of tran sactional and
relational expectations fulfilment. In the Pakistani context, these findings suggest the potential
relevance of making explicit employment conditions, ensuring regular payments, consistent
communication, respectful treatment, and reasonable e xpectations of support within the
organization. It is not a requirement that all work contracts must offer the same terms. Instead,
organizations should explicitly communicate the conditions of each of these agreements, and ensure
that their promises reflect their actual capacity. A key conflict is between flexibility as an attractive
feature of employment and the insecurity that can be a side effect of alternative work. Employment
from home and/or on a "gig" basis might lead to increased independence but also to an unpredictable
working schedule and income, to the absence of work and to career growth. It's pertinent to emphasize

Journal of Social Research Development, Volume 7, Issue 8, October, 2026
                 543
that flexibility is not always a good thing, and concealing this flexibility without acknowledging
these limitations can result in  unrealistic expectations. Employers must, therefore, inform the
employees about the opportunities and limitations of each. It is also important that workers have
opportunities to discuss concerns about their working conditions. Analysis of this theme take s into
account the connection between the formal employer -brand messages and the workers' actual
experiences. Some extra points of comparison can be obtained from the recruitment adverts,
managerial descriptions of the onboarding, feedback, recognition and  workplace support, and from
the onboarding, feedback, recognition and workplace support descriptions from the workers
themselves. To identify if the employment offer for the organization is aligned with the experiences
of different groups in the labor mar ket, the two sources of data can be studied together. The core is
the relevance of employment conditions and organizational support for employees in various
organizational arrangements to ensure an employer brand that is flexible. Nontraditional workers
thus are stakeholders with unique expectations and cannot simply be viewed as an additional
audience.
Theme 2: Digital Media's Influence on Employer-Brand Perception
The second theme is related to the shifting nature of digital communication in the context o f
employer reputation. In the past, there was more control over what information was provided to
prospective employees. In today's job market, professional networks, job sites, social media and
independent review sites give job seekers opportunities to obt ain information about an employer
from multiple sources. These channels offer a means for sharing organizational values, job
opportunities, and culture. They also help to highlight the differences between official messages and
employee experiences. Authent icity, transparency, employee -generated content and platform -
specific communication are some of the essential features that are gaining greater value in social -
media employer branding, according to Kaur et al. (2026). Evidence from Pakistan further support s
the relevance of digital communication. Shahid and Ayyaz (2023) link corporate reputation and
employer attractiveness with the intention to apply and suggest a potential role for social media in
this relationship. This information shows that digital visi bility and credibility are distinct
considerations.  Although a strong online presence can attract attention, the credibility of the
employer is dependent on the credibility of the messages they share – if they are reliable, relevant
and consistent with the information that is available. For organizations, the challenge is to remain in
the digital realm but not to be all things online. Employers can post workplace policies and
opportunities on official web sites and social -media accounts. However, the compa ny's own
narratives, as well as public disclosures, may provide conflicting opinions. Lack of recognition of
negative feedback can contribute to a lack of trust in an organization’s dedication to problem-solving
in the workplace. A positive response would confirm concerns, provide an organization's position, and
describe steps taken to resolve the concerns. There also needs to be a focus on the differences in access
to digital devices and platform preferences in the Pakistani context. Applicants could have been
processing the information in a different manner, and they may have been using different
information sources online. Depending on their digital literacy, the role they have, their age, sector
and their professional profile, they may use different chan nels. The communication of the

Theme 3: Resilience in Employer Branding During Crisis
 The third theme looks at employer brand credibility in times of disruption. COVID -19 brought
workplace safety, employee well -being, remote work, and communication during uncertainty into
greater focus. While these challenges are still applicable in public -health crises, organizational
crises are not confined to public -health emergencies. Economic  instability, supply chain issues,
operational issues, cyber security incidents and unforeseen market changes can also place pressure
on employers. In times like these, organizational judgment can be a more reliable indicator of
employer values than usual promotional communications. Research has shown that crises can
influence how existing and prospective employees perceive an organization. The results of the study
conducted by Tkalac Verčič and Špoljarić (2023) showed that relationships between the dimensions
of internal communication and the dimensions of employer attractiveness shifted around the
beginning of the COVID-19 outbreak, and in particular, the communication climate and the access
to information became important. According to Kanwal et al. (2022), crisis responses that showed
warmth and competence were found to make applicants more interested in applying than any other
group which did not respond; the warmth was given special importance. These findings combined
show that there is a need for dependable information and noticeable concern for people impacted by
organizational decisions in order to communicate effectively during a crisis. Communication alone is
insufficient to maintain credibility. Companies can advertise that they priorities employee well -
being, but at the same time make alterations to pay rates, working schedules, shifts, or work locations
that employees feel are contrary to their stated commitment in this regard. Other times, it m ight be
inevitable to have an operational limitation, but maintaining the trust of the group can be achieved
by communicating promptly while acknowledging uncertainty, and by letting them know why a
decision was made that they will find difficult. What mat ters is that what is said is reflected in
managerial behavior and in the way various groups of workers are treated. Similarly, the information
sources and pre-existing corporate reputation along with the type of response that is taken can shape
the applicants' interpretation of organizational responsibility in times of crisis (Chang et al., 2023).
Learning from disruption is another aspect of resilience. Responding effectively to one crisis does not
necessarily mean that an organization is ready for the nex t. Employers can enhance their readiness
efforts by establishing communication protocols, defining roles, developing employee feedback
systems and reviewing remote and emergency operations plans. It is also crucial to consider
temporary and contract worker s as well as whether they receive appropriate support and

                 545
information in a timely manner. Such agreements are especially important where staff members are
geographically dispersed or has different contract requirements. As such, the theme is defined as the
ability of an organization to maintain the credibility of its employer brand despite a crisis by
communicating truthfully, acting fairly, making responsible decisions and learning from
organizational experiences. The analysis does not suggest that resilie nce is about maintaining a
flawless public image, but that the credibility of efforts to "be resilient" might also hinge on the
response of employers when troubled times become evident. Organizational experiences in which
workers and managers hold differen t perceptions are particularly relevant to understanding these
processes.
Theme 4: Societal Responsibility and Ethical Employer Branding
 The fourth theme examines how corporate social responsibility influences perceptions of an
employer (CSR), diversity, inclusion and fair employment practice. However, previous studies
suggest that CSR can have an impact on the attractiveness of an employer, though this effect is not
consistent. Gintal ė et al. (2024) found that positive relationships between environmental and
philanthropic CSR and employer attractiveness were identified, and the influence of ethical CSR
was less pronounced. Buzzao and Rizzi (2024) determined that applicants' social identities,
educational backgrounds, and career interests resulted in varyin g reactions to CSR and other
symbolic organizational features. These findings indicate that the perception of attractiveness of CSR
activities is influenced by certain characteristics and expectations of individuals who are evaluating
the CSR activities. T he importance of CSR for employer branding extends beyond public
communication that companies give regarding their environmental protection or community
involvement. This can be done through initiatives resonating with an organization’s values, but key
values can also be assessed by employees' perceptions of recruitment fairness, workplace safety,
employee development, inclusion and access to grievance mechanisms. Andruszkiewicz et al. (2024)
examines the link between CSR, sustainable -development practices and employer branding in an
international corporation. This study, in parallel with the results of Gintalė et al. (2024), confirms the
importance of considering various dimensions of organizational responsibility and rather than
treating CSR as a single, u niformly influential concept. When organizations commit to fairness and
inclusion in the public, credibility becomes particularly important. It is possible to foster an inclusive
culture in the workplace and exclude the temporary or contract employee from key information.
Likewise, charitable contributions or community programs alone cannot confirm fair employment
within the organization. These disconnects can impact the employer brand as employees can
compare what they see outside of work with what they see at work. Organizations must thus promote
CSR communication in action, in a realistic way, and with accountability. If there are shortfalls in
funds or operations, it might be easier to establish a goal and track progress than it would be to make
broad promises and then not deliver. The interpretation of CSR can also vary among the different
stakeholders of the organization. For managers, social responsibility can be seen as primarily a way
to enhance reputation and recruitment. Staff, however, may focus m ore on whether the workplace
decisions are based on the values that the organization shows in public. These differences are
significant for thematic interpretation as they can indicate the level of integration in organizational

Journal of Social Research Development, Volume 7, Issue 8, October, 2026
                 546
practices of responsibility.  They can also show if CSR is going primarily to outsiders or if there is a
different understanding of CSR among employees. This theme provides emphasis for consistency
between employer brand responsibility claims and their everyday employment practices. W hile
external initiatives can help build the brand, they cannot replace fair employment practices or
effective employee support. Specific, demonstrable commitments that address the needs of both
employees and the wider community provide a stronger foundation for employer-brand credibility.
Relationships Among the Four Themes
 Although the themes are presented separately, the issues addressed within them are closely
interconnected. While an organization can market flexible working arrangements in the digital
recruitment space (Theme 1), remote employees may report communication difficulties through
online platforms (Theme 2). In a crisis, commitment to inclusion and responsibility can be seen in
practice (Themes 3 and 4) through decisions made with regard to temporary or contract workers.
Public CSR initiatives may also contribute to a positive reputation if employees identify the same
values in their workplaces.
The relationships show that employer branding be explored across the employment lifecycle –
recruitment, induction, support, performance management and change. Cross -theme analysis
considers both reinforcing relationships and potential contradictions. For instance, a flexible job may
be discovered on a digital platform, which can raise awareness of fle xible employment, but at the
same time reveal disparities in employee benefits and support. Similarly, CSR activities encourage
applicants to join an organization without necessarily improving retention when employees
experience a gap between stated values and actual working conditions.
Discussion
The four themes Illustrate digital employer branding as a continuous cycle of organizational promises,
employment experiences, and public perception. This approach considers employer branding not
merely a communi cation activity but also the extent to which organizational practices support the
messages and promises given in the recruitment communications. The employers' commitments are
linked together by the concern of their credibility, when adapting to changing w ork arrangements,
to digital reputation and to crisis management, and in social responsibility.
The first contribution concerns the range of workers that should be taken into employer -brand
considerations. Although recruitment communication may primarily t arget permanent employees,
other workers such as temporary, remote or gig workers can face various employment scenarios and
fewer opportunities to engage in workplace culture. Expectation fulfilment and organizational
identification are demonstrated to be relevant to the attainment of the retention -related outcomes
in research on gig-worker psychological contracts (Yu & Abdul Hamid, 2024). This implies that the
promises by an employer should be judged with reference to the employment conditions under
which the employees work. A commitment that is meaningful for those with permanent employment

                 547
relations, may have different implications for workers in less stable or structured employment
relationships.
The second contribution relates to the dual role of digita l communication. Employers can
communicate with applicants and advertise job opportunities online, but they can also open
themselves up to independent analysis of their organization's claims. The study by Kaur et al. (2026)
reiterates the need for clear co mmunication and employee -generated content, and the study by
Shahid and Ayyaz (2023) offers evidence from a Pakistani perspective on the link between
employer reputation, social media usage, and application intentions. The results of this qualitative
analysis can inform the examination of stakeholder trust in specific sources, how they determine that
a message is authentic, and if management and employee accounts convey a consistent perspective
of the organization. The response to public criticism is also important as it can shape how stakeholders
view openness and accountability in the organization.
The third contribution relates to the resilience of employer brand in challenging organizational
conditions. When faced with crisis -related decisions, the relat ionship between values and practice
can become more obvious. Based on the findings of the previous studies, internal communication and
organizational responses to crisis are linked to the attractiveness of the employer and the perception
of applicants (Kan wal et al., 2022; Tkalac Ver čič & Špoljarić, 2023). The qualitative perspective
draws attention to how these processes work in specific contexts. Similar responses to the crisis from
organizations can be understood differently due to the timing of the mess age, the clarity of the
message, its treatment of different workforce groups, and perceptions of the fairness of the treatment.
This does not imply that crisis communication always improves employer attractiveness; rather, its
credibility depends on the nature and effectiveness of the organizational response.
The fourth contribution has to do with the conditions under which CSR enhances employer
reputation of the employer. Buzzao and Rizzi (2024) found that the perceived value of CSR is
dependent on the dim ension of the responsibility and characteristics of the applicants. Therefore,
CSR needs to be considered in the context of actual organizational behaviour, rather than as an
automatic means of increasing employer attractiveness for employers in all cases.  An employee's
account can tell whether actions are taken on the ground in respect of employment practices, in line
with sustainability, inclusion and social welfare pledges. Differences between managerial intentions
and employees' actual experiences can o ffer valuable information as to the authenticity of the
pledges.
It is in this Pakistani context that these relationships are to be understood. Expectations of employers
and employees and employer -brand practices can vary by size, sector and employment typ e, and
local economic and labor -market conditions can shape employer and employee expectations.
However, the results obtained from a qualitative sample cannot be generalized for all the firms in
Pakistan unless the research design allows for such generalization. They are more useful, however, in
describing a process that is specific to a particular environment and could be applicable to similar
emerging economies. This contribution is dependent on clear reporting of organizational

Theoretical and Practical Implications
Theoretical Implications
 The study builds up a framework of four analytical parts linking with the concepts of employment -
arrangement adaptation, digital reputation, crisis resilience, and societal responsibility. The
theoretical implication of its main is that the credibility of  the employer brand can be examined
based on the relationship between three elements: commitment communicated to the employees,
working conditions experienced by employees, and information that is used to evaluate the
organization by employees' stakeholders. The framework builds on the employer-brand approach of
Backhaus and Tikoo (2004) and draws upon recent research in the fields of digital communication,
gig work, organizational crisis and CSR. It also acknowledges that the significance of these elements
may differ in the context of organizations and jobs.
Another implication is to account for context in the creation of explanations of employer -brand
perceptions. The literature surveyed is rather limited in addressing the Pakistani context. The local
quantitative evidence presented by Shahid and Ayyaz (2023) is largely oriented towards
prospective workers rather than those who are permanent or temporary workers or other
nontraditional workers. A qualitative approach can offer further insights into the inte rpretation of
organizational messages, whose experiences are attended to, and the differences in perspectives of
occupational and organizational groups in the organization. Any contribution must be supported by
evidence and not just the geographical context.
Managerial Implications
Develop differentiated employer value propositions. Organizations should consider the distinct
expectations of permanent, temporary, remote, and gig workers when developing recruitment
messages and employment policies. Clear information about contractual conditions, benefits,
available support, flexibility, and potential limitations can help workers form realistic expectations.
Matching these commitments with actual employment arrangements is important for maintaining
confidence in the employer.
Strengthen digital employer-brand credibility. Employers should communicate consistently across
official websites, recruitment platforms, and social -media channels. Genuine employee
contributions can provide applicants with a more realistic understanding of workplace experiences.
Organizations should also respond constructively to public criticism and use recurring concerns to
identify areas where working conditions or internal communication require improvement.
Establish effective crisi s communication protocols. Clear responsibilities and established
communication procedures can help organizations provide accurate information during periods of

Journal of Social Research Development, Volume 7, Issue 8, October, 2026
                 549
disruption. Managers should communicate decisions promptly, explain their implications, and
acknowledge uncertainty when complete information is unavailable. Particular attention is needed
to ensure that permanent employees, temporary workers, gig workers, and remote staff are
appropriately informed about changes affecting their work.
Align CSR comm itments with employment practices. Organizations should ensure that public
commitments to social responsibility are reflected in internal policies and everyday decisions. Fair
recruitment, safe working conditions, inclusive opportunities, professional deve lopment, and
meaningful employee participation provide practical evidence of these commitments. An employer
brand is more likely to be perceived as credible when external CSR communication corresponds with
workers' actual experiences.
Limitations and Future Research
 The qualitative design does not aim to measure the prevalence of specific employer-brand practices
throughout Pakistan. Experiences and interpretations of employer-branding practices may differ by
industry, size of organization, occupation, geo graphic location and type of work. Participants may
also be hesitant to share sensitive information in the workplace, especially when organizational
hierarchies may influence their responses or concerns about job security. Digital content can provide
additional context, but might be dominated by individuals who are particularly motivated to share
their experiences.
Future studies could examine differences in perceptions of employers' brands between permanent
and contingent employees or long-term trends in the development of employers' online brands over
time. A comparative study of the reaction of organizations to various crisis types and how trust in the
organization is affected could also be undertaken. Cross -country comparisons involving other
emerging economies, could be used to recognize patterns and specific differences between them, but
not necessarily to extrapolate results to other economies. Longitudinal studies would be particularly
useful for assessing the credibility of CSR and inclusion commitm ents after people join the
organization and experience the practices firsthand.
Conclusion
 The connection between an employer's employment promises and the experiences of employees is a
growing influence on employer branding. There is need for more differ entiated commitments to
workers that are provided by nontraditional employment arrangements. Employer information and
employer activities on digital platforms is more widespread and visible to others. Communications
and employee support are put to further strain during times of crisis and the value of CSR rests on the
extent to which employment practices within the organization align with public pledges. This study
studies each theme individually, but offers a unified understanding of these developments in
Pakistani organizations. The bottom line here is that employer branding isn't all about convincing
potential job seekers to apply by communicating with them. This involves maintaining realistic
expectations and commitments, fair treatment of employees, and  responding appropriately when

communication and CSR (Buzzao & Rizzi, 2024). The empirical contribution is based upon the
degree of evidence provided in the study, which includes four themes and how well the study's
analysis explains the functioning of the four themes in organizational contexts and in the
employment contexts in Pakistan.
