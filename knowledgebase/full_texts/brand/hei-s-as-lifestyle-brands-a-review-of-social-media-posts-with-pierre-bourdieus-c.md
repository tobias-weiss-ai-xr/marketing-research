ABSTRACT
Purpose: The research focuses on lifestyle brands set out to identify the different financial displays of social class
and status associated with them . Lifestyle brands that  are focused on are important for the choices made.  Pierre
Bourdieu's Cultural Capital Theory illustrates how social inequalities are represented beyond money.  Bourdieu
argued that capital isn't just economic (money, property). It also comes in cultural, social, and symbolic forms.
These forms can be converted into one another. Cultural capital is the knowledge, skills, tastes, and credentials that
confer higher status in society.
Methodology:  This study employs a qualitative, theory -driven methodology grounded in Pierre Bourdieu’s Cultural
Capital Theory . Forty-eight (48)  higher education institutions were purposively selected to maximise variation
along Bourdieusian axes: institutional age (older vs newer), regional vs global orientation, and dominant
objectified capital style (heritage architecture vs  modern premium amenities). A total of 1200 posts were collected
from their public Facebook and Instagram pages ( 25 per institution) .
Findings: Institutions deploy objectified cultural capital (premium amenities, campus aesthetics) and appeal to
habitus (regional language use, alumni success narratives) across visual platforms like Instagram. Regional content
shared dispositions, while objectified displays presuppose middle -class ease. Alumni and graduation ceremonies
showcase institutionalised capital , filled with traditions, festivities, student life, institutional legacy and affiliation,
and economic success. Engagement reflects habitu s matching rather than neutral interest, revealing how lifestyle
branding reproduces class distinctions.
Originality:  Higher education has increasingly functioned as a lifestyle brand – a brand that is part of CV, reference
group and peers. The phenomenon that Bourdieu’s concept of cultural capital helps to serves as a mechanism of
social distinction. This study examine s how branding higher education as a lifestyle construct creates a unique
advocacy.

Pierre Bourdieu’s Cultural Capital Theory
Pierre Bourdieu’s Cultural Capital Theory explains
how people’s social advantages are shaped by non -
financial assets like education, skills, and cultural
knowledge. Cultural resources will help individuals
gain status and success in society. These are ofte n
passed down within families and perpetuate  social
inequality. Cultural capital is the knowledge, skills, and
even tastes that you acquire as a result of your social
standing. Cultural capital is an embodied state within
society. (Bourdieu, 1979)
Cultural Capital in an Objectified State: The capital that
is gained by the availability of physical objects that are:
Books, art or historical artifacts. The objects show
standing in society and communicate social status.
Cultural Capital in an Institutio nalized State:
Institutionalized cultural capital is a formal recognition
from an institution that a person has some asset. This is
usually in the form of a degree or certification
credential. Cultural capital is often formed with fami l y

The thematic analysis process was used. Analysis
provides insights. This study employed a theory -driven,
qualitative thematic analysis to systematically exami ne
social media content. The analysis was guided by the
principles of directed content analysis (Hsieh &
Shannon, 2005). The initial coding framework was
derived from Pierre Bourdieu's theory of cultural
capital. The process was not inductive (letting themes
emerge from the data) but rather deductive, using
Bourdieu's concepts. These codes served as a lens for
interpreting the social media posts. The analysis was
conducted on 1,200 visual and textual posts and their
associated comments. The procedure followed six
distinct phases, adapted from Braun and Clarke's
(2006) thematic analysis framework.
Phase 1: 1,200 social media posts (images, videos,
captions, hashtags, and comments) were reviewed
multiple times to gain holistic immersion, with
preliminary notes recorded.
Phase 2: A deductive coding framework was developed
based on Bourdieu's theoretical constructs. Data
segments were systematically labelled using
predetermined codes (e.g., CC -OBJ for Objectified
Cultural Capital, CC -INST for Institutionalised
Cultural Capital).
Phase 3: Initial codes were organised into a hierarchical
thematic framework comprising primary codes and
sub-codes (e.g., OBJ -ARCH for heritage architecture,
OBJ-AMEN for modern premium amenities).
Operational definitions and inclusion criteria were
established for each code.
Phase 4: Coded data extracts were reviewed iteratively
to ensure coherence within themes and distinctness
between themes. The framework was refined through
repeated comparison with the raw data.
Phase 5: Each theme and sub -theme was clearly
defined, articulating its specific contribution to
answering the research questions. The final coding
framework (Table 4) was established.
Phase 6: Findings were synthesised with illustrative
data extracts (anonymised  screenshots, captions, and
comments) and presented narratively according to the
Bourdieusian dimensions.
The final phase involved writing the research report.
This included weaving together the analytic narrati ve
with compelling data extracts (e.g., screenshots,
illustrative captions, and anonymised  comments) to
demonstrate the presence of the themes. The findings
were structured around Bourdieu's dimensions to show
how institutional branding aligns with the theory.
