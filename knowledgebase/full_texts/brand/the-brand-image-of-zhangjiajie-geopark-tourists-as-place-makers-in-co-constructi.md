Abstract
Geoparks are designated areas aimed at promoting geoheritage and fostering sustainable regional development through
geotourism. The construction and dissemination of the geopark brand is crucial for the institutionalisation of geoparks,
yet academic attention to geopark branding in the framework of place-making is limited. This study explores how tourists
perceive and contribute to the construction of place meanings and brand images of geoparks, using Zhangjiajie UNESCO
Global Geopark in China as a case study. Employing a mixed-methods approach combining 369 surveys, 346 mental
maps, and 57 stakeholder interviews, our results revealed that tourists have limited recognition of the geopark’s scientific
and social significance. Spatial analyses of mental maps further show that tourists’ perceptions concentrate heavily on
iconic scenic areas while overlooking peripheral, rural communities. The mismatch between tourists’ perceptions and
meaning constructions and the geopark’s official branding efforts may undermine the park’s goals of geoheritage conserva-
tion and community development. Theoretically, this paper contributes to the literature by reflecting on the intersection of
place-making, place branding, and geotourism, emphasizing tourists’ role as place-makers in co-constructing geoheritage
meanings. Our findings suggest that effective use of geopark products and branding should move beyond expert-driven
narratives and integrate tourists’ perceptions into management and assessment practices.

heritage, tourism and place (Ashworth 2000; Lew 2017).
In particular, the aspired win-win-win outcome of heritage
conservation, tourism growth and regional development
does not result from an “automatically harmonious” process
(Ashworth 2000, p.20). Researchers noted that achieving
sustainable tourism is a process that involves many prac -
tical governance issues (Hunter 1995; Ioannides and Stof -
felen 2025; Stoffelen et al. 2020; Zhang and Xiao 2014).
The unequal distribution of benefits and costs between dif -
ferent partners, the socio-political factors influencing the
selection and communication of tourism values, and the
limited capacity of local practitioners in marketing and
product development are some of the crucial complicating
factors that must be addressed (Ashworth 2000; Bosak et al.
2010; Dodds et al. 2018; Stoffelen 2020). Therefore, achiev-
ing sustainable outcomes requires addressing the dynamics
between heritage tourism, local governance and place-based
processes, and is not simply about the attractiveness of geo-
heritage values to tourists as assumed in many geopark stud-
ies (Stoffelen 2020).
Whereas recent papers have started to include alternative
(i.e., non-expert) voices in geopark studies (Azman et al.
2011; Briggs et al. 2022; Drápela et al. 2025; Lopes et al.
2022; Salgado-Jauregui et al. 2024; Stoffelen et al. 2019),
most research assumes that education can enhance tourists’
appreciation of geoheritage values, thereby regulating their
visiting behaviour and increasing support for the geopark
brand (Fung and Jim 2015a; Newsome and Dowling 2025).
Such conclusions tend to portray visitors as passive stake -
holders who are either educated by heritage experts or influ-
enced by the heritage tourism industry, as the heritage itself
is assumed to have authoritative, intrinsic and static values
(Smith 2006). However, tourists are active and engaged
stakeholders who are highly selective in heritage experi -
ences and value interpretations based on their personal
knowledge and expectations (Ashworth 2000; Smith 2006,
2020). Through cognitive processes such as perceiving and
forming mental images of destinations, as well as through
activities such as collecting, interpreting and disseminating
tourist images, tourists play a crucial role in the place making
of destinations (Milne et al. 2013; Stoffelen 2022). Ignoring
the role of tourists as active stakeholders in geotourism by
treating them as passive absorbers of earth science-related
tourism experiences would restrict the discussion of tourist
perspectives in geopark research and hinder the use of geo-
heritage as an instrument for regional development.
This paper argues that it is also necessary to question why
tourists are interested in (geo)heritage and how, through their
place perceptions, they contribute to the co-construction of
(geo)heritage (Aitken and Campelo 2011; Ashworth 2000).
By reflecting on tourists as place-makers, we aim to exam -
ine the challenges and limitations of the branding efforts in
a specific geopark in relation to its sustainable development
goals. This study raises two questions: (1) What are tour -
ists’ perceptions of geoheritage values and geopark brand
image? (2) How do tourists’ place perceptions compare to
the mobilization of geopark products and branding for local
tourism development?
We use a mixed-methods approach applied to Zhangjia -
jie UNESCO Global Geopark. Renowned for its unique
quartz sandstone peak landforms, Zhangjiajie Geopark
attracts millions of tourists annually. After 2007, rising
competition from similar domestic destinations prompted
Zhangjiajie to shift from product-focused development to
a branding-oriented strategy, emphasizing landscape-based
experiential tourism (Zhang and Xiao 2014). This back -
ground makes Zhangjiajie a compelling case for studying
geopark branding, particularly in analysing how the geopark
brand functions within the broader context of regional tour-
ism development. We collected 369 questionnaires and 346
mental maps drawn by tourists to investigate their perceived
landscape values and brand image of the geopark. Com -
bined with interviews with 42 local tourism practitioners
and 15 geopark managers, we explored the extent to which
the tourists’ place perceptions resonate with the current
strategies related to geopark branding and the implications
of these mismatches.
Conceptualisation of the Study
Place Making and Branding in Geotourism
The “place” discussed here can be understood as “space”
assigned with meanings by people based on their experi -
ences and perceptions (Gnoth 2007; Vanclay 2008). Place
making refers to “how people recognize, define, and create
the places”, and is considered fundamental to the attractive-
ness of destinations (Lew 2017, p.450; Vanclay 2008). Lew
(2017) identified two primary forms of place making: the
top-down, planned actions aimed at influencing people’s
behaviours and place perceptions, defined as “placemak -
ing”; and the bottom-up, organic processes, termed “place-
making”, which relate to how individuals or cultural groups
imprint values and meanings on places. It can be argued
that all places, from urban to rural, are socially constructed
in various ways, and that the place meanings also affect
physical space by influencing policy choices (Alkon and
Traugot 2008). In practice, most places incorporate marks
of both organic place-making and planned placemaking
in their development (Lew 2017). As a form of economic
development and cultural process, tourism influences places
through both place-making and placemaking processes, but
tends to focus more on the latter due to economic drivers

(Lew 2017; Ringer 1998). Hence, tourism should be seen
as place-centred and constructive, so that we can discuss its
role in creating and co-constructing geographic landscapes
(Ringer 1998; Stoffelen and Vanneste 2015).
Among the various place making processes (see Lew
2017), the concept of place branding is highly favoured
in destination management due to its perceived influence
on people’s place perceptions (Kavaratzis and Kalandides
2015). Driven by the need to enhance place competitive -
ness, branding is often used as a tool aimed at evoking posi-
tive place associations for consumers and managing place
reputations (Boisen et al. 2018; Gnoth 2007; Kavaratzis and
Kalandides 2015). Yet in implementation, place branding
is often misunderstood as merely designing and promot -
ing place logos and slogans, or launching campaigns aimed
at communicating selected place images (Ashworth and
Kavaratzis 2009). Such efforts are more accurately framed
as “place promotion” (Boisen et al. 2018).
For place branding, the geographical dimension inherent
in its concept and the consequent political debates cannot
be ignored (Boisen et al. 2018; Kavaratzis and Kalandides
2015). Specifically, place branding is an identity-driven
process, in which people’s (partially organic) perceptions
of and interactions with place continue to influence the
forming and meaning of place brands (Aitken and Campelo
2011; Boisen et al. 2018). The resulting brand image is not
simply the sum of tourism symbols, but a dynamic, collec -
tive network of place associations (Aitken and Campelo
2011; Gnoth 2007; Zenker et al. 2017). Furthermore, place
branding is believed to not only reveal and communicate
place identity, but also influence the identity construction
of consumers and local communities (Aitken and Campelo
2011; Gnoth 2007). Viewing landscapes as a social construct
that mediates the relationship between people and physical
environments, some scholars proposed a “landscape brand-
ing” approach, which involves using aesthetic and symbolic
landscapes for place branding (De San Eugenio Vela et al.
2017). This approach is argued to be more than just using
landscape resources to promote places, but consciously
leveraging the signifier role of landscapes to project place
essences and reinforce place identities (De San Eugenio
Vela et al. 2017). Overall, place branding should be seen
as an open-ended and co-creative process which depends
on participatory approaches, so that the purpose and sus -
tainability of a place brand can be achieved (Aitken and
Campelo 2011; Kavaratzis and Kalandides 2015).
In contrast to the above discussion, the mainstream
geopark and geotourism research tends to view tourism as
an exogenous influence on places. Some geopark studies
have explicitly or implicitly referred to the importance of
tourist perceptions and the geopark brand for place manage-
ment and regional development (see Fung and Jim 2015b;
Macadam, 2025b; Rodrigues et al. 2021; Wang and Yuan
2020). However, these studies often lack an in-depth analy-
sis of these concepts within the framework of place mak -
ing. Driven by objectified interpretations of landscape, they
typically see landscapes as the static and neutral backdrop
to geotourism development (Stoffelen and Vanneste 2015).
Consequently, most discussions of geoparks related to local
development do not consider how geoparks and associ -
ated geopark brands are instruments and representations of
larger place making processes. This static view overlooks
the fact that geoparks are not environments isolated from
people and social structures (Stoffelen 2020). For exam -
ple, Salgado-Jauregui et al.’s ( 2024) study of East Port -
land, Jamaica, revealed the significance of place identity in
geoconservation, emphasizing the importance of relating
geoparks, geoheritage and geotourism to people’s lived and
experienced realities.
Branding is highly valued in protected area management
because of its potential in raising public awareness and
encouraging positive visitor behaviours (King and Halpenny
2014; Wang and Yuan 2020). In geopark studies, scholars
have recognized that building geopark brand awareness
and promoting geotourism products may enhance the place
identities of local communities (Farsani et al. 2011, 2014;
Rodrigues et al. 2021). Some studies noted that the poten -
tial or expected outcomes of geopark brands have not been
fully realised, thus highlighting the need to strengthen brand
awareness through proper education activities and visitor
management (Azman et al. 2010; Wang and Yuan 2020).
Nevertheless, these discussions often focus on planned and
professional placemaking efforts, resembling place-promot-
ing activities rather than the identity-driven nature in the
formation of place brands (Boisen et al. 2018). The impact
of organic place-making in this process has not yet been
fully revealed (cf. Lew 2017).
Tourists as Place-Makers
The role of tourists in destination place making is thought to
be ambiguous (Lew 2017). Regardless of the tourism form,
people always travel with a set of imaginaries (i.e., ideas,
expectations, or perceptions) of destinations (Salazar 2012).
Tourists’ imaginaries of destinations can be shaped by indi-
vidual life courses, tour operators, governments or broader
cultural forces external to the tourism industry (Hall 1994;
Milne et al. 2013; Urry and Larsen 2011). Meanwhile, as the
imaginaries of tourists, local communities and tourism inter-
mediaries meet and sometimes clash within destinations,
tourists’ imaginaries may also have an impact on the socio-
cultural and physical environment of destinations by shaping
local policies and community behaviours (Alkon and Trau-
got 2008; Salazar 2012). Such an interactive process between

tourists and places is sometimes criticised for creating inac-
curate images of destinations (Silver 1993), especially con-
sidering that the narratives of elites or major interest groups
often have a greater impact than other stakeholders (Alkon
and Traugot 2008; Salazar 2012). This criticism resembles
the concerns of some heritage experts that the commodifi -
cation of heritage for tourism purposes can undermine sup -
posedly certain, sometimes deemed “objective”, heritage
values. Nevertheless, the interactive process between heri -
tage, tourism and place still “redefines social realities” (Hall
1994, p.178), creates new place meanings and local identi -
ties, and profoundly influences local development (Britton
1991; Ringer 1998; Stoffelen and Vanneste 2015).
Thus, tourists contribute to place making as co-producers
and co-performers through their imagination and activities,
such as creating stories and sharing experiences (Chang
2012; Everett 2012; Salazar 2012; Stoffelen 2022; Thurlow
and Jaworski 2014). They act as both observers and partici-
pants in this process, seeking to understand and re-shape the
places they visit by consuming and interacting with these
places (Lew 2017; Thurlow and Jaworski 2014). Consider-
ing the influences of various stakeholders on place making
processes, particular attention should be paid to the ways in
which tourists’ values, meanings and knowledge of places
are or are not changed and re-constructed during the differ -
ent stages of tourism (Salazar 2012).
Some geopark studies have noted the importance of man-
aging geotourism by considering the motivations and expec-
tations of tourists (Fung and Jim 2015a; Macadam 2025a).
These studies provided a starting point for understanding
the voices of tourists in geoparks from a cognitive perspec-
tive. We aim to go further here by considering tourists as
place-makers (cf. Lew 2017), rather than passive receivers
of authoritative, expert-based official geoheritage informa -
tion. We will explore how tourists perceive the landscapes
and brands of geoparks, and how they participate in the (re)
creation and co-construction of the place and brand image
of geoparks at the cognitive level through their place asso -
ciations (Aitken and Campelo 2011).
Study Area and Methods
Zhangjiajie UNESCO Global Geopark
Zhangjiajie Geopark is located in Wulingyuan District of
Zhangjiajie City, Hunan Province, China. The geological
significance of Zhangjiajie Geopark covers many aspects,
including geomorphology, stratigraphy, palaeontology,
hydrogeology, and geoaesthetics (PRC Ministry of Land
and Resources, 2003). The core geoheritage, in physical
landscape terms, consists of quartz sandstone peak forest
landforms (Fig. 1). The sandstone peaks in Zhangjiajie are
of global geological significance due to their height, narrow-
ness, and the scale and density of their distribution (Yang
et al. 2012). These sandstone peaks, marketed for their
uniqueness and aesthetics, are a major attraction for tourists
in Zhangjiajie (Sun 2012). In 2010, the local authorities pro-
posed to get the name “Zhangjiajie Landforms” recognized
in international geological circles as the archetype of quartz
sandstone peak forest landforms, in recognition of their dis-
tinctiveness and to further enhance Zhangjiajie’s popular -
ity (Sun, 2012). Besides sandstone peaks, this geopark also
includes karst caves, stratigraphic sites and hydrogeologi -
cal sites (typically streams, underground rivers and under -
ground falls) (PRC Ministry of Land and Resources, 2003).
Zhangjiajie Geopark is also renowned for its biological
and cultural significance. Due to the geographical setting
and high forest cover, the area is the habitat of many endan-
gered species of flora and fauna, and serves as an important
biodiversity reserve (UNESCO, n.d.). The area’s cultural
elements include historical sites, ancient temples, and folk

are populated by about 55,000 residents, for whom tourism
is the main source of livelihood (Li et al. 2023).
Sampling and Data Collection Instruments
We employed a mixed-methods approach to address the
research questions. Quantitative methods were used to
obtain descriptive statistics on the travel decisions and
landscape associations of the broad tourist group. Qualita -
tive methods were then used to further explore the meaning
that tourists attached to landscapes and the place, as well
as the views of geopark managers and tourism practitio -
ners on place management and branding. Together, these
data provide explanations or contextual insights into tour -
ists’ perceptions. We used different data collection methods,
including questionnaire and mapping surveys with tourists,
interviews with geopark managers and tourism practitio -
ners, and general field observations. We also obtained offi -
cial documents related to tourism development and geopark
management in the study area with the assistance of geopark
managers.
On-site data collection was carried out between Febru -
ary and April 2023. Due to project timeline constraints, this
period did not cover a full annual cycle; however, February
to April spans both lower and higher visitor periods, with

to the area. During field observations and after each inter -

Using convenience sampling, 369 face-to-face question -
naires were completed in different areas of the geopark.
About 85% of the questionnaires were collected in the
core scenic area. This sampling proportion was designed
to ensure that our sample reflects the actual distribution
of tourists across the geopark: the daily number of tourists
entering the core scenic area accounted for at least 83% of
the total daily tourists of the geopark in January 2023, with
this proportion increasing for the past three years (Zhangji -
ajie Bureau of Culture and Tourism, 2023). Nevertheless,
we also recognise that this convenience sampling approach
may overemphasize the perspectives of tourists visiting the
core scenic area. In terms of respondent characteristics, 47%
travelled independently, and the remaining 53% travelled
as part of an organised tour group. Educational attainment
ranged from elementary school (and below) to master’s
degree (and above), with 36% holding a university degree
or above. Most respondents (96%) completed the question -
naire after their visit. The remaining respondents partici -
pated based on their preliminary knowledge of the area.
The questionnaire consisted of nine closed questions and
four open questions. The closed questions evaluated respon-
dents’ travel decisions and their knowledge of designations
of Zhangjiajie. Similar to the methods used by Milne et
al. (2013), this approach helped to assess the influence of
geopark branding on forming tourists’ basic place percep -
tions. Open questions asked respondents about the elements
they typically associated with the area’s landscapes as well
as their knowledge of the landforms, focusing on the reasons
tourists attribute meaning or values to certain geographical
elements. This way, we asked them both for the elements of
place they deemed important (hereafter: “place elements”)
and the reason for their importance (hereafter: “place asso -
ciations”). The face-to-face survey method ensured that
tourists had ample space and freedom to provide intuitive
answers. 727 place elements were collected after cleaning
up the data.
We conducted two mapping exercises alongside the
questionnaire, in order to visualize tourists’ place percep -
tions spatially (including valued locations and perceived
boundaries) and to compare them with the geopark’s official
territorial planning (Brown et al. 2015; Brown and Ray -
mond 2007; Stoffelen et al. 2019). Firstly, the respondents
were asked to pinpoint their valued locations on a base map.
The base map was constructed from the geopark’s official
visitor guide map, which includes place names familiar to
visitors as well as the names of towns and villages within
and around the geopark. For respondents unable to pin -
point locations on the map, we encouraged them to provide
textual responses to name or describe the locations they
thought about. We also asked respondents to describe the
meanings they associated with these locations as an open
question. Secondly, we asked the respondents to draw a
polygon on the same map to represent what they perceived
as the range of Zhangjiajie Geopark. Most respondents were
able to outline the area based on map features (e.g., attrac -
tions, roads, and place names), or at least mark the places
they were familiar with or had been to. Tourists were free
to mark their valued locations and perceived boundaries at
any location within the geopark or beyond its extent, regard-
less of the location where they completed the exercise. In
the end, 346 respondents completed the boundary mapping
exercise, mentioning 778 valued locations.
We also conducted semi-structured interviews with 15
government officials and 42 tourism practitioners. For gov -
ernment officials, we used snowball sampling starting from
the Geopark Office, and subsequently interviewed officials
from village, district and municipal levels. Tourism practi -
tioners were randomly selected and included tour guides,
accommodation owners, and staff from local tourism agen -
cies. The interviews were thematically organized, focusing
on the promotion of geoheritage and geotourism develop -
ment, as well as place and brand management strategies in
Zhangjiajie. The interviews ranged from 18 to 95 min. With
verbal informed consent from participants, the interviews
were recorded and transcribed for analysis.
Data Analysis
Given the exploratory nature of our study, we primarily
focused on descriptive statistics, including frequency distri-
butions and mean scores for Likert-scale responses, to anal-
yse data from closed questions. This allowed us to capture
broad trends in tourists’ perceptions and brand awareness
of Zhangjiajie Geopark. The analyses were conducted using
Excel and key results are presented in Fig. 3 in the results
section.

review of official documents, we identified eight key place
elements that carry the geopark’s authoritative values. These
elements were mainly based on geology, as well as ecology,
aesthetics and culture (see Table 1). By iteratively coding a

Following a coding guideline described by Stoffelen

merged, and re-organized into a data-based pattern coding

conservation and management; commercialization and pro-
motion of geoheritage; and tourism-based regional devel -
opment. All transcripts were subsequently coded using the
final coding scheme. In the post-coding stage, each coding
node was thoroughly summarized and cross-verified with
official documents and fieldwork notes. This coding and
post-coding process ensured that our qualitative analysis not
only reflected the inherent richness of the data but also effec-
tively connected emergent themes with broader contextual
insights (Stoffelen 2019). These qualitative data provided
a narrative context for understanding tourism development
and place management in the study area.
We scanned the boundary maps and valued locations
marked by tourists. By georeferencing the base map against
satellite imagery, we were able to digitise tourists’ hand-
drawn boundaries and points into polygons and points,
respectively, in ArcGIS Pro. Locations mentioned only in
written responses were geocoded to the geometric centroid
of the relevant named location on the base map. Using the
Count Overlapping Features tool, we created a density map
that reflected tourists’ cognitive boundaries of the geopark.
A hotspot map was generated using the Point Density tool,
which showed the spatial distribution of tourists’ valued
locations. These results were compared with the official
boundaries of the geopark, the scenic area boundaries and
tourism facilities.
Results
Zhangjiajie’s Place Branding
Most interviewees identified the quartz sandstone peaks as
the most important element of Zhangjiajie’s place brand.
Many cited “three thousand majestic peaks and eight hun -

they received in the past for unchecked tourism growth,
some government officials believed that building the

believed that “geotourists” were just niche markets and “the
more visitors, the better. Both geotourists and other tourists

want all of them” (government official 08).

scientific significance with market exposure to reinforce
the internal and external identity of Zhangjiajie. However,
despite promotion efforts (e.g., the use of landmarks, the
display of slogans, and narration by tour guides), respon -

11% of respondents mentioned “Zhangjiajie landforms” in
their answers, which was more than those who could recall
the formal geomorphological name “Quartz Sandstone Peak
Forest Landforms” (7%), but also slightly less than those
who recognised karst landforms (13%). About two thirds of
the respondents (67%) claimed that they did not know or
could not remember the name of the landforms.
Tourist Destination Choices
Landscape (in the meaning of “scenery”) emerged as the
most influential factor in shaping respondents’ decision to
visit the area, with 85% of respondents agreeing or strongly
agreeing (Fig. 3a). The region’s popularity, including rec -
ommendations from friends or tour agencies, also influenced
tourists’ decisions to visit. Only about 30% of respondents
agreed or strongly agreed that international or domestic
designations had influenced their choices. This is slightly
higher than the responses for accessibility (21%, Fig. 3a)
and “included by default in packaged itinerary” (20%,
Fig. 3a), indicating that these were less decisive factors.
Of all designations, most respondents had heard of
the Zhangjiajie National Forest Park prior to their visit
(Fig. 3b). More than half of the respondents had also heard
of two other national designations, National 5 A Tourist
Attraction and National Scenic and Historic Interest Area,
before their visit. Compared with the domestic designations,
slightly fewer respondents were aware of the region’s global
conservation designations, namely UNESCO World Natural
Heritage Site and UNESCO Global Geopark (50% and 46%
respectively, Fig. 3b).
Tourism practitioners and government employees only
had fragmented and partial insights into what drives visitors
to the area and how they recognise the area’s designations
and associated place brands. Some interviewees believed
that UNESCO designations are “basically about building
market reputation” and that “world-class branding definitely
works best” (government official 06, tourism practitioner

Tourists’ Landscape Vision of Zhangjiajie
Considering the role of landscapes in shaping tourists’ des -
tination choices, we examined the tourists’ place elements
and associations by focusing on landscape elements in the
study area. Sandstone peaks emerged clearly as the most
frequently recognized place element (85%; Fig. 4), followed
by biological elements (35%). Other elements, including
those identified from official documents were mentioned
less frequently (Fig. 4).
The top five categories of place elements identified by
tourists were also frequently mentioned by local practitio -
ners and associated with positive feelings and a sense of
pride. For example, one interviewee stressed, “our air is
fresh, and the natural [environment] is good, with beauti -
ful shan-shui” (tourism practitioner 04). Considering the
destination’s official branding elements and practitioners’
perspective of local culture as mentioned in Sect. 4.1, it is
interesting to note tourists’ low recognition of cultural ele -
ments. This probably suggests that official promotion of
local culture and tour guides’ interpretations did not impress
or convince most tourists of their value.
Aesthetic values provided the primary reason why
respondents recognized sandstone peaks (61%; Table 3).
The uniqueness and symbolic value of sandstone peaks fol-
low as the second and third main reasons. However, only
14% of respondents who mentioned sandstone peaks recog-
nized the expert’s view of the geological significance. This
suggests a mismatch between tourists’ interest in those land-
forms (i.e., those who selected them as important place ele-
ments) and their knowledge of the geological background
(i.e., the association of these place elements with higher-
order cognitive values, such as those that moved beyond aes-
thetic values). Several practitioners pointed out the risks of
sandstone peaks being appreciated solely for their aesthetic
value. They felt that the main tourism form in this area was
just sightseeing, which could easily lead to aesthetic fatigue
for tourists due to a lack of diverse experiences.
The reasons why respondents recognized biological ele -
ments were diverse and evenly distributed (Table 3). One
of the most common reasons was their recreational value
(31%). This may be due to the monkeys that can be found
throughout the area. Despite tourists’ high awareness of the
area’s biological value and its status as a national forest park,
their valuation of biological elements for their biological
and/or ecological significance was not very prominent com-
pared to that of sandstone peaks and their aesthetic value. A
possible explanation is that the biological value of the area
is perceived by tourists more as a whole than embodied in
certain elements.

Discussion
The findings from Zhangjiajie Geopark revealed that tour -
ists’ (re)interpretations of geoheritage posed challenges
to the current branding efforts related to leveraging the
geopark brand for regional development. Tourist percep -
tions of geoheritage in Zhangjiajie Geopark were subjec -
tive and selective (Ashworth 2000; Smith 2006), yet tourists
showed limited engagement with the landscapes and the
geopark brand. The relative mismatch between respondents’
perceptions (i.e., important place elements, place associa -
tions, and cognitive boundaries) and the geopark’s place
branding efforts may undermine the ability of the geopark to
meet its sustainable development goals, such as landscape
conservation and community development (Farsani et al.
2014; McKeever et al. 2010). Tourists’ dominant aesthetic
appreciation of the sandstone peaks has nonetheless been
strategically leveraged by local authorities and tourism
practitioners, who placed the peaks at the core of place
image-building and thereby attracted tourists and stimulated
economic growth in recent decades (Zhuang et al. 2024).
However, the strong interest of mass tourists in aesthetic
landscapes was closely related to the imbalanced tourism
development within the geopark, evident in the concen -
tration of both tourist visitation and tourists’ valued loca -
tions around the core scenic zone rather than the peripheral
areas. Another study showed that the tourist presence and
economic growth did strengthen local people’s place iden -
tity, mostly around tourism features rather than the rural
areas where they live (Li et al. 2023). Thus, collectively, it
appears from both the tourists’ and residents’ side that the
social and spatial inclusivity of the geopark was limited
at best as a result of spatially concentrated mass tourism,
dominant place images developed around those locations,
and related economic effects. Furthermore, in pursuit of

visitor numbers and economic benefits, many practitioners

yet challenging role tourists play as place-makers in tour -

perceptions, as mediated through tourism practitioners’
responses to these preferences, reinforced the imbalanced
core-periphery development of the area. In this way, tour -
ists’ place perceptions influenced the “placemaking” (Lew
2017) in this region, with their physical and social impacts
occurring largely through the promotional and management
choices they prompted (Alkon and Traugot 2008; Milne et
al. 2013; Salazar 2012).
The issue facing Zhangjiajie Geopark is that its branding
efforts, particularly in the use and promotion of geoheritage,
seem to be ineffective in materializing the geopark’s goals
amidst mass tourism impacts. Some place managers who
valued the geopark brand chose to focus firmly on educa -
tion, as emphasized in the geopark movement (Hose 2012;
Newsome and Dowling 2025). While those educational ini-
tiatives were important, their focus overlooked the impact
of most tourists on the ongoing place-making processes
(Lew 2017). For other place managers, the geopark brand,
along with other designations, was mainly used as a tool
to stimulate tourist visits and enhancing destination image
(Hosseini et al. 2021). They barely discussed how to inte -
grate the geopark brand with place-making processes and
overlooked key aspects, including the local embedding of
the geopark brand and management for the dynamics of
place identity, which scholars have highlighted as essential
in place branding (Aitken and Campelo 2011; Boisen et al.
2018; Richards 2017; Stoffelen et al. 2019).
Also, the geopark designation had a limited influence on
shaping the destination image for tourists. The influence of
heritage designations on tourist visitation has been noted
as controversial in existing research (V olgger and Taplin
2024). Yet, many studies pointed out that heritage designa -
tions have some, albeit limited, impact on tourists’ destina -
tion choices, even if tourists are indeed aware of a site’s
nominated status (Dewar et al. 2012; King and Halpenny
2014; Marcotte and Bourdeau 2006; V olgger and Taplin
2024). One explanation could be that many local authorities
do not make the most of the designations in place promotion
(King and Halpenny 2014), or have a limited understand -
ing of tourists’ attitudes towards these designations as in our
case (Marcotte and Bourdeau 2006). When analysed in the
framework of place branding, we argue that tourists’ knowl-
edge of the designation is closer to “attention”, rather than
“awareness that positively impact the image of the place”
(Boisen et al. 2018, p.7). In other words, despite a certain
authority of the geopark in science popularization and
education, for most tourists, the brand meaning of Zhangjia-
jie Geopark could not represent the overall place image.
As indicated in another study, the ineffectiveness of using
the geopark as a regional development instrument in this
area was partly due to its governance complexities (Zhuang
et al. 2024). Here our findings show that managers had lim-
ited knowledge of tourists’ brand recognition, place percep-
tions, and the dynamics involved in dealing with various
landscape interpretations was limited. They were unable
to bridge the gap between geopark aims and branding pro -
cesses (i.e., placemaking processes) and tourists’ place
perceptions and behaviours (i.e., part of place-making pro -
cesses) (Lew 2017). At present, the geoscience-centred edu-
cation efforts were disconnected from the experiences and
impacts of most tourists, limiting the geopark to museum-
like sites. This confirms previous research which found that
tourists, influenced by their cultural background, are likely
to focus more on the aesthetic and storytelling aspects of
landscapes than the factual geological information (Xu et
al. 2013). Therefore, an aesthetic interpretation strategy is
recommended to foster tourist engagement with geologi -
cal conservation efforts (Xu et al. 2013). Considering the
critical role of tourists as place-makers, the geopark man -
agement authorities should recognize the need for more
balanced approaches to reconcile geoscience education ini -
tiatives with tourists’ place perceptions.
Conclusions
The social significance of heritage sites cannot be realized
without their audiences, and the same applies to geopa -
rks (Azman et al. 2010; Smith 2020). Using Zhangjiajie
UNESCO Global Geopark as a case study, our findings
emphasize the challenging role of tourists as place-makers
in the development of the geopark. We conclude that tour -
ists’ place perceptions and their dominant aesthetic prefer -
ences played an important role in the re-interpretation and
co-construction of geoheritage meanings. Most tourists
showed a relatively simplistic and limited understanding
of the geopark brand and landscapes, which highlights the
challenges in geoheritage communication and promotion
efforts to realize the geopark’s sustainable development
goals. Some geopark studies tend to segment geotourism
into a niche market focused solely on tourists interested
in the scientific aspects of geological elements (see Sadry
2020). However, given that geoparks are often situated in
the complex context of tourism and regional development,
our case study highlights the role of general tourists in shap-
ing place meanings, which cannot be disregarded when
assessing the achievement of geopark goals (Milne et al.
2013; Stoffelen 2020).

This paper makes a theoretical contribution by extend -
ing reflections on the complex relationship between heri -
tage, tourism and place, as studied in tourism geographies
and critical heritage studies, to the context of geoparks and
geopark studies. In particular, we reflected on the intersec -
tion between place making, place branding, and geotourism
development in geoparks. The bottom-up approach empha-
sized in the geopark concept is consistent with the participa-
tory and co-constructive process (i.e., organic place-making
processes) highlighted in place branding theories (Aitken
and Campelo 2011; Azman et al. 2011; Kavaratzis and
Kalandides 2015; Lew 2017). However, partly due to the
presence of an expert-driven, authoritative heritage dis -
course underpinning the research, institutionalization, and
promotion of the geoheritage concept, as well as the gover-
nance complexities, the role of non-expert actors in geopark
development has not been fully revealed (Stoffelen 2020;
Zhuang et al. 2024). This further illustrates the challenges
of using geoparks as place branding tools for regional
development.
While Zhangjiajie presents a particularly pronounced
case given the scale of its mass tourism sector and the prom-
inence of its sandstone peaks, the tension it reveals between
expert-driven geoheritage narratives and tourists’ aesthetic
engagement is not unique to this site. Many UNESCO
Global Geoparks operate under a comparable tension: geo -
conservation and science communication on one hand, and
visitor economy and regional development on the other
(Drápela et al. 2025; Esengulova et al. 2026). Geoparks that
draw a substantial proportion of leisure-oriented visitors are
likely to encounter a similar gap between the geoscientific
meanings promoted through branding and the aesthetic or
symbolic meanings tourists actually construct (Fung and
Jim 2015b). The Zhangjiajie case therefore offers a transfer-
able lesson for geopark management more broadly: place
branding strategies built primarily around expert geoher -
itage discourse risk under-representing how the majority of
tourists actually engage with and assign meanings to these
landscapes.
In light of the above findings, we propose that the
effective use of geoheritage and geopark branding should
go beyond a one-way flow of information from experts
to the public through education by incorporating tour -
ists’ perceptions into the management and assessment of
geoparks. In the case of Zhangjiajie Geopark, managers
should give greater consideration to interpretive routes
or homestay networks linking the ticketed scenic zones
with the currently overlooked rural areas. This planning
strategy could be paired with visitor dispersal targets built
into performance assessment. Structured feedback mecha -
nisms, such as periodic visitor perception surveys or guide
input, should also be established to systematically track
and bridge the gap between tourists’ landscape values and
official narratives. In the process, geopark managers need
to be wary of either falling into the trap of simply high -
lighting already-popular sites or place images because of
expectations of mass tourists, or doggedly holding on to
objectified geoscience education that mismatches with
tourists’ place associations.
This study captures tourists’ role as place-makers in the
geopark primarily at the cognitive and perceptual level.
The concentration of surveys in the core scenic area, along
with the timing and duration of data collection, may also
have introduced sampling bias affecting the composition
and representativeness of respondents. Future research
incorporating tourists’ communicative and behavioural
place-making practices, such as social media content or
narrative accounts, would extend these cognitive-level find-
ings towards a fuller picture of co-construction. Addition -
ally, further research is needed to examine the influence of
other non-expert stakeholders, such as local communities
and tourism intermediaries (e.g., tour operators), in shap -
ing tourists’ place meanings in the geopark. Ultimately, the
diverse perceptions, behaviours and engagement of mul -
tiple stakeholders intersect in geoparks’ place making pro -
cesses. In order to be effective, geopark branding efforts
should engage with or represent the diversity of these place
making processes, and connect these to regional develop -
ment objectives.
