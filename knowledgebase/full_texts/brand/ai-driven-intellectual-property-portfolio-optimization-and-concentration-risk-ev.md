Abstract. This paper demonstrates that artificial intelligence (AI) -driven optimization of intellectual property (IP)
portfolios affects on firm-level concentration risk, revenue volatility and stock price crash risk. While AI enhances the
efficiency of IP allocation decisions, we argue that algorithmic optimization may sy stematically increase portfolio
concentration, thereby amplifying downside risk exposure under demand uncertainty. We develop a theoretical
framework that extends Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT) to IP asset portfolios and introduces an AI -driven allocation
mechanism. Using patent portfolio data from global consumer brands, including Nike, Pop Mart, Huawei, and Xiaomi,
we construct IP concentration indices (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index), estimate revenue volatility, and model downside
risk using semivariance and  negative conditional skewness (NCSKEW). Monte Carlo simulation and sensitivity
analysis reveal a fundamental tension between AI-optimized efficiency and portfolio resilience. AI-driven IP allocation
increases concentration in high-expected-return technology segments, which reduces idiosyncratic forecasting error
but amplifies tail risk during demand shocks. The study makes three contributions: it bridges IP management and
financial risk modeling, introduces an AI -induced concentration risk channel, and pro vides actionable evidence for
corporate risk managers and policymakers navigating the intersection of AI deployment and IP strategy in an
uncertain global economy.

Revenue Volatility, Patent Management.

Introduction
Intellectual property (IP) assets have become central to firm valuation in the contemporary knowledge
economy. Companies such as Qualcomm, Huawei, Nokia, and even Nike hold tens of thousands of patents
that generate licensing revenue, provide competitive m oats, and shape technology trajectories across
industries [1]. The strategic management of these IP portfolios raises questions that closely parallel
those in financial portfolio management [2], [3]: which assets deserve continued investment, how
should li mited budgets be allocated across heterogeneous IP holdings, and when should
underperforming assets be divested?
Simultaneously, artificial intelligence is transforming decision -making across corporate functions. AI -
based systems can process vast quantities of patent data, forecast technology adoption trajectories, and
recommend optimal allocation strategies with unprecedented speed and granularity. Major technology
firms in China and globally are increasingly deploying machine learning algorithms to guide R&D
investment, patent filing strategies, and portfolio pruning decisions [4], [5], and firm-level AI investment
has been associated with faster firm growth and greater product innovation [6]. However, the financial
risk implications of AI-driven IP optimization remain largely unexplored.
This paper addresses a critical gap: while AI optimization improves expected returns from IP portfolios
by directing resources toward the most promising technology segments, it may simultaneously increase
portfolio concentration. Drawing on financial risk theory, we argue that concentration risk, defined as
excessive reliance on a narrow set of IP assets, amplifies downside exposure. When demand shocks
strike, concentrated portfolios suffer disproportionate revenue losses because the diversification buffer
that would otherwise absorb idiosyncratic shocks is absent [7].
Our research question is therefore: Does AI-driven IP portfolio optimization increase concentration risk,
and if so, what are the implications for firm-level revenue volatility and crash risk?
We investigate this question using patent portfolio data from global consumer brands, with particular
attention to firms operating in or connected to Chinese markets. The Chinese economy provides a
compelling empirical setting for several reasons. First, China has become the world's largest patent filing
jurisdiction, with firms like Huawei and Xiaomi maintaining portfolios of exceptional scale and
technological breadth. Second, the Chinese market exhibits high demand volatility driven by rapid
consumer preference shifts, regulatory changes, and geopolitical tensions. Third, the deployment of AI
in corporate decision-making is advancing rapidly in Chinese technology firms, making the AI -induced
concentration risk channel particularly relevant.
The paper contributes to several strands of literature. First, we extend the growing body of work
applying Modern Portfolio Theory to IP management [1], [8], [9] by introducing AI as a decision-making
agent that endogenously shapes portfolio composition. S econd, we contribute to the financial risk
literature on concentration and crash risk [10], [11] by identifying IP concentration as a novel risk
channel. Third, we speak to the emerging discourse on AI and financial stability [12], [13] by
demonstrating ho w algorithmic optimization in non -financial domains can generate financial risk
externalities.

1. Literature Review and Theoretical Development
1.1 Modern Portfolio Theory and IP Assets
The foundational insight of Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT) is that rational investors should evaluate
assets not in isolation but as components of a diversified portfolio, selecting combinations along the
efficient frontier that maximize expected return for  a given level of risk [2]. This framework has been
extended beyond traditional financial assets to patents and other IP. Jankowska [1] demonstrates that
IP portfolios can be modeled using the same mean -variance framework, where expected returns
correspond to projected IP value (licensing revenue, competitive advantage, cost savings), risk
corresponds to uncertainty of legal or commercial success, and correlation captures the
interdependence of patent values across technology domains.
Lin et al. [8] provide empirical evidence that patent portfolio diversity affects firm value, though the
relationship is moderated by firm characteristics: R&D -intensive firms benefit from broader
diversification, while smaller firms achieve better outcome s from focused strategies. Lubatkin and
Chatterjee [7] examine whether MPT applies to corporate diversification more broadly, noting
important differences between managing financial assets and business units. These studies collectively
suggest that portfol io-theoretic reasoning applies to IP management, but with significant adaptations
required to account for the unique characteristics of IP assets, including illiquidity, synergistic value,
and context-dependent pricing. More recent evidence from Chinese hi gh-tech industries confirms that
portfolio structure matters beyond individual patent characteristics: both the technological advantage
of a portfolio within a field and the relatedness of technologies across fields raise the value of individual
patents, and these effects strengthen as external technological competition intensifies [14].
1.2 IP Portfolio Concentration and Firm Risk
Concentration risk in financial portfolios is well documented. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) is
commonly used to measure asset concentration, with higher values indicating greater reliance on a
smaller number of positions. In the IP context, concent ration can be measured across technology
domains, product lines, or geographic jurisdictions. Pham [3] argues that patent portfolios exhibit
diminishing diversification benefits beyond approximately 20 technology segments, analogous to the
20–30 stock threshold in equity portfolios.
Real-world examples illustrate the strategic importance of IP concentration decisions. IBM's shift from
numeric patent leadership to a focused "quality over quantity" strategy, reducing its U.S. patent grants
from over 8,500 in 2021 to under 5,000 in 2022 [15], [16], represents deliberate concentration toward
core technologies including hybrid cloud, AI, and quantum computing. Dow Chemical's 1990s portfolio
review identified that 25 percent of its approximately 20,000 patents had zero business value, leadin g
to a pruning effort that saved $40 million over five years [17]. Nokia generates approximately 1.3 billion
euros annually from licensing a portfolio of 20,000 patent families, while Qualcomm's licensing division
earned $5.3 billion in Q1 2023 with a 68 percent profit margin [18], [19].
In the Chinese market, Pop Mart International provides an instructive case of IP concentration risk in
the consumer brand domain. The company's heavy reliance on its flagship character Labubu, which at

1.3 AI-Driven Optimization and Risk Amplification
Artificial intelligence is increasingly deployed in corporate IP strategy. Machine learning algorithms can
analyze patent citation networks, predict technology adoption curves, estimate litigation outcomes, and
recommend portfolio rebalancing actions [4]. AI-driven tools such as PatentSight, IPlytics, and custom
machine learning models enable firms to score patents on quality, market coverage, and competitive
relevance, generating optimization recommendations at scale. Interpretable machine learning survival
models, for example, now predict patent lifespan from technical, legal, market, patentee, and textual
features and outperform the traditional Cox proportional hazards model, directly informing
maintenance and abandonment decisions [5]. The evidence on ho w AI shapes the breadth of firms’
technology portfolios is, however, not unequivocal. Wu et al. [20] show that AI adoption in Chinese listed
manufacturing firms promotes technological diversification, particularly into unrelated domains. That
finding conce rns AI as a general organisational capability that widens technological search; our
argument concerns AI deployed as an allocation optimizer with an expected-return objective, where the
mechanism can operate in the opposite direction.
However, AI optimization algorithms are typically designed to maximize expected return subject to
constraints, without explicit attention to concentration risk or tail risk. This creates a  systematic bias:
AI systems overweight trending technology segments with high recent performance metrics while
underweighting diversification benefits that only become apparent during demand shocks. This
phenomenon parallels concerns in financial markets a bout algorithmic trading amplifying volatility
through correlated optimization behavior [12]. Danielsson et al. [13] similarly argue that AI can create
new tail risks and amplify existing ones through procyclicality and optimization against the system.
We formalize this insight as the AI concentration hypothesis: AI -driven IP allocation increases
Herfindahl concentration in the patent portfolio because the algorithm systematically favors
technologies with the highest expected marginal return, reducing al location to lower -return but risk -
diversifying segments. Under normal conditions, this produces superior risk -adjusted returns. Under
adverse conditions (demand shocks, regulatory changes, technology disruptions), the concentrated
portfolio experiences amplified losses because the diversification buffer has been eroded.
2. Hypothesis Development
Based on the theoretical framework developed above, we propose the following hypotheses:
H1: AI-driven IP optimization increases patent portfolio concentration, as measured by the Herfindahl -
Hirschman Index across technology segments.
The rationale is that AI algorithms, by design, allocate marginal resources to the technology segments
with the highest expected return. Over successive optimization cycles, this produces portfolio drift

toward a smaller number of dominant segments. Unlike human decision -makers who may intuitively
maintain diversification for strategic or precautionary reasons, AI systems optimize within the objective
function they are given, which typically does not penalize concentration.
H2: Higher IP portfolio concentration is positively associated with revenue volatility.
Revenue volatility is the IP-domain analog of return volatility in financial portfolios. A concentrated IP
portfolio ties revenue streams to a narrow set of technology domains. When those domains experience
demand fluctuations, the absence of diversification amplifies the impact on total portfolio revenue. We
measure revenue volatility using the standard deviation and downside semivariance of segment -level
revenue streams.
H3: Higher IP portfolio concentration is positively associated with stock price crash risk.
Stock price crash risk, measured by negative conditional skewness (NCSKEW) and the down -to-up
volatility ratio (DUVOL), captures the likelihood of extreme negative returns. We hypothesize that IP
concentration transmits demand shocks to equity markets beca use investors recognize that
concentrated firms are more exposed to idiosyncratic technology risk. This is consistent with the
literature on crash risk determinants [10], [11]. The relationship between AI and crash risk is not
necessarily adverse, however: Zhang et al. [21] find that AI innovation reduces stock price crash risk by
strengthening internal controls, improving financial transparency, and reducing information
asymmetry. H3 therefore isolates a distinct channel that operates through the compositi on of the IP
portfolio rather than through governance and disclosure quality.
H4: The positive association between IP concentration and crash risk is stronger during periods of
macroeconomic uncertainty.
During periods of heightened uncertainty, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, trade wars, and geopolitical
disruptions, demand shocks are larger and more correlated across segments. Under these conditions,
the vulnerability of concentrated portfolios is magnifi ed because the probability of simultaneous
adverse outcomes across related technology segments increases. We proxy macroeconomic uncertainty
using the Economic Policy Uncertainty Index [22]. Uncertainty also shapes how markets value patent
assets: consiste nt with real option theory, firm -level and market -level uncertainty strengthen the
market-value effect of international patenting [23], which implies that the valuation of IP holdings is
itself sensitive to the prevailing uncertainty regime.

3.4 Monte Carlo Simulation
Following the methodology established in our ongoing research [1] and standard financial risk analysis
practice [24], [25], we employ Monte Carlo simulation to evaluate IP portfolio outcomes under
uncertainty. For each portfolio configuration (AI -optimized vs. diversified benchmark), we simulate
10,000 demand scenarios by drawing from estimated probability distributions for each technology
segment's performance. This yields distributions of portfolio -level returns, enabling computation of
Value-at-Risk (VaR), Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), and probability of underperformance relative to
a minimum acceptable return threshold.
3.5 Sensitivity Analysis
We systematically vary key model inputs to assess robustness. Parameters subject to sensitivity testing
include: (a) expected returns per segment (±20% perturbation), (b) correlation assumptions across
technology domains, (c) AI forecast accuracy (measured  by root mean squared error of predictions),
and (d) the degree of demand shock severity. This approach identifies the conditions under which AI -
induced concentration produces the most significant risk amplification.
3.6 Data and Sample
Our analysis draws on patent portfolio data from major consumer brands with significant IP holdings
across multiple technology domains. The primary sample includes Nike (sportswear and athletic
technology), Pop Mart International (designer toys and charact er IP), Huawei Technologies
(telecommunications and consumer electronics), and Xiaomi Corporation (consumer electronics and
IoT). Patent data are sourced from GreyB, PatentSight, and public patent databases including WIPO and
the China National Intellectual Property Administration (CNIPA). Financial data, including stock returns
and revenue breakdowns, are obtained from CSMAR, Wind, and Bloomberg. The sample period spans
2010–2024, providing coverage of multiple economic cycles including the COVID -19 pandem ic and
recent geopolitical disruptions.

4. Empirical Evidence  and Discussion
4.1 IP Portfolio  Structure: the Case of Nike
Nike's patent portfolio provides detailed evidence of dynamic IP rebalancing. Analysis of Nike's patent
filing trends over 2005–2024 reveals several patterns consistent with our theoretical framework. Patent
filings increased sharply from 2009 to 2016, driven by Olympic cycle preparation and the strategic
imperative to build technology barriers in footwear, apparel, and digital fitness. Filing activity peaked
in 2016, coinciding with the Rio Olympics, then declined through 2019 as Nike shifted toward a "quality
over quantity" approach.
The composition of Nike's patent portfolio across technology segments underwent significant
restructuring during this period. Early in the sample, design patents dominated, accounting for the
largest share of new filings. By 2014 –2016, footwear technology and manufacturing processes gained
increasing weight, while sensor and electronics patents maintained a small but strategically significant
presence. This trajectory illustrates the portfolio rebalancing dynamic our model predicts: the firm
concentrated in vestment in high -expected-return segments (footwear technology, manufacturing
innovation) while reducing relative allocation to lower-return segments (certain design categories).
The COVID-19 pandemic produced a sharp contraction in patent filings followed by a selective recovery
that did not restore the pre-2016 trajectory. This pattern is consistent with an exogenous demand shock
exposing the risks of concentration: firms that had concentrated IP investment in segments tied to
physical retail and event-driven demand experienced disproportionate disruption when those demand
channels collapsed.
4.2 Concentration  Dynamics in Chinese Consumer Brands
Chinese consumer brands provide particularly compelling evidence for the AI concentration hypothesis.
Pop Mart International, the Hong Kong -listed designer toy company, exemplifies the risks of IP
concentration in character-based businesses. Pop Mart's revenue has been heavily dependent on a small
number of flagship IP characters, with Labubu and Molly together accounting for a dominant share of
sales at various periods. The company's stock price has exhibited significant volatility tied to character
popularity cycles, consistent with H3.
Huawei's patent portfolio, one of the world's largest, presents a different concentration dynamic. With
over 120,000 active patents globally, Huawei has achieved substantial diversification across
telecommunications, cloud computing, artificial intelligenc e, and automotive technology. However, its
concentration in standards -essential patents for 5G technology creates a specific form of regulatory
concentration risk, as geopolitical tensions and sanctions regimes directly affect the commercial value
of these assets. This illustrates that IP concentration risk is multidimensional and cannot be captured
by technology-domain HHI alone.
Xiaomi's patent strategy represents an intermediate case. The company has rapidly expanded its patent
portfolio since its founding, with particular emphasis on smartphone technology, IoT devices, and
connected home ecosystems. Xiaomi's relatively recent entry into patent-intensive competition means

4.3 Monte Carlo Simulation Results
Our Monte Carlo simulations compare the risk profiles of AI -optimized and diversified benchmark
portfolios across demand scenarios. The results reveal a consistent pattern: the AI -optimized portfolio
produces higher expected returns (approximately 8–15 percent improvement in mean portfolio value,
depending on the firm and simulation specification) but exhibits significantly wider return distributions.
Specifically, the 5th percentile outcome (approximating a 95 percent VaR) is 22 –35 percent worse for
the AI-optimized portfolio compared to the diversified benchmark.
The CVaR analysis reinforces this finding. The expected loss conditional on being in the worst 5 percent
of scenarios is materially higher for concentrated portfolios. This result is driven by the correlation
structure of technology segments: during advers e demand conditions, segments that AI had identified
as high -return tend to be positively correlated, producing simultaneous losses that a diversified
portfolio would partially offset.

4.4 Sensitivity Analysis
Sensitivity analysis reveals that the AI-induced concentration risk effect is most pronounced when: (a)
the AI forecast accuracy is moderate rather than very high (at very high accuracy, concentration may be
justified because the AI correctly identifies pe rsistently superior segments), (b) cross -segment
correlations increase (which occurs during demand shocks and macroeconomic uncertainty), and (c)
the firm operates in a market with high demand volatility (which characterizes Chinese consumer
markets more than mature developed markets).
The finding regarding forecast accuracy has important practical implications. AI systems with moderate
accuracy produce a particularly dangerous combination: they are accurate enough to induce
management confidence in concentrated strategies but not accura te enough to consistently identify
regime changes that would justify such concentration. This creates a zone of "false confidence" where
AI-guided concentration maximizes expected harm. This is consistent with evidence that machine

4.5 The Role of Macroeconomic Uncertainty
Consistent with H4, the interaction between IP concentration and macroeconomic uncertainty
significantly amplifies crash risk. During the COVID -19 period (2020–2021), firms with above-median
IP concentration (HHI > 0.25) experienced NCSKEW values approximately 0.35 units higher than firms
with below-median concentration, compared to a difference of only 0.12 units during the pre-pandemic
period (2017 –2019). This suggests that the marginal risk contribution of IP concentration
approximately triples during periods of high macroeconomic uncertainty.
For firms with significant exposure to Chinese markets, the effect is further amplified by China-specific
uncertainty factors, including rapid regulatory changes in the technology sector, trade tensions affecting
IP enforcement and technology transfer, and  the distinctive dynamics of Chinese consumer markets
where viral trends can produce exceptionally sharp demand shifts for character -based and brand -
dependent IP assets.
5 Implications for Corporate Risk Management and Policy
5.1 Corporate Risk Management
Our findings have direct implications for corporate IP strategy. First, firms deploying AI for IP portfolio
optimization should explicitly incorporate concentration constraints into their optimization
frameworks. Just as modern financial portfolio optimize rs include diversification requirements and
maximum position limits, AI -based IP tools should impose floors on the number of active technology
segments and ceilings on individual segment weights.
Second, IP portfolio reviews should adopt the risk -adjusted performance metrics used in financial
portfolio management. The Sharpe ratio, adapted for IP portfolios as the ratio of excess IP value to IP
portfolio risk, provides a single metric that penalizes concentrated strategies that achieve high returns
only by accepting disproportionate risk [27]. Regular computation of IP-domain Sharpe ratios, VaR, and
CVaR can discipline IP investment committees against the temptation of AI -recommended
concentration.

5.2 Policy Implications
For policymakers, our analysis highlights the need for greater attention to the systemic risk implications
of AI deployment in corporate IP management. If multiple firms in the same industry adopt similar AI
optimization algorithms, their IP portfolios may  converge on the same technology segments, creating
industry-wide concentration risk and reducing the diversity of innovation pathways. This has
implications for competition policy, patent system design, and innovation ecosystem resilience.
In the Chinese context, where government industrial policy actively shapes technology investment
patterns, the interaction between policy -directed R&D priorities, AI -optimized IP allocation, and
market-driven demand creates complex concentration dynamics. Policymakers should consider
whether current IP incentive structures (such as patent subsidies and high-tech enterprise tax benefits)
inadvertently reinforce AI -driven concentration by rewarding volume in narrow technology domains
rather than strategic diversification.
5.3 Brand Equity, Intellectual Capital, and the Imperative of IP Optimisation
The analysis presented in this paper underscores a broader strategic reality: intellectual property does
not operate in isolation but is deeply embedded in the wider architecture of a firm's intellectual capital
and brand equity. Intellectual capital, unde rstood as the aggregate of human capital (expertise,
creativity, organisational knowledge), structural capital (processes, databases, information systems),
and relational capital (customer relationships, brand reputation, stakeholder networks), constitutes the
primary driver of value creation in knowledge -intensive industries [28], [29]. Meta -analytic evidence
confirms that voluntary disclosure of intellectual capital is value-relevant, being associated with higher
market value, lower cost of equity, and hi gher accounting performance [30]. Patents, trademarks,
designs, and trade secrets are the codified, legally protectable subset of this broader capital stock, and
their strategic management determines how effectively a firm can extract and defend the value
embedded in its intangible resources.
Brand equity serves as a critical amplifier of IP value. A strong brand transforms a patent portfolio from
a defensive legal instrument into a revenue -generating asset class. Nike's swoosh trademark, for
instance, magnifies the commercial value of every fo otwear technology patent in its portfolio by
associating the underlying innovation with consumer trust, aspiration, and willingness to pay a
premium. Conversely, a weak brand diminishes the licensing and litigation value of even technically
superior patents, because market participants discount the commercial relevance of IP that lacks brand-
mediated market access. This interdependence means that IP portfolio optimisation cannot be
conducted in a vacuum; it must account for the brand architecture and intellectual capital ecosystem in
which the IP is deployed.
IP optimisation, therefore, extends beyond the narrow question of which patents to maintain or
abandon. It encompasses the alignment of the entire IP portfolio with the firm's brand strategy,
innovation pipeline, and competitive positioning. Effective IP optimisation requires firms to treat their

The importance of IP optimisation is particularly acute in the context of AI -driven portfolio
management. As our findings demonstrate, AI algorithms that optimise for expected return without
regard for the broader intellectual capital context risk concentr ating IP investment in technology
segments that are algorithmically favoured but strategically misaligned with the firm's brand
positioning or relational capital. A consumer electronics firm, for example, might be algorithmically
directed to concentrate patents in hardware components while neglecting the software ecosystem and
user experience innovations that sustain its brand differentiation and customer loyalty.
Moreover, the valuation of intellectual property is itself contingent on intellectual capital factors that
standard financial models often overlook [29]. The economic value of a patent depends not only on its
technical claims and market coverage but also o n the human capital available to commercialise it, the
structural capital (manufacturing processes, supply chains, distribution networks) needed to bring the
patented innovation to market, and the relational capital (licensing partnerships, industry allian ces,
regulatory relationships) that determines the scope of its commercial exploitation. AI optimisation
frameworks that reduce IP value to a  single expected-return metric risk systematic misallocation by
ignoring these contextual dependencies.
Firms seeking to optimise their IP portfolios in the AI era should therefore adopt a holistic intellectual
capital perspective. This means integrating IP portfolio analytics with brand valuation models, human
capital assessments, and relational capital mapping to ensure that optimisation decisions reflect the full
spectrum of value creation pathways. It also means recognising that IP optimisation is not merely a cost-
reduction exercise but a strategic capability that, when executed well, strengthens the firm's competitive
moat, enhances brand equity, and builds long-term organisational resilience against the demand shocks
and technological disruptions that our analysis identifies as the primary risk drivers for concentrated
IP portfolios.
Conclusion
This paper develops and tests a framework for understanding how AI-driven optimization of intellectual
property portfolios affects firm -level concentration risk. By extending Modern Portfolio Theory to IP
assets and introducing an AI allocation mechanism, we demonstrate that algorithmic optimization
creates a systematic tendency toward portfolio concentration that amplifies downside risk exposur e,
particularly during periods of macroeconomic uncertainty.
Our empirical analysis, drawing on patent portfolio data from Nike, Pop Mart, Huawei, and Xiaomi,
supports the four hypotheses: AI optimization increases concentration (H1), concentration increases
revenue volatility (H2) and crash risk (H3), and these eff ects are amplified during uncertainty (H4).
Monte Carlo simulations quantify the risk trade -off: AI-optimized portfolios deliver modestly higher

The practical implication is clear: AI is a powerful tool for IP portfolio management, but it must be
deployed within a risk management framework that explicitly accounts for concentration risk. The
financial portfolio management industry learned this less on through painful experience with
algorithmic trading systems that amplified market volatility. The IP management domain should learn
from financial markets rather than repeat their mistakes.
Future research should develop empirical identification strategies to distinguish AI -optimized from
non-AI-optimized portfolio decisions, examine the systemic risk implications of industry -wide AI
adoption in IP management, and explore optimal concentration constraints for AI systems operating in
different technology and market environments.
As the intersection of AI, IP, and financial risk becomes increasingly salient in the global economy, the
framework developed in this paper provides a foundation for rigorous analysis of the trade -offs
involved. Models should inform but not dictate: the qu alitative judgment of experienced patent
professionals and risk managers remains essential to navigate the uncertainty that no algorithm can
