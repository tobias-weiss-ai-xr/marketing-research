Abstract

The 2023 designation of the Cosmological Axis of Yogyakarta as a UNESCO World Heritage Site created opportunities
to strengthen its global identity, preserve its philosophical values, and support local communities. However, how
stakeholders translate these values into a coherent place brand remains insufficiently understood. This study examines
stakeholder roles, communication processes, and coordination constraints in heritage place branding. A qualitative
case study was conducted through semi -structured i nterviews with seven informants representing the Balai
Pengelolaan Kawasan Sumbu Filosofi (BPKSF), three local administrative units, and two Tourism Awareness Groups
(Pokdarwis). The interviews were supplemented by UNESCO, heritage -management, regulatory, institutional, and
communication documents, and the data were analyzed thematically. The findings demonstrate that BPKSF functions
primarily as a coordinator, facilitator, and institutional bridge; local administrative units mediate communication
between higher-level authorities and local communities; and Pokdarwis translates institutional heritage narratives
and philosophical values into locally grounded cultural activities, tourism experiences, and kampung narratives.
Communication occurs through coordination meetings, working groups, community consultations, heritage education,
and cultural events. Nevertheless, information remains unevenly distributed, stakeholder initiatives are fragmented,
and local potential has not been integrated into a coherent her itage place brand. The study contributes by
conceptualizing heritage place branding as a multilevel heritage -value translation process involving institutional
coordination, local contextualization, and community-based experiential translation.

INTRODUCTION
World Heritage designation can transform cultural heritage into a source of place identity,
international visibility, tourism development, and economic opportunity (Van der Zee et al., 2024;
Zhang et al., 2022 ). However, heritage place branding should not be reduced to UNESCO labels,
campaigns, logos, or slogans. Heritage sites are living environments embedded in local identities,
practices, livelihoods, and everyday experiences. Branding must therefore balance conservation,
tourism, and community interests to avoid commercialization, disruption of livelihoods, and
stakeholder conflict (Liu et al., 2022).
Heritage place branding is a communication process that interprets and translates cultural
meanings into recognizable place identities. Heritage -management institutions formulate formal
narratives, local administrations contextualize them, and community organizations connect them

The Cosmological Axis of Yogyakarta and its Historic Landmarks was inscribed on the
UNESCO World Heritage List on September 18, 2023. Extending from Panggung Krapyak through
the Yogyakarta Palace to Tugu Pal Putih, it represents Javanese understandings of the human life
cycle and of relationships among humans, God, and nature (Syahdani & Kardanardi, 2022; UNESCO
World Heritage Centre, 2023). As both a monumental arrangement and a living cultural landscape,
its management must reconcile conservation and international visibility with community practices,
mobility, and livelihoods (Hendariningrum et al., 2025; Kresnanto et al., 2025).
This study examines how comm unication among BPKSF, local administrative units, and
Pokdarwis supports the heritage place branding of Yogyakarta’s Cosmological Axis. It analyzes
stakeholder roles, communication channels, the translation of heritage values, and coordination
constraints. It contributes by conceptualizing heritage place branding as a multilevel process of
institutional coordination, local contextualization, and community-based experiential translation.

LITERATURE REVIEW

Heritage Place Branding
Heritage place branding draws on tangible heritage, intangible cultural values, collective
memories, and local narratives to construct and communicate a distinctive place identity. Unlike
conventional destination promotion, it links heritage preservation to the meanings and experiences
of local communities (Csurgó & Smith, 2022; Taha & Abdelfattah, 2023). UNESCO designation can
enhance a place’s international visibility and legitimacy; however, effective branding requires more
than promoting monuments and physical attractions. It must also incorporate local traditions,
collective memories, cultural values, and everyday community practices ( Amer et al., 2023 ).
Consequently, heritage place branding is in creasingly understood as a collaborative socio -spatial
process rather than a purely promotional activity, with growing attention to lived historic
environments and local community participation (Logar, 2025; Jafarli & Canavari, 2025).

Stakeholders in Heritage Place Branding
Recent studies emphasize that effective heritage branding requires collaboration among
government institutions, local communities, tourism organizations, and other stakeholders (Nobre
& Sousa, 2022; Taha & Abdelfattah, 2023). Heritage place branding involves multiple stakeholders,
including government institutions, heritage managers, businesses, local communities, and visitors.
Local communities are particularly important as co -creators, cultural knowledge holders, and
ambassadors who communicate a heritage place's identity and values (Amer et al., 2023; Jain et al.,
2022). Howev er, stakeholder participation is often unequal because government and business
actors possess greater authority and resources, while community involvement may remain
consultative rather than influential (Reynolds et al., 2022; Källström & Siljeklint, 2024).

Stakeholder Communication in Heritage Management
Stakeholder communication in heritage management encompasses the processes and
power dynamics through which government institutions, local communitie s, experts, tourism
organizations, and private actors exchange information, negotiate heritage values, articulate
community aspirations, and coordinate conservation decisions ( Li et al., 2026 ; Seila et al., 2025).

RESEARCH METHOD
This study used a qualitative case-study design to examine stakeholder communication in
the heritage place -branding process ( Creswell & Poth, 2018 ). The unit of analysis was the
communication process among selected heritage -management authorities, local administrative
units, and community-based tourism organizations. The analysis focused on stakeholder roles,
communication channels, heritage -value translation, community engagement and feedback, and
coordination constraints.
Seven informants from six organizational units were purpo sively selected based on their
institutional roles, direct involvement in the Cosmological Axis, and experience in heritage
management, community engagement, or local cultural and tourism initiatives. They comprised
two BPKSF representatives, three representatives of local administrative units, and two Pokdarwis
representatives. Panggungharjo is designated as a kalurahan, whereas Prawirodirjan and Patehan
are kelurahan. Therefore, this study uses the collective term “local administrative units.”

Data were collected between May and August 2026 through semi -structured interviews
and document analysis. The interviews examined institutional roles, communication relationships
and channels, interpretations of the Cosmological Axis and its philosophical values, the translation
of heritage values into local programs and tourism activities, community engagement, feedback
mechanisms, and coordination barriers. All interviews were conducted face-to-face in Indonesian.
With participants’ consent, interviews were audio -recorded and transcribed verbatim. Relevant
quotations were translated into English and cross -checked against the original Indonesian
transcripts to preserve their contextual meaning. Documentary data included the UNESCO
nomination dossier, heritage -management plans, regulatory and institutional documents,

FINDINGS AND DISCUSSION

Stakeholder Roles in the Heritage Place-Branding Process
The findings reveal that the heritage place-branding process for Yogyakarta’s Cosmological
Axis involves differentiated yet interdependent roles across institutional and community levels.
BPKSF, local -level administrations, and community -based tourism orga nizations contribute
through distinct communication functions. BPKSF coordinates institutional actors and formal
heritage narratives; local-level administrations contextualize heritage policies within local settings;
and Pokdarwis translates heritage meani ngs into cultural and tourism experiences. These
complementary roles demonstrate that the heritage place brand is not produced or controlled by a
single institution but is collectively constructed through multilevel stakeholder communication.
1. BPKSF as Coordinator, Facilitator, and Institutional Bridge
BPKSF occupies a central but primarily non -executive role in the heritage place-branding
process. Its main functions include coordinating government agencies and other
stakeholders, facilitating cross -institutional meetings, documenting issues and
management developments, communicating recommendations to relevant authorities, and
aligning regional government directions with sectoral institutions. BPKSF lacks the
authority or resources to implement all policies related to conservation, spatial planning,
transportation, tourism, and community empowerment. Consequently, its effectiveness
depends on its ability to facilitate coordination and influence institutions with the relevant
mandates and resources.
As one rep resentative explained, “Our role is primarily coordination because we do not
have the formal instruments to execute policies directly” (BPKSF -1). BPKSF also frames
UNESCO recognition as a means rather than the final objective of heritage management. Its
representatives emphasized that the Cosmological Axis should not be promoted merely as
a collection of physical landmarks or as a UNESCO label. The more important objective is to
internalize its philosophical values and to connect heritage conservation with community
well-being. BPKSF therefore communicates an institutional vision in which the
Cosmological Axis is understood as a living cultural landscape that embodies the human life
cycle and harmony among humans, God, and nature.
2. Local-Level Administrations as Administrative Mediators and Local Activators
Local-level administrations serve as intermediaries between provincial institutions and
local communities. They facilitate socialization programs, connect government agencies
with neighborhood organizations, communicate local aspirations, and integrate heritage -
related values into local cultural and development programs. However, their capacity to
perform these functions varies with local leadership, resources, institutional relationships,
cultural assets, and access to decision-making.
Panggungharjo demonstrates relatively strong local activation by translating sangkan
paraning dumadi into Merti Panggung Krapyak, the Cosmological Axis batik motif, the

3. Pokdarwis as Translators of Heritage into Tourism Experiences
The participating Pokdarwis  organizations operate closest to local cultural practices,
tourism businesses, community products, and visitor experiences. Their primary role is to
identify local assets and translate them into tourism narratives, routes, activities, products,
and experiences. In this sense, Pokdarwis serves as an experiential translator, connecting
institutional heritage narratives with the everyday cultural life of communities. In
Mantrijeron, this role is expressed through religious tourism in Jogokariyan, traditional
dance activities in Mangkuyudan, cultural markets, and local microenterprise products.
As the Panembahan representative stated, “Our role as a Pokdarwis is to package the area’s
potential more effectively to strengthen its identity and tourism appeal” (PW -2).
Nevertheless, the relationship between local tourism initiatives and the overarching
Cosmological Axis narrative remains inconsistent. Some Pokdarwis actors possess
extensive knowledge of tourism products and local cultural practices but have a more
limited understanding of the full philosophical narrative. Consequently, their initiatives
may be culturally relevant and locally meaningful without being clearly connected to a
unified Cosmological Axis place brand. Strengthening interpretive capacity and cross-
locality coordination is therefore necessary to align local tourism experiences with the
broader heritage narrative.

Multilevel Heritage Place-Branding Framework
The findings indicate that heritage place branding along the Cosmological Axis operates
through a multilevel translation process. The official values associated with UNESCO recognition
and the Cosmological Axis are not transmitted directly and uniformly to communities and visitors.
Instead, they pass through institutional and community level s, where they are coordinated,
contextualized, translated, and experienced.
At the heritage -management level, BPKSF interprets formal heritage principles,
conservation priorities, and philosophical values as institutional narratives and coordination
agendas. At the local-administrative level, local administrative units adapt these narratives to local
histories, cultural assets, social conditions, and development priorities. At the community-tourism
level, Pokdarwis transforms these contextualized narratives  into cultural routes, festivals,
performances, crafts, culinary products, kampung identities, and tourism experiences.
These expressions are intended to be interpreted by residents and visitors through
participation, everyday experiences, and mediated com munication. However, their actual
interpretations were not directly examined in this study. In the proposed framework, their
responses could inform community organizations and government institutions, although the
interviewed stakeholders perceived the available feedback mechanisms as inconsistent.

Discussion
The findings demonstrate that heritage place branding along Yogyakarta’s Cosmological
Axis is a multilevel communication process rather than merely a promotional activity. BPKSF
performs institutional translation by linking UNESCO principles and philosophi cal values to
conservation priorities; local administrative units contextualize these narratives to local histories
and community needs; and Pokdarwis transforms them into cultural activities, tourism products,
routes, and visitor experiences. These differentiated yet interdependent roles align with previous
studies that conceptualize place branding as collectively constructed through stakeholder networks
(Kalandides et al., 2012; Muñiz Martínez, 2016). However, the findings further show that heritage
communication is not linear because meanings are repeatedly interpreted, negotiated, and
reconstructed across institutional and community levels.
The presence of multiple levels does not automatically produce integrated communication.
Vertical fragmentation occurs when institutional information is not fully understood or acted on
locally and when community aspirations do not reach higher -level decision-making. Horizontal
fragmentation arises when local administ rative units and Pokdarwis develop initiatives without
shared interpretive principles or cross -locality coordination. Unequal authority, resources,
institutional access, and feedback mechanisms further shape stakeholder participation ( Reynolds
et al., 2022 ). Consequently, formal consultation may remain procedural rather than provide
meaningful influence, thereby weakening community ownership and brand legitimacy.
The study’s theoretical contribution is conceptualizing heritage pla ce branding as a
multilevel, recursive process of translating heritage values. It extends stakeholder -based place-
branding research by identifying distinct institutional, contextual, and experiential translation
functions. Unlike studies that focus primari ly on the effects of World Heritage designation on
tourism, visibility, and community livelihoods (Liu et al., 2022; Van der Zee et al., 2024), this study
explains how formal heritage values are transformed into locally meaningful narratives and
experiences. It also extends collaborative heritage -governance research by showing that
incomplete feedback loops and unequal institutional authority can disrupt heritage -value
translation and produce narrative fragmentation (Bashir & Sawhney, 2023; Kim et al., 2023).

CONCLUSIONS
This study demonstrates that heritage place branding along Yogyakarta’s UNESCO
Cosmological Axis is collectively constructed  through multilevel communication. BPKSF
coordinates institutional narratives, local administrative units contextualize heritage values, and
Pokdarwis translates them into cultural activities and tourism experiences. This process is

LIMITATIONS & FURTHER RESEARCH
The study is limited by its sample of seven informants, three stakeholder categories, and a
qualitative single-case design. Future research should employ longitudinal, comparative, or mixed-
methods approaches with broader stakeholder groups to examine how communication,
institutional power, participation, and feedback influence community ownership and brand
legitimacy.
