Abstract
Sports events are increasingly used to shape city branding and image, yet their role in
secondary cities—especially in Southeast Asia—remains underexplored. This qualitative study
employs a self-narrative inquiry approach to examine how sports events have contributed to the
branding of Chiang Mai, Thailand, between 2015 and 2025. Eleven key informants were purposively
selected from stakeholder groups including event organizers, business owners, sports officials,
and government representatives. By analyzing narratives, the research uncovers strategies that
have positioned Chiang Mai as an active, culturally rich, and globally connected city. Key factors
include integrating local heritage, utilizing natural landscapes, and encouraging community
engagement. The findings highlight the importance of cross-sector collaboration, grassroots
participation, and authentic storytelling. Sports events have not only enhanced civic pride but also
increased international visibility. The study provides transferable lessons for similar urban contexts,
emphasizing the use of culturally embedded sports events as tools for soft power and sustainable
city branding.

Introduction
Sports events in recent decades have become influential tools in shaping city image and
branding on a global scale (Alshikhy et al., 2025; Oshimi & Harada, 2019). Mega-events, such as
the FIFA World Cup and regional games, present opportunities for host cities to reframe or refine
its public image, enhance tourism and stimulate economic development (Chankuna, 2023;
Chankuna & Sukdee, 2025). Beyond their athletic spectacle, sports events serve as strategic
instruments for urban transformation, identity formation, and international positioning (Chankuna
& Amphai, 2021; Chankuna & Khositdham, 2020). Therefore, since the early 2000s, the relationship
between sports events and city branding has been drawing ever-increasing scholarly and
professional attention of those who work in the area of urban and sports management.
Despite the growing body of literature on sport-related urban development, existing
research primarily focuses on large metropolitan areas or global mega-events. Studies have
explored branding outcomes of the Olympics and World Cups (Smith, 2012; Kaplanidou & Vogt,
2010,) and others have investigated the impact of sports events on urban regeneration in U.S.
cities (Burbank, et al., 2001). However little research has focused on the role of sports events in
secondary cities. Nor have studies fully explored the relationship between secondary cities and
national efforts to harness soft power, especially in the context of Southeast Asia (e.g., Alshikhy
et al., 2025; Chankuna, 2023; Chankuna & Khositdham, 2020; Loima & Kaewnuch, 2020; Phob-
udom et al., 2025). This imbalance highlights a critical research gap concerning how non-Western,
mid-sized, peripheral cities culturally distinct from a nation’s center, navigate and operationalize
sport-led branding strategies in non-mega event contexts (Chankuna and Tongsook, 2024;
Chankuna et al, 2024; Chankuna and Sukdee, 2025). To address this gap, the present study
focuses on Chiang Mai, Thailand—a secondary city with a rich cultural heritage, increasing regional
visibility, and a growing reputation as a host of diverse sports events (Chankuna & Khosidtham,
2020; Chankuna & Sukdee, 2025). Rather than merely describing the city’s assets, this research
critically investigates Chiang Mai’s record of harnessing sporting events between 2015 and 2025
to construct a distinctive city image and brand identity.
This case study offers theoretical and practical contributions by contextualizing sport
events as instruments of soft power within culturally embedded urban narratives. In its original
conception, Nye (1990) defined soft power as the ability to influence others through attraction
rather than coercion or economic inducement, grounded in culture, political values, and foreign
policy legitimacy. In this study, soft power is not equated merely with city branding or tourism
marketing. Rather, sports events in Chiang Mai are interpreted as platforms that generate symbolic
attraction, intercultural engagement, and reputational capital that may contribute to Thailand’s

Methodology
This qualitative study adopted a self-narrative inquiry design, grounded in interpretivism
and guided by the principles of Grounded Theory (Cullen & Brennan, 2021; Strauss & Corbin,
1990). The method was selected to deeply explore stakeholders’ lived experiences and
interpretations of Chiang Mai’s sports events and their branding implications.
Justification for Methodological Approach
Self-narrative inquiry was selected based on its roots in interpretivist and narrative
research traditions, which emphasize meaning-making through lived experience (Pino Gavidia &
Adu, 2022). Compared to phenomenology, which focuses on the essence of lived experiences,
and case study approaches, which emphasize bounded systems, narrative inquiry enables
participants to construct temporal and meaning-rich stories that reflect the dynamic interaction
between sports, culture, and identity. This approach was particularly appropriate for examining
how sports events have shaped Chiang Mai’s city branding over time, as it allows participants to
connect personal experiences with evolving urban identity processes. In practice, interviews began
with broad narrative prompts encouraging participants to recount their involvement with sports
events, followed by reflective probes to deepen interpretation and clarify links to city image and
branding.

Sampling and Participant Recruitment
The study targeted five stakeholder groups: local government and tourism officials, event
organizers, sports association members, local business owners, and residents with sports event
involvement. Participants were recruited through direct outreach and public announcements on
official websites and Facebook pages. An initial pool of 52 individuals expressed interest during
a two-month enrollment window (May–June 2025).
Sampling Criteria
Three inclusion criteria were applied:
1. Voluntary, informed consent was mandatory for participation. Only 33 applicants met
the qualification criteria.
2. Participants had to be involved in at least five sports events in Chiang Mai between
2015 and 2025—as organizers, spectators, athletes, or business operators. A total of 24 applicants
met this inclusion criterion.
3. They needed the ability to discuss Chiang Mai’s cultural identity and city branding,
assessed via a 10-item questionnaire. A minimum score of 8/10 was required. Ultimately, 11 key
informants met all criteria, aligning with Sandelowski’s (1995) recommendation for qualitative
sample sufficiency. Participant profiles are presented in Table 1.
Sample Event Lists
The study focused on approximately 20 distinct sports events held in Chiang Mai between
2015 and 2025, spanning a range of disciplines. Notable annual road races included the Muang
Thai Chiang Mai Marathon, Chiang Mai International Half Marathon, PEA Mini Marathon, and
various university and charity runs such as the ATHLETIC CMU RUN and the Mövenpick x Rajavej
Charity Run. Chiang Mai also emerged as a hub for trail and ultra-marathon events, notably the
UTMB Chiang Mai Trail Series (featuring races such as the Trans-INT 160 and Elephant 100),
CNX 100K, and the Rainy Season Run. The city further showcased its diverse sports landscape
through multi-sport and international competitions, including the Thailand Throwdown (CrossFit),
Chiang Mai International 7’s Rugby, Ball Hockey Lanna Cup, and the Chiang Mai International
Touch Tournament. It also hosted preliminary rounds of the 2025 SEA Games in football and
volleyball. Combat sports like Muay Thai at Thapae Stadium and community-oriented activities
such as the Chiang Mai Hash House Harriers trail runs were also featured (OpenAI, 2025).

Data Collection and Interview Protocol
Semi-structured interviews were conducted via Zoom, scheduled at least two weeks in
advance to allow participants to prepare. Each interview followed a five-theme guide adapted from
Oshimi & Harada (2019) and Loima & Kaewnuch (2020) (Table 2). Interview questions were
backward-translated from English to Thai and validated by five experts in sport management and
business. The Item Content Validity Index (I-CVI) was calculated at 0.96, indicating high relevance
and clarity (Ayre & Scally, 2014). A research assistant was responsible for audio recording and
notetaking. Interviews lasted 45-75 minutes and were later transcribed for analysis.

Data Analysis
Data were analyzed using a systematic three-step inductive coding process grounded
in the principles of narrative and grounded theory analysis: open coding, axial coding, and selective
coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1990).
In the first stage, open coding, the researchers conducted a line-by-line review of each
transcribed interview to identify discrete units of meaning. Codes were generated inductively from
participants’ language and expressions, capturing actions, values, experiences, and emotional
tones without relying on any predefined categories. Examples of initial codes included “cultural
pride in marathons,” “community-driven organization,” and “temples as race landmarks.”
Next, during axial coding, relationships among these open codes were examined to group
them into broader conceptual categories. For instance, codes related to cultural elements—such
as traditional dress, blessings by monks, and race routes through heritage sites—were clustered
under a more abstract category: “cultural integration in event design.” Axial coding allowed the
research team to establish thematic links between individual experiences and structural factors
such as branding strategy, identity formation, and civic participation.
Finally, in the selective coding phase, the researchers synthesized the major categories
into five overarching themes that reflected the core narrative arcs shared across participants.
Selective coding prioritized coherence and explanatory power by identifying central phenomena
that encapsulated the broader meaning of participant stories—such as “community-based
branding,” “soft power through sport,” and “cultural-symbolic positioning.” All coding decisions
were reviewed collaboratively by the research team to ensure consistency, and divergent
interpretations were resolved through memo writing and iterative dialogue.
Importantly, no pre-existing coding frameworks or theoretical categories were imposed
during the process. This inductive strategy ensured that the themes remained grounded in the
participants’ own words and perspectives, preserving the authenticity of lived experiences while
allowing abstract patterns to emerge organically from the data.
Ethical Consideration
All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Although formal IRB
approval was not required for this study, procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (2013
amendment). Confidentiality was maintained through the use of pseudonyms, anonymized
transcripts, and secure storage of audio files. Only the principal researcher and a trained assistant
had access to raw data. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at
any point without consequence.

Reflexivity and Researcher Positionality
The principal researcher practiced reflexive monitoring throughout the data collection
process by maintaining a reflexive journal, engaging in ongoing self-questioning about personal
assumptions, and consciously regulating emotional responses during interviews. For example,
after discussions involving sensitive critiques of local authorities or event management, the
researcher documented potential biases and adjusted subsequent questioning to remain neutral
and non-leading. Research assistants also observed interview dynamics and provided feedback
to further ensure objectivity. The reflexivity protocol enhanced the study’s transparency and
trustworthiness.
Quality Assurance
Trustworthiness was ensured through four strategies:
1. Credibility: Enabled by the lead researcher’s 17+ years of interview experience.
2. Transferability: Achieved via thick description of context and participant roles.
3. Dependability: Strengthened through triangulation (interviews, field notes, participant
feedback).
4. Confirmability: Maintained through a neutral, structured data collection protocol and
research assistant verification.
To improve readability and avoid overwhelming readers, detailed sample event lists,
participant profiles, and interview themes are presented in the appendices.
Results
This section presents findings from semi-structured interviews with 11 purposively
selected key informants, using an inductive thematic analysis grounded in open, axial, and selective
coding. The analysis produced five overarching themes, each representing a distinct but
interconnected aspect of how sports events shaped Chiang Mai’s branding. To strengthen analytical
clarity, examples of the coding process are provided, and key findings are situated within broader
theoretical constructs such as soft power, place identity, and cultural branding.
Theme 1: Personal Engagement with Sports Events
Initial open codes such as “emotional attachment,” “annual participation,” and
“volunteering pride” were clustered during axial coding under the concept of deep-rooted
engagement. This theme reflects how sustained and emotionally meaningful participation in sports
events contributed to participants’ sense of place identity and belonging.

and emotional investment participants felt toward these recurring events, which transformed them

Several participants expressed that recurring sports events have become symbolic
expressions of Chiang Mai’s collective identity. As one informant noted, “I look forward to the
Chiang Mai Marathon every year—not just for the run, but because it’s a reminder of who we are
as a city. It’s more than sport—it’s about pride and unity.” (Informant 4). This sentiment was echoed
by others. Informant 7 stated, “When the city hosts these events, you can feel the energy. People
from different backgrounds come together. It shows that Chiang Mai is active and welcoming.”
Similarly, Informant 2 reflected, “For me, the marathon is not just about athletes. It’s when local
businesses, families, and volunteers all participate. It strengthens our sense of belonging.”  The
convergence of these perspectives suggests that sports events function not merely as recreational
activities but as recurring rituals that reinforce civic identity and social cohesion.This form of place-

co-creation,” whereby sport becomes a medium through which residents actively shape local
meaning and identity.
Theme 2: Perceived Impacts on Chiang Mai’s Image and Identity
Codes such as “changing image,” “from temples to trails,” and “modern yet traditional”
were synthesized into a theme capturing the evolution of city identity. Informants noted that sports
events contributed to shifting Chiang Mai’s reputation from a solely cultural or historical destination
to one that also represented vitality, health, and global connectivity.
“Chiang Mai used to be about old temples and slow life. Now we’re a city
of movement—people come for marathons, trail runs, and feel the energy”
(Informant 9).

what Anholt (2010) terms a “competitive identity”—a blend of cultural authenticity and aspirational

Theme 3: Cultural Integration and Local Pride
This theme emerged from codes such as “temples on the route,” “local dress and cheer,”
and “rituals in sports,” highlighting how local cultural elements were embedded into event design.

events to enhance their uniqueness and travel “authenticity.”
“The race passed Wat Phra Singh, and local children in traditional clothes cheered us

Theme 4: Strategies for Branding and Stakeholder Engagement
From codes like “peer promotion,” “cross-sector alliances,” and “social media buzz,” this
theme reflects both formal and informal strategies used to promote Chiang Mai through sports
events. Participants noted the effectiveness of digital storytelling, partnerships with tourism
agencies, and grassroots volunteerism in spreading positive city imagery.
“Instagram did more for us than any brochure. Visitors posted trails, temples, and finish
lines—these stories travel faster than official campaigns” (Informant 2).
This aligns with contemporary sport branding theories that emphasize co-created
narratives and decentralized promotion (Oshimi & Harada, 2019). Moreover, these informal
branding mechanisms extend Chiang Mai’s soft power influence by allowing authentic, citizen-
driven narratives to shape perceptions organically.
Theme 5: Transferable Lessons for Comparable Urban Contexts
This final theme was developed from codes including “community ownership,” “natural
advantage,” and “don’t copy-paste.” Informants expressed that Chiang Mai’s success was rooted
in culturally embedded, participatory practices, rather than top-down event planning.
“The secret isn’t just having events—it’s how we make them ours. If another city wants
to do it, they have to find what’s authentic to them” (Informant 10).
These findings emphasize the importance of contextualized soft power, wherein strategies
must reflect local identities and cultural assets. Attempting to replicate Chiang Mai’s approach
without cultural alignment risks superficial branding and weak resident engagement (Chankuna
& Sukdee, 2025).
Cross-Theme Synthesis
Taken together, the five themes suggest that sports events in Chiang Mai functioned not
only as economic stimulants but also as symbolic platforms for constructing place identity,
demonstrating “soft power,” and strengthening civic cohesion. The integration of cultural storytelling,
stakeholder co-creation, and social media amplification represents a holistic model of sport-led
urban branding.

Discussion
This discussion addresses the two research questions: (1) How have sports events
influenced the branding of Chiang Mai? and (2) What transferable insights can inform branding
strategies in similar cities? Findings are interpreted through the lens of sport-led urban development,
soft power, and cultural branding. To improve clarity and coherence, this section is structured
around five strategic insights derived from the thematic analysis.
1. Sports as Vehicles of Local Identity and Global Visibility
Sports events in Chiang Mai have played a vital role in shaping a distinctive brand that
fuses tradition with vitality. Events such as the UTMB Trail Series and Chiang Mai Marathon
allowed the city to present itself as both culturally grounded and internationally appealing. These
findings support the concept of “competitive identity” (Anholt, 2010), where branding extends
beyond tourism promotion into soft power influence. Unlike mega-events in global capitals, Chiang
Mai’s approach demonstrates how small to mid-sized cities can use recurring sports events to
reposition themselves on the global stage—without losing their cultural authenticity.
2. Community-Based Event Ownership
The success of Chiang Mai’s sports events is strongly tied to grassroots engagement.
Local communities co-designed event narratives, volunteered, and infused traditional elements
into event experiences. This participatory approach echoes Holt’s (2004) model of cultural branding,
where brand strength derives from bottom-up storytelling rather than top-down campaigns.
However, such localized engagement may pose challenges in mid-sized cities characterized by
weaker civic infrastructure, limited public–private collaboration, fragmented cultural identity, or
lower levels of community participation—conditions that may constrain the effectiveness of
culturally embedded sports-based branding strategies. Implementing similar models elsewhere
requires careful adaptation to local social structures and stakeholder dynamics.
3. Integrating Natural and Cultural Assets
Chiang Mai’s unique geography and heritage sites were skillfully incorporated into event
design, enhancing participant experience and symbolic value. This use of place-specific narratives
supports Oshimi and Harada’s (2019) argument that cultural resonance enhances brand impact.
Nevertheless, the transferability of Chiang Mai’s model remains context-dependent. Mid-sized
Southeast Asian cities that are peripheral to national capitals and possess modern infrastructure
suitable for hosting sporting events—but lack similarly distinctive natural landscapes or consolidated
historical-cultural identities—may find it more challenging to generate powerful place-based brand

highlights that the effectiveness of culturally grounded sports branding strategies depends not
only on event organization capacity but also on the strength and coherence of local identity assets.
4. Cross-Sectoral Collaboration and Informal Promotion
Formal partnerships with tourism boards and informal mechanisms like social media
storytelling played complementary roles in branding success. The viral potential of participant-
generated content, particularly on Instagram and Facebook, amplified city image beyond
conventional marketing efforts (Chankuna & Amphai, 2021). This supports the notion that sport
branding today is shaped by co-created, decentralized narratives. Yet, reliance on digital virality
introduces unpredictability—cities must be prepared to manage reputational risks, misinformation,
or uneven representation across events.
5. Flexible, Scalable, and Culturally Aligned Strategies
Chiang Mai demonstrated adaptability through smaller, scalable event formats during
disruptions like the COVID-19 pandemic (Chankuna & Sukdee, 2025). These events retained
cultural integrity and public engagement, showing that impactful branding does not always require
scale, but rather strategic fit and narrative alignment. Still, the success of this model depends on
sustained institutional support and long-term planning. In Chiang Mai, such support has not
stemmed from a single centralized authority but from collaborative efforts among the Chiang Mai
Provincial Administrative Organization, the Chiang Mai Municipality, the Provincial Office of Tourism
and Sports, the Tourism Authority of Thailand (Chiang Mai Office), and local universities and private
event organizers. Since the mid-2010s, these bodies have provided logistical coordination,
infrastructure investment, tourism promotion, and regulatory oversight that enabled recurring
sports events to scale and gain international visibility. While this multi-actor governance model
has facilitated event continuity and growing international recognition, it has also revealed
challenges, including fragmented branding coordination and the absence of a unified long-term
sports diplomacy strategy. Therefore, institutional sustainability—not merely event frequency—
remains a critical condition for maintaining the city’s evolving sports-based identity. In Chiang Mai,
sports events have typically been supported through a mixed funding structure combining locally
generated revenues (such as participant registration fees, corporate sponsorships, and private-

Critical Reflections and Alternative Interpretations
While this study highlights Chiang Mai’s branding success, it is important to consider
alternative viewpoints. One potential critique is that embedding culture into events risks
commodification or superficial representation. Additionally, overreliance on sports events may
marginalize other forms of cultural expression or lead to resident fatigue if not diversified over
time. For example, cities such as Barcelona (Spain) and Venice (Italy) have experienced public
concern over tourism saturation and event-driven commercialization, where recurring large-scale
events intensified pressure on infrastructure, housing markets, and local cultural practices.
Similarly, in cities like Amsterdam (Netherlands), debates have emerged regarding the balance
between international event attractiveness and quality of life for residents. These cases illustrate
how sustained event-led visibility, while economically beneficial, can generate social tensions if
not carefully managed. Therefore, Chiang Mai’s strategy would benefit from maintaining a
diversified cultural portfolio in which sports events complement—rather than dominate—the broader
urban cultural ecosystem. Moreover, while narrative inquiry provided deep insights into personal
experiences, it may underrepresent dissenting or less visible stakeholders in Chiang Mai—such
as informal vendors affected by road closures during marathons, elderly residents experiencing
traffic disruption, migrant workers in peripheral districts, or cultural groups whose traditions are
not incorporated into sports programming—thereby limiting the breadth of perspectives captured.
Future research could address this by incorporating broader community surveys or critical discourse
analysis.

Conclusion of Discussion
In summary, Chiang Mai’s use of sports events for city branding can be understood within
a nuanced soft power framework. In Nye’s (1990) original formulation, soft power refers to the
capacity to influence international preferences through attraction grounded in culture, political
values, and legitimacy. While Thailand’s national government has adopted “soft power” as a policy
narrative—often emphasizing tourism promotion and cultural industries—Chiang Mai’s sports
initiatives operate primarily at the subnational level. Rather than constituting a centralized
geopolitical strategy directed from Bangkok, these events represent locally embedded practices
that align with broader national aspirations while remaining shaped by provincial governance
structures and community participation.
Comparatively, other mid-sized cities in Thailand such as Khon Kaen or Ubon Ratchathani,
and in Southeast Asia such as Yogyakarta (Indonesia) or Da Nang (Vietnam), possess
infrastructural capacity and peripheral positioning similar to Chiang Mai. However, variations in
historical symbolism, landscape distinctiveness, tourism integration, and civic cohesion may
influence their ability to replicate Chiang Mai’s culturally grounded sports branding model.
Therefore, while the Chiang Mai case offers a transferable conceptual framework, its effectiveness
depends on each city’s cultural assets, governance coordination, and relationship with national
policy agendas. This contextual sensitivity reinforces that sport-led soft power at the city level is
adaptive and relational rather than uniform or centrally imposed.
Conclusion
This study explored how sports events have influenced the city branding of Chiang Mai,
revealing that culturally embedded and community-driven approaches can shape a city’s identity,
visibility, and global appeal. Drawing on self-narratives from key stakeholders, the findings
emphasize that sports events served not only as tourism attractions but also as platforms for
place-based storytelling, cultural expression, and civic engagement.
Rather than positioning Chiang Mai as a definitive “model” for sustainable city branding,
this study highlights its value as a context-specific case that illustrates the potential of sport-led
branding strategies in secondary cities with rich cultural heritage and active civic participation.
The conclusions should be interpreted with caution, as the insights are grounded in a qualitative
inquiry focused on a single locale over a specific time frame (2015-2025).
The study offers practical implications for event organizers, policymakers, and tourism
authorities. Prioritizing cultural integration, fostering local ownership, and leveraging authentic
narratives can enhance branding outcomes without requiring large-scale, resource-intensive

Research Limitations
This study is subject to several limitations. First, as a qualitative inquiry based on self-
narrative interviews, the findings are inherently context-specific and may not be generalizable to
all urban or sports tourism contexts beyond Chiang Mai. The reliance on purposive sampling
introduces a self-selection bias, as participants who volunteered were more likely to have strong
opinions or positive experiences with sports events. This may have skewed the narratives toward
more favorable interpretations of Chiang Mai’s branding strategies. Second, narrative data—while
rich in personal meaning—can be influenced by social desirability bias. Participants may have
emphasized positive impacts or aligned their accounts with expected cultural or civic narratives,
potentially underreporting critical perspectives or challenges associated with event implementation.
Third, the temporal range of the study (2015-2025) introduces recall limitations. Participants were
asked to reflect on events spanning a decade, which increases the likelihood of memory distortion,
selective recall, or conflation of experiences. Although efforts were made to verify details through
triangulation, the retrospective nature of data collection remains a potential source of inaccuracy.
Finally, these limitations affect the transferability of the findings. While the study offers valuable
insights for cities with similar socio-cultural dynamics, its conclusions should be interpreted within
the context of Chiang Mai’s unique cultural, geographic, and political conditions. Replicating such
strategies elsewhere would require careful adaptation and further empirical testing.

Practical Implications for City Branding and Event Planning
1. Integrate Cultural Identity into Sports Event Design: Local event organizers and
branding authorities should intentionally incorporate Chiang Mai’s unique cultural elements—such
as Lanna traditions, heritage routes, and local festivals—into sports event planning and promotion.
This approach reinforces emotional connections with the city and enhances authenticity in branding
strategies.
2. Strengthen Stakeholder Collaboration: Cross-sector partnerships between municipal
agencies, private sponsors, tourism authorities, and local communities are essential. In resource-
constrained contexts, prioritizing community volunteers and public–private partnerships can reduce
operational costs while maximizing local participation and branding impact.
3. Leverage Sports as Soft Power Tools: Even mid-sized cities can position sports events
as instruments of soft diplomacy by encouraging intercultural exchange, inviting international
athletes, and aligning with ASEAN sports tourism strategies (Phob-udom et al., 2025). However,
cities should focus on scalable, context-appropriate events rather than replicating mega-event
models.
4. Promote Grassroots and Inclusive Participation: Design accessible, community-level
events that encourage broad citizen engagement—not only as participants but also as storytellers,
volunteers, and cultural ambassadors. This fosters civic pride and reduces dependence on large-
scale events.
Recommendation for future research:
1. Comparative Case Studies Across Secondary Cities: To assess transferability, future
studies could compare cities with similar socio-cultural and economic characteristics. Suggested
sample types include municipal planners, sports association leaders, and event participants from
cities such as Luang Prabang, Malang, or Hue. These cases can help determine which strategies
are locally bounded and which are adaptable across Southeast Asia.
2. Use of Mixed Methods and Digital Ethnography: Building on the current qualitative
approach, future research should explore digital ethnography—such as analyzing user-generated
content on platforms like Instagram and TikTok—to capture branding narratives in real time.
Sentiment analysis tools can complement interviews by quantifying public perception, offering a
broader view of city image formation beyond stakeholder accounts.
