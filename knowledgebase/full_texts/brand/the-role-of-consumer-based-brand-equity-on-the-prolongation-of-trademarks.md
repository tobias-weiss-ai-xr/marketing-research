Abstract
Trademarks are an integral part of a firm’s brand equity. While extant research looked at the outcomes of trademarks, e.g.,
a firm’s financial valuation, profitability, and productivity, limited knowledge exists regarding the factors influencing a
company’s decision to prolong or terminate trademarks. For the first time, the presented research investigates the effects of
consumer-based brand equity (CBBE) dimensions on the decision to prolong or terminate trademarks and how trademark
characteristics and the level of regulation in a product category moderate these effects. Utilizing a unique dataset covering 25
countries and 56 product categories from 2001 to 2019, tracking 1,446 trademarks, this research establishes empirical evi-
dence for CBBE’s critical role in trademark prolongations. The CBBE dimension relevant stature (reflecting brand knowledge,
esteem, and relevance) positively affects trademark prolongation, whereas the CBBE dimension energized differentiation
(reflecting brand uniqueness) negatively impacts trademark prolongation. Specifically, brand-association trademarks and
trademark age attenuate the negative effect of energized differentiation on trademark prolongation. Moreover, low levels of
regulation reduce the probability of trademark prolongation for brands scoring high on knowledge, esteem, and relevance.
Vice versa, low levels of regulation increase the probability of trademark prolongation for highly differentiated brands. These
results translate into important managerial implications.
Keywords Trademark · Brand equity · Relevant stature · Energized differentiation · Hazard model
Introduction
A trademark can consist of “any word, name, symbol […]
device, or combination thereof” (Lanham Act 1982, 15 US
Code, § 1127). These trademarks are integral to a firm’s
brand equity and are the most widely used form of intel-
lectual property protection (Krasnikov and Jayachandran
2022). Companies are using trademarks to legally protect
their brand assets from imitation, false advertising, misuse
by competitors, counterfeit operations, and grey market
sales (Ertekin et al. 2018; Fink et al. 2022; Wilson et al.
2016). Infringements of this nature can lead to significant
consequences, ranging from negative brand associations to
the erosion of brand integrity and revenue loss (Cao et al.
2022). For instance, copycat practices mimicking brands’
visual designs represent serious challenges for many com-
panies; trademarks limit a brand’s ability to imitate the
visual design of a competitor, ensuring that the uniqueness
of a firm’s visual assets is legally protected (Melnyk et al.
2014). Hence, achieving a precise comprehension of the
logic of strategic trademark management poses a pressing
and captivating challenge for management research, holding
significant guiding importance for the development of firms
and the facilitation of high-quality economic transformation
(Cao et al. 2022).
Strategic trademark management serves to streamline
product lines, rebrand, consolidate, manage the brand port-
folio, or better meet market demands and enhance opera-
tional efficiency (see Appendix 1 for examples). Remark -
ably, except for Melnyk et al. (2014), very little is known
about strategic trademark management, including trademark

prolongation and account for trademark characteristics, cul-

However, the role of brand equity in trademark prolonga-
tion remains unclear despite the close connection between
trademarks and brand building (Cao et al. 2022). This lack
of research is surprising because consumer-based brand
equity (CBBE) should be the essential factor in prolonging
or terminating trademarks since CBBE is closely related to
future cash flows generated from the trademark (Mizik and
Jacobson 2008). Specifically, we expect that changing cus-
tomer mindset metrics precede declining future cash flows,
indicating a need to change the trademark. Put differently,
customer mindset metrics are forward-looking and, thus, a
potential predictor for future cash flows. Therefore, for the
first time, this research addresses the effect of CBBE on the
decision to prolong or terminate trademarks and how trade-
mark characteristics and the level of regulation in a product
category moderate these effects.
In particular, we focus on two key CBBE dimensions (cf.
Stahl et al. 2012; Heitmann et al. 2020): (1) relevant stature
(RS), summarizing the pillars of brand relevance, esteem,
and knowledge, and (2) energized differentiation (ED), cap-
turing a brand’s uniqueness (Datta et al. 2017 ; Mizik and
Jacobson 2008). We further investigate boundary condi-
tions of these effects, i.e., how this effect is moderated by (a)
trademark characteristics, i.e., trademark age and trademark
registration type (brand-association trademarks versus brand
identification trademarks), and (b) level of regulation in a
product category.
Our research utilizes a unique dataset of international
operating firms from 25 countries offering products in 56
product categories between 2001 and 2019, tracking the
prolongation versus the termination of 1,446 trademarks.
All trademarks conduct business in the US market and oper-
ate in the fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) industry.
Moreover, we compiled CBBE data from Young & Rubicam
and the US Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). We used
a hazard model that estimates the probability of trademark
termination. Essentially, we model the likelihood of trade-
mark termination as a function of CBBE dimensions and its
moderators of the observed brands during the observation
period between 2001 and 2019.
In summary, this paper aims to understand the currently
unexamined role of CBBE as well as the moderators of
CBBE in driving a firm’s decision to prolong or terminate
trademarks. In doing so, we contribute twofold to the extant
literature. First, we considerably enrich the literature on
trademarks by showing that CBBE dimensions significantly
affect trademark prolongation. Second, we add to research
on brand equity as we quantify the effects of RS and ED
on a hitherto unrelated dependent variable, i.e., trademark
prolongation.
Conceptualization and hypotheses development
Trademarks and trademark prolongation
Virtually all types and sizes of organizations employ trade-
marks to foster, endorse, promote, and fortify the recogni-
tion and reputation of their brands, thereby securing brand
equity. A trademark serves as a vital tool for identifying and
distinguishing various business entities, acting as a neces-
sary requirement for firms to participate in market activi-
ties such as trademark licensing and franchising (Cao et al.
2022). Specifically, trademarks play a crucial role in com-
merce, enabling manufacturers and retailers to identify and
differentiate their goods from others (Krasnikov et al. 2009)
with the ultimate goal of creating unique brand associations
in the minds of consumers (Melnyk et al. 2014). However,
maintaining a trademark involves high direct (financial) and
indirect (administrative) costs. These costs include applica-
tion and renewal fees for each class of goods and proof of
sufficient use in commerce. A trademark cannot be main-
tained without proper use in commerce for the registered
classes, even if a firm is willing to pay for it (US Patent and
Trademark Office, USPTO). Under the Lanham Act of 1946,
the primary federal trademark law in the USA, a trademark
can only be lost if the company decides to stop paying for
it or if the trademark has not been used in a certain period
of time.
There are two distinct streams of research on trade-
marks. The first stream focuses on the consequences of
investing in trademarks (e.g., Krasnikov et al. 2009; Mizik
and Jacobson 2008 , 2009; Srinivasan et al. 2008). This lit-
erature is often backward-looking and based on past cash
flows. In contrast, the second stream analyzes the effects
of different types of trademark elements on consumer
perceptions (e.g., Henderson and Cote 1998; Henderson
et al. 2004; van der Lans et al. 2016). However, research
directly related to trademark prolongation is scarce (Mel-
nyk et al. 2014). In general, firms should only prolong
a trademark if the discounted future cash flows gener -
ated from the trademark are greater than its renewal costs
(Economides 1988). This trade-off constitutes a complex
decision since firms must initially invest in a trademark
before benefiting from its protection. Put differently, firms
depend on future cash flows resulting from the trademark.
However, the generation of future cash flows is uncertain.
In particular, future cash flows depend on how consumers
perceive the trademark. The consumer perspective con-
verts a registered trademark to a brand with associations
and a specific image. Thus, future cash flows rely on con-
sumer-based brand dimensions. In particular, high brand

awareness and positive brand associations ensure that
consumers engage with a specific brand and consider it in
future buying decisions (Dwivedi et al. 2016). Knowledge
about CBBE reduces the uncertainty of future cash flows
(Datta et al. 2017). Consequently, firms should rely on the
corresponding brand equity when deciding to prolong a
specific trademark.
Conceptual framework
Two consumer-based brand dimensions, i.e., relevant stat-
ure (RS), reflecting relevance, esteem, and brand knowl-
edge, and energized differentiation (ED), serve as the focal
variables of our proposed conceptual framework, affecting
trademark prolongation (see Fig.  1). Previous research has
established these dimensions and proved their relevance
in various domains, e.g., customer management (Stahl
et al. 2012) and product design (Heitmann et al. 2020).
RS measures the relationship between a brand and its con -
sumers. In particular, relevance indicates the likelihood of
brand consideration and measures the individual appro-
priateness of the brand for the consumer (Keller 2001;
Datta et al. 2017). Esteem concentrates on the quality and
leadership of a specific brand. It measures consumer per -
ceptions of a brand being a leader, reliable, and of high
quality. Knowledge assesses the consumers’ overall aware-
ness of a brand and the understanding of the brand iden-
tity. In contrast, ED evaluates the relationship between
a brand and its competitors. Specifically, differentiation
comprises consumer perceptions of a brand’s dynamics,
innovativeness, distinctiveness, and uniqueness. Moreover,
we investigate how the effects of RS and ED depend on
other characteristics of a trademark (i.e., type and age),
and the regulation in a product category. We chose trade-
mark characteristics drawing upon Melnyk et al.’s (2014)
research. However, our research marks the first one to
investigate their moderating influence on the relationship
between CBBE and a firm’s decision to either extend or
terminate a trademark.
Relevant stature
Since RS reflects the awareness of a brand as well as its rel-
evance and reputation (Heitmann et al. 2020), it goes hand
in hand with solid brand associations from the consumer’s
perspective (Stahl et al. 2012). The better known a brand is,
the more extensive and specific the associations linked to
it. Consumers often take advantage of brand associations in
their purchasing decisions by inferring the perceived qual-
ity (Macdonald and Sharp 2000) or popularity of a brand
from its familiarity. Brand awareness is thus used as a simple
decision heuristic (Hoyer and Brown 1990; Macdonald and
Sharp 2000), especially for low-involvement (Huang and
Sarigöllü 2012) and repeat purchase products (Hoyer and
Brown 1990; Macdonald and Sharp 2000). For instance,
Macdonald and Sharp (2000) show that well-known brands
were indeed preferred over unknown brands despite price
and quality differences (Hoyer and Brown 1990). Consumers
purchase well-known brands preferentially and are willing
to pay higher prices for well-known brands due to their high
brand equity (Bello and Holbrook 1995; Buil et al. 2013;
Huang and Sarigöllü 2012). Accordingly, familiar brands
have the edge over less familiar ones, as reflected in higher
profit margins and increased customer loyalty (Huang and
Sarigöllü 2012), ultimately securing future cash flow. As a
result, we postulate that firms will prolong trademarks with
high RS because they will likely maintain a market advan-
tage in the future.
H1 Relevant stature is positively related to trademark
prolongation.
Energized differentiation
ED encompasses the distinctiveness of a brand and, thus,
its ability to stand out from the competition (Datta et al.
2017; Heitmann et al. 2020). In general, brands with a high
CBBE are associated with unique and distinct (as well as
favorable and strong) brand associations (Keller 1993). As a
result, highly differentiated brands can develop a competitive

advantage that is hard to imitate, establish significant entry
barriers, and set industry standards and trends, e.g., through
innovations (Suprihono and Prasetya 2022). These factors
collectively help maintain market share and profitability over
time. As such, one might expect that higher levels of ener -
gized differentiation may lead to higher future cash flows
generated from the trademark, and thus, energized differ -
entiation may positively relate to trademark prolongation.
In contrast, the more differentiated a brand is, the more
it operates in a niche (Dalgic and Leeuw 1994; Toften and
Hammervoll 2009). A corresponding strategy focuses on
unique products optimally tailored to a specific target group
(Toften and Hammervoll 2009), delivering added value for
consumers over competing products. Moreover, differenti-
ated brands provide above-average service, quality, or image
(Dalgic and Leeuw 1994), which in turn is accompanied by
high investments in the development, packaging, distribu-
tion, and advertising of a product (Toften and Hammervoll
2009; Wright 1986). Although products in specialized mar-
kets often have higher profitability due to their distinctive-
ness and the ability to charge a price premium (Kotler 1989),
these markets are limited to a certain number of consumers
(Dalgic and Leeuw 1994 ; Toften and Hammervoll 2013),
leading to low sales despite high margins (Wright 1986).
Demand may even decrease over time, e.g., when trends are
no longer in demand or new competitors start targeting these
markets (Stahl et al. 2012; Toften and Hammervoll 2013).
In sum, the effect of energized differentiation on trade-
mark prolongation seems to be ambiguous.
H2 Energized differentiation influences trademark
prolongation.
Trademark type
Considering trademark type is crucial as a firm is required to
indicate whether a trademark should be registered in its plain
form (brand identification) or adorned with specific features
(brand association) within the legislative Patent and Trade-
mark Office’s registration process (Krasnikov et al. 2009).
Brand identification includes plain trademarks related to
brand names (e.g., Nike), thereby facilitating brand identifi-
cation for consumers, whereas brand-association trademarks
are more complex and can include additional information
or decorative elements like slogans, logos, color, font, and
shape (e.g., Nike Swoosh), thereby fostering brand associa-
tions of consumers (Krishnan 1996; Erjansola et al. 2021).
Keller (1993) argues that brand associations positively
influence consumers’ brand-related attitudes and thus
enhance the probability of brand choice. In addition, a more
complex association network has been shown to increase
consumers’ confidence in their attitudes (Pullig et  al.
2006), reduce susceptibility to attitude change (Pham and
Muthukrishnan 2002), and counteract the effects of com -
petitor persuasion (Pechmann and Ratneshwar 1991 ). As
a result, more brand associations lead to increased brand
loyalty as well as increased consumer value and satisfaction
(Buil et al. 2013; Yang et al. 2019), which in turn manifests
in securing future sales and a positive effect on trademark
prolongation. Furthermore, brand associations are closely
linked to brand awareness, as more complex associations
increase familiarity with a brand. For example, consumers
may connect Coca-Cola not only with soft drinks but also
with various associations, such as color and font, advertise-
ment, or past product experiences. In their study, Pina and
Dias (2021) discovered that sensory elements (such as taste
and smell) and emotional aspects (including feelings and
moods) of brand experiences significantly contribute to
enhancing brand awareness, perceived quality, and brand
loyalty within the context of CBBE. Nostalgic brand asso -
ciations represent another category of emotion-related brand
connection (Almazyad et al. 2023; Cattaneo and Guerini
2012), leading to higher brand attachment (Shimul 2022), as
well as simplified processing and retrieval of brand-related
information, thereby increasing the likelihood of consider -
ing the brand for purchase (Keller 1993). Thus, we expect
brand-association trademarks to moderate the effect of RS
on trademark prolongation positively, such that positive
brand-related associations and brand equity reflected by RS
reinforce each other.
H3a Brand-association trademarks increase the positive
effect of relevant stature on trademark prolongation.
The primary goal of a differentiation strategy is to
develop strong and unique brand associations (Stahl et al.
2012). Strongly differentiated brands are not designed
to appeal to the broad masses but to satisfy the needs of
a specific target group in the best possible way (Dalgic
and Leeuw 1994; Toften and Hammervoll 2009). How -
ever, if the number of associations with a brand increases,
brand diversity also increases with the consequence that
brands gradually lose their distinctive position in the mar -
ket. Consequently, a brand’s sales potential is no longer
limited to consumers of that niche, thereby creating new
opportunities for market and product expansion as well as
for reaching new target groups (Dacin and Smith 1994).
Brand-association trademarks can thus mitigate the nega-
tive effect of energized differentiation on trademark pro -
longation since broader brand associations enable brand
extension and the generation of additional future sales. By
that, a brand-association trademark, compared to a brand
identification trademark, can be used in a more versatile
way, making it more lucrative and worth protecting from
a firm’s point of view.

H3b Brand-association trademarks attenuate the negative

Trademark age
We include trademark age in our framework as age mirrors a
firm’s experience, resilience and investments in the develop-
ment, maintenance, and marketing of a trademark over time
(Melnyk et al. 2014). Put differently, the age of a trademark
directly reflects its experience within the operating indus-
try or category. The combination of competitive advantages
stemming from years of expertise, resilience, and ongoing
investment suggests that older trademarks are more likely to
endure compared to their younger counterparts.
Furthermore, the age of a trademark plays a crucial role
in amplifying the positive effect of RS on its prolongation.
As brand awareness develops over time, older trademarks
benefit from the cumulative impact of their extended pres-
ence in the market. Brand awareness, defined as consum-
ers’ ability to recognize and recall a brand name within a
specific product category (Aaker 1996; Keller 1993), relies
on anchoring brand knowledge deep in consumers’ memory
(Gong et al. 2020). To achieve a sustainable brand awareness
effect, consumers need regular and continuing exposure to
a brand.
H4a Trademark age reinforces the positive effect of relevant
stature on trademark prolongation.
Highly differentiated brands operate in specialized mar -
kets, which carry the risk of limited and possibly declining
demand. However, the more time a brand spends in such a
market, the better it understands and addresses customers’
wants and needs. In this way, brands can expand their cus-
tomer knowledge with time, which Campbell (2003: 376)
defines as “the processes that generate and integrate infor -
mation about specific customers.” By interacting closely
with customers, firms can gain superior knowledge about
customers’ expectations, purchase habits, and expertise to
offer products faster, cheaper, and of higher quality than
their competitors (Gebert et al. 2002). Due to the small size
of niches, customer-firm relationships are particularly inti-
mate, making knowledge exchange more effective. Hence,
the older a brand, the more optimized its offer and the better
it can satisfy customer needs, cushioning the negative effect
of ED on trademark prolongation.
H4b Trademark age attenuates the negative effect of ener -
gized differentiation on trademark prolongation.
Level of regulation
Product categories differ in terms of new product develop-
ment regulations in that food products are subject to tighter
guidelines than non-food products, directly affecting a
firm’s competitive environment (Shah 2015). In the USA,
the development of new products is regulated to varying
degrees. For instance, the vast majority of non-food prod-
ucts, such as cosmetics, are hardly subject to mandatory
regulations, but rely on voluntary guidelines governed by
the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). In contrast, food
products are heavily regulated to ensure their safety for
consumers (FDA). These include mandatory registration
of food manufacturers, processors, and packagers, manda -
tory ingredient and allergen labeling, packaging regulations,
and sanitation standards. Consequently, barriers to entry are
lower in the non-food industry compared to the food indus-
try. Thus, the threat from new competitors and products is
greater in the non-food industry than in the highly regulated
food industry, negatively affecting trademark prolongation.
The level of regulatory oversight substantially impacts
consumers’ perceived value of brands. Specifically, consum-
ers tend to attach greater importance to brands operating
in sectors with high regulatory standards, as these brands
are more likely to be trusted due to regulatory compliance.
However, the concept of brand trust encompasses not only
brand reliability, gauged by the degree to which consumers
hold the belief that the brand delivers on its value proposi-
tion, but also brand intentions, assessed by the degree to
which consumers hold the belief that the brand prioritizes
consumers’ well-being over its interests in instances of
unforeseen product consumption issues (e.g., food contami-
nation) (Delgado-Ballester 2002). This effect is amplified
for well-known brands, as they possess a signaling func -
tion and serve as a proxy for high product quality from a
consumer’s perspective (Erdem and Swait 1998 ). Conse -
quently, well-known brands from highly regulated indus-
tries are associated with stronger choice preferences (Hoyer
and Brown 1990 ) and purchase intentions (e.g., Carneiro
et al. 2005), giving firms an incentive to prolong trademarks
within these industries compared to trademarks from less-
regulated domains. Accordingly, the positive effect of RS on
trademark prolongation should be smaller in less-regulated
industries compared to those in more regulated industries.
H5a A low level of regulation weakens the positive effect of
relevant stature on trademark prolongation.
Differentiated brands often stand out from the competi-
tion through product innovations. Such innovations require
considerable financial resources, organizational expertise,
and capabilities (Reed and DeFillipi 1990) to build a com-
petitive advantage (Ofek and Turut 2008). Specifically,

innovations in low-regulated industries are difficult to imi-

federal regulations. In contrast, it is relatively easy for com-
petitors to imitate successful products from more strictly
regulated industries with more transparency through reverse
engineering, i.e., copying the packaging, shape, and ingre-
dients of existing products (Zander and Kogut 1995). In
summary, differentiated brands in less-regulated markets
face less risk of diminishing demand since their products
are harder to imitate, allowing for sustaining a differentiated
position in the market over time. Consequently, the negative
effect of ED on trademark prolongation should be reduced
for products from low-regulated industries compared to
those from high-regulated industries.

Data and method
Data and measures
This research uses a unique dataset to track the prolonga-
tion (as opposed to termination) of 1,446 trademarks for
19 years from 2001 to 2019 belonging to firms from 25
international countries of origin. This period allows us to
exclude Covid-19-related trademark terminations in our
dataset, as these only occurred from 2020 onwards. All
trademarks conduct business in the US market and oper -
ate in the fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) indus-
try, where companies frequently possess a broad array of
trademarks. The FMCG industry is one of the largest prod-
uct industries subject to constant dynamics. Its global mar -
ket size is expected to grow to approximately $18.9 trillion
by 2031, an increase exceeding $7 trillion compared to
2021 (Allied Market Research 2023). Since consumers
are particularly familiar with FMCG brands, as they are
bought more frequently, they are particularly suitable for
investigating trademark prolongation.
Therefore, we compiled data from Young & Rubi-
cam and the US Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO).
Since companies are not required to have brands legally
protected, not all brands captured by Young & Rubicam
(2,666 brands) could be assigned clearly to the US Pat-
ent and Trademark Office records. Finally, we were able
to identify the corresponding trademark for 81.4% of the
brands (2,170) using USPTO’s Trademark Electronic
Search System (TESS). To ensure comparability among
the investigated brands, we filtered out product brands
(e.g., Coca-Cola Light) and focused on brands as a whole
(e.g., Coca-Cola). Moreover, a termination of a product
brand may not imply a termination of the brand as a whole.
Vice versa, a termination of the brand as a whole also
implies a termination of the product brand. Thus, our final
dataset includes 1,446 aggregated parent trademarks from
56 categories and 25 international countries of origin (i.e.,
Australia, Belgium, Canada, Chile, China, Czech Repub-
lic, Denmark, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Israel,
Italy, Japan, Mexico, Netherlands, New Zealand, Puerto
Rico, Russia, Singapore, South Africa, Sweden, Swit-
zerland, the UK, the USA). In total, 86 trademarks were
terminated during the observation period (see Table  1 for
descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations).

The BAV measures consumer perceptions of a brand based
on the four pillars relevance, esteem, knowledge, and dif-
ferentiation. Initially, we observed high correlations between
relevance, esteem, and knowledge, indicating that these
pillars cannot be treated as distinct constructs (Heitmann
et al. 2020). Therefore, we conducted a principal compo-
nent analysis summarized in Appendix 2 (Datta et al. 2017;
Heitmann et al. 2020), identifying two factors, explaining
86% of the variance. The first component (RS) loads highly
on relevance (0.89), esteem (0.94), and knowledge (0.83).
Contrary, the second component (ED) loads highly on dif -
ferentiation (0.98) and less so on the other pillars. We chose
component labeling in line with Mizik and Jacobson (2009),
Datta et al. (2017), and Heitmann et al. (2020).

CBBE dimensions in Fig. 2. Differently regulated food and

(an Italian candy brand) as undifferentiated, and the brand
is unlikely to be considered relevant for future purchases
compared to others. In contrast, Hershey’s is perceived as
more differentiated and more relevant for consumers. In the
non-food category, we observe that Tena represents a dif-
ferentiated brand, which, however, is unlikely to enter the
consumers’ consideration set. Vice versa, always is less dif-

Trademark type
Similar to Melnyk et al. (2014), we measure trademark type
using mark drawing codes 1–5 1 from the USPTO dataset
(https:// www. uspto. gov). Brand identification trademarks
include all trademarks designated as typed drawings (mark
drawing code 1). According to the Trademark Manual of
Examining Procedure (TMEP, http:// tmep. uspto. gov), these
may only contain Latin letters, Roman or Arabic numer -
als and common punctuations, but no design elements or
stylized lettering (§807.03). In contrast, brand-association
trademarks have the codes 2–5, including “two or three-
dimensional design; colour; and/or words, letters, or num-
bers or the combination thereof in a particular font style or
size” (TMEP, §807.04). We used dummy coding for brand

Hazard model

of specific events, e.g., customer retention (Zhou et al. 2022)
or dissolution of high-tech firms (Srinivasan et al. 2008). This

longation (cf. Melnyk et al. 2014), focusing on CBBE. Essen-
tially, we model the likelihood of trademark termination of
the observed brands during the observation period, i.e., 2001
to 2019. In particular, we consider trademark termination as a
function of RS and ED. Moreover, we follow previous research
(Melnyk et al. 2014) and exemplary integrate variables relating
to a brand’s registration, level of regulation, and experience.

mination, we expect these variables to moderate the impact of
RS and ED on trademark termination. In summary, we specify

Discussion
Trademarks’ positive effects have been investigated exten-
sively, such as providing protection of a brand’s value against
imitation or detrimental activities of competitors (e.g., Block
et al. 2015; Ertekin et al. 2018). However, research on the
antecedents of trademark prolongation is scarce (Melnyk
et al. 2014). Against this shortcoming, we theorize and test
for the first time how CBBE dimensions drive a firm’s deci-
sion to prolong or terminate trademarks. In particular, we
show how the CBBE dimensions RS and ED impact the
longevity of a trademark. Furthermore, we identify several
boundary conditions related to trademark characteristics and
the level of regulation in a product category. Our findings on
the determinants of trademark prolongation are based on a
unique dataset of 1,446 FMCG trademarks operating in the
USA and belonging to international firms originating from
25 different countries, studied over a period of 19 years from
2001 to 2019.
Theoretical implications
First, this research significantly contributes to the literature on
trademarks (Krasnikov et al. 2009; Krasnikov and Jayachandran
2022; Melnyk et al. 2014) by empirically testing for the effects
of CBBE dimensions on trademark prolongation. While litera-
ture often treats brands and trademarks separately, they form a
continuous and mutually beneficial spectrum (Cao et al. 2022).
Consequently, we integrate concepts from brand and intellectual
property literature to derive implications for strategic trademark
management. We find that CBBE dimensions significantly con-
tribute to trademark prolongation. Specifically, we observe that

Managerial implications
Trademarks and brands are essential components of a com-
pany’s intangible assets (Block et al. 2014). Trademarks
protect brands from imitative practices and rival activities,
thus safeguarding the brand’s value as intellectual property
rights (Krasnikov and Jayachandran 2022). The potential
consequences of trademark infringement include revenue
loss, brand reputation damage, and consumer confusion
(CompuMark 2020). Consistently, earlier studies identified
a positive relationship between strategic trademark manage-
ment and firm performance, underscoring the importance of
managers closely monitoring a firm’s trademark strategy and
management to bolster sustainable competitive advantages
(Cao et al. 2022). Hence, brand managers must gain a com-
prehensive grasp of the interplay between trademarks and
brands. This understanding is vital to fine-tune trademark
strategies, optimize brand portfolios, and effectively allocate
resources. Our research significantly contributes to achiev -
ing these objectives.
Generally, our results suggest that companies should invest
in building brand knowledge, esteem, and relevance, as brands
high in RS tend to show an increased likelihood of trade -
mark survival. We attribute this finding to a variety of brand-
specific associations created in consumers’ minds, positively
affecting profits and loyalty (Huang and Sarigöllü 2012).
Accordingly, the need to legally protect brands increases with
its perceived complexity from a consumer’s perspective. In
contrast, greater brand differentiation does not generally con-
tribute to the longevity of the associated trademark, as ED
negatively affects a trademark’s probability of prolongation.
Remarkably, the results reflect the difficulty of sustaining a
differentiation advantage already mentioned by Porter (1985).
The effects of RS and ED on trademark prolongation are
subject to several boundary conditions. Specifically, man -
agers of differentiated brands should think beyond plain
trademarks, foster brand associations such as slogans, logos,
color, font, and shape related to the brand name, and protect
them via a brand-association trademark since these associa-
tions mitigate the negative effect of ED. For this purpose, the
World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) provides
an artificial intelligence-based image search tool (http:// brand
db. wipo. int) to find similar trademarks that have been regis-
tered in the past. This tool allows trademark owners to check
for logo distinctiveness before applying for a trademark.
Similarly, experience helps understand a niche thoroughly
and, in turn, contributes to longevity.
The choice for a differentiated strategy should also depend
on the level of regulation in a product category. Our results
suggest that differentiated brands are more likely to survive
in less-regulated non-food categories. Put differently, a low
level of regulation leaves more degrees of freedom to equip
a differentiation advantage. In contrast, RS is more critical
for trademark prolongation in highly regulated product cat-
egories, especially food products. Essentially, regulations
are introduced to protect consumers from potential risks,
e.g., food poisoning, but consumers strive to protect them-
selves beyond legal regulations and, thus, purchase trusted,
well-known brands with a good reputation (Erdem and Swait
1998, 2004). In summary, the results indicate that managers
should evaluate their strategy and brand equity investments
based on their contribution to longevity.
Limitations and further research
This research is subject to limitations, which provide poten-
tial avenues for future research. First, we investigate products
with relatively short life cycles within the FMCG industry.
Therefore, the results are unlikely to generalize to durable
goods such as mobile devices or cars. Future research might
investigate the effects on trademark prolongation in further
product categories beyond FMCG. Note, however, that the
FMCG industry is a highly relevant market with an expected
market size of approximately $18.9 trillion by 2031 (Allied
Market Research 2023).
Second, the identified boundary conditions related to
trademark characteristics and level of regulation showcase
that the effects of CBBE dimensions on trademark prolon-
gation are subject to internal and governmental conditions.
However, they do not reflect a complete set of possible mod-
erations. For instance, more industry-specific variables could
also play a role, such as the concentration of market shares,
or the national–cultural values of the country of origin of
the brand could alter the effects of RS and ED (cf. Diaman-
topoulos et al. 2020, 2021).

Henrik Sattler is Professor of Marketing and Branding and head of the

nal of Retailing, and Journal of Service Research, among others. He

administration in Germany. He has served on the editorial boards of
