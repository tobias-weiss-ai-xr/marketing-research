# Foreign or Indian beauty brands? Stated preferences and consumer ethnocentrism in an India-focused survey

International Journal of Advanced Academic Studies 2026; 8(10): 35-40
Foreign or Indian beauty brands? Stated preferences and
consumer ethnocentrism in an India-focused survey
Ksankupar Khongstit1, Sonali Narsingh Patta2, Anshika Sharma3
DOI: https://www.doi.org/10.33545/27068919.2026.v8.i10a.2202
A BSTRACT
This descriptive research examines 70 questionnaire records filled in on Indian and foreign beauty
brands. It explains proclaimed category tastes, decision in an identical-price and identical-ingredient sit-
uation, brand attitude, origin of credibility and a hypothetical ₹ 5,000 spending plan. The response data
were calculated into descriptive statistics, paired Wilcoxon tests, and exploratory Spearman correla-
tions. Forty-four respondents (62.9% indicated that they were likely to pick the foreign brand in the
equal condition scenario, and 10 (14.3) and 16 (22.9) respondents inclined towards an Indian brand and
no preferences respectively. The foreign brand choice was also popular (33.3% vs. 30.0%). Forty-nine
respondents (70.0%), chosen an option in favor of foreign brands in all categories listed. The average in
the ₹ 5000 spending case was 3715 on the foreign brands and 1285 on the Indian brands. The one that
said the purchases of foreign brands are un-Indian did not correlate with a significant relationship with
the budget share of foreign brands (Spearman ρ = .11, p = .367). The results are only descriptive of
these respondents. The survey lacks a validated multi-item ethnocentrism scale and the study records
provided are not associated with the location of the respondents, dates of the survey and participants
consent. These findings thus do not give national consumer patterns or causal factors of preference.
Keywords: Stated preference, country of origin, ethnocentrism of consumers, beauty brands, India
Introduction
Domestic production can be appreciated by the consumers and still use foreign brands when they relate these
brands to quality, technology, status or global identity. This tension can be seen in beauty products due to the
fact that product efficacy and safety claims work together with the brand image, packaging, and social mean-
ing. The fact that a specific category prefers foreign brands, as such, does not imply, by itself, that national
identity or promotion of products purchased domestically is gone.
Consumer ethnocentrism refers to the attitudes in regard to the suitability or righteousness of purchasing im-
ports. As a measure of this construct, Shimp et al. (1987) [1] constructed Consumer Ethnocentric Tendencies
Scale (CETSCALE) which was a multi-item scale. Indian studies have argued against the argument that
there should be one standardized meaning of this construct in all contexts. According to Bawa (2004) [2], the
context of ethnocentrism among consumers in India has some nuances, whereas the scale created by Joshi et
al. (2021) [3] has an India-specific scale with protective and patriotic aspects. One response can be character-
ized by a single attitude statement though it can not replace a validated scale.
Prior Indian researches also indicate that desirable reviews on foreign brands can co-exist with preference
towards domestics. The ethnocentric tendencies were reported in their Indian sample by Upadhyay et al.
(2006) [4]. In their research of customers in Lucknow, Kinra (2006) [5] discovered that the respondents were
able to articulate national preference and rated foreign brand names well on such characteristics as technol-
ogy, quality and status. The most recent research by Yadav et al. (2023) [6] found moderate-high ethnocen-
trism in a bigger metropolitan sample. Combining these results implies that brand-origin preferences must be
understood within the context of the measure, sample, and product.
The way ethnocentrism is associated with the reported purchasing behavior can depend on the product cate-
gory (Balabanis et al., 2017) [7]. Global brand messages can also have other connotations other than that of
country of origin. A study of consumer culture positioning and the attitudes towards global and local prod-
ucts across the world explains how one can indicate that a brand is part of a larger consumer culture or not
(Alden et al., 1999; Steenkamp and de Jong, 2010) [8, 9]. These concepts provide a way to contextualize the
current survey, but responses obtained by the survey cannot tell what factor made a particular respondent
reach his or her decision.
In this paper an exploratory report on 70 response records to the questionnaire is reported. It aids in answer-
ing three questions: (1) What does the brand preference of Indians versus foreigners respondents claimed?
(2) What brand perceptions, channels of trust and attitudes were cited in addition to those preferences? (3)

Methods
Study plan and data
This is a cross-sectional, descriptive survey study. The description of
the study that comes along with the questionnaire provides that an on-
line questionnaire was distributed via digital mediums. The dataset of
responses consists of 70 records, with 21 variables. The rows are con-
sidered as one-respondent. There are no blank cells or precise copies
of the rows of responses in the workbook. The analysis is done based
on the values recorded without any imputation.
The records of the study that can be analyzed do not present the sam-
pling frame, the date of recruitment, place where the respondents
were conducted or the criteria used in the selection. This sample is
thus viewed as self-selected, non-probability online sample. The
questionnaire is narrowed down to Indian and foreign beauty brands;
however, nationality or residence is not documented in the workbook;
the questionnaire is prevalent to India orientation as opposed to estab-
lished location of respondent. It does not take any national population
claims.
Measures
The workbook documents age; gender; a preferred category of beauty
product; how satisfied they feel with buying an international brand; a
channel used by respondents, which they thought most strongly im-
pacted their trust; the choice of a same-price, same-ingredient brand;
4 attitude statements; 5 bipolar attributes ratings; 4 purchase-factor
rank fields; and allocations on a hypothetical ₹ 5,000 buying-beauty
scenario. The same-price option is not a documented acquisition, but
a stated option.
The ethnocentrism item concerned the issue of purchasing foreign
cosmetic products as it is un-Indian in the sense that it robs the locals
of jobs. It is evaluated as an attitude of one item, as opposed to being
a CETSCALES or ICE-Scale score. The element on modern or global
vibe, the imported superiority component and declaration about be-
liefs in Ayurvedic versus hi-tech products are also addressed sepa-
rately as opposed to being validated scales.
Endpoints of the five attribute items were between traditional, unreli-
able, basic, questionable or limited perceptions or modern, high-pre-
mium, luxury, highly regulated or diverse shade perceptions. The
questionnaire text requests the respondents to rate Indian and foreign
beauty brands but the one to be rated at a specific mark is not clearly
stated. The scores are hence reported as being recorded attribute rat-
ings and such scores are not interpreted as being a straight-forward
foreign-versus-Indian benefit.
Data analysis
Categorical responses are summarized with frequencies and percent-
ages. Numeric ratings and budget allocations are summed up by
means, standard deviations, medians, and ranges. A two-sided
Wilcoxon test was used to test the difference between the two within-
respondent budget allocations at the level of Wilcoxon test signed-
rank test. The relation between the single item that measured ethno-
centrism and (a) foreign-brand budget share and (b) ordered same-
price choice were investigated with the help of rank correlations be-
tween Spearman and (a) foreign-brand budget share and (b) the or-
dered same-price choice. They are exploratory tests, and there is no
meaning of causation or a population-level description.
The four-factor ranking item tested asked the respondents to rate four
factors out of 4. Data examinations revealed that at least one factor
had data over 4 in 37 of the 70 records, ranks tied and five records
had an entirely unique 1-4 ranking showing. This means that there is
no use of average ranks to determine the most important purchase fac-
tor. The five bipolar attribute ratings also do not include scores of 1 or
2 in their five attribute ratings, which are highlighted in the interpreta-
tion of their mean value.

Discussion
This sample has evident foreign-leaning stated preference evident in
the response file. Two-thirds of the respondents agreed with the fact
that they favored foreign brands in all of the categories listed, and vir-
tually two-thirds fancied a foreign brand in the scenario of equal
price/equal ingredient. The imaginary budgetary points allocation are
heading towards the same direction. These findings are a descriptive
response to the first question of the study: in the setting of the survey
these 70 respondents, there were higher preferences in choosing for-
eign brands than Indian brands.
This trend cannot be termed as reflecting on a declining tendency of
consumer ethnocentrism in India. CETSCALE and ICE-Scale items
are not used in the questionnaire, and the only direct item that exam-
ines just a single belief in the perception whether it is unpatriotic or
damaging to local job to purchase foreign brands. In this sample, most
of them disapproved this statement yet nearly one-quarter approved it
and a fifth were neutral. It was not statistically associated with foreign
allocation, and same-price choice. The results of such are too insuffi

Both the presence of foreign preference, and the mixed national atti-
tudes, are in line with previous Indian research. Kinra (2006) [5]
claimed that foreign brand assessment may be done positively and
ethnocentric. A reason is that at least Upadhyay et al. (2006) [4] and
Yadav et al. (2023) [6] demonstrate why national or domestic prefer-
ence is still an issue in India. This work cannot be directly compared
to these works since the sample size is smaller, the setting of the study
is the context of beauty brands, and a multi-item ethnocentrism mea-
sure is not validated. It reports the pattern of response in this survey
and it does not negate the previous evidence.
Sympathetic and perceived-quality beliefs were also identified: 64.3%
of the respondents said that they feel more modern or global with for-
eign brands and that 65.7% of them thought that imported beauty
products are just generally better. These interpretations are consistent
with the literature of the global brand cues and attitudes towards
global products (Alden et al., 1999; Steenkamp and de Jong, 2010) [8,
9]. Nonetheless, it does not emerge in this survey that either of the be-
liefs had a bearing on preference of foreign brands. One measurement
of the statements and choices was taken and the data does not have
any brand-level experiments and purchase histories.
The results of the trust-channel are towards mixed information envi-
ronment. The most commonly chosen influencer was international in-
fluencers (28.6) then family or friends (28.6) and scientific claims
(22.9) and lastly was retail experience (20). This leads to the sugges-
tion that sources of trust used by researchers based on the reports
made by their respondents are interpersonal or social sources and
product-evidence sources. It leaves it to be implied which channel is
best at modifying purchasing behavior. The differences do matter as
most records did not have a valid completion of the rank-order item
that could have been used to determine purchase priorities.
The brands and context of the product are important as well. Previous
studies indicate that consumer ethnocentrism does not extend to the
same behaviour across the categories (Balabanis et al., 2017) [7].
Similar in the current responses, preferences to foreign brands were
frequent in the listed beauty categories but the survey provided a
small selection of categories and did not capture brand names, prod-
uct use, and actual purchases of various products. The data thus can-
not determine how those respondents selected certain foreign brands
due to reasons of quality, shade line, safety, prestige, prices or any
other reason that cannot be measured.
Practical implications
Indian beauty brands can use the results to substantiate testing con-
cepts with efficacious product testing and trustworthy brand story.
Follow up research could be conducted practically in areas such as
product efficacy, safety documentation, ingredient communication,
price, packaging and shade representation since they have been in-
cluded in the questionnaire or the attributes included in the said ques-
tionnaire. The existing outcomes do not indicate that any one of the
features consistently resulted in choice; brands ought to test out these
propositions by using a product-specific test and observed sales or re-
peat-purchase data.
Favourable stated image in the case of foreign brands does not prom-
ise a long-lasting trust. Respondents listed not only influencers, but
also personal recommendations and a third place (more than a fifth)
found scientific claims as credible sources. Clear substantiation, ac-
countable influences communication, and regular retail experiences
are sensible spheres to consider. These implications are speculative
and have to be taken as speculations to be tested in the market but not
as prescriptions.
Limitations and future research
There are a number of limitations to the study. To begin with, the
sample size of 70 surveyed individuals is too small and small self-se-
lected. No mention of the location of the respondents, nationality, date
of collection, procedure of recruitment or screening criteria is pre-
sented in the records of the studies. The findings will not apply to the
overall Indian consumers. Second, both preference and spending
questions are not observations of purchases, but hypothetical self re-
ports. Third, the scale of ethnocentrism is not a validated scale that
could be used to reach a general statement on the ethnocentrism ten-
dencies. Once language and construct validity has been verified with
respect to its use on a specific population, future work ought to incor-
porate an existing measure, ensuring that the target population is mea-
sured by a validated CET SCALE or a scale unique to India.
Fourth, the wording of the attribute scales fails to distinctly denote the
group of brand that has been rated and the values it records only take
the highest position in the response scale. Fifth, 65 out of 70 rows fail
to reveal a complete unique ranking of 1-4 in the ranking task; infer-
ences were not made on the average purchase priorities. Sixth, four of
the responses are reported by those younger than 18 and the available
study data to be analyzed do not contain any comment on consent,
permission by parents or ethics review. Latest dates of collection,
consent and recruitment processes and under-18 response treatment
must be checked and then formally submitted.
Follow-up study may utilize a bigger, geographically recorded sample
that has quotas of probability based recruitment, tabulates exact prod-
uct and brand backgrounds, and differentiates reported attitudes vs.
new acquisitions. Whether each of the attributes rating is the Indian
brands, foreign brands, or direct one should also be defined clearly in
the questionnaire. A four-factors rank task ought to involve a valid
unique order, or an importance rating scale ought to be utilized in
place of it. Stronger tests of the relationship between ethnocentrism,
quality beliefs, symbolic value, demographics and product category
and choice would be allowed by using multi-item measures and a pre-
specified analysis plan.
Conclusion
The 70 responses that it analyzed showed that foreign beauty brands
were more strongly stated preferred (when equal condition was used)
as compared to the Indian brands in both equal condition choice and
when hypothetically budgetary allocation was used as well.
Participants tended to equate foreign identity and modernity with for-
eign brand and high quality imported products but most of them did
not consider imports as not Indian. However, the single ethnocentrism
measure did not have a significant correlation with both of the speci-
fied foreign-brand spending share and same-price selection. The data
confirms that it is a sample-instead-of-generally-in-any-where income
group taking the more foreign-leaning preferences; it does not con-
firm the more sweeping finding that ethnocentrism is falling through-
out India or that the preferences were because of brand image. To ad-
dress those questions a bigger research with proven measurements,
confirmed location of the respondents and noted buy patterns is
required.
