Abstract
Sustainable New Product Introductions (SNPIs) enable firms to meet consumers’ increasing demand for sustainable options
while addressing rising expectations for corporate environmental responsibility. This study demonstrates that SNPIs can
also strengthen brands by enhancing brand equity. Drawing on signaling theory, we propose that SNPIs serve as product-
based sustainability signals that enhance brand equity by generating social goodwill. A staggered event study of the ini -
tial launch of a sustainable product line by a major laundry detergent brand shows that SNPIs can increase parent-brand
equity. A complementary analysis of subsequent SNPIs across 15 leading brands in 12 retail chains reveals that this effect
is moderated by parent firms' ethical reputation and retail chains' sustainable assortment share. These findings advance
our understanding of whether and under what conditions sustainable products serve as credible signals of environmental
responsibility and enhance brand equity.

both firms’ operational practices and their product offerings.
On the operational front, an increasing number of countries
now mandate corporate sustainability reporting (Australian

tors are placing greater emphasis on climate-resilient growth

customers’ attitudes toward the parent brand (Olsen et al.,

Brand equity, defined as the incremental value that a brand
name confers on a product (Keller, 1993), is a key anteced-

brands (Datta et al., 2017; Vadakkepatt et al., 2021). How-
ever, despite its importance, empirical research linking sus-
tainable innovations to brand equity remains scarce. This
lack of empirical evidence constitutes a significant barrier to
increased R&D investment and, consequently, to the devel-
opment of sustainable innovations (Scott, 2019). Indeed,
Olsen et al. ( 2014) explicitly call for research examining
the effects of SNPIs on brand-level outcomes such as brand
equity, while Vadakkepatt et al. ( 2021) similarly highlight
the need to connect sustainability initiatives to brand equity.
In this study, firstly, we examine the effect of SNPIs
on parent-brand equity, drawing on signaling theory
and the social goodwill perspective as our primary theo -
retical lenses. If SNPIs do indeed influence parent-brand
equity, this begs the question: under what conditions are
these effects amplified or diminished? To provide action -
able insights for managers, we build on the postulates of
signaling theory to identify two key factors—the signal
credibility (parent firm’s ethical reputation) and the signal
distinctiveness (sustainable assortment share) that moder -
ate the relationship between SNPI and parent-brand equity.
Specifically, this research addresses the following research
questions:
(1) How do SNPIs influence the parent brand’ s equity?
(2) Does the parent firm’ s ethical reputation moderate the
effect of SNPI on parent-brand equity?
(3) Does the effect of SNPI on parent-brand equity vary
with the retail chain’ s sustainable assortment share?
To address these research questions, we conduct two stud -
ies that employ the market share attraction modeling frame-
work. In the first study, we leverage the initial launch of
a sustainable product line by a leading laundry detergent
brand in the U.S. to estimate the causal effect of SNPI on
parent-brand equity. Specifically, we employ a group-time
treatment effects doubly robust estimator that exploits the
staggered rollout of the new product line across stores within
retail chains (Callaway & Sant’Anna, 2021; Janakiraman et
al., 2025). In the second study, we assess the generalizabil -
ity of our findings by examining the subsequent SNPIs of
multiple brands and also investigate the moderating roles
of brand and retail chain characteristics. We use NielsenIQ
retail scanner data for both studies.
As the first study to examine the impact of SNPIs on
brand equity, this research advances the literature on prod -
uct innovation and brand equity (Olsen et al., 2014). By pro-
viding robust empirical evidence on the effect of SNPIs on
actual market performance outcomes, the study addresses
a critical barrier to sustainable new product development.
Furthermore, by demonstrating that the effect of SNPIs on
brand equity depends on the credibility and distinctiveness
of the sustainability signals they convey, the study offers
actionable guidance for managers seeking to maximize the
effectiveness of SNPI initiatives. More broadly, the findings
contribute to the growing body of research on the strategic
value of sustainable innovation by showing how and when
sustainability-oriented product innovations can strengthen
a brand’s equity and, ultimately, its competitive advantage.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows:
firstly, we discuss the relevant literature and theoreti -
cal background, and develop our hypotheses. This is fol -
lowed by a detailed description of the data, variables, and
empirical methodology. We conclude with a discussion of
the results and their implications for academic research and
managerial practice.
Background literature
Our study builds on and contributes to three key streams
of research: (1) implications of new product introductions
for brands, (2) drivers of and barriers to sustainable prod -
uct consumption, and (3) brand equity. Table 1 summarizes
the relevant literature and highlights the distinct perspective
offered by our research.
New product introductions and brand performance
The introduction of a new product is a crucial marketing
activity that plays a significant role in driving brand success
(Van Heerde et al., 2010). However, despite its importance,
relatively few studies have examined the effects of new
product introductions on brand performance, and the market
conditions that moderate these effects. Prior studies show
that new product introductions can increase brand market
share (Gielens, 2012), although they may also cannibalize
the sales of existing products (Van Heerde et al., 2010).
Specifically, Gielens ( 2012) find that introductions by
both leader and follower national brands increase their own
market shares, but have heterogeneous effects on category
demand and competitors’ shares. These effects are further
moderated by brand strength and the degree of product new-
ness. Similarly, Pauwels and Srinivasan ( 2004) show that

At the same time, research points to potential downsides
of product innovation. Van Ewijk et al. ( 2022) show that
while new product introductions can improve perceived
brand quality, they may also increase quality uncertainty,
thereby reducing brand choice. This negative effect is stron-
ger among light users and for non-sensory innovations.
However, this literature has largely focused on conven -
tional products, which differ from SNPIs in important ways.
Conventional new products primarily compete in terms of
functional attributes such as price and quality. Conversely,
SNPIs can also serve as signals of a brand’s environmen -
tal responsibility (Lee et al., 2022; Van Doorn et al., 2021).
This signaling mechanism is fundamentally distinct from
the purchase reinforcement and quality learning mecha -
nisms that explain the effects of introducing conventional
new products (Van Ewijk et al., 2022). Consequently, the
effect of SNPI on parent brands, and the factors that moder-
ate these effects, is also likely to differ from that of conven-
tional new product introductions.
Empirical research on the implications of SNPIs for par-
ent brands remains limited. Olsen et al. ( 2014) show that
SNPIs can enhance attitudes towards brands by reinforcing
socially responsible brand associations, drawing on social
identity theory. They further demonstrate that this effect is
moderated by the number of green messages, and the envi -
ronmental credibility, brand longevity, and product category
type. However, their focus is on attitudinal outcomes rather
than marketplace behavior. Building on this limited lit -
erature, our study examines the impact of SNPIs on brand
equity, thereby addressing calls for research on objective,
brand-level outcomes of sustainable innovation (Olsen et
al., 2014; Vadakkepatt et al., 2021).

Brand equity
Our study focuses on brand equity because it indicates the
enduring value that accrues to a brand beyond the perfor -
mance of any individual product and is widely regarded
as a key source of competitive advantage (Datta et al.,
2017; Vadakkepatt et al., 2021). Brand equity is commonly
defined as the differential preference for and response to a
product that arises from its brand identification relative to
the response the same product would receive in the absence
of that identification (Keller, 1993). As a cumulative, port -
folio-level asset, brand equity provides an appropriate lens
for determining whether SNPIs improve the overall perfor -
mance of the parent brand.
Brand equity is typically operationalized using two
broad approaches: consumer-based brand equity (CBBE)
and sales-based brand equity (SBBE). CBBE is a perceptual
construct that captures what consumers think and feel about
a brand, implying that brand equity resides in consumers’
minds (Keller, 1993). From this perspective, brand equity
emerges from consumers’ evaluations of a brand in terms of
multiple dimensions such as relevance, esteem and knowl -
edge (Datta et al., 2017).
On the other hand, SBBE represents the marketplace
manifestation of these underlying consumer perceptions.
Conceptually, SBBE is the component of a brand’s utility
that remains after accounting for the contribution of objec -
tively measured product attributes and marketing-mix vari -
ables. Empirically, it is commonly operationalized as the
brand-specific intercept in choice or market-share attraction
models (Datta et al., 2017).
In marketing research, there is a long tradition of measur-
ing brand equity using such brand intercepts, often referred
to as the “residual” approach (Kamakura & Russell, 1993;
Sriram et al., 2007). Despite their conceptual and opera -
tional differences, Datta et al. (2017) demonstrate a moder-
ately strong positive association between SBBE and several
key dimensions of CBBE, providing empirical evidence of
alignment between sales-based and consumer-based mea -
sures of brand equity. As our aim is to determine objective
market performance, we adopt SBBE as our measure of
brand equity.
Prior research has examined how marketing activities
and macro-environmental factors, such as business cycles,
influence SBBE (Rajavi et al., 2022; Sriram et al., 2007).
Advertising and CSR initiatives have been found to increase
brand equity, whereas price promotions have limited or no
significant impact (Sriram et al., 2007). Importantly, studies
show that product innovation is a key driver of brand equity
(Sriram et al., 2007), and its effect is more pronounced dur-
ing macroeconomic expansions (Rajavi et al., 2022). Our
study also contributes to this literature by investigating the
impact of SNPIs on SBBE.
Theoretical background and hypothesis
development
Our theoretical framework is implemented as follows.
Firstly, we propose that SNPIs can serve as observable prod-
uct-based signals of a brand’s environmental responsibility.
We then explain how perceptions of environmental respon-
sibility can generate social goodwill toward a brand. Finally,
integrating these arguments, we develop our hypothesis by
proposing that this goodwill spills over into broader evalua-
tions of the brand, ultimately improving brand equity.
Sustainable new products as signals of
environmental responsibility
As visible product-market manifestations of a brand’s envi-
ronmental responsibility, SNPIs can be a means of invest -
ing in and building environmental reputation (Van Doorn et
al., 2021). Compared with firm-level environmental perfor-
mance, which is often difficult for stakeholders to observe
and evaluate, product-level attributes are more visible and
diagnostically accessible (Kim & Bharadwaj, 2021).
By embedding sustainability within their core offerings,
firms make their environmental responsibility both observ -
able and verifiable (Lee et al., 2022). Therefore, SNPIs can
convey a stronger sense of authenticity and commitment
than peripheral CSR initiatives, enhancing stakeholders'
perceptions of the firm's legitimacy (Kim & Bharadwaj,
2021). Collectively, these arguments suggest that SNPIs can
function as a form of CSR communication that signals a
firm's environmental responsibility.
Research on brand extensions shows that new offer -
ings can feed back into parent brand evaluations, reinforc -
ing or reshaping brand attitudes and purchase behavior

(Swaminathan et al., 2001). Such reciprocal effects are
explained through theoretical lenses such as associative
network theory, categorization theory, and signaling theory
(Carter & Curry, 2013). However, SNPIs differ from con -
ventional products in that their impact may arise from the
symbolic and reputational meaning embedded in their intro-
duction, even in the absence of product trial or inferences
about tangible quality. Consequently, signaling theory pro -
vides a particularly appropriate framework for understand -
ing how SNPIs shape parent-brand evaluations.
Signaling theory posits that, using signals such as pric -
ing, warranties and co-branding, firms convey to consum -
ers private information about unobservable attributes, such
as values and capabilities, under conditions of information
asymmetry (Carter & Curry, 2013; Connelly et al., 2010;
Dawar & Parker, 1994; Rao et al., 1999). Recent research
extends this logic to the CSR domain, showing that vol -
untary sustainability reporting can signal a firm’s decision
to engage in sustainable business practices (Friske et al.,
2022). Similarly, by embedding sustainability into their
product offerings, SNPIs provide a visible and credible sig-
nal of a firm's environmental responsibility.
Signaling theory also informs our examination of bound-
ary conditions. According to Connelly et al. ( 2010), signal
effectiveness depends on the characteristics of the sender, the
receiver, and the environment in which the signal is trans -
mitted. We focus on two of these dimensions that are espe -
cially relevant in the context of SNPIs. Firstly, we consider
a sender characteristic, the parent firm's ethical reputation,
which may affect the credibility and interpretation of sus -
tainability signals. Secondly, we examine an environmen -
tal characteristic: the retail chain’s sustainable assortment
share, which captures the distinctiveness of sustainability
signals. Our conceptual framework is illustrated in Fig. 1.
Environmental responsibility and social goodwill
Research on CSR, a key component of which is environ -
mental responsibility, suggests that a brand’s socially
responsible actions engender goodwill among key stake -
holders (Chernev & Blair, 2015). Social goodwill refers to
stakeholders’ benevolent attitudes and positive sentiment
toward a brand, reflected in trust, moral approval, and repu-
tation (Chernev & Blair, 2015). Three interrelated mecha -
nisms underlie this effect.
Firstly, CSR signals a brand’s commitment to the well-
being of society, the economy, and the natural environment
(Du et al., 2011). Consumers can be considered as multidi -
mensional “generalized stakeholders” who evaluate brands
not only on consumption-related outcomes but also on their
broader societal impact (Luo & Bhattacharya, 2006). Envi-
ronmental responsibility appeals to this broader evaluative
lens by enabling consumers to derive both functional and
moral satisfaction from their consumption choices, thereby
improving brand evaluations (Luo & Bhattacharya, 2006).
Secondly, from a social identity perspective, CSR initia-
tives strengthen consumer–brand identification by human -
izing brands and imbuing them with moral meaning (Luo &
Bhattacharya, 2006; Olsen et al., 2014).
Thirdly, a brand’s socially responsible identity can
have a positive influence on beliefs about its competence
in delivering functional benefits. Brands perceived as ethi -
cal are often viewed as more benevolent and trustworthy,
which can spill over into perceptions of product quality and

Effect of SNPI on parent brand equity
Integrating the social goodwill and signaling perspectives,
we propose that SNPIs can signal a brand’s environmental
responsibility, thereby generating social goodwill toward
the brand. In turn, this goodwill permeates broader brand
attitudes and ultimately enhances brand equity (Chernev &
Blair, 2015).
From a signaling perspective, SNPIs serve as observable
and verifiable indicators of a brand’s environmental com -
mitment (Lee et al., 2022). The social goodwill generated
by such signals can extend to broader brand perceptions and
evaluations (Bhattacharya et al., 2020; Chernev & Blair,
2015), resulting in increased customer loyalty, stronger
advocacy, and reduced-price sensitivity (Bhattacharya et al.,
2020; Du et al., 2007).
However, prior research also suggests that SNPIs may
produce unintended negative effects. Specifically, lay
beliefs about a sustainability–quality trade-off can lead con-
sumers to infer that sustainable products are of lower quality
or inferior in regard to certain attributes, thereby undermin-
ing their perceived value (Luchs et al., 2010; Newman et
al., 2014; Van Doorn et al., 2021). Consumers’ perceptions
of diminished quality, and their heightened uncertainty, may
spill over to the parent brand, ultimately weakening brand
choice (Van Ewijk et al., 2022).
Despite these concerns, research points to an emerg -
ing positive shift in consumer perceptions of sustainable
product quality (Pickett-Baker & Ozaki, 2008). Moreover,
SNPIs can strengthen consumer trust in a brand’s ability
to deliver functional value, as socially responsible actions
enhance perceptions of firm competence across domains,
including product quality and performance (Du et al., 2007;
Olsen et al., 2014). When sustainability initiatives are
embedded at the product level, they are also more likely to
be perceived as genuine and value-driven rather than oppor-
tunistic, thereby reducing consumer skepticism (Du et al.,
2007, 2011). Further, CSR efforts that meaningfully reduce
a firm’s negative externalities have been shown to improve
sales performance (Nickerson et al., 2022).
Consistent with these arguments, empirical evidence
demonstrates that SNPIs positively influence brand atti -
tudes, evaluations, and behavioral intentions toward the
brand (Olsen et al., 2014). Given that brand attitude is a
central driver of brand equity (Keller, 1993), we propose
that these favorable responses will translate into enhanced
parent-brand equity. Formally, we hypothesize:

Moderating effect of parent firms’ ethical reputation
The effect of SNPIs on brand equity is likely to depend on the
parent firm’s ethical reputation. Research on CSR highlights
the importance of alignment between a firm’s sustainability
initiatives and its broader organizational identity. Consum -
ers are more likely to reward firms with stronger loyalty and
advocacy when CSR activities are perceived as authentic
and consistent with the firm’s core values (Du et al., 2007;
Lee et al., 2022). Conversely, the social goodwill gener -
ated by CSR efforts is attenuated when such initiatives are
viewed as self-serving rather than genuinely value-driven
(Chernev & Blair, 2015). Consistent with this view, Olsen et
al. (2014) argue that when firms lack environmental legiti -
macy, sustainability claims are more likely to be interpreted
as opportunistic or symbolic, increasing perceptions of gre-
enwashing. Therefore, SNPIs would be more effective for
firms with strong ethical reputations, whose sustainability
initiatives are perceived as credible, legitimate and congru -
ent with the firm’s identity.
An alternative perspective suggests that firms with
weaker ethical reputations may, under certain conditions,
benefit more from CSR initiatives. Specifically, Nickerson
et al. (2022) argue that CSR activities undertaken by firms
with less favorable reputations can generate stronger market
responses due to positive disconfirmation of prior expecta -
tions. However, this effect is contingent on the nature of
CSR activity. For initiatives aimed at cultivating social
goodwill, consumers may remain skeptical of firms with
weaker ethical reputations, questioning the sincerity of their
motives (Nickerson et al., 2022).
From a signaling perspective, the effectiveness of a sig -
nal depends on the credibility of the signaler (Connelly et
al., 2010), and source credibility is central to persuasion
outcomes (Olsen et al., 2014). Firms with weaker ethi -
cal reputations are less likely to be perceived as credible
sources of sustainability-related signals, which diminishes
the effectiveness of SNPIs in shaping consumer responses.
Consequently, the positive impact of SNPIs on brand equity
would be attenuated for firms with lower levels of ethical
reputation. Stated formally,

Moderating effects of retailer sustainability
assortment share
A greater sustainable assortment share reflects a more satu-
rated market context in which sustainability attributes are
widely present and thus less distinctive. Prior research on
assortment structure demonstrates that a higher concen -
tration of shared attributes increases perceived similarity
among products while reducing perceived variety and dif -
ferentiation (Chernev, 2012). As a result, the diagnosticity
of sustainability as a decision-making cue diminishes, lead-
ing consumers to rely less on this attribute when forming
preferences. Consistent with this reasoning, greater simi -
larity among alternatives weakens preference strength and
shifts attention toward other attributes during evaluation
(Lamberton & Diehl, 2013). Consequently, sustainability
becomes less effective as a basis for differentiation as its
prevalence within the assortment increases.
In addition, a large number of products featuring similar
sustainability claims increases the effort required for con -
sumers to identify meaningful distinctions among options.
This, in turn, increases the cognitive effort needed to evalu-
ate the assortment (Boyd & Bahn, 2009). Heightened cog-
nitive costs can lead to cognitive overload, manifested in
greater choice uncertainty, increased decision-making dif -
ficulty, suboptimal choices, and even choice avoidance
(Sethuraman et al., 2022). Taken together, dense assort -
ments characterized by homogeneous sustainability claims
limit the ability of SNPIs to capture attention and generate
brand equity gains (Boyd & Bahn, 2009; Sethuraman et al.,
2022).
This reasoning is consistent with signaling theory, which
posits that signals are most effective when they are both
distinctive and informative (Connelly et al., 2010). As sus-
tainable products become more ubiquitous within retail
assortments, their signaling value diminishes. Accordingly,
the positive effect of SNPIs on brand equity is attenuated
in retail environments characterized by a high sustainable
assortment share.
H3 The share of sustainable products in a retail chain’s
assortment negatively moderates the effect of sustain -
able new product introductions on parent-brand equity.
Empirical analysis
We begin with an overview of the datasets used in the stud-
ies. We then present the design and results of Study 1, an
event study examining the first-time launch of a sustainable
product line by a major laundry detergent brand. This study
seeks to establish causal evidence of the effect of SNPI on
parent-brand equity. In Study 2, we extend the analysis to
subsequent SNPIs by six major laundry detergent brands, in
order to evaluate the generalizability of the findings and the
moderating roles of brand and retailer characteristics. Table
2 presents a summary of the two studies conducted in this
research.
Data
The primary data source for this study is the NielsenIQ
retail scanner dataset, 1 which provides detailed weekly,
store-level product (Universal Product Code [UPC]) sales
data for multiple retailers throughout the United States. In
addition to sales, the dataset provides information on key
marketing mix variables, including price (i.e., the average
price paid for the UPC in a given week), feature promotions
(i.e., whether a UPC was included in a promotional feature)
and display promotions (i.e., whether it was part of an in-
store display).

Study 1: Event study of first-time launch of
sustainable new products
In this event study, we examine the first-time launch of a
sustainable product line by a major laundry detergent brand
in 2016. The focal brand accounted for over 32% of the total
annual market share in the laundry detergent category dur -
ing the study period. By focusing on the inaugural launch,
our analysis minimizes potential confounds arising from
pre-existing sustainable offerings in the brand’s portfolio.
We utilize store-level data from 2016–2017 from three lead-
ing U.S. retail chains operating over 2,000 stores in total.
To analyze the effect of SNPI on parent-brand equity
(SBBE), we first have to estimate the SBBE of laundry
detergent brands over time. Following prior literature, we
adopt the residual approach and operationalize brand equity
as the brand-specific intercepts derived from market share
attraction models (Datta et al., 2017; Gielens, 2012; Rajavi
et al., 2022). After obtaining the brand equity estimates, we
assess the causal impact of SNPI on parent-brand equity by
exploiting quasi-experimental variation arising from the
staggered rollout of the new sustainable product line across
stores within three retail chains. Accordingly, our empirical
strategy uses a two-stage modelling approach that is com -
monly used in marketing research (Datta et al., 2017; Giel-
ens, 2012; Rajavi et al., 2022).
First-stage model
In the first stage of our analysis, we use a market share
attraction model to estimate SBBE at the brand-month level
for each store location. The market share attraction model
has been widely employed in prior research to estimate
SBBE due to its methodological advantages (Datta et al.,
2017; Rajavi et al., 2022). Specifically, the model can be lin-
earized and estimated within a regression framework while
adhering to important logical constraints: market shares are
bounded between 0 and 1, and the shares across all brands
in a category sum to one (Cooper & Nakanishi, 1988).
Additionally, this model enables the estimation of SBBE
as a time-varying, brand-specific parameter, allowing us to
capture dynamic changes in brand equity over time (Rajavi
et al., 2022).

require endogenous regressors to follow a normal distri -

Second stage model
To estimate the causal effect of SNPI on parent-brand equity,
we leverage the staggered rollout of the sustainable product
line across different stores of the three retail chains. In our
setting, the sustainable product line was introduced at dif -
ferent stores in different months. This cross-store variation
in treatment timing enables the use of a staggered differ -
ence-in-differences (DiD) design that accommodates time-
varying treatment effects (Callaway & Sant’Anna, 2021).
The model compares SBBE in stores where the sustainable
new product line has already been introduced with SBBE
in stores where it has not yet been introduced within the
same time period. Thus, in any given period, stores that had
not yet adopted the sustainable product line serve as control
stores.
The key identifying assumption underlying our approach
is that the cross-store variation in the timing of rollout of
the sustainable new product line is driven by operational
factors, such as inventory logistics, rather than by strate -
gic decisions based on local market demand for sustainable
products. This assumption is supported by prior research
showing that assortment decisions are typically made at the
corporate (chain-wide) level rather than by individual stores
(Hwang et al., 2009). Additional support comes from our
analysis of the rollout patterns across stores in different mar-
kets, as detailed in Appendix 4. First, the sustainable new
product line was introduced in at least some stores within
almost all markets during the initial launch period. More -
over, within a given market, we observe variations in roll -
out timing across stores. Taken together, these observations
suggest that the rollout was not strategically prioritized in
specific markets based on favorable conditions. Second,
the sustainable product line was introduced in almost all
stores of the focal chains within a few months of the initial
launch, which is inconsistent with a phased rollout based on
performance monitoring or localized market testing. Taken
together, the literature and our empirical analysis of the
rollout pattern support the plausibility of our identification
assumption.
The traditional two-way fixed effects model has well-
documented limitations in settings with staggered treatment
adoption and heterogeneous, time-varying treatment effects.
In particular, it can yield biased estimates when treatment
effects evolve differently over time for early versus late-
treated units (Baker et al., 2022). In our context, consumer
awareness, adoption, and purchasing behavior related to
sustainable new products can potentially develop along dis-
tinct trajectories over time. As a result, the effect of SNPI
on parent-brand equity may vary across treatment cohorts
over time, leading to heterogeneous, time-varying impacts.

Robustness checks
We use several approaches to assess the robustness of our
findings. Firstly, we estimate the overall brand equities

To determine the robustness of our findings, we conducted

SBBE calculated from dollar-based market shares instead of
volume-based market shares in the market share attraction
model. The results of this analysis presented in Appendix 5

we also assessed the effect of SNPI on the overall brand
equity instead of separating conventional parent brands and
their sustainable extensions in the market share attraction

Discussion
This study investigated the impact of SNPI on parent-brand
equity. By conducting an event study on the launch of sus -
tainable product line by a major laundry detergent brand
within three major U.S. retail chains, and analyzing the
effects of subsequent SNPIs from six major brands in 12
large U.S. retail chains, we demonstrated a positive effect of
SNPI on parent-brand equity. Additionally, the study inves-
tigated how contextual factors, specifically, brand and retail
chain characteristics, moderate this relationship. These
insights contribute to the marketing literature and provide
valuable managerial implications for industry stakeholders,
as summarized in Table 10.
Theoretical contribution
Our findings contribute to research on product innovation
and brand performance in several important ways. First,
we demonstrate the distinctive ability of SNPIs to enhance
brand equity. Whereas prior research has largely focused on
conventional product innovations as functional sources of
value creation (e.g., Sriram et al., 2007), SNPIs influence
brand performance through an additional and qualitatively
distinct pathway by signaling symbolic and reputational
meanings that shape evaluations of the parent brand. In
doing so, our study also responds to calls by Olsen et al.
(2014) for research examining the market-based outcomes
of sustainable new products, and by Vadakkepatt et al.
(2021) to more explicitly link sustainability initiatives to
brand equity.
Second, we advance the application of signaling theory
in the CSR domain by showing how sustainability signals
embedded at the product level operate. Prior signaling-CSR
research has largely examined firm-level signals, such as
voluntary sustainability disclosure (Friske et al., 2022). We
extend this work by demonstrating that product-embedded
sustainability, being observable and verifiable in use rather
than asserted, can function as a signal of environmental
responsibility and strengthen brand equity.
Third, we show that the effect of SNPIs on brand equity
is contingent on a firm’s ethical reputation (H2). In devel -
oping H2, we contrasted two competing perspectives: a
credibility-based view, which predicts that sustainabil -
ity signals from firms with weaker ethical reputations are
perceived as less credible (Connelly et al., 2010; Olsen et
al., 2014), and a positive-disconfirmation view, which pre -
dicts that such firms may benefit more from CSR initiatives
because they exceed consumers’ expectations (Nickerson et
al., 2022). Our findings provide support for the credibility-
based view. Moreover, a sub-analysis of brands with ethical
disrepute reveals that the effect of SNPI on brand equity not

only weakens, but reverses. This pattern suggests that sus -
tainability signals from these brands may be interpreted as
opportunistic and inauthentic (Chernev & Blair, 2015; Du
et al., 2007). By documenting this directional reversal, our
findings advance the CSR fit and authenticity literature by
suggesting that the consequences of misaligned sustainabil-
ity signals can be asymmetric: when sustainability claims
are perceived as being non-aligned with a firm’s ethical
reputation, they may generate reputational penalties rather
than merely attenuate positive returns.
Lastly, we find that the positive effect of SNPI weakens
in markets where a larger proportion of available products is
already sustainable. This finding integrates signaling theory
with the assortment differentiation literature. As sustain -
able offerings become more common, perceived similarity
among alternatives increases and sustainability becomes a
less diagnostic basis for consumer choice (Chernev, 2012;
Lamberton & Diehl, 2013). At the same time, the growing
prevalence of sustainability claims may increase the cog -
nitive burden associated with having to evaluate compet -
ing options (Boyd & Bahn, 2009; Sethuraman et al., 2022).
Consequently, the distinctiveness that underpins signal
effectiveness (Connelly et al., 2010) diminishes, attenuating
the brand equity gains associated with SNPI. These findings
contribute to the sustainable innovation literature by dem -
onstrating how market-level sustainability saturation can
constrain the effectiveness of SNPIs.

As consumer demand for sustainability continues to rise,
firms should integrate SNPIs into their core innovation strat-
egy rather than treating them as peripheral efforts. Leading
brands have already begun to embed sustainability into their
innovation strategies. For example, Tide’s launch of Tide
Purclean and Colgate’s introduction of Colgate Keep indi-
cate an industry shift toward aligning product development
with environmental goals (Colgate-Palmolive, 2021; Tide,
2016). Our findings suggest that such initiatives do more
than meet consumer expectations: they also enhance brand
equity, thereby delivering a competitive advantage.
Persistent brand equity gains
Importantly, the benefits of SNPIs are not limited to first-
time launches. Our analysis shows that continued invest -
ment in sustainable product innovation yields persistent
brand equity gains. This supports the case for expanding
sustainable product portfolios as an ongoing strategic pri -
ority. Hence, sustainability should be positioned not as an
ancillary marketing effort to cater to consumer demand for
sustainable choice options, but as a central pillar of brand
development. Procter and Gamble’s Ambition 2030  ini -
tiative, aiming to make 100% of packaging recyclable or
reusable and significantly reduce environmental footprint,
illustrates how firms can integrate sustainability into their
broader innovation agenda (P & G, 2025).
Alignment with firm identity
Firms with strong ethical reputations are better positioned
to capitalize on sustainability-oriented innovations because
they possess greater credibility and trust, enabling SNPIs to
reinforce the existing goodwill and generate stronger brand-
equity gains. However, SNPIs can reduce the brand equity
of firms with poor ethical reputations, as consumers may
interpret such initiatives as opportunistic. These findings
suggest that alignment between sustainability initiatives
and a firm's overall identity is not merely advantageous, but
essential for avoiding adverse brand consequences (Chernev
& Blair, 2015; Du et al., 2007). Firms with weaker ethical
reputations may first need to establish credibility in regard
to environmental responsibility before their sustainable
products can generate positive gains in brand equity.
Role of market context
Finally, we find that the impact of SNPIs is attenuated in
markets with a high share of sustainable products. As the
distinctiveness of the sustainability attribute diminishes,
its signaling value and, in turn, its ability to enhance brand
equity, also weakens, suggesting a potential ceiling effect on
the impact of sustainability signals. In retail environments
where sustainable assortments are already dense, firms may
need to make the sustainability innovation more distinctive,
specific, or verifiable through, for example, sustainability
certifications. In addition, managers may need to comple -
ment sustainable product innovations with additional mech-
anisms that increase visibility and differentiation, such as
in-store displays or promotional support. Alternatively,
prioritizing product introductions in less-saturated market
contexts where consumers may be more receptive to sus -
tainability claims can help maximize the SNPI impact on
performance.
Limitations and future research
Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations
that offer opportunities for future research. First, although
we provide evidence on the effects of SNPIs on brand
equity in multiple ways (e.g., single-brand vs. multi-brand
settings), our analysis is confined to the laundry deter -
gent category. Future research could extend this inquiry to
other product categories to determine whether and how the
effects of SNPIs vary according to different product types
and usage contexts. Second, our empirical setting is lim -
ited to the U.S. market, even as sustainable products con -
tinue to gain traction globally. Extending the analysis to
international settings would enable a more comprehensive
understanding of cross-country differences in terms of the
effectiveness of SNPIs and the role of institutional, cultural,
and market factors.
Third, our study focuses on a period when sustainable
products were at a relatively early stage of market devel -
opment. As consumer attitudes toward sustainability evolve
and concerns about climate change intensify, the impact of
SNPIs on brand equity may also shift over time. In particu-
lar, the effects of SNPIs may have become more pronounced
in recent years as the salience of sustainability issues has
continued to increase. Future research using more recent
data could examine these dynamic effects and provide
insights into their temporal evolution.
Fourth, while our findings document the effects of SNPIs
on brand equity, further research is needed to unpack the
underlying mechanisms driving these relationships and to
identify additional moderating factors that may condition
these effects. In particular, consumer-side characteristics,
such as sustainability orientation, may influence the inter -
pretation of and receptivity to sustainability signals, thereby
warranting closer examination.
Fifth, our focal measure of brand equity (SBBE) cap -
tures a brand’s current market position after accounting

for marketing-mix influences. However, given that brand
equity is inherently a long-term, multifaceted construct,
future research could examine the impact of SNPIs on alter-

Finally, although this study focuses on the implications
of SNPIs for parent-brand performance, future work could
explore broader competitive and ecosystem-level conse -
quences. Investigating how SNPIs influence competitors,
retailers, and other stakeholders would provide a more
holistic understanding of their market-wide implications.
Conclusion
In recent years, there has been a marked shift toward the
consumption of sustainable products, with sustainable offer-
ings representing the fastest-growing segment in numerous
product categories. As sustainability becomes increasingly
mainstream, it is essential to explore the broader implica -
tions of this trend, particularly its impact on the market
performance of brands—a factor that has historically posed
a barrier to investments in the development of sustainable
new products. This study contributes to the growing body
of literature by advancing the understanding of the market
consequences of SNPIs. The finding that sustainable new
products positively influence brand equity offers important
insights for both scholars and practitioners, and may serve
as a catalyst for increased investment and innovation in sus-
tainable product development.
