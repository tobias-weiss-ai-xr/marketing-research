Abstract
The paper investigates the effect of brand inclusivity and brand personality on consumer-based brand equity.
A survey design using cross-sectional approach to data collection was employed. Individual consumer of expensive
luxury items constitutes the sample for the study. Convenience technique was used for selecting respondents who
participated in the study. Closed-ended questionnaire was used for collecting data. SPSS version 22 and SEM PLS were
employed for data analysis which consists of both descriptive and parametric statistics. The study found that brand
inclusivity and brand personality have significant and positive effect on consumer-based brand equity. Similarly, it
was found that personal income and gender moderate the influence of brand personality on brand equity. Again, it
was discovered that gender moderate the effect of brand personality on brand equity; on the other hand, gender did
not moderate the relationship between brand inclusivity and brand equity. It was recommended that organizations
marketing expensive luxury should focus on enhancing their brand personality and brand inclusivity.
Keywords Branding, Brand inclusivity, Brand personality, Brand equity, Luxury brand
Introduction
The importance of branding in business cannot be
overemphasized. Again, branding is the keyword for
the success of any market offering in the form of either
a product or a service [45]. Branding is a multifaceted
process that goes beyond just a logo or slogan and
encompasses the deliberate identification, creation,
and management of a brand’s assets and actions, each
contributing to how the brand is perceived by stake -
holders [17]. This process is foundational in establish -

alike. Crafting a coherent brand message and image,
organizations can create trust, foster loyalty, and drive
customer engagement. Branding, therefore, is not
merely a marketing tool but a strategic priority, often
led by top management to ensure alignment with the
organization’s vision and goals. Srinivasan et  al. [54]

mately, branding creates a unique, memorable identity
that differentiates an organization from its competi -
tors, capturing the minds and emotions of its audience.
Similarly, branding is described as “the promise of the
bundles of attributes that someone buys and provides
satisfaction” [12]. Branding encompasses a brand’s
identity through its name, culture, and vision, while
also capturing its emotional appeal, personality, and
intelligence (The Economic Times, 2021). The brand

Literature review
In this section, the related and relevant literature with
regard to the variables under investigation is reviewed.
Brand inclusivity
Conceptually, in branding, “diversity” and “inclusion” are
often employed alternatively depending on the focus of
discussion [28, 29]. However, it is noted by scholars that
the two terms have different meanings. Furthermore,
in the context of branding, diversity and inclusion have
become synonymous, leading to a loss of clarity around
their distinct meanings [28, 29]. However, for Jacobsen
et  al. [25], the distinction between diversity and inclu -
sion branding warrants attention. Current research
mainly explores diversity brand communication, while
the dynamics of inclusion branding remain understud -
ied, likely due to its relatively recent introduction. So,
inclusivity is less investigated as compared to diversity by
researchers.
As explained by Zanotti [60], luxury branding inclu -
sivity creates spaces that welcome all consumers to feel
important and included regardless of their origins so they
can experience a sense of community with others. There -
fore, the focus of diversity lies in consumer or employee
demographics, which show how different groups exist
within the brand’s target audience or workforce. This
means that the perception of consumers changes through
inclusivity because it builds fairness and cultural sensitiv-
ity, which strengthens emotional bonds and loyalty, but
diversity by itself might only be seen as statistical data
without improving consumer engagement or equity in
luxury settings.
The paradox of inclusivity in luxury branding exists
because luxury brands must maintain their exclusive
nature while meeting consumer demands for open -
ness and social responsibility, which tests the exclusiv -
ity principle [18, 19]. Luxury brands have traditionally
relied on exclusivity and rarity to build their brand image,
yet strategic inclusivity initiatives such as representa -
tion diversity and accessibility expansion can strengthen
brand connection without compromising prestige. This
approach demonstrates that inclusivity and exclusivity
can work together to enhance brand appeal for various
consumer groups, thus advancing theoretical knowledge
about luxury branding dynamics.
In addition, a key question arises: How extensively do
organizations utilize diversity branding and inclusion
branding? These two concepts differ significantly; diver -
sity branding stresses differences, while inclusion brand -
ing centers on fostering a culture where employees feel
valued and included. As Oswick and Noon [63] point out,
diversity and inclusion are codependent, highlighting the
necessity of inclusive practices to truly leverage diversity,

Band personality
Research has established that conceptualizing brands
as personalities enables firms to endow them with emo -
tional attributes, fostering enduring consumer–brand
relationships and ultimately enhancing brand equity
[43]. This approach draws from anthropomorphic theory,
which posits that humans naturally personify abstract
entities to facilitate interaction [55]. Building on this con-
cept, extant literature defines brand personality through
human-like descriptors, as observed by Maehle et al. [44].
Aaker [3, 4] defines brand personality as a collection of
human characteristics attributed to a brand. These traits
develop through various consumer interactions with the
brand, encompassing both direct experiences and indi -
rect influences [50]. This alignment encourages consum -
ers to gravitate toward brands that resonate with their
own personalities, allowing them to see the brand as an
extension of their identity. In this way, brand personal -
ity facilitates self-expression, as individuals incorporate
these branded possessions into their self-concept [13,
14]. Ultimately, the relationship between consumers and
brands is deeply rooted in the compatibility of personal
and brand personalities, enhancing brand loyalty and
identification [41, 52].
The concept of brand personality, encompassing per -
sonality traits associated with brands [41], is pivotal in
relational marketing. It enables marketers to establish
meaningful consumer–brand relationships, which have a
profound effect on individual consumer behavior, as evi -
denced by research [24, 56, 57].

Brand equity
Brand equity is a complex, multidimensional concept
with no universally agreed-upon definition, resulting in
diverse interpretations and methods for measurement
[7]. At its core, brand equity reflects the added value
a brand confers upon a product or service, extending
beyond its functional features [27, 36, 38]. This added
value arises from a combination of brand-related assets
and liabilities that elevate the brand’s appeal, creating an
advantage that resonates beyond mere utility. As Kap -
ferer [31] succinctly puts it, brand equity boosts profit or
demand through the brand’s name. Brand equity differ -
entiates an organization’s offering from those of competi-
tors and also facilitates smooth brand extensions [45].
Customer-based brand equity arises when consumers
possess favorable, strong, and unique brand associations
in memory, fostering brand familiarity [33]. This equity
is positive when consumers react more favorably to a
brand’s marketing mix elements compared to those of
other brands and negative when reactions are less favora-
ble. Aaker [1] and Keller [32] introduced consumer-based
brand equity (CBBE) to marketing literature, providing a
systematic measure of brand performance rooted in con -
sumer perceptions. This concept recognizes that brand
strength lies in consumers’ minds, influencing financial
valuations. Specifically, Aaker [1] and Keller [33] iden -
tified four core brand equity assets: perceived quality,
brand loyalty, brand awareness, and brand associations.
Similarly, the study by Yoo, Donthu, and Lee [59] reveals
a positive correlation between brand equity and key fac -
tors such as brand quality, loyalty, associations, and
awareness. High perceived quality is a significant driver
of consumer preference. Consequently, brand equity
increases as consumers perceive higher brand qual -
ity. Building on this, Kapferer [30] argues that customer
equity lays the groundwork for financial equity, with
brands’ financial value derived from the intangible assets
they establish in stakeholders’ minds.

Equity theory and Aaker’s brand personality framework
in developing brand inclusiveness and personality for brand
equity
The equity theory underscores the significance of fair -
ness and balance in relationships, a concept that directly
impacts branding [5, 6, 47]. This means that, when con -
sumers perceive a brand as fair and inclusive, their posi -
tive engagement increases, enhancing brand equity. For
instance, a particular brand that ensures equitable treat -
ment across diverse consumer demographics by provid -
ing accessible products and services can foster trust and
loyalty, integral components of brand equity.
Aaker’s brand personality framework [3 , 4, 21, 58]
provides companies with a structured approach to cul -
tivating a distinctive brand personality that enhances
brand equity. The framework outlines five key dimen -
sions: sincerity, excitement, competence, sophisti -
cation, and ruggedness, which, when thoughtfully
applied alongside inclusive branding practices, create a
dynamic and relatable brand identity. Leveraging these
dimensions, companies can foster stronger emotional
connections with a broad range of consumers, thereby
reinforcing brand loyalty and resonance across diverse
audiences. For example, a brand that emphasizes sin -
cerity and competence can enhance perceived qual -
ity and reliability, which are crucial for brand equity.
Incorporating inclusivity into these dimensions, such as
promoting honest communication and demonstrating
competence through accessible and diverse offerings,
can strengthen consumer trust and loyalty. This means
that a brand characterized by sophistication can appeal
to diverse consumer segments by showcasing elegance
and prestige while maintaining cultural sensitivity and
inclusivity.
Therefore, brands leverage equity theory to make
their inclusive practices perceived as fair, thereby
enhancing customer satisfaction and loyalty. Simulta -
neously, Aaker’s framework guides the development of
a distinct and inclusive brand personality, which in turn
fosters a strong emotional connection with consumers.
This dual approach not only aids in the development of
variable brand inclusiveness but also solidifies brand
personality, ultimately leading to enhanced brand
equity (Fig. 1 ).

Methodology
In this paper a survey research design using a quan -
titative approach is adopted. The unit of analysis of the
research consists of individual consumers in the Hail dis -
trict in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. A cross-sectional
approach to data collection was used, and thus, the data
required was gathered within the months of May and
June 2024. Data collection was conducted through a
structured questionnaire featuring a 5-point Likert scale.
This 5-point Likert scale enabled respondents to indicate
their degree of agreement with a series of statements,
with responses ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to
5 (“strongly agree”). This approach facilitated nuanced
insights into participants’ perspectives by capturing vary -
ing levels of agreement. The intermediate points were 2
for “disagree, ” 3 for “neutral, ” and 4 for “agree. ” This ordi-
nal scale allowed for the measurement of respondents’
attitudes and perceptions toward the survey items, pro -
viding nuanced insights into their level of agreement.
The constructs used include brand inclusivity, brand
personality, and brand equity, and the items of each of
the constructs are in the questionnaire. In addition, the
instrument was duly validated through construct validity
and reliability in the measurement model.
Furthermore, due to the unavailability of a popula -
tion frame, respondents were selected through conveni -
ence sampling, and data analysis was conducted using
a combination of descriptive and inferential statistics.
Demographic data analysis was performed using ver -
sion 22 of the Statistical Package for Social Sciences
(SPSS), providing robust statistical insights into par -
ticipant characteristics. This SPSS software facilitated
a thorough examination of the demographic variables,
ensuring accuracy and reliability in the data processing.
For the assessment of the interrelationships and the influ-
ence among the hypothesized variables, we employed
structural equation modeling (SEM) in conjunction with
partial least squares (PLS). The SEM/PLS methodologi -
cal approach allowed for a comprehensive analysis of
the complex relationships within the dataset, providing
deeper insights into the dynamics between the variables
under study.

Discussions
The paper aims at assessing the influence of brand inclu -
sivity, brand personality, personal income, and age on
consumer-based brand equity. To this end, the following
hypotheses were formulated and tested as follows:
H1: Brand inclusivity has a significant effect on brand
equity, a finding supported by engaging with the latest lit-
erature. After conducting rigorous statistical tests, it was
determined that brand inclusivity positively influences
consumer-based brand equity, with results showing
(t = 9.352, p < 0.000). Research by Smith and Wilson [53]
and Kumar et  al. [37] supports these findings, indicat -
ing that inclusive branding strategies enhance consumer
perceptions and loyalty. However, the lack of engage -
ment with the broader body of the literature on exclusiv -
ity limits the clarity of these contributions. Interestingly,
our research highlights that even exclusive, high-end
luxury brands in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia benefit
from adopting inclusive practices. This study counters
the conventional notion that exclusivity alone drives
brand equity. Instead, it emphasizes that inclusivity can
enhance consumer perception and loyalty, thus lead -
ing to higher overall brand equity. Further exploration is
needed to fully understand the balance between inclusiv -
ity and exclusivity in diverse markets.
H2: Brand personality has a significant effect on brand
equity, a finding supported by engaging with the exist -
ing literature. The results show that brand personality
significantly influences consumer-based brand equity in
the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) (t = 3.789, p < 0.000).
Studies by Aaker [3, 4] and Alserhan and Baalbaki [10]
corroborate these findings, indicating that strong brand
personality traits positively impact consumer percep -
tions and loyalty. In the context of luxury brands in the
KSA, brand personality enhances consumer-based brand
equity by creating a distinct and relatable brand image.
This suggests that the stronger the perception of brand
personality, the higher the consumer-based brand equity
in KSA. However, the unclear engagement with broader
theoretical frameworks limits the depth of substantive
contributions, highlighting the need for further research
to explore the nuances of brand personality’s impact
across different market segments.
H3 is supported, revealing personal income’s signifi -
cant moderating effect (t  = 1.874, p < 0.05) on the brand
personality–brand equity relationship. Consistent with
Keller [34] and Ahmed [8 ], higher personal income
levels enhance this relationship. Notably, in KSA’s lux -
ury goods sector, brand personality’s impact on brand
equity intensifies as consumer income rises. Despite
these contributions, theoretical framework limitations
hinder deeper analysis.
H4 statistical tests confirm H4, revealing personal
income’s significant moderating effect (t  = 1.874,
p < 0.05) on the brand personality–brand equity rela -
tionship. This finding aligns with research by Keller [34]

Implications
Theoretical implications
The research findings expand existing knowledge about
brand management, particularly for luxury brands
operating in Saudi Arabia. The study confirms previous
research about how brand personality and brand inclu -
sivity create positive effects on consumer-based brand
equity. The research validates these relationships through
empirical evidence in the Saudi luxury market, which
demonstrates that consumer perceptions and loyalty
depend on strong, relatable brand personality traits and
inclusive branding practices.
The research provides a detailed understanding of how
personal income level and gender influence relation -
ships between brand personality and brand inclusivity
and brand equity. The study shows that personal income
strengthens the impact of brand personality and brand
inclusivity on brand equity for consumers who earn more
money. The findings support existing theories about how
socioeconomic status affects brand perception and valua-
tion while extending their scope.
The study offers more information about how gender
affects how people perceive brands. The research shows
gender affects how males and females perceive brand per-
sonality traits, but inclusive branding works equally well
for both genders. The results demonstrate why brands
need to develop gender-specific branding approaches
and validate the importance of demographic variables in
brand equity models.
The research demonstrates the necessity of cultural
and regional factors when using general branding theo -
ries. The study demonstrates that brand personality and
inclusivity work differently based on local cultural stand -
ards, consumer practices, and economic conditions,
which supports research methods that consider specific
branding contexts. The research helps develop consumer
behavior and branding theories by showing how brand
attributes interact with demographic moderators and
cultural factors in Saudi Arabia’s emerging market.
The research establishes a strong methodological
approach through structural equation modeling and
PLS-SEM approaches, which future studies should use to
investigate complex variable interactions and moderat -
ing effects in brand equity research. The study provides
theoretical value by adding empirical evidence to cur -
rent branding theories while demonstrating the need for
cultural sensitivity and demographic analysis in brand
equity development.
Practical implications
This research offers practical insights to luxury B2C mar -
keters and brand managers working in Saudi Arabia’s
market environment. The study provides organizations
with strategic insights to boost brand equity by empha -
sizing brand personality and brand inclusivity while
adapting approaches to personal income and gender
demographics.
A strong, relatable brand personality directly affects
how consumers view brands and leads to positive brand
equity results. Luxury brands need to establish distinc -
tive and appealing brand personality traits that connect
with their target audience. A brand needs to establish
specific characteristics of sophistication, elegance, and
prestige that match their brand identity and match con -
sumer dreams. Organizations should implement consist -
ent communication methods along with visual branding
components and brand narratives that support personal -
ity traits to build consumer emotions that boost loyalty
and perceived value.
The positive effect of brand inclusivity on brand equity
demands organizations to integrate inclusivity within
their branding and marketing approaches. Organizations
should implement three practical measures for brand -
ing success, which include providing diverse products to
match different consumer preferences and using acces -
sible communication channels while promoting diverse
and accepting messages. When advertising includes vari -
ous models alongside product adaptations for different
consumer requirements, it creates stronger perceptions
of inclusivity. Such business practices develop a favora -
ble brand image, which attracts more consumers while
building trust and satisfaction to create lasting loyalty.
Brand equity results from both brand personality and
inclusivity; showing personal income as a moderating
factor, according to the study. Luxury brands need to
implement separate marketing approaches for clients
who earn more money versus those who earn less. High-
income consumers will perceive brands more favorably
when they receive exclusive treatment and premium-
quality products alongside personalized service expe -
riences. Brands targeting lower- personal income yet
affluent consumer segments should focus on aspirational
messages while maintaining their quality and prestige
perception. Brands achieve better branding outcomes
through customized product solutions and promotional
content that aligns with specific personal income group
tastes and beliefs.

equity varies based on gender, so luxury brands must
take gender differences into account during their brand -
ing development process. Brands must create brand per -

Implications for cross-cultural contexts
The study’s findings can be compared with other mar -
kets by exploring how cultural values and social norms
influence consumer perceptions of brand inclusivity and
personality. For instance, in Western markets like Europe
and the USA, there is generally a higher emphasis on
diversity, individualism, and social activism, which may
lead consumers to place greater importance on brands’
inclusivity efforts and personalized branding. Conversely,
in African or other developing regions, collectivist val -
ues and economic factors might influence consumers to
prioritize brands that demonstrate reliability and social
status, potentially moderating the impact of inclusivity
and personality differently. Additionally, examining dif -
ferences in how personal income and gender moderate
these relationships across markets can reveal whether
these dynamics are universal or culturally specific, pro -
viding insights into tailoring branding strategies for
diverse global audiences.
Limitations
The study acknowledges several limitations that should
be considered when interpreting its findings and that
provide avenues for future research:
The research used convenience sampling because it
was impossible to create a complete population frame
for the Hail district of Saudi Arabia. The use of this sam -
pling method provides practical advantages yet restricts
the sample’s ability to represent the wider population of
Saudi Arabian luxury consumers and other market con -
sumers. The study results most likely represent the spe -
cific characteristics of the studied sample instead of the
complete consumer population.
The research study only examines consumers located in
the Hail district, which might not represent all regional
differences across Saudi Arabia. Consumer behaviors and
perceptions show major differences between different
regions because of their unique cultural elements, eco -
nomic conditions, and social characteristics. The results
need careful consideration when attempting to apply
them to Saudi Arabia as a whole or other countries.
The data collection process used a cross-sectional
approach, which measured consumer perceptions during
a single moment in time. The research design prevents
researchers from establishing cause-and-effect relation -
ships and tracking how brand personality interacts with
inclusivity and brand equity throughout time. Research
that spans multiple times would be required to study how
consumer perceptions and brand dynamics change.
The study faces its main challenges because of its sam -
pling approach and geographical boundaries and its use
of a cross-sectional research design, which limits the gen-
eralizability of the findings. The study’s results require
contextual understanding because of these limitations,
which also highlight the necessity for future research
to use diverse longitudinal and qualitative approaches
to study branding dynamics in Saudi Arabia and other
regions.
Directions for future research
Based on the identified limitations of the study, several
practical and strategic recommendations can be pro -
posed to enhance future research and enable more com -
prehensive insights into the branding dynamics within
the B2C luxury market in Saudi Arabia:
Future research needs to apply probability sampling
techniques, including stratified or random sampling
methods, to enhance the generalizability of their find -
ings. The approach will provide an extensive and precise
representation of consumer perceptions throughout dif -
ferent regions and demographics in Saudi Arabia.
Researchers should expand their research to include
different regions of Saudi Arabia beyond the Hail district
to understand how consumer behavior and perceptions
vary between regions. The expanded research scope will
improve understanding of cultural and market variations
that affect branding strategies.
The study should adopt longitudinal methods to moni -
tor changes in consumer perceptions regarding brand
personality and inclusivity and equity throughout time.
The research design will enable researchers to establish
cause-and-effect relationships while studying market
dynamics, which will result in stronger branding strategy
insights.
Future research should employ qualitative methods,
including interviews, focus groups, and case studies, to
study cultural nuances and better understand consumer
motivations. These research methods reveal information

Conclusion
This study illuminates the transformative power of brand
inclusivity and personality in shaping consumer percep -
tions and loyalty within Saudi Arabia’s luxury market.
It reveals that brands committed to embracing diversity
and cultivating distinct personalities can significantly
elevate their equity in the eyes of consumers, fostering
deeper emotional connections that transcend mere trans-
actional exchanges. In a society where cultural nuances
and demographic factors like personal income and gen -
der influence consumption, these findings underscore the
importance of personalized, culturally sensitive brand -
ing strategies that resonate authentically with diverse
audiences.
Moreover, the research highlights a fascinating
dynamic: While brand personality’s impact on brand
equity is modulated by gender and personal income,
inclusivity’s influence remains steadfast across these
demographics. This suggests that inclusivity functions as
a universal language, inviting everyone to feel valued and
represented, regardless of personal background. Brands
that recognize and harness this universal appeal stand to
build bridges of trust and loyalty that withstand cultural
and economic shifts, truly setting the stage for lasting
success in competitive luxury markets.
Ultimately, this study serves as a clarion call for future-
brand strategists: To thrive in a globalized yet profoundly
local landscape, brands must go beyond superficial
notions of exclusivity. Instead, they should craft authen -
tic, inclusive narratives and distinctive personalities that
reflect and respect cultural subtleties. By doing so, luxury
brands can transcend traditional boundaries, unlocking
the full potential of consumer engagement and cement -
ing their legacy as culturally aware, inclusive icons of
prestige and desirability.
