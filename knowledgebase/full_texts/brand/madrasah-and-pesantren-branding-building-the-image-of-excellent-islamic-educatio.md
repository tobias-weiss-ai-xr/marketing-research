 Madrasahs and pesantren (Islamic boarding schools) in Indonesia face
both challenges and opportunities in building and maintaining their
image as excellent educational institutions in an era of global
competition and digitalization. This article aimed to analyze effective
branding strategies for Islamic educational institutions by integrating
Islamic values as core values. Utilizing a systematic literature review
approach, this study examines recent literature on marketing
management and educational branding in Islamic contexts. The
analysis indicates that constructing an institutional image of
excellence requires a paradigm shift from traditional institutions to
professionally managed entities. Key strategies include: (1)
formulating differentiation based on the integration of general science
and religious studies, (2) implementing integrated image
communication through digital branding and public relations, and (3)
maintaining a balance between da'wah (proselytization) orientation
and managerial professionalism. The article concludes that the success
of madrasah and pesantren branding depends on their ability to
consistently articulate an authentic brand identity across all
communication channels. The practical implication of this study is a
conceptual model that institutional managers can adopt to enhance
competitiveness and public image. Future research is recommended to
empirically test the effectiveness of these strategies and explore
stakeholder perceptions in depth.

1. INTRODUCTION
The field of education in Indonesia, specifically the Islamic education sector represented by
madrasahs and pesantren, is undergoing a significant transformation. Historically, these institutions
were often perceived as alternatives or even "second-tier" options, focusing solely on religious
education and offering limited facilities. However, a paradigm shift in public perception is
underway[1], [2]. The public increasingly views modern madrasahs and pesantren not only as
institutions that excel in religious character formation, but also as entities capable of competing

academically and offering specialized programs such as Tahfizh Al-Qur'an (Qur'anic memorization),
integrative sciences, and foreign language mastery[3], [4]. This shift is driven by a growing
awareness of the importance of holistic education alongside the demands of the global and digital
era, which require every institution to clearly demonstrate its identity and competitive advantages.
In this context of intensifying competition, branding has become an indispensable strategic
necessity[5]. Branding extends beyond logos or slogans; it encompasses the process of building
perception, reputation, and brand equity in the minds of stakeholders, including prospective students,
parents, the community, and the government[6]. Nevertheless, a gap remains between institutional
potential and professional image management. Some madrasahs and pesantren still struggle with the
stigma of traditionalism and a lack of systematic reputation management[2]. Meanwhile, institutions
that have initiated branding efforts often face a dilemma between maintaining the values of sincerity
(ikhlāṣ) and da'wah versus the demand to market themselves competitively [7]. Therefore, the
research problem of this article is formulated as follows: How can effective branding strategies be
formulated and implemented by madrasahs and pesantren to build an image as excellent Islamic
educational institutions without sacrificing their core Islamic values? What critical success factors
must be taken into consideration?
The objective of this article is to elucidate a comprehensive model and strategy for
developing brand identity and brand image in Islamic educational institutions, specifically madrasahs
and pesantren. This explanation is based on a synthesis of theories and empirical findings from recent
studies. Theoretical contributions are expected to enrich the literature on Islamic educational
management, particularly in institutional marketing and communication. Practically, this article
serves as a guide for administrators of madrasahs, pesantren, and similar Islamic educational
institutions in designing and executing contextual and effective branding strategies.
In the educational context, branding is defined as a strategic process of creating and
managing a set of unique associations attached to an institution. Key concepts include brand identity
(the intended identity communicated by the organization), brand image (the brand perception formed
in the public mind), and brand equity (the added value possessed by the brand)[8]. For non-profit
organizations such as madrasahs and pesantren, branding plays a crucial role in building legitimacy,
attracting resources (such as students and donors), and maintaining community loyalty [9].
Educational branding does not merely market services; it primarily conveys values, experiences, and
promised futures to learners.[10]
The uniqueness of madrasahs and pesantren lies in the deep integration of general knowledge
(kauniyyah) and religious knowledge (syar'iyyah). Their core values often stem from rich Islamic
traditions, including the cultivation of noble character (akhlaq al-karimah), simplicity, devotion to
spiritual leaders (kiai) or teachers, and an independent spirit nurtured within the boarding
environment[11], [12]. For instance, the Tahfizh Al-Qur'an tradition has evolved into one of the most
prominent and sought-after brand images in modern Islamic education[3], [4]. These values provide
the foundation for an authentic brand identity that is difficult for general educational institutions to
replicate.
To establish a consistent image, institutions must adopt an Integrated Marketing
Communication (IMC) approach. IMC emphasizes aligning all communication messages and channels
ranging from websites, social media, and brochures to staff behavior and direct parental experiences to
convey a coherent identity[13], [14]. In the digital era, this approach extends to digital branding, where
social media and online content play a central role in shaping narratives and engaging the public[15],
[16]. Furthermore, reputation management through active public relations (PR), such as publishing
academic achievements and community service activities, is critical for establishing credibility and
trust[1], [17].

2. METHOD
This article was constructed using a qualitative research method with a systematic literature
review approach. This approach was selected to synthesize findings from various recent empirical
and conceptual studies to develop a comprehensive understanding and conceptual model of branding
in Islamic educational institutions. The primary data sources consist of scientific journal articles,

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1.  Paradigm Shift: From Traditional to Professional
Findings from various studies confirm a paradigm shift in the management of madrasahs and
pesantren. Institutions recognize that religious excellence alone is no longer sufficient to remain
competitive. The public demands institutions that are not only spi ritually sound but also
academically, managerially, and operationally superior[1], [2]. Consequently, branding is viewed as
a strategic imperative to shift public perception from a "second-tier option" to a "first choice". As
highlighted in prior research, professional image management indicates that an Islamic educational
institution is committed to delivering quality services and adapting to contemporary
advancements[5]. However, this paradigm shift must be executed carefully to avoid
commercialization that could erode fundamental values of sincerity (ikhlāṣ) and da'wah[7].

3.2.  Formulating Differentiation and Authentic Core Values
The key to successful madrasah and pesantren branding lies in the capacity to define and
communicate authentic differentiation. This differentiation must be rooted in their distinct strength:
value integration. Prominent differentiation models identified from the literature include: Tahfizh
Plus: Establishing Tahfizh Al-Qur'an programs as the primary brand image, supported by a
competitive general curriculum[3], [4]. Islamic Science and Technology: Offering a science
education framework integrated with Islamic values, backed by adequate laboratory infrastructure.
Entrepreneurial Pesantren: Building a brand image  centered on entrepreneurship by engaging
students (santri) in productive business units. Language-Based Pesantren: Emphasizing mastery of
Arabic and English as an academic advantage for global communication[12].
These core values must be embedded into all operational aspects, including the curriculum,
organizational culture (such as the kiai-santri relationship), and physical facilities, thereby forming
a consistent brand experience for all stakeholders[14], [18].

3.3. Image Communication Strategies: A Multichannel Approach
Once core values are established, the next step is communicating them effectively. Literature
synthesis indicates that successful strategies adopt a multichannel approach:  Digital Branding:
Utilizing social media platforms (Instagram, Facebook, YouTube), informative and interactive
official websites, and creative content creation (profile videos, event documentations, testimonials)
is vital for reaching the public, particularly younger generations and millennial parents[15], [16].
Digital branding enables broad, cost-effective dissemination of positive narratives[19]. Public
Relations and Media Coverage: Building positive relationships with news media to publicize
academic and non-academic achievements, social initiatives, and institutional innovations.
Successful alumni also serve as powerful PR assets for generating t estimonials and network
growth[9], [20]. Visual and Experiential Branding: A professional visual identity (logo, color
scheme, uniforms) alongside clean, safe, and presentable physical facilities contributes to the
perception of quality[13]. Additionally, high service standards and welcoming interactions from all
staff and educators form direct experiences that shape public image.

3.4. Challenges and Balance in Branding
Implementing a branding strategy presents several notable challenges: Human Resource
Constraints: Many pesantren and madrasahs face limited human resources skilled in digital
marketing management and modern communication[7]. Value Dilemma: Balancing the necessity of
marketing the institution with maintaining spiritual sincerity (ikhlāṣ) and preventing excessive
commercialization that could provoke internal conflict[6]. Consistency: Ensuring that externally
communicated branding messages align with actual internal experiences. Discrepancies can diminish
credibility and cause a crisis of trust.

3.5. Conceptual Model for Branding Excellent Islamic Educational Institutions
Based on the discussion above, a conceptual model for branding madrasahs and pesantren is
outlined as follows: Planning Phase: Identify unique core values and differentiation based on science-
religion integration. Conduct stakeholder analysis to assess expectations. Formulation Phase: Design
a brand identity (name, logo, slogan, narrative) reflecting core values. Develop an IMC strategy
combining digital tools, PR, and experiential elements. Implementation Phase: Maintain consistent
communication across all channels. Engage the academic community (teachers, staff, students,
alumni) as brand ambassadors. Leverage achievements and activities to foster positive narratives.
Evaluation Phase: Monitor image and reputation via parental feedback, social media engagement,
and satisfaction surveys. Make periodic strategic adjustments. The overall outcome of madrasah and
pesantren branding is inherently linked to institutional quality. A robust brand is built not solely
through logos, slogans, social media, or promotions, but through the synthesis of strong Islamic
values, authentic excellence, professional management, effective communication, service quality,
and positive stakeholder experiences. In this framework, Islamic values serve as the foundational
identity, while modern management and technology act as instruments to express and advance that
identity. Therefore, ideal branding for madrasahs and pesantren does not imitate other institutions,
but rather professionally presents and communicates their distinct advantages without relinquishing
their Islamic identity and heritage.
The conceptual model for branding excellent Islamic educational institutions (madrasahs and
pesantren) is structured around four sequential phases: planning, formulation, implementation, and
evaluation. In the planning phase, institutions identify unique c ore values—particularly the
integration of general knowledge and Islamic sciences—and conduct stakeholder analyses to map
the expectations of parents, students, teachers, and the surrounding community. This step ensures
that institutional positioning is not only conceptually strong but also relevant to stakeholders’ real
needs, thereby building the brand foundation on authentic values and clear differentiation[21].
In the formulation phase, brand identity elements (name, logo, slogan, narrative) are
designed to reflect these core values, followed by the development of an Integrated Marketing
Communications (IMC) strategy that combines digital channels, public relations, and experiential
components such as flagship programs and student achievements. This strategy emphasizes message
consistency and channel integration so that the institution’s image is formed coherently, while
simultaneously strengthening internal identity as a character-driven and competitive institution.
Thus, branding becomes more than promotional activity; it is a strategic alignment of identity,
communication, and educational service experience[22].
The implementation phase requires consistent communication across all channels and the
active involvement of the academic community—teachers, staff, students, and alumni—as brand
ambassadors who disseminate positive narratives through tangible achievements and activities. Such
involvement transforms branding into a collective movement rather than a marketing department
task, ensuring that every interaction with stakeholders becomes an opportunity to reinforce
institutional reputation. Within this framework, Islamic values serve as the foundational identity,
while modern management and technology function as instruments to express and advance that
identity professionally.
The evaluation phase involves monitoring institutional image and reputation through
parental feedback, social media engagement, and stakeholder satisfaction surveys, which then inform
periodic strategic adjustments. The ultimate outcome of madrasah and pes antren branding is

4. CONCLUSION
This article has addressed the significance, strategies, and challenges of establishing the
brand of madrasahs and pesantren as superior Islamic educational institutions. The primary
conclusion is that branding success relies heavily on an institution's ability to creatively integrate
authentic Islamic values with modern requirements of professionalism and innovation. A paradigm
shift toward systematic and strategic image management is essential. Core strategies involve
formulating differentiation grounded in unique core values, executing integrated communication
across digital and non-digital channels, and involving all stakeholders. Above all, alignment between
the brand promise and the tangible experiences delivered to students and parents is paramount.
Based on the conclusions of this study, several recommendations can be proposed to
strengthen the branding practices of madrasahs and pesantren in responding to the increasingly
competitive and digitally mediated educational environment. Institutional branding should not be
understood merely as a promotional activity, but as an integrated strategic process that reflects the
institution’s identity, values, educational quality, and long-term vision. Therefore, the development
of branding strategies needs to be aligned with the substantive quality of educational services so that
the institutional image communicated to the public corresponds to the actual experience of students,
parents, alumni, teachers, and the wider community.
For institutional administrators, particularly those responsible for managing madrasahs and
pesantren, it is recommended that dedicated teams or communication units be established to manage
institutional branding in a systematic and sustainable manner. The se teams should consist of
personnel who possess competencies in digital communication, content creation, public relations,
social media management, and institutional communication. Where dedicated teams cannot yet be
established, institutions should provide relevant training and professional development for teachers
and administrative staff so that they are able to communicate institutional programs and
achievements effectively through digital platforms. This capacity building is important because the
effectiveness of contemporary branding increasingly depends on the ability of educational
institutions to communicate their identity through credible, informative, engaging, and consistent
digital content.
Madrasahs and pesantren should also develop comprehensive brand guidelines to ensure
consistency in the presentation of their institutional identity. Such guidelines may include the use of
logos, colors, typography, visual materials, institutional narratives, communication styles, and the
representation of Islamic values across different communication channels. Consistency does not
mean that all institutional communication must be rigid or identical; rather, it ensures that various
forms of communication continue to convey a coherent institutional identity. A consistent brand
identity can contribute to strengthening public recognition and distinguishing one institution from
other educational providers operating within the same competitive environment.
At the same time, branding strategies should remain firmly rooted in the distinctive Islamic
identity of madrasahs and pesantren. Institutional administrators should avoid adopting commercial
branding practices without considering their compatibility with Islamic educational principles.
Branding should instead be positioned as a means of communicating the institution’s genuine
educational strengths, Islamic character, moral values, traditions, and commitment to educational
excellence. In this context, successful branding is not necessarily determined by the extent to which

an institution resembles modern commercial organizations, but by its ability to communicate its
unique identity in a professional, authentic, and relevant manner. The integration of Islamic values
with contemporary management and communication practices can therefore become an important
foundation for developing a distinctive and sustainable institutional brand.
Institutions are further encouraged to strengthen their relationships with alumni, parents,
community leaders, educational partners, and other relevant stakeholders. Alumni networks, in
particular, can become strategic assets in strengthening institutional reputation because alumni
experiences can provide authentic evidence of the educational outcomes and values developed by the
institution. Testimonials, alumni achievements, community contributions, and graduate success
stories can be communicated through institutional websites, social media, newsletters, and other
digital platforms. However, such communication should be based on factual experiences and ethical
representation rather than exaggerated promotional claims. Authenticity is essential because public
trust can be weakened when the institutional image presented through branding does not correspond
with actual educational practices.
Another important recommendation concerns the integration of branding with the
improvement of educational quality. Branding cannot compensate for weaknesses in academic
services, teacher competence, student development, infrastructure, governance, or insti tutional
management. Consequently, institutional administrators should ensure that branding initiatives are
accompanied by continuous quality improvement. The programs, achievements, facilities, and values
promoted through institutional communication should be supported by tangible evidence and actual
institutional performance. In this respect, branding should function as a reflection of quality rather
than a substitute for quality. This approach can help institutions build a reputation based on
credibility, consistency, and long-term public trust.
The use of digital media should also be accompanied by clear ethical principles. Madrasahs
and pesantren need to pay attention to accuracy, privacy, intellectual property, responsible content
production, and respectful communication when utilizing social media and other digital platforms.
Content involving students, teachers, parents, and other members of the institutional community
should be published responsibly and with appropriate consideration of consent and privacy. Digital
branding should also avoid excessive sensationalism, misleading information, unrealistic claims, or
content that prioritizes popularity over educational substance. Ethical digital communication is
particularly important for Islamic educational institutions because their public image  is closely
associated with moral credibility and religious values.
In addition, institutional branding should be evaluated periodically to determine whether
communication strategies are effectively reaching the intended audiences. Administrators can utilize
various indicators, such as engagement with digital content, webs ite visits, public responses,
enrollment trends, stakeholder feedback, alumni participation, and community perceptions.
Evaluation should not focus exclusively on quantitative indicators such as the number of followers
or social media interactions. Qualitative indicators, including public trust, perceived credibility,
satisfaction, institutional reputation, and alignment between the projected brand identity and
stakeholders’ actual experiences, should also be considered. Through continuous evaluation,
institutions can identify weaknesses, adjust communication strategies, and ensure that branding
remains relevant to changing societal expectations.
For future researchers, further empirical studies are recommended to examine and evaluate
the proposed branding conceptual model within specific madrasah and pesantren contexts. Because
branding practices may differ according to institutional size, geographical location, educational
orientation, organizational culture, and available resources, empirical research can provide a more
nuanced understanding of how branding strategies operate in different institutional settings.
Quantitative studies could examine relationships between branding dimensions and outcomes such
as institutional reputation, parental trust, student enrollment, satisfaction, and institutional
competitiveness. Meanwhile, qualitative studies could explore how administrators, teachers,
students, parents, alumni, and community members interpret institutional branding and how these
interpretations influence institutional reputation.

Future research should also investigate parents’ perceptions, expectations, and satisfaction
as important outcomes of institutional branding strategies. Parents are among the most significant
stakeholders in educational decision-making, and their perceptions may influence institutional
choice, loyalty, recommendations, and long-term relationships with educational institutions.
Research examining parental decision-making could explore how factors such as Islamic identity,
educational quality, institutional reputation, digital presence, tuition costs, facilities, student
achievement, and alumni outcomes interact in shaping parents’ preferences. Such studies would
provide valuable evidence for institutions seeking to develop branding strategies that are responsive
to the needs and expectations of their primary stakeholders.
Further studies could also investigate the role of students and alumni in the formation of
institutional brand identity. Students are not merely recipients of educational services but also
participants in the construction and dissemination of institutional reputation. Their experiences,
achievements, and interactions with the institution can influence how the public perceives the
madrasah or pesantren. Similarly, alumni can become important brand ambassadors when they
demonstrate positive educational outcomes and maintain meaningful relationships with their former
institutions. Research focusing on these stakeholder groups could therefore contribute to a more
comprehensive understanding of branding as a participatory process rather than a communication
strategy controlled solely by institutional administrators.
Another important area for future research is the relationship between institutional branding,
financial sustainability, and commercialization. Madrasahs and pesantren require adequate resources
to maintain educational quality, develop infrastructure, adopt digital technologies, and improve
human resource competencies. Nevertheless, the pursuit of financial sustainability should not result
in the excessive commercialization of Islamic education or weaken the institution’s religious mission.
Future research could therefore explore alternative business models, fundraising strategies, alumni-
based resource mobilization, social entrepreneurship, partnerships, and other sustainable financing
mechanisms that enable Islamic educational institutions to strengthen their organizational capacity
while preserving their educational and religious identity.
Future researchers are also encouraged to conduct comparative studies between madrasahs,
pesantren, and other educational institutions to identify similarities and differences in branding
strategies. Comparative research could examine how institutional characteristics, organizational
culture, leadership styles, technological adoption, and stakeholder structures influence branding
effectiveness. Such research would provide a broader theoretical basis for understanding whether
branding models developed in general educational institutions can be directly applied to Islamic
educational institutions or whether they require contextual adaptation.
Finally, future studies should consider developing and testing measurable instruments for
assessing Islamic educational institutional branding. A validated instrument could include
dimensions such as Islamic identity, educational quality, institutional rep utation, digital
communication, stakeholder engagement, authenticity, organizational culture, and public trust. The
availability of such an instrument would enable researchers and institutional administrators to
evaluate branding systematically and compare branding performance across institutions and contexts.
In this way, future research can move beyond conceptual discussions toward evidence-based models
that provide practical guidance for the sustainable development of madrasahs and pesantren.
Overall, the recommendations emphasize that institutional branding in madrasahs and
pesantren should be developed as a strategic, ethical, and quality-oriented process. Branding should
strengthen, rather than replace, the substantive quality of Islamic edu cation. By integrating
professional communication, digital innovation, stakeholder engagement, Islamic values, continuous
quality improvement, and responsible management, madrasahs and pesantren can develop
institutional identities that are distinctive, credible, and sustainable. Such an approach can enable
Islamic educational institutions to respond to contemporary competition while maintaining the
religious, moral, and educational foundations that constitute their core identity.
