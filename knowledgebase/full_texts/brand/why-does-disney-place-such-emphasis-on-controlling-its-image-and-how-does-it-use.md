# Why does Disney Place Such Emphasis on Controlling its Image and How does it Use its Image as an Element of its Business Practices?

Abstract: This article examines why The Walt Disney Company places sustai ned emphasis on controlling its corporate and
brand image and how it incorporates that image into its business practices. Drawing on scholarship in brand management, media
industries, cultural studies and transmedia storytelling, together with examples from Disney’s intellectual properties, theme parks,
streaming services and corporate training, the article argues that image control performs three interrelated functions. It supports
IP expansion and consumer loyalty by maintaining recognisable a udience expectations; provides a stable symbolic framework
through which cultural values are encoded and circulated; and operates as a form of corporate governance that protects reputation,
coordinates diversified businesses and reduces commercial risk. The analysis further shows that Disney institutionalises its image
through standardised content production, coherent cross-media n arratives, licensing oversight and employee management.
However, strict image control may also constrain creative auton omy and reinforce American-centred aesthetic and ideological
norms in global markets. Disney’s image is therefore not merely  a promotional asset but a central organising principle of its
cultural production and commercial operations.
Keywords: The Walt Disney Company; Brand Image Control; Intellectual Prop erty; Transmedia Storytelling; Corporate
Governance.

1. Introduction
Since its establishment in 1923, Walt Disney has grown
from an animation studio to one of the most influential media
conglomerates worldwide through acquisitions, technological
innovation and diversification into theme parks, commodities
and digital platforms. Throughout these expansions, Disney
has maintained a unified brand image. As Francoeur (2004,
p.2) noted, the company is regarded as a model for shaping
brand image. The fantasy world built by Disney has brought
commercial success and promoted the dissemination of
cultural values and shaped the corporate management model
(Wasko, 2020). Therefore, analyzing Disney’s image control
should go beyond the traditional scope of brand management
and examine its commercial function and cultural impact.
This control lays the foundation for integrating brand image
into business practice. Against this background, this article
will analyze the importance of Disney’s image control and the
strategy of integrating its brand image into business
operations. The article first evaluates the role of image control
in market efficiency, cultural value communication and
enterprise management. This article will explain how Disney
integrates brand image into business practices through
content production, cross-media narrative and internal
management. Additionally, the article will also explore the
possible limitations of Disney’s strict image control in the
face of global cultural differences.
2. The Significance of Image Control
2.1. Market Expectations
Walt Disney strictly controls its corporate image, mainly
because it is important to m eet market demand, including
relying on intellectual property (IP) to develop and meet
consumer expectations.
The construction of IP is a key factor in Disney’s image
control, because the company  is highly dependent on IP-
based operations, which can bring considerable economic
benefits. Disney’s stable IP image enables it to expand to
various cultural products, such as commodities, theme parks,
and adaptations of novels, video games, and other multimedia
forms. According to Disney’s 2023 report, revenue from
goods, food, and drinks in the theme park and experience
business unit reached $7.712  billion. Wasko (2020, p.78)
noted that the total sales of Frozen authorized goods exceeded
$1 billion. Singh (2023, p.1) believed that the creation of IP
is impossible without stable and effective brand
communication, while Wasko ( 2020, p.87) indicated that
Disney is characterized by its ‘ extreme control’ of creative
assets. These findings show that image control is not an
additional strategy for Disney, but a basic condition for its IP
growth and popularity. The reason why Disney’s animated
characters can become recognized IP images is precisely
because of their consistent i mage control. Audiences can
quickly identify these characters in various forms of media.
Mickey and Donald Duck maintain their innocent and lovely
images, and Disney Princess is still a symbol of beauty and
dreams. Zi Han (2025, p.2) indicated that IP management
requires clear brand image. For Disney, this means protecting
its ‘Joy and Dreams’ brand image and ensuring that each
character and story is consistent with this overall impression,
to create strong and successful  IP and generate commercial
profits.
Disney’s control over its image is closely related to meeting
audience expectations. As a long-established global media
conglomerate, Disney has created a unique brand image with
the transmission of innocence, happiness and dreams as the
core. For audiences familiar with Disney content, the
consistent expression of these themes enables them to identify
the brand image and connect with their existing cognitions,

image helps maintain the expression of these core themes, and
any deviation may undermine the emotional coherence that
audiences rely on. Meanwhile, a stronger emotional

even leading to impulsive or compulsive purchases (Japutra

2.2. Cultural and Ideological Demands
The media is a mixture of economic interests and ideology,
driven by capital, and spreads the values of mainstream
groups (Al-Azab, 2024, p.3). As one of the largest media
conglomerates worldwide, Disney plays the role of cultural
communicator. Therefore, the study of its image control must
go beyond market considerations and incorporate cultural
perspectives. Additionally, the cultural values pursued by
Disney are transmitted through its fixed and stable cultural
image, because the identification, reproduction and
internalisation of cultural meaning depend on consistency.
The construction of Disney’s cultural values depends on its
own stable image. This consistent image contains
recognisable character definitions and thematic expressions,
which serve as carriers of symbolism. As Sullivan (2020,
pp.172-173) pointed out, cultural producers encode meanings
into symbols, and the audience interprets these symbols
according to their cultural b ackground and ideology. For
Disney, the cultural values it wants to convey are encoded in
the stable image and narrative design of its animated
characters. Therefore, these character images are an important
medium for audiences to perceive Disney’s values. If the
image is inconsistent or changes frequently, the symbolic
connection between design and meaning will weaken. For
example, Disney princesses are usually portrayed as beautiful
and brave. Although the narrative of the princess story
evolves with changes in social and historical contexts, its core
reflects shifting gender roles and expectations in American
society (Wasko, 2020, p.145).  Based on the ever-changing
social history, strict control of the character image is crucia l
to ensure the spread of Disney’s expected values. Improper
handling of characters will lead  to deviation from Disney’s
established image, out of audiences’ perception, and weaken
the effect of cultural communi cation. Therefore, Disney’s
strict control of its image allows its expected values to be
more accurately coded and disseminated.
Furthermore, since Disney’s cultural values are integrated
into its image, strict control i s significant because in cross-
cultural communication, audiences usually identify the
content through established symbols. According to Chu’s
(2003, p.78) research, symbols with cultural continuity are
more readily accepted across cu ltures, and such continuity
depends on stable and consistent symbolic images. For
Disney, a concept called Disneyfication, which means ‘a
social order which is controlled by an all-powerful
organization’ (Bryman, 1999, p.27), is defined by a thematic
narrative that relies on a unified image to ensure expressions
across cultural contexts remain coherent and reduce cultural
barriers. American cultural values like individualism, heroism
and self-actualization which Disney wishes to convey are
often rooted in its fairy-tale-like storytelling and
characterization which are centred around Disney’s themes of
childlike wonder and innocence and may be easily understood
by diverse audiences. Therefore, only by consistently
maintaining Disney’s theme of innocent childhood image can
a stable medium be established for the dissemination of its
cultural values, thereby achieving more effective cross-
cultural communication.
2.3. Corporate Management Requirements
Disney’s strict control of its brand image is not only related
to the consistency of aesthetics and narrative, but also a
strategy rooted in corporate brand governance and long-term
profit stability. Disney’s strict control of corporate image is
necessary for maintaining brand consistency. Urde (2013,
p.750) noted from a management perspective that brand-
oriented enterprises must coordinate internal and external
factors around the core of their brand in operations. Keller
(1993, p.16) added that a strong brand depends on consistent,
coherent brand meaning and maintains stability of the brand
knowledge structure through tracking research to ensure
brand meaning is consistent across stages and audiences.
These studies show that maintaining corporate brand
consistency is important to the company’s market
development. As a brand-driven enterprise, Disney’s business
expansion in various fields has always revolved around its
brand significance. Therefore, Disney’s brand image must
remain stable and recognisable in different media and
environments, which can only be achieved by controlling its
self-presentation. Through a stable brand image and
consistent expression of the brand’s essence, Disney can
sustain long-term business growth and its customer base.
Disney’s control of its brand image is a key measure in the
company’s risk management framework. Specifically, a
company’s risk management is usually aimed at protecting its
business reputation, which in turn affects stakeholders,
including customers, investors and suppliers. These impacts
usually affect the company’s performance through changes in
revenue or costs (Gatzert, 2015, p.486). Houqe et al. (2024,
p.1107) further pointed out that companies with a good
reputation can obtain lower capital costs and more favourable
financing conditions to some extent. This logic is especially
important for Disney, which relies on reputation and attaches
great importance to brand awareness. Disney’s long-term and
child-friendly brand image not only constitutes the core of its
market positioning, but also affects the way investors and
partners monitor their risks. Therefore, any deviation from its
characterisation, narrative styl e or brand values can lead to
revenue uncertainty and have a negative impact on the
company’s relationship with stakeholders. Therefore,
Disney’s strict control of its image should be regarded as a

mechanism to maintain its reputation, reduce market risks,
prevent damage to brand value across its diversified media
business, and stabilise long-term stakeholder relationships
that are crucial to sustainable revenue growth.
3. The Usage of The Image in Business
Practice
Disney’s strict control of its brand image runs through all
its business practices, which is an essential factor. Based on
the evolution of Disney’s business development, we can
divide the analysis of its brand identification into three stages:
early content production, medium-term cross-media narrative,
and later enterprise management.
3.1. Content Production
In Disney’s content creation, the company integrates
symbolic images into character shaping, narrative structure,
and visual style through an institutionalised production
process. According to Sullivan (2020, p.173), the media
encodes its thematic images into the content it produces.
Caldwell (2008, p.233) argued that ‘all scripts are also
business plans’ from an industrial perspective, emphasising
that business needs essentially influence the art texts of the
film and television industries. For Disney, its established
symbolic images have set specific standards for content
creation. Disney’s characterisation has always met the
requirements of innocence, kindness and moral integrity. Its
visual style is full of fairy -tale elements, including
exaggerated, expressive char acter facial expressions and a
soft colour palette. Meanwhile, its narrative content always
follows the logic of the growth story and runs through an
optimistic, hopeful theme. In addition, the Disney+ streaming
platform’s entrance adopts brand classification, and its visual
design aligns with Disney’s consistent childish aesthetics. It
is worth noting that Wasko (2020, p.44) mentioned that
Disney has enhanced its brand influence and expanded its
multiverse after acquiring Pixar, Marvel and Lucasfilm.
Kumar and Upadhyay (2024, pp.121-122) further added that
although Disney gave these companies creative autonomy, it
successfully integrated Star Wars IP into its business portfolio
through effective management. For example, Star Wars
incorporated Disney-style themes such as ‘growth stories and
family ties’ during the Disney period, while Marvel’s
promotional images adopted brighter tones. This shows that
Disney’s institutionalised control of brand image extends to
the management level of its acquisition content. In a word,
these characteristics show that Disney has always integrated
its brand concept of ‘family, positive and fantastic’ into the
content production standards.
3.2. Cross-media Narrative
Disney’s business operation takes brand image as the
structural principle of narrat ive expansion, realising cross-
platform narrative extension and creating economic benefits.
Disney’s commercialisation has surpassed its main medium
of animated films. Based on its long-established brand image,
Disney extends characters, stories and themes to games, stage
musicals, theme parks and products. Caldwell (2008, p.233)
noted that entertainment works can be presented visually,
auditorily, interactively or experientially. Jenkins (2006,
p.113) holds a similar view, believing that a successful cross-
media system depends on the coherence of each work, so that
it is seen as part of a unified whole. Audiences build a
narrative world by collecting information from different
media, but this process depends on unified thematic
expression (Johnson, 2013, pp.30-31). These theories show
the basis of Disney’s cross-media narrative: the entertainment
content itself can be expressed in many forms, and each
medium must show recognisable consistency, reflecting the
inevitable result of brand recognition becoming a narrative
rule.
Disney has strengthened the extension of its brand image
through this ‘symbolic consistency’ and institutionalised its
unified identity. This ensures the narratives in various fields,
such as movies, games, theme parks, and products, are
consistent in visual style and revolve around themes such as
optimism and childlike. For example, in Disney theme parks

3.3. Corporate Management
When analyzing Disney’s business operations, the
company’s management must be considered in its control of
overall business behavior. Through institutional management,
Disney integrates the brand image into the organizational
structure and operational processes, making it an important
corporate principle. Caldwell (2008, p.233) noted that in the
later stages of film and television content development,
narrative content is closely linked to the company’s economic
goals, showing that brand image is crucial as a standardized
governance standard. Disney’s brand management is
achieved mainly through franchising. Johnson (2013, p.38)
emphasized that franchising depends on media companies’
control over brand image and ideology. This control can
ensure a more lasting partnership, so a consistent brand image
is essential for business cooperation and expansion.
This is reflected in Disney’s expansion, including the
establishment of monitoring teams and audit departments to
manage the brand image. Any new movie, TV series,
authorized goods or theme park projects must go through
these processes to ensure that they are consistent with
Disney’s long-standing family, optimistic and magical brand
image. Keller (1993, pp.7-8) noted that enterprise
management relies on brand consistency to establish a stable,
lasting relationship with market demand. Disney practices
this principle through employee management. As an
animation studio, it unifies the creative style through
standardized training to cater to the market’s commercial
entertainment orientation. Besides, Disney institutionalizes
brand behavior through Disney University training, strict
dress code, probation monitoring, hidden supervision, and
education on company history and values (such as creating
happiness and guarding magic) for theme park performers
(Wasko, 2020, pp.95-101). These measures show that
Disney’s brand image has been integrated into its

4. The Drawbacks of Strict Image
Control
Disney’s strict management of its brand image has laid a
crucial foundation for its great commercial success. However,
it may also prevent the company’s commercial development.
Firstly, strict image management restricts artistic creativity.
As Caldwell’s (2008, pp.198-200) industrial author theory
suggests, media corporations frequently take a commercially
driven approach to creative in tervention. This diminishes
directors’ creative autonomy, forcing compromises toward
‘low-risk, high-return’ outcomes. Consequently, creativity
drifts from its artistic nature, leading to superficiality. Disney,
relying on its brand image for commercial expansion, is
forced to compromise artistic creativity to some extent for
commercial gain, thereby affecting the quality of creative
content.
Furthermore, given Disney’s global reach and the influence
of American cultural hegemony, the aesthetic and value
systems embedded in its fixed image become internationally
internalized standards. Di Giovanni (2003, p.207) notes that
‘Disney films are therefore rightly considered an important
part of popular culture worldwide, especially in the United
States, a country whose cultural standards and beliefs have
crossed national boundaries and penetrated local lifestyles as
a consequence of the increasing hegemony of American
cinematic products. As observed by Wasko (2020, p.142), the
shaping of female characters in  Disney movies reflects the
aesthetic standards of mainstream American culture, in which
the heroine is always beautiful and touching, while the villain
is described as ugly, extremely fat or thin. This dominant
aesthetic has long been rooted in Disney’s character design,
forming a stable visual symbol. For decades, this aesthetic
perspective has profoundly influenced the aesthetic standards
of global audiences. Disney’s fixed visual image lays the
foundation for the dissemination of its values, but when
discussing the cultural content of other countries, it
essentially presents those countries’ values through American
narratives. For example, the movie Coco celebrates the
Mexican Day of the Dead, but its core remains American-
style heroism and individualism, just packaged as a universal
theme.
5. Conclusion
This article analyzes the importance of Disney’s strict
image control for business needs, cultural value
communication and corporate governance. Strict image
management is important to build intellectual property and
meet audiences’ expectations. In cultural communication,
strict image control enables Disney to integrate its values into
the narrative and help audiences understand its meaning. This
control can also protect brand value and reduce business risks.
This article also discusses how Disney integrates these control
images into practice through content creation, cross-media
narrative and organizational governance. It institutionalizes
the creation of the company’s products through strict image
control, extends the narrative across multiple media platforms,
and integrates the corporate image into operational practice
and decision-making. Besides, this article notes that strict
image management may limit creative freedom and
strengthen the value system centered on the United States. In
the future, I will explore the a dvantages of image control for
Disney compared with other media conglomerates such as
Netflix.
