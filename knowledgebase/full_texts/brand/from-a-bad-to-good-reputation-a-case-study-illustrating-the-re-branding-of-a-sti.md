Abstract
Neighbourhoods with low social capital and bad reputations are becoming even more deprived, with increased homeless-
ness, poverty and gang violence. In Sweden, and internationally, there is a need for knowledge of ways to stimulate positive
regeneration of neighbourhoods with a bad reputation. This ethnographical case study explores regeneration factors in a
neighbourhood that was transformed over 14 years from one with low social capital and a bad reputation to one with higher
social capital and a good reputation. Three key factors in this positive regeneration and de-stigmatization process of a neigh-
bourhood with an undesirable reputation were identified: the engagement of private sector actors with place branding and
spatial anchorage skills; collaborative co-creation; and home-love with emotion-based building. The findings regarding this
successful case highlight the importance of collaboration and including actors with re-branding skills when rewriting the
narrative of a stigmatized neighbourhood.
Keywords Re-branding · Spatial stigma · Private sector · Co-creation · Place attachment
Introduction
Both research and political discourse about what the Swed-
ish police define as disadvantaged neighbourhoods [Särskilt
utsatta områden] have painted a picture of increasing segre-
gation, as neighbourhoods with low social capital and bad
reputations are becoming even more deprived, with increas-
ing homelessness, poverty and gang violence (Listerborn
et al. 2020). Hence, in Sweden and internationally there is
a need for knowledge of ways to break this negative spiral.
This paper explores a neighbourhood in northern Sweden
where the negative spiral of deprivation was reversed, with
a reputational change over 14 years from bad to good. The
paper builds on results from a repeated cross-sectional sur -
vey in 46 neighbourhoods in Umeå municipality, northern
Sweden, where social capital was measured at two points in
time, 2006 and 2020, to assess their development over time
(Eriksson et al. 2021). While most of the neighbourhoods
retained the same social capital rank over time, there was a
prominent example of a positive shift, in the neighbourhood
referred to here as ‘North Valley’. This neighbourhood was
transformed in the 14 years between 2006 and 2020 from
one with very low social capital levels (measured as percep-
tions of neighbours helping, caring and talking to each other,
with expectations of being involved in issues of concern for
the area) to one with moderately high social capital.
No similar positive development was seen in other neigh-
bourhoods that had low levels of social capital in 2006. This
raised the following question (addressed here): What are the
reasons for the positive development in North Valley?
Aim
The aim of this paper is to explore processes that occurred in
North Valley during the period 2006–2020 that could have
promoted its regeneration and de-stigmatization, particularly
the role of re-branding. The research was guided by the fol-
lowing questions:
• What was the chronology of crucial historical events in
the regeneration and de-stigmatization process in North
Valley?
• Which key stakeholders contributed to the positive regen-
eration in North Valley, and how did they contribute?

The empirical analysis highlights three potentially impor -

important mechanisms in urban planning and regeneration

neighbourhood with a bad reputation, such as North Valley.
Previous research
Regeneration and community development studies
Various studies on regeneration and community develop-
ment have provided valuable foundations for this exploration
of regeneration processes in North Valley. A neighbourhood
can be defined as a spatial area in which a collectivity of
people live, work, send their children to school, drive on
the same roads, buy groceries, and participate in associa-
tions during part of their leisure time (Matarrita-Cascante
and Brennan 2012). According to Matarrita-Cascante and
Brennan (2012), community development is a process that
provides vision, planning, direction and coordinated action
allowing people to establish ways to create the community
they want to live in. Regeneration is a form of community
development that often takes place in a post-industrial place
(Hayter 2000). A regeneration and community development
process involves various stakeholders, including government
agencies and municipalities, private sector and non-profit
voluntary actors, and residents (Taylor 2007). It also involves
physical transformations (e.g. of green areas, streets, and/or
recreational and leisure facilities) that have important aes-
thetic and symbolic functions in building a positive place
identity (Matarrita-Cascante et al. 2010). Besides these com-
ponents, a neighbourhood also needs social cohesion with a
mutual feeling of ‘belonging’ to a place, with networks, trust
and mutual obligations (Putnam 2000). Some residential
places acquire ‘spatial stigma’ (Wacquant 2008), as people
develop negative views of them (which may or may not be
true), often based on preconceptions that they are hazard-
ous, socio-economically weak and/or have populations with
a different ethnic background. Wacquant (2008) believes that
this does not necessarily align with the way things really
are, rather, if individuals believe it to be true, they will
judge the residential area based on their perceptions. Back -
vall (2019) suggests that “practices of stigma often employ
similar methods by creating an image based on rumors and
word-of-mouth reputation, stigmatization only produces
negative representations of a place”. Negative media cover-
age can also stigmatize a residential area and lower its status,
thereby reducing the self-esteem of its residents (Boverket
2010). Therefore, many real estate organizations (public or
privately owned) have sought to change the narrative of a
stigmatized residential district by refurbishing the physical
environment in efforts to boost the sense of community and
identity, and help people take pride in living there (Bover -
ket 2010). The next section summarizes previous research
and concepts that are helpful for the exploration of the key
stakeholders and factors (private sector actors, collaboration
and home-love) that contributed to the positive regeneration
in North Valley.
Neoliberal urbanism and increased power
of the private sector
Regeneration is a highly variable process, with elements
and outcomes that strongly depend on characteristics of the
principal stakeholder leading the community development,
the beneficiaries of the development, and the relative lev -
els of people- and developer-centred approaches (Botes and
Van Rensburg 2000). During the 1990s, neoliberal urban-
ism entered Swedish urban planning (Pries 2020; Lister -
born et al. 2020), accompanied by privatization of the public
realm and associated weakening of the public sector and the
possibility for municipalities to control the urban planning
process (Punter 2007). The shifting housing politics opened
possibilities for private actors, such as property investors and
developers, to participate in the process of urban planning.
However, neoliberal urbanism has been criticized for leading
to developer-centred urban planning, with property investors
and developers mainly focussing on financial profit (Cane-
las and Raco 2021) at the expense of the residents living in
the neighbourhoods. Another potentially adverse outcome
is a laissez-faire planning regime that lowers the quality of
the public realm comprising the streets, open spaces, public
places and facilities or areas where people come together for
social and economic purposes (Punter 2007). Pries (2020)
argues that the private sector hinders the regeneration pro-
cess (Punter 2007) and thus contributes to the negative
housing spiral leading to the development of disadvantaged
neighbourhoods, such as North Valley.
Cooperative governance and co‑creation
Noring (2023) argues that researchers should place less
focus on criticizing municipalities for eating out of the hands
of private developers and argues that the municipalities’
collaboration with private developers is a way to survive
and adjust their urban planning and building out of the cur -
rent political conditions. As she points out, redevelopment

projects would not be possible at all without what she calls
cooperative governance between public sector actors and
non-public sector actors. In the complex process of urban
planning, cooperative governance involves a third-sector
organization (TSO), i.e. a hybrid organization with public,
private and non-profit actors (Pestoff 2014; Camatti and
Wallington 2022). In addition, Zenker and Erfgen ( 2014)
identify three stages of cooperative governance or ‘co-cre-
ation’: definition of a shared vision for the place, including
core place elements, in Stage 1; implementation of a struc-
ture for participation in Stage 2; and provision of support for
residents’ own place branding projects in Stage 3.
Place branding and place attachment
This paper focusses on illuminating the role of place brand-
ing in a regeneration process, as well as the contribution of
different stakeholders in building ‘new positive’ narratives
about a place brand image (Syssner 2010). Co-creation, with
engagement of diverse stakeholders, has recognized impor-
tance in place branding, in order to maximize the resonance
of the resulting place brand with the community the brand
represents (Camatti and Wallington 2022). It also promotes
local stakeholders’ identification with the brand, and their
action as ‘ambassadors’ of the place (Braun et al. 2013 ).
Place branding theory was originally developed as a mar -
keting theory, focussing on traditional advertising agencies’
promotion of place with brochures and short meaningful slo-
gans in efforts to attract investment companies, tourists and
residents (Anholt 2008; Syssner 2010). However, Hospers
(2010) argues that this ‘cold’ place marketing is not effective
for drawing in residents or re-branding a place. Instead, to
successfully re-brand a place, there needs to be some place
attachment and a “sense of place” (Niedomysl 2007), a bond
to the city or neighbourhood in question. Thus, important
factors include “the love of place” or “topophilia” (Tuan
1974), with people talking warmly about their hometown
(Relph 1976), and “location-specific capital”, i.e. some
assets being more valuable in their specific location than
anywhere else (DaVanzo 1981). Entrepreneurs and compa-
nies can also be home-loving with high location-specific
capital businesses relying on local networks and clients
(Hospers 2010). Accordingly, Hankinson (2004) advocates
a place branding model that focusses on behaviour and real-
ity rather than trying to re-brand through communicating
a fictive all-too-dreamy image of what a place wants to be.
A branding technique that Syssner (2010 ) named “spatial
anchorage” has provided a useful tool for analysing the re-
branding process in the regeneration of North Valley. This
refers to changing the narrative of a place through place
branding activities that focus on a selection of “spatial char-
acteristics” (landmarks, buildings, locations and milieus),
or nodes, that are expected to help present a positive place
image (inwards and outwards), rather than the entire region
or neighbourhood.
Method
The empirical research involved interviews (with 24 inter -
viewees), collection of documents issued by the public sec-
tor actor (the municipality) and ethnographic observations
during 2021. This is a sub-study of a larger project that has
been ethically approved (Dnr: 2019-04395, Dnr: 2020-00160
and Dnr: 2020-02757). The 24 interviewees persons were
representatives of the municipality (with strategic and opera-
tional roles), political sphere, police authority, educational
system, leisure associations and private companies who had
knowledge of the 14-year North Valley regeneration process.
Some interviews were conducted while walking through the
neighbourhood (5) or talking in relation to a map of the
neighbourhood (9). The others were conducted as regular
interviews in office spaces, over the phone or via a secure
digital platform (7). The documents included in the study
concerned the urban planning of the city and were sub -
jected to discourse analysis (Wetherell and Potter 1992). The
analysis presented here focussed on the following three key
themes: (1) the role of the private sector in urban planning
and building; (2) collaboration and co-creation; (3) home-
love and emotion-based building.
Results
The case and the results of the analysis are presented in this
section in the form of a chronological summary of the focal
neighbourhood and regeneration process.
Leaning on a long history
North Valley is a suburban neighbourhood in the immediate
outskirts of Umeå. At the time of writing, it has about 4000
inhabitants in an area of 1.4 square kilometres. North Valley
bears similarities with single-industry towns (Hayter 2000).
However, unlike these rural places, North Valley is a place
with a proud ancient history, as the city itself began here, in
around 1600. The harbour was originally established here,
close to an old church dating back to 1500, with a vicarage
and row of beautiful old houses along the riverbank. From
1600 until the early twentieth century, North Valley was an
important, wealthy place with large farmhouses and a vivid
centre. When all the farmers in the region decided to start a
union in 1928, North Valley was the natural place for gath-
ering all the wealthy and influential persons for debate and
making decisions. Such historical knowledge is essential for
discerning the resources used in the rhetorical re-branding

process that helped the de-stigmatizing regeneration process
of the neighbourhood.
The mental hospital, 1930–1980
During the late 1930s, the city grew and moved in an east-
erly direction away from North Valley. North Valley was
described by the interviewees as an “empty place outside of
everything, consisting of nothing but woods” at that time.
Once central, the place image became peripheral, making it
an attractive site for a mental hospital that the government
planned to build in northern Sweden, in line with a political
decision to institutionalize patients with mental illness, in
hospitals spread out in minor rural areas of Sweden. The
mental hospital in North Valley was designed by the famous
architect Carl Westman in the1930s. Westman also designed
the largest and most well-known Swedish mental hospital
(Beckomberga, with 1600 patients) and the courthouse in
the Swedish capital Stockholm. North Valley was the Beck-
omberga of northern Sweden, with 1000 patients. Twenty-
two buildings in the North Valley complex were designed
by Westman (workshops, rooms for the patients, treatment
rooms, a pavilion for distressed women and autopsies, and
houses for the physicians working there). According to
the interviewees, the premises were fenced to secure pub-
lic safety. One of the interviewees said that “North Valley
was a society in itself with all facilities and closed to the
outer world”. During the late 1980s, political changes led
to a reform of mental health care and closure of institutions
such as North Valley. The planning and establishment of the
mental hospital in what was then seen as an ‘empty place’
bear similarities to processes described in with studies of
single-industry town (Hayter 2000). The mental hospital in
North Valley can be seen as analogous to a large industrial
enterprise, in a place “out in the sticks” that boomed and
flourished for a short time because of governmental poli-
cies, kept a large number of people in occupations, and met
societal needs. Nevertheless, due to changes in the political
winds, the mental hospital in North Valley vanished almost
as quickly as it arose. The magnitude of the mental hospital’s
impact on the place is illustrated by the fact that its building
led to a change in the name of the place.
Company A takes over, 1987–2020
The mental hospital officially closed in 1986, and a year
later a building company (hereafter ‘Company A’), bought
its premises, including all the buildings and land, from the
regional health authority. The owner of Company A saw
potential in the area and had a vision for the place. What was
seen as the beginning of the end turned out to be the begin-
ning of the regeneration and re-branding process of North
Valley. Since the late 1980s the neighbourhood has grown
from a socially isolated and stigmatized place, and is now
being merged with the city.
The image of North Valley before regeneration
Before presenting findings regarding the key factors that
contributed to North Valley’s positive regeneration, this sec-
tion reports the interviewees’ descriptions of North Valley
before and after the regeneration, and outlines crucial histor-
ical events in the regeneration and de-stigmatizing process.
The interviewees all painted a picture of a stigmatized place
that changed during the early 2000s from a neighbourhood
with a bad reputation to one with a good reputation, and
described North Valley as a place that used to be far away
from the city. As one of the professionals said, “back then
North Valley was far outside everything, out in the sticks,
and it took long time to get there, but now it’s closer to the
city and everything else. You can just jump on a bus and
you´re there”. The interviewees implied that North Valley
had become geographically closer to town and quicker to
get to, although the geographical distance between North
Valley and the city remained the same during the period
2006–2020. In fact, it took longer to travel by bus in 2020
than in 2006, due to increases in traffic and bus stops, as the
city had grown.
North Valley was also described as a dangerous place
during some periods. Interviewees of middle age remem -
bered being warned as children not to play in North Valley
and forbidden to do so by their parents. One of the profes-
sional interviewees remembered “…my parents said—don’t
you go near that place because that’s where people walk
around in white shirts, meaning persons with mental illness
who were insane”. North Valley’s stigmatized place iden -
tity was thus closely connected with residents of the mental
hospital, or to be more precise, to persons suffering from
mental illness, or labelled as mentally ill. During the 1990s,
North Valley continued to be described as a dangerous place,
even after closure of the mental hospital. During this period
the danger was related to criminality, according to experi-
ences of the interviewees, as North Valley was rumoured to
be a place where criminals lived and there were high rates
of crimes such as deadly knifings or shootings, and drug-
related offences. These rumours of criminality are confirmed
by statistics, as North Valley had higher crime rates than the
two other neighbourhoods often labelled as disadvantaged
by the municipality (Norberg et al. 2003). The buildings of
the old mental hospital designed by Westman were described
as being ugly and scary during this period, just like its for -
mer residents. For example, a guided ghost tour through the
buildings and area of the former mental hospital was organ-
ized by a civic society as an event and ‘thing to do’ when
visiting the city. Besides being described as a dangerous
place, North Valley was also a place that people mocked,

The image of North Valley after regeneration
The identity and status of the place shifted several times
during its history. As described, it was an important place in
the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, and remained
wealthy and prosperous, but increasingly a rural backwater
until the 1930s, when it became a stigmatized area due to its
association with mentally ill people. During the 1980s and
early 2000s, the idea of North Valley as a dangerous place
lived on, but based on a fear of criminality rather than men-
tal illness. However, at the time of data collection (around
2020), North Valley was described as “a place like any
other”. One interviewee said that “my feeling is that North
Valley is a common normal family-friendly neighbourhood”.
When asked about the criminality, interviewees were con-
fused and wondered “why I ask about crime in North Valley,
since this can only be described as a safe place”. The crimes
that occurred in this neighbourhood were described as things
that happened in the past, long ago. North Valley today is
no longer a place that people mock; instead, the rhetoric is
filled with pride. The old buildings designed by the archi-
tect Westman are now highlighted as the treasures of North
Valley and described as beautiful, cultural and historical
elements of architectural heritage, which make the place
unique. Thus, the very buildings that once stigmatized the
place have somehow become re-branded as valuable, which
raises questions about how the neighbourhood of North Val-
ley was so successfully re-branded, and by whom.
Key factors and stakeholders in the positive
regeneration of North Valley
The following key stakeholders were identified: private
sector company A, the municipality (public sector) and a
handful of other private sector entrepreneurs who all initi-
ated or participated in the process of drawing up visions
and making important decisions. Non-profit organizations
were also identified as important stakeholders in the process,
but with positions as followers of the visionaries. The main
power in drawing up a vision for North Valley was held by
Company A.
The following three key factors explain the positive
regeneration and de-stigmatization process identified in the
analysis.
1. The engagement of private sector actors with place
branding and spatial anchorage skills
2. Collaborative co-creation
3. Home-love with emotion-based building
The engagement of private sector actors with place
branding and spatial anchorage skills
When Company A took over the land and buildings of the
mental hospital, they saw both the potential and a need to
re-brand the stigmatized neighbourhood, because a place
with low attractiveness is bad for profits. Together all stake-
holders built a new brand for North Valley through various
place branding activities, including three spatial anchorages
(Syssner 2010), with aims to up-value the place identity and
rumours surrounding North Valley, and attract both middle-
class individuals and families with small children. The fol-
lowing sections describe these three main spatial anchor -
ages: a gym and spa facility, a restaurant for fine dining and
an art park.
The gym and spa facility
Company A contacted a sport and gym company with a
prominent position in the city, encouraging them to invest
and build sport facilities in one of the buildings of the old
mental hospital in North Valley. At this time, North Valley
was not only a highly stigmatized community but a place
that no one travelled to, so building a spa and gym facility
here would theoretically be seen as ‘bad business’. Inter -
viewees representing the sport and gym company described
the building project as follows:
Well, in retrospect, this investment was on our part a
bold move, to invest in a place on the outskirts. But
Company A approached us with such enthusiasm
and we truly believed in their vision and realized that
something big is going on here, and that we would like
to jump on the train and be part of this great develop-
ment of North Valley.
One of the buildings of the old mental hospital was re-built
as a luxurious spa and gym facility. During the renovation,
the vision was to maintain the character of Westman’s archi-
tecture. However, the planning and building of the gym and
spa in North Valley was criticized by representatives of civic
society, who argued that a gym and spa facility, in what then
was considered to be out in the sticks, was unjustified since
there was no clear group of gym users in the area. They also
argued that it would be too expensive for students, who were

the main target group of the gym company. Representatives
from the gym and spa facility countered that the North Val-
ley project would increase their brand’s value and enhance
the sport company’s reputation as a luxurious company.
A restaurant for fine dining
Company A also identified the need for a fine dining restau-
rant in North Valley, and reached out to a successful chef,
who owned and worked in one of the best kitchens in Swe-
den, in a ski resort. The chef was well known for excellent
cuisine, and people travelled to the ski resort just for the
food experience. When interviewing the chef, it turned out
that the offer had been turned down several times with the
following explanation:
(Laughter) The offer was like a joke, why would I,
with a successful restaurant in a beautiful high-status
place, give up everything I built up for a ‘career’ in a
deprived neighbourhood. You know, North Valley was
like a slum before we moved there. To move to North
Valley would, at that time, definitely seem like a step
in the wrong direction for my career.
The chef finally gave in because, “eventually they made me
an offer I couldn´t refuse”. Representatives for Company A
said that they really wanted to recruit this particular chef
because they knew that “this person had a local attachment
and made a name for themselves in this part of Sweden,
therefore they were the right person to carry the banner…
They were exactly what was needed to create place attrac -
tivity”. Although there are restaurants that provide fine din-
ing in the city, the restaurant in North Valley soon became
known locally in the city as a place that people travelled to
for fine dining experiences.
The Art Park
The leaders of Company A had a genuine interest in art.
Thus, art came to play an important role in the re-branding
and washing away of North Valley’s bad reputation. Instead
of ‘only’ having a private art collection in a gallery, Com-
pany A put most of the art outside, in the form of statues in
an Art Park. When it was first established, in 1994, 21 works
were exhibited in the Art Park, which soon became an ‘in-
thing’ that was frequently visited by locals, and eventually a
national and international tourist attraction. The art is placed
all around the area of North Valley owned by Company A
and sites of the two other spatial anchorages. Many of the
featured artists used the history of the mental hospital as
inspiration for their art. For example, bathtubs were used as
illustrative symbols of one of the mistreatments of women
diagnosed with hysteria. Similarly, a clothesline with the
white shirts that all mental patients had to wear as part of
their institutionalization was used symbolically. The white
shirts, which were historically oppressive symbols of the
place’s stigmatization, were given new meaning through the
art. Instead of being used to make fun of the place and label
it as stigmatized they were now used as re-branding tools to
up-value it and illustrate an awareness of, and pride in, the
history of the place through art. Art is in itself a symbol of
a ‘cultivated place’, but art and sculptures are often spread
out all over a city and its neighbourhoods. A concentration
of art in outside environments, as in North Valley’s Art Park,
is rare and the rare examples are generally placed in city
centres, rather than in stigmatized neighbourhoods ‘in the
sticks’. Thus, the Art Park can be considered a rather bold
and expensive choice for placing attractive features. Moreo-
ver, it illustrates that art and culture can play important roles
in strengthening the place branding of a neighbourhood, and
strengthen a city’s branding.
Through place branding attractions such as the mentioned
spatial anchorages (the spa, fine dining restaurant and Art
Park), people from the city and other neighbourhoods (with
‘better’ reputations), began to visit North Valley, as ‘talk
spread’ about what a positive and interesting place it had
become. It became an attractive place for tourism and sight-
seeing on special occasions such as birthdays or wedding
anniversaries. Through these three spatial anchorages, key
stakeholders changed the place identity of North Valley from
a dangerous to a re-branded interesting, cultivated, spiritu-
ally uplifting place—the opposite of dangerous. Tourists
from the entire region also began travelling to North Valley,
because of these spatial anchorages and re-branding initia-
tives. All residents living in the neighbourhood mentioned
the spatial anchorages as attractive places that made them
feel proud of ‘their neighbourhood’. Although the residents
rarely visited any of these places themselves because they
were too expensive, they were proud that local authorities
had chosen their neighbourhood to build what are considered
‘fine’ high-status attractions.
Housing and infrastructure as place branding
necessities
North Valley needed investments in traditional characteris-
tics, such as single-family houses to attract residents with
higher socioeconomic status. This led to both a mixed pop-
ulation and gentrification, as the presence of higher-status
residents improved the neighbourhood’s brand. Company
A and the municipality initiated building of the houses,
and infrastructure that made daily necessities and facilities
accessible within 15 min. These two main stakeholders
also built family-friendly attractions, for example, ‘a sled-
ding hill’, as part of the Art Park’s interactive art installa-
tions, and supported the local sports association, enabling
non-profit associations to build a new sports facility. The

enriching the social life of families and creating a lively
neighbourhood. The key stakeholders also organized a
local group called ‘The Future of North Valley’ in which
representatives from churches, schools, youth clubs and
the police were invited to engage in efforts to make North
Valley a ‘good place to live’. In the interviews, representa-
tives of Company A repeatedly stated that “We not only
build houses like other companies in the business, we
build whole neighbourhoods and cities”. A key factor in
the successful re-branding and regeneration process seems
to have been that Company A, representing the private
building sector, saw themselves as urban planners and
builders who wanted to take responsibility for building a
thriving neighbourhood, with social cohesion, where the
residents feel a sense of belonging to the place.
In sum, although all the mentioned stakeholders played
important roles, Company A acted as a catalyst in the posi-
tive regeneration and re-branding process. The company
identified North Valley as a good investment opportunity
and saw its potential. Company A also inspired other
stakeholders to follow their vison of what a neighbour -
hood needs, and provided the financial muscle required
to build spectacular spatial anchors. Moreover, it had a
single leader and flexible organization, enabling an ‘ad
hoc’ approach to urban planning, with sensitivity to what
seems to work in specific localities, follow new trends and
implement more rapid planning and building processes
than those led by a democratic organization such as the
municipality.
Collaborative co‑creation
One of the key success factors was the willingness of all
stakeholders, including the two main stakeholders (Company
A and the municipality), to collaborate in the co-creation of
a vision for North Valley’s regeneration. Personal relation-
ships played important roles in understanding the grounds
of their collaboration and shared vision, since the leaders
of Company A and municipal council were friends. One
of the interviewees representing the municipality’s leaders
explained that these were give-and-take relationships. All
key stakeholders knew each other, as another interviewee
said: “Everyone knew everyone, the city was like a duck
pond back in those days”. The co-creation process with
active engagement of the key stakeholders strongly pro-
moted the positive regeneration as it facilitated the central
actors’ collaborative efforts to achieve the same main goals.
In addition, the fact that everybody believed in the vision
for a renewed North Valley fostered a rumour that positive
changes were about to happen.
Home‑love with emotion‑based building
As is often the case, the people who participated in the
community development work of the non-profit organiza-
tions involved in North Valley’s re-branding were moti-
vated by pride in and love for the place. In a prominent
example, an enthusiastic member of the local sport asso-
ciation for youths made the association so popular, with
financial support from Company A, that the sport arenas
became the ‘places to be’. Similarly, the schools and prop-
erty company of the municipality (both the organizations
and enthusiastic individuals within them) reportedly made
important contributions to efforts to create safe schools
and housing. However, a distinguishing aspect of the
North Valley case is that Company A expresses great love
and compassion for the city. Swedish real estate companies
of its size often spread out their investments in different
cities, mainly the capital, since this is more profitable.
Unlike other private builders, however, Company A chose
to specialize in this specific city and invest money in build-
ing an entire neighbourhood, not just cost-effective houses.
A representative of Company A justified this alternative
strategy with home-love (Tuan 1974), saying:
The city is our hometown, this is where we’re from,
this is where we live, and this is where we want to
make a difference. We built the city out of love for
the city. So, what´s good for the city is also good for
Company A.
The love and compassion for North Valley is even more
locally specific, since key members of Company A have
personal relationships with this particular neighbourhood.
Discussion
Noring ( 2023 ) acknowledges that private sector agents
may hinder socially sustainable regeneration processes,
in line with Listerborn et al. (2020) and Punter (2007).
However, she takes a pragmatic approach and argues that
co-governance can provide a solution, through actors in
the private, public and non-profit sectors collectively plan-
ning societies in a co-creative symbiosis. This raises the
question of whether stakeholders in the North Valley case
worked in such a manner. The regeneration process of
North Valley partially fulfilled criteria for the three stages
of co-governance proposed by Zenker and Erfgen (2014).
In accordance with criteria for Stage 1, private and public
sector actors had a shared vision for the place, includ -
ing core place elements, although the non-profit sector
was excluded in this stage. Key stakeholders established

230 M. Sjöberg
a network of relations and actors who were invited to
participate in the process, and thus fulfilled criteria for
Stage 2. The North Valley regeneration process also partly
fulfilled criteria for Stage 3, since financial support for
civic societies was provided and both a social network
and structure of local-specific actions within the non-profit
sector were established. However, the structure was mainly
top-down, with residents and actors in the non-profit sector
following the visions drawn by key stakeholders. Moreo-
ver, although the North Valley regeneration process partly
fulfilled criteria for the three stages of co-governance pro-
cesses presented by Zenker and Erfgen (2014), the inter -
personal relationships and power relations warrant closer
attention. The vision was drawn by two powerful men,
one in the public sector and one in the private sector, but
the latter had the financial muscle and thus led the regen-
eration process. This is rational from the perspective of
the current housing regime, but the fact that a handful of
people (invited by two powerful individuals) who were not
democratically chosen had such power to take urban plan-
ning and building decisions for an entire neighbourhood
clearly raises questions. From a democratic perspective,
this might contribute to a reproduction of building less
than ideally socially sustainable societies, even if the per -
sons leading the North Valley regeneration process had the
best intentions and were driven by home-love (Tuan 1974)
for their city. They decided to invest in North Valley, but
not the other two neighbourhoods in the city that had low
social capital and were described as needing social inter -
ventions, although North Valley was not described as trou-
bled and in need of social intervention in the municipality
documents. From the municipality’s perspective, North
Valley would not necessarily have been the first choice
for investment in a de-stigmatizing regeneration process,
but the neighbourhood was chosen by Company A since
they saw the greatest financial investment prospects there.
The regeneration in North Valley did not meet the crite-
ria for a democratic regeneration process, but it had posi-
tive effects in shifting the spatial stigmatization that North
Valley bore (Wacquant 2008). Wacquant (2008) argues that
the territorial stigmatization of a place lies in the negative
perceptions of people (who often do not live there), which
explains why place branding skills are important factors
in de-stigmatizing processes. Private sector stakeholders,
such as Company A, have skills and competence in place
branding, i.e. they know how to re-write the narrative of a
place in a trustworthy way that inspires people (other impor-
tant stakeholders) to believe in their vison and re-branding
narrative. The private sector has an entrepreneurial bank
of branding know-how and creative thinking that can lead
urban planning in the direction of spectacular re-branding
investments. The results of this study show that the type of
investments made by Company A and other stakeholders
differ from the types of social interventions that munici-
palities usually conduct in terms of urban planning. What
Company A, the municipality and other key stakeholders
did in the North Valley case was to treat the neighbourhood
as a valuable place, even if it was stigmatized. For exam-
ple, they illuminated the architectural value of the mental
hospital rather than focussing on its stigmatized reputation,
while such buildings have often been torn down in simi-
lar areas to distance them from their histories (Eivergård
et al. 2012). The re-branding involved a total make-over of
the neighbourhood. Social interventions, such as dialogue
with citizens, may give voices to residents living in a neigh-
bourhood regarding the types of changes that they desire,
but would a municipality ever dream of, or even have the
financial capacity to initiate such types of large-scale invest-
ments in a de-stigmatizing regeneration process? Company
A and the other key stakeholders used their own resources
of location-specific capital (DaVanzo 1981) when planning
spatial anchorages that worked both inwards and outwards:
outwards in terms of the place’s attractiveness (locally,
nationally and internationally) as a destination, and inwards
in terms of the residents identifying with and feeling pride in
their neighbourhood. Proud residents become promotors of
their neighbourhood and contribute to the re-branding pro-
cess. Proud residents become promotors of their neighbour-
hood and contribute to the re-branding process. An obvious
limitation of this case study is the setting in a single Swed-
ish city of moderate size by international standards. Nev -
ertheless, the findings clearly highlight the potential value
of including the private sector and place branding in simi-
lar urban development, de-stigmatization and regeneration
cases. They also highlight the importance of co-creation in
regeneration processes, inspiration by branding techniques
from the private sector and engagement of private sector
actors who are imbued with home-love. Thus, the findings
may also have relevance in other settings, and their general-
ity warrants further attention.
