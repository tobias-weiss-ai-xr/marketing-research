Abstract
The importance of stakeholder involvement in place branding is emphasized in recent literature. However, ideas about how
this should be managed are underdeveloped. To address this gap, we draw from network management theory and compare
the management of two region branding processes (top-down vs bottom-up). We find that the choice of network management
strategy matters for fostering stakeholder involvement because they lead to differing outcomes and levels of involvement;
that different management strategies are needed at different points in time to address specific priority needs; and that con-
sequently a fluid approach to applying network management strategies is recommendable, especially in regional contexts
affected by multi-level governance structures and a myriad of stakeholders. The empirical data show the practice potential of
the introduced network management framework to inform strategies that fittingly involve stakeholders in place brand devel-
opment processes. This study challenges current knowledge by demonstrating that the employment of network management
strategies is more path dependent and less voluntary than often stated in the network governance or place branding literature.

Introduction
The success of place branding depends on a wide variety of
actors. The collaboration of the network of actors around
the place brand is, therefore, elementary (Klijn et al. 2022).
Recent branding literature (Eshuis et al. 2018; Källström and
Siljeklint 2024) highlights that absence of involvement of
stakeholder groups may lead to (a) lack of identification with
the brand; and/or (b) lack of support for the resulting brand
and associated policies. Hence, many authors plea for more
bottom-up and stakeholder-based approaches in place brand-
ing (Kavaratzis and Kalandides 2015; Martin and Capelli
2017; Tøttenborg et al. 2023). However, place brand studies
often focus on the participation of citizens, which leads to a
lack of attention to other stakeholders (Vuignier 2017). Fur-
ther, studies on stakeholder involvement commonly address
factors that influence the degree of citizen participation such
as (i.e., Zenker and Beckmann 2013), or effects of citizen
participation on the place brand. Thus, how stakeholder par-
ticipation is organized and managed remains understudied.
Network management as vehicle to analyze
interactive branding processes
Against this background, the literature about (collabora-
tive & network) governance offers interesting insights (and
empirical evidence) about how to manage multiple stake-
holders in place branding processes (see also Feuvre et al.
2016; Eshuis et al. 2018). The range of stakeholders involved
in branding initiatives constitute a network of actors as
branding publications also emphasize (Hankinson 2004;
Hanna and Rowley 2015). Empirical research in collabora-
tive and network governance also shows that stakeholder
participation is positively correlated to the outcomes and
performance of governance networks (Klijn and Edelenbos
2013). The governance literature further supports findings in

The concepts of collaborative and network management,
nonetheless, have hardly been applied to place branding pro-
cesses. Combining place branding theory with collabora-
tive and network governance theory can contribute to our
understanding of how to manage stakeholder involvement.
Collaborative and network governance theories (especially
ideas about network management) offer more refined, empir-
ically validated (see Klijn et al. 2010) ideas about horizontal
management that better fit the interactive nature of branding
processes. These insights:
– allow for more fine-grained analyses of managerial strat-
egies in place branding, how they relate to stakeholder
involvement and how managerial choices affect outcomes
of the branding process;
– help to further knowledge about how place branding pro-
cesses evolve and how they can be governed;
– help inform a transition in place brand managers’ focus
from outcome (place brand identity and image) to a
whole process (developing and communicating place
brands) perspective;
– help operationalize in practice participatory place brand-
ing approaches; and offer the possibility of combining
top-down and bottom-up approaches to involve stake-
holders in place branding processes at different stages.
Exploring the managerial challenge of involving
stakeholders in region branding
This article examines the governance arrangements and
management strategies by the network of stakeholders
engaged in two region branding processes aimed at sup-
porting regional development in Spain and Australia. Stake-
holders are defined in the strategic management literature as
‘any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the
achievement of the organizations’ objectives (Freeman 1984,
p. 46). In place branding processes, they may include differ-
ent groups of residents, local companies, community groups,
voluntary organizations, and governments, among others
(Muñiz Martínez 2016). The cases selected here represent
an attempt to enhance the interactive character of regional
place branding (although to different degrees), as well as
to increase support for the brand and to increase citizens’
identification with the brand. The regions selected offer a
contrast between two main strategies observed in region
branding: bottom-up versus top-down (Ripoll Gonzalez
and Gale 2020). This enables us to analyze and compare
the two different strategies in detail to contribute to litera-
ture on brand management and stakeholder involvement in
branding. Moreover, beyond observing the effect of manage-
rial approaches at the process level (i.e., stakeholder engage-
ment) we tease out the relation between chosen (network
management) strategies and the outcomes of the branding
process. Our research questions are
– How do organizational differences in the branding pro-
cess influence the managerial challenges of brand manag-
ers?
– How do differences in governance and network manage-
ment strategies affect the branding process and its out-
comes?
Next, we present the theoretical underpinnings of this
paper, then we explain our qualitative methodological
approach combining document analysis and in-depth inter -
views and we outline the background of the two cases under
study: (1) the Northern Rivers in Australia, and (2) Mun-
tanya d’Alacant (from Catalan, ‘Mountains of Alicante’) in
Spain. Finally, we discuss the empirical findings, theoreti -
cal implications, and practical lessons before outlining our
conclusions.
Branding places: from image to identity
management
The literature on place branding highlights the importance of
place identities as constitutive of social and political spaces
(Ginesta and de San Eugenio 2021; Kavaratzis and Kalan-
dides 2015; Lucarelli 2018). Creating place identities can
also be a powerful strategy to appeal to citizens’ emotions
and collective action, via their identification with the region
(Relph 1976). Hence, brand managers often try to strengthen
place development by building a common identity and image
with the aid of place brands tools (Zenker and Beckmann
2013).
Two approaches on brands and branding
In the literature on place branding, two main approaches to
creating effective brand identities (the core of place brands)
can be distinguished, differing mainly on what is central to
a brand and what the starting point of branding processes
should be.
In the first approach, place branding is used as a strategy
to create a strong place identity based on a set of symbolic
elements that exert appeal to target groups. Under this view
place brands are mostly developed by brand owners (or man-
agers) that distill the essence of the place to its core com -
ponents to determine the regional brand identity and create
associated brand symbols (such as name, logo, and support-
ing brand visuals, among others—see Park et al. 1986 for

an example of this approach. The unity of the brand and the
strategic character of the brand are central in this approach,
and thus, this approach is essentially top-down (see also Kot-
ler and Keller 2006; Arvidsson 2006; Aitken and Campelo
2011). Proponents of this view have argued, however, that
symbols can also be created in collaborative ways together
with place stakeholders (Hanna and Rowley 2015). This will
enhance brand legitimacy and identification among local
communities and increase their support for the brand (see
Klijn et al. 2022). After all, identities are ‘contested social
constructs and processes’ (Paasi 2003: 476) open to constant
(re)imagination by actors in the network of the place and
external actors (see Massey in Kavaratzis and Kalandides
2015). Thus, involvement of stakeholder is considered valu-
able, but is often organized top-down and controlled by the
brand initiator. It is used as an ‘instrument’ for the brand
initiator to make the brand more effective. In this way also
the unity and clarity of the brand identity, which is a key
asset in this approach, can be to some extent controlled (see
Park et al. 1986).
The second approach prioritizes the image and experi-
ences that target groups have of the place brand. Here, the
associations and experiences of the users of the brand take
prominence against the brand symbol (Ind and Schmidt
2020; for an overview of approaches to place branding, see
Eshuis and Ripoll Gonzalez 2018). Within this perspective,
place brands are constructed by a myriad of stakeholders
and target groups (Stubbs and Warnaby 2014). Under this
more bottom-up second view, brand managers have much
less control over the meaning of brands compared to the
first perspective (Ind and Schmidt 2020). After all the brand
is shaped in the interactions between interdependent place
actors in complex networks where different perceptions and
interests are at play. And interactions in complex (in this
study regional) networks cannot be fully controlled (Klijn
and Koppenjan 2016).
In recognition of the abovementioned dynamic and inter-
actional nature of place brands, an alternative conceptualiza-
tion of place brands is emerging in the literature, where place
brands are defined as “marketing systems that consist of
dynamic performative assemblages of symbolic, discursive,
institutional, and material elements that selectively invite
certain multisensory and embodied experiences of place by
stakeholders and target groups” (authors anonymized, forth-
coming). Place brands can, thus, be built from a variety of
elements, from classic marketing elements such as signs and
stories that identify places and imbue places with meaning,
but also rules and regulations, iconic buildings, and other
elements of the physical placescape (Medway 2014; Eshuis
and Ripoll Gonzalez 2018).
Interestingly, both approaches on brands, although they
emphasize different dimension in the content of the brand
(coherent and constructed versus pluriform and constructed)
and prioritize different strategies to engaging stakeholders
(top-down vs bottom-up), both have growing attention to
the complexities of branding places and the importance of
stakeholder engagement, in the development of place brands.
Place branding as a governance process: the need
for network management
Place branding literature often presents brand management
as clear-cut marketing activities like creating brand images
and other brand elements, as well as communicating and
maintaining the place brand (see Park et al. 1986; Hanna
and Rowley 2015). Although the importance of stakeholder
involvement in those activities is widely acknowledged (e.g.,
Ind and Schmidt 2020; Ripoll González and Gale 2020; Van
den Berg and Braun 1999), the place branding literature does
not explicitly address how stakeholder involvement can and
should be managed. We, therefore, turn to the literature on
collaborative and network governance that has extensively
focused on horizontal forms of (network) management.
There is broad consensus in network governance and col-
laborative governance theories that the type of management
required in networks differs significantly from the classical
form of governing where the government has the power and
authority to take decisions and implement them hierarchi-
cally without involving others. Network governance requires
a more facilitative style of leadership (Ansell and Gash
2008). We define network management as “all the deliber -
ate strategies aimed at facilitating and guiding the interac-
tions and/or changing the feature of the network with the
intent to further the collaboration within network processes”
(Klijn and Koppenjan 2016, p. 11). The theoretical assump-
tion in most collaborative and network governance literature
is that collaborative and network governance processes are
complex, and that achieving good outcomes for the actors
in the network is extremely difficult (see Ansell and Gash
2008; Emerson and Nabatchi 2015; Klijn and Koppenjan
2016). This is caused by the presence of various actors with
different perceptions on the problem (and desired solutions)
and the strategic complexity of the processes due to (chang-
ing) strategies of autonomous, but interdependent actors
(Emerson and Nabatchi 2015; Klijn and Koppenjan 2016).
This makes governance processes—and also place brand-
ing processes which show exact the same characteristics-
complex and unpredictable. An approach where brands are
constructed together by stakeholders as the second discussed
approach to branding only contributes to this ‘messiness’ of
place branding (Kavaratzis and Hatch 2013; see for empiri-
cal evidence Thelander and Säwe 2015).
Thus, there is a need to manage these complex processes
which is mostly termed as network management. Most lit-
erature on collaborative and network governance assumes
that without extensive and good network management

The literature mentions a range of activities that net-

emphasized by the way that network management activi-

Strategies for exploring content are important to clarify

clarifying actors’ perceptions, finding common ground in
perceptions of actors, exploring new solutions (by research
activities, working with scenarios, joint sessions etc.).

tion, such as project organization, communication lines, and
so forth (Klijn and Koppenjan 2016). And connecting refers
to all activities to identify necessary actors, mobilize them
and facilitate interaction between them.

(also) on a good analysis what difficulties there are in the
process and what the process need to keep going (see Klijn
and Koppenjan 2016). In general, however, research shows
that more is better in the sense that managers that employ
more different strategies seem to be more successful (see
George et al. 2024).

Research Design
To illustrate how the choice of network management strat-
egies has consequences for stakeholder engagement, we
engage in a comparative qualitative case study analysis of
two cases that offer geographical similarities but a sharp
contrast in their approach: the Northern Rivers region (NRN)
in northern New South Wales, Australia and the Muntanya
d’ Alacant region (GALRMA) in Spain. This approach is
particularly well-suited to observe practice (Bartlett and
Vavrus 2016). Both cases have in common that they are non-
administratively defined (sub-regions) and they share similar
geographical traits (rural regions, rich in natural landscapes
close to mountain ranges, with strong agribusiness traditions
and high-quality produce). However, they vary in their initial
purpose and approach to place branding (see Table  2 ) and
to engaging stakeholders (see Fig.  1 for an overview of the
research process).
We first draw on multiple documentary data sources to
investigate how the branding process was managed across
the branding and regional development strategies in two
regional settings (see online Appendix I). Then we con -
ducted 14 semi-structured in-depth interviews to expand on
the brand development process, with a focus on observing
network management. 1 During the interviews, additional
documents were shared by participants and included in the
analysis. We also scanned the official communication chan-
nels (website and social media) for the two cases.

approaches, ensuring representation of all key stakeholders
and theoretical saturation (Eisenhardt 1989). The follow -
ing criteria were applied: participants have been and were
involved in the development of the regional brand identity

Methodology
In-depth qualitative interview data combined with analysis
of relevant strategic and policy documents (Eisenhardt 1989)
allowed for an in-depth understanding of the structural and
network issues under study. The interviews of 40–70 min
were conducted in January to March 2022 online, via Zoom,
video recorded, and subsequently transcribed and de-iden-
tified to protect participants’ anonymity and confidentiality.
The research team was additionally involved as participant
observers in several meetings organized by the Local Action
Group (LAG) Muntanya d’ Alacant and regional develop-
ment forums in the Northern Rivers (2017–2024), where
they were able to observe actor interactions in the region
branding processes. The researchers also shared and dis-
cusses the results of this qualitative exploration with stake-
holders from cases, which has greatly benefited the discus-
sion and reflection sections.
Interview and document data were coded with Atlas.ti.
using a mix of deductive (following the main constructs of
the theoretical framework) and inductive coding, highlight-
ing the themes and patterns deemed most relevant to the
research questions, as well as additional emerging themes
and relationships between themes (Miles and Huberman
1994). Inter-coder reliability was observed through a con-
stant comparative analysis approach. The data were then
thematically analyzed. We first coded the branding and
regional development plans based on (1) aim of branding
efforts; (2) stakeholders involved; and (3) network manage-
ment strategies utilized. We subsequently did the same for
the interviews with key actors, also looking for additional
contextual data explaining such choices. We triangulated
the deductive findings with online communication strategies
and annual reports to see the extent to which engagement
with stakeholders was occurring through (1) invitation to
gatherings or events; (2) opportunities to share feedback.
Interpretation was supported by researchers’ insights as par-
ticipant observers and personal communication with brand
managers during the project.
Background to the two cases
This section compares main characteristics of the two cases
in relation to the region branding process, including geo-
graphical traits, aims, stakeholders involved, and governance
arrangements.
Muntanya d’Alacant (GALRMA)
The Local Action Group (LAG) Muntanya d’Alacant (lit-
erally translated as ‘mountains of Alicante’) originated
from a series of workshops in 2015 that brought together
representatives of public entities (city councils and com-
monwealths), private organizations (associations, coopera-
tives), and other territorial agents involved in developing a
candidacy for the (re)establishment of a LAG for the region
under the European Union participatory rural and regional
development LEADER program, for rural and regional
development (2014–2020) (see Table 2). GALRMA became
a formal non-profit association in 2016 with the main aim
to foster collaboration in support of rural development by
competitively allocating funds from the LEADER program.
The area of action (boundaries) of GALRMA has been
artificially (not administratively) defined. The organization
acts within the frame of a regional (autonomous commu-
nity) strategy for rural development ( Pla de Desenvolupa -
ment Rural de la Comunitat Valenciana 2014–2020). Its
main strategic document, the Estrategia de Desenvolupal-
ment Local Participatiu (Participatory Local Development
Strategy, or EDLP), was developed through a participa-
tory process that remains open to incorporating new agents
at all stages, thus, maintaining the principles of the EU’s
LEADER methodology of collaboration, co-operation, con-
sensus, and concretion (Esparcia et al. 2015).
Branding the Muntanya d’ Alacant: strengthening the
community around the brand.
Although branding the region was not a core activity
specified in the EDLP, the Junta of the GALRMA devel-
oped a brand identity and set of community values, with the
aim to assist the process of establishment and bring actors
together. The brand visual encompasses a mountain and the
name of Alacant, the main capital city and province denomi-
nation and the tagline ‘Sorgits the molts pobles, tots som un’
(from many different villages, we all make one). It provided
a unified vision (based on territorial characteristics, moun-
tains) and a strong vocation for community building. It was
used as a tool to establish the collective identity of the terri-
tory, bind stakeholders around the process, and create brand
community. The main communication and marketing vehicle
is their website (www. galru ralmu ntany adala cant. com) sup-
ported by Facebook and Twitter pages.
While efforts were concentrated on summoning collective
identities around actions that encourage sustainable territo-
rial development, positioning for products and services has
been mainly done through additional sub-brands, such as
‘Essències, som terra i vincles’ (Essences, we are country
and ties) focused on promoting local produce and rural tour-
ism. The tagline ‘vine a viure el nostre poble’ (come live our
village) is aimed at attracting tourists and the logo represents

Northern Rivers New South Wales (NRN), Australia
The Northern Rivers region is also not defined by adminis-
trative borders but rather common geographical character -
istics (its rivers and volcanic areas). The area amalgamates
the local government areas of Tweed Heads (limiting to
the north with the Australian state of Queensland), Byron
Bay, Ballina, Clarence Valley, Richmond Valley, Lismore,
and Kyogle in the state New South Wales, and contains the
catchments and valleys of the Clarence, Richmond, and
Tweed rivers.
Branding the  Northern Rivers: positioning the  region The
NRN brand was developed ‘through a collaboration of
regional partners, including key business, industry, and gov-
ernment representatives’, with the aim to ‘unite and share
the values of the region, its place and its people and a way to
tell [their] regional story’ (NRN 2020). The NRN brand was
released in 2021 following a deliberate process to develop
a stronger positioning for the region, especially to support
economic development. A coalition of the willing was
formed around the brand (brand committee). The organiza-
tion also offers a membership program (free to join) whereby
members are allowed to use the ‘official’ branding materi-
als (i.e., logo and imagery) across their marketing collateral
and digital platforms. The brand committee operated under
the auspices of Regional Development Australia—North-
ern Rivers, a national organization that provided a legal
framework and administrative support for initial actions.3 A
Memorandum of Understanding was signed between found-
ing partners to ensure the necessary resources and commit-
ment for the project.

The studied cases reflect distinct differences in the original
aim and approaches but coincide on their focus on creating
a place brand, in this case a regional brand identity, to sup-
port region-building through a common discourse. On one
hand, the NRN brand aims to evoke a selective identity that
positions the region in a specific way and makes it stand
out vis-à-vis other regions. On the other hand, GALRMA’s
aim was to support an integral ‘regional identity’ around the
challenges to achieving sustainable regional development
due to the geographical traits of the region. We see also dif-
ferent emphasis on network management strategy to support
these aims. The network management activities of NRM are
more oriented to connecting actors to an already developed
brand content and focuses on arranging the different actors
(especially economic interests) around the brand. The GAL-
RMA effort are strongly focused on connecting strategies
to bind all possible actors in a very bottom-up process and
some exploring strategies but more to collect various ideas
than build a common united brand. Table 6 summarizes the
network management approach and effects in the two cases.
What are the effects of network management
on the communicated brand identity?
The NRN brand committee clearly devotes significant efforts
to develop a brand narrative and strong visuals (logo and
selected imagery) supported by success stories to attract
investment and visitation to the region. The communication
efforts are supported by a dedicated marketing team of a
large regional organization and is hence very professional
and well resourced. However, a realization of the need to
engage the local community in communicating the brand has
led to an effort to highlight also internal branding (what is
in it for them). Hence a renewed effort was noted on social
media to engage the local community and a new section of
the website dedicated to celebrating success of and recruit-
ing local community members.
In contrast, the GALRMA website acts as a portal to
access information about the organization and its actions.
The emphasis is on reinforcing a sense of community and
purpose and informing interested internal stakeholders of
the actions and strategies of the network around GALRMA,
with a focus also on transparency and equal access to infor-
mation. In addition, the organizations’ use of social media,
in particular Twitter, is aimed at celebrating the successes
and innovations of its community members and transmit-
ting to network actors’ information about upcoming fund-
ing rounds, events planned, and additional support pro-
vided by the organization (i.e., training sessions). A logo
is used in the organizations’ communication but is aimed
more at providing a common identity for the groups’ activi-
ties. The functionality of digital communication choices by
the groups, thus, matches the objectives of their respective
regional identity processes.

Conclusion and reflections
This study provides a comparative analysis of stake-
holder engagement in the initial development phase of two
region branding processes, focusing on the governance
arrangements and network management strategies that ena-
bled two different approaches to branding the region.
Two different branding aims, two different network
management approaches
The two region branding processes highlight how differ -
ences in the management of the place branding network can
lead to differing stakeholder engagement and outcomes.
Stakeholder participation differs in both cases. In the case
of Muntanya d’ Alacant, the brand development process
featured high level of participation of actors in the design,
decision-making and implementation phases of the branding
in line with their objective to bind actors together and create
a sense of community and purpose to enhance regional co-
operation. The specificities of governance (self-organized
network) and stakeholder participation mechanisms under -
lying LAG initiatives under the framework of LEADER
are key in warranting maximum citizen participation in the
process. Thus, we see a strong application of connecting
strategies to foster this desired high level of stakeholder par-
ticipation. However, what also crystallizes from the case is
that network management is indeed key to achieve desired
levels of stakeholder participation. In the NRN case we see
less (varied) stakeholder participation in the beginning and
more emphasis on developing the brand, as mostly the group
of brand initiators is involved in this stage, and only later
when the brand is developed, we observe an attempt to con-
nect other stakeholders.
Our analysis highlights the relationship between the types
of network management strategies employed and the moti-
vation and aims of regional branding processes. This leads
to two interesting observations. The first is that the employ-
ment of network management in the two cases creates dif-
ferent outcomes, different brand messages and different
processes. The conclusion that network management mat-
ters is in accordance with what we know from the network
governance literature (see for instance George et al. 2024).
However, a second key observation not always present in
the network governance or place branding literatures is that
the employment of network management strategies is more
path dependent and less voluntary than otherwise stated in
the literature (Bianchi et al. 2021). Network management
literature often assumes that network managers can pick
almost randomly strategies that suit the problems that net-
works face (see Klijn and Koppenjan 2016 ; Emerson and
Nabatchi 2015). Our findings however suggest some kind of
path dependency where the choice of strategies follows the
original aims and contextual situation in which the branding
project originates, which is logical. How the process evolves
is, thus, determined by first choices and starting efforts in
the management of different actors. Even if in situations
where perhaps other strategies are needed, and we reflect

Managing stakeholder involvement in place branding: the need for network management
on this in the next sections, we do not see a lot of change
in the approach. Thus, in both cases presented here manag-
ers seem to mostly stick to their original choices in regard
to managing stakeholders. The fact that solid arrangements
have been made (see NRN) and/or process rules have been
accepted (GALRMA) clearly contributes to the outlined
path dependency and leads to limitations in the use of cer -
tain network management strategies. At the same time, it
becomes evident that different approaches are needed in the
lifecycle of branding processes, as both network strategies
and governance structures may need to adapt to enhance
certain aspects of branding (i.e., binding actors together or
increasing support) which requires more directly engaging
(additional) different stakeholders.
Bottom‑up or top‑down: is there a best strategy?
Both approaches in the two cases also show vulnerable spots
although these are quite different. In the case of the more
bottom-up approach (GALRMA), the weakness is in the
communication of the brand values despite a strong com-
munity created around the brand project. GALRMA tries to
overcome this through the creation of sub-brands like Essèn-
cies. The need lays in developing content and coordinating
separate existing actions (alignment). The Spanish case also
offers a cautionary tale on the risks of a single focus on
collaborative and inclusive branding approaches aimed at
achieving broad consensus. This risk is often underplayed in
collaborative governance literatures (Ansell and Gash 2008),
also in the literatures on stakeholder participation in place
branding that commonly emphasize the positive aspects of
intense stakeholder participation (Ripoll González and Gale
2020). As we show above, in the Spanish case, clear rules to
guide decision making with regard to stakeholder engage-
ment are lacking, as there mostly an informal mandate to
strive for wider participation, following EU LEADER prin-
ciples for sustainable regional development (funder man-
date). However, since the main aim is to build community
and create a common sense of identity, the flexibility inher-
ent in the informality of engagement with stakeholders is
warranted.
On the other hand, the Australian top-down initiated pro-
ject resulted in a clear regional brand identity and brand
content (logo that can be communicated to external target
groups). However, albeit a recognized willingness to involve
the local community, most efforts were devoted to creating
awareness among the regions’ stakeholders and increase
their support for the brand (communicating the brand val-
ues). The need here is about connecting to the wider commu-
nity and involving them with the brand to avoid resistance.
This is ever more important given the dynamic, open and
experiential nature of place brands (authors, forthcoming).
Thus, both initial choices and resulting network manage-
ment strategies create their own dilemmas. For the Mun-
tanya d’ Alacant case the challenge is to manage the frag-
mentation that comes with the bottom-up approach, keep
the network together and develop a clear and communica-
ble brand identity. For the Northern Rivers case the chal -
lenge is to reach out and receive support and buy-in from
community and citizens. Hence, both cases would benefit
from intensifying network management strategies that are
currently more dominant in the other case. Similarly, this
confirms empirical (quantitative) findings that more vari-
ety of network management strategies are positively related
to outcomes (Klijn et al. 2010). It also shows why picking
only a few network management strategies fosters one sided
outcome. The framework applied here can be used by place
brand managers to reflect on the strategies at hand at every
step of the process, from development to implementation
(Eshuis and Ripoll Gonzalez 2018).
