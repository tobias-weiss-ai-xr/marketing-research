Abstract
Sustainability claims have become a pervasive marketing strategy, yet their proliferation has been
accompanied by greenwashing, the deliberate exaggeration or distortion of environmental claims for
reputational or economic gain. This review synthesizes empirical evidence on how greenwashing affects
consumer trust and behavior, and asks why a stimulus that reliably damages consumer trust translates
only inconsistently into reduced purchasing. Following the PRISMA 2020 protocol, searches of Scopus
and Dimensions (2015–2026) returned 808 unique records, of which 47 empirical studies passed
screening and Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) quality appraisal; a structured narrative synthesis
combining thematic analysis and vote-counting was complemented by a bibliometric mapping of 806
records. Greenwashing consistently increases skepticism, perceived risk, confusion, and perceived
betrayal, and erodes trust, brand equity, image, and authenticity. Its effect on purchase intention,
however, is predominantly indirect and conditional: the simple negative direct path held in only 9 of the
15 studies that estimated it, with the effect routed through mediators (most often green skepticism) and
gated by boundary conditions, as brand loyalty and credibility buffer the damage whereas product
involvement and perceived value amplify it. Trust repair and stakeholder-level impacts remain the
clearest gap, addressed by only 7 of 47 studies and resting on a single experiment. The review
contributes an integrative dual-pathway account in which greenwashing propagates through parallel
cognitive and affective routes onto a behavioral escalation continuum, from passive disengagement to
active retaliation, formalized as testable propositions that reconcile the ﬁeld's divergent direct-effect
ﬁndings and deﬁne a trust-repair research agenda.
1 Introduction
Sustainability claims have become an increasingly common feature of marketing strategy as ﬁrms
compete to signal environmental concern to consumers. As this sustainability narrative has intensiﬁed,
so too has greenwashing, the deliberate exaggeration or distortion of environmental claims for
reputational or economic gain (Delmas & Burbano, 2011; Lyon & Maxwell, 2011; Nguyen et al., 2026). The
prevalence of advertising containing greenwashing has risen markedly in recent years, creating a need to
understand how consumers respond to it (Parguel et al., 2011; Timmons et al., 2024). The phenomenon
now spans a wide range of industries (from fashion and food to automotive and electric vehicles) and
frequently masquerades as legitimate environmental communication (Dudziak et al., 2025).
For consumers, greenwashing is not merely a communication issue but a trigger of adverse
psychological reactions. Foundational research established that greenwashing erodes green trust
through green consumer confusion and green perceived risk (Chen & Chang, 2013), and that perceived
greenwashing interacts with green advertising to shape consumer reactions (Nyilasy et al., 2014).
Perceived greenwashing heightens green skepticism, which in turn lowers purchase intention (Nguyen et
al., 2019). It also erodes consumer trust and damages brand equity and image (Mu & Lee, 2023; Ha,
2022; Qayyum et al., 2023). Moreover, greenwashing ampliﬁes perceived risk and confusion in green
consumption, thereby shaping purchase decisions (Braga Junior et al., 2019).
Page 2/35

Ironically, green marketing designed to build trust and encourage pro-environmental behavior can
backﬁre when it is perceived as greenwashing (Shi & Omar, 2024). Greenwashing concerns widen the
gap between consumers' positive attitudes and their actual purchasing behavior (Margariti et al., 2024).
In extreme cases, greenwashing even provokes brand hate that escalates into boycott and sabotage
intentions (Senan et al., 2025).
Although scholarly attention to greenwashing has grown rapidly, the available consumer-side evidence
remains fragmented and unconsolidated, and recent reviews have approached the phenomenon from
vantage points other than the individual consumer. Persakis et al. (2025) mapped greenwashing in
marketing through a combined bibliometric and systematic review, yet concluded that its consumer-
oriented effects remain comparatively underexplored relative to corporate social responsibility and
environmental-reporting domains. Vangeli et al. (2023) synthesized the migration from greenwashing to
green marketing in business-to-business settings; Santos et al. (2024) reviewed greenwashing chieﬂy in
relation to its broader stakeholder ecosystem; and Gupta and Singh (2025) charted global research
trends and inﬂuential contributors bibliometrically. None of these reviews integrates, at the consumer
level, the interlocking evidence on how perceived greenwashing erodes trust and translates into behavior
through speciﬁc psychological mechanisms and boundary conditions. This omission is consequential,
because the way greenwashing is conceptualized and measured varies widely (from perceived deception
to experimental manipulation and its converse in authenticity and transparency), which hampers cross-
study comparison (Tran et al., 2025; Nygaard & Silkoset, 2023). The direction of its effects is likewise
inconsistent: some studies report no direct effect of greenwashing on purchase intention (Natasya et al.,
2023; Shojaei et al., 2024), whereas others ﬁnd that greenwashing awareness can actually raise
purchase propensity (Apostolopoulos et al., 2025); and even the dominant mediating mechanism,
skepticism, is not always signiﬁcant (Adil et al., 2024). At the same time, evidence on post-greenwashing
trust repair rests on only a handful of studies (Wang & Walker, 2023). These inconsistencies point to an
unresolved theoretical tension rather than a simple absence of evidence. Greenwashing's damage to the
consumer's internal state, namely heightened skepticism, perceived risk, and betrayal alongside
diminished trust, is robust and convergent, yet it does not translate cleanly or consistently into the
downstream behavior that marketers and regulators ultimately care about. Prior consumer-side studies
establish that greenwashing harms, but they do not jointly explain which psychological route carries that
harm, which behavioral response each route produces, or what conditions gate the translation from
internal reaction to external action. It is this mechanistic gap, rather than the mere absence of an
integrative review, that the present study sets out to close.
To structure this fragmented evidence, the review is organized around a stimulus-organism-response
logic (Mehrabian & Russell, 1974): perceived or manipulated greenwashing acts as an external stimulus;
a set of cognitive and affective states (skepticism, perceived risk, confusion, betrayal, and (dis)trust)
constitutes the intervening organismic layer; and consumer behavior, from purchase intention to word of
mouth, boycott, and sabotage, forms the response. This cognitive-affective-behavioral lens, consistent
with accounts applied within the corpus itself (e.g., Haque & Lang, 2025), foregrounds the review's
Page 3/35

central concern with the mechanisms and conditions through which green marketing backﬁres, rather
than with the mere existence of harm.
To address this gap, this study conducts a systematic literature review of empirical evidence on the
impact of greenwashing on consumer trust and behavior. Speciﬁcally, the review answers ﬁve questions:
(RQ1) how greenwashing is conceptualized and operationalized in consumer research; (RQ2) how it
affects trust, behavior, and brand outcomes; (RQ3) what mediating mechanisms explain these effects;
(RQ4) what moderating and contextual factors shape their boundaries; and (RQ5) how trust and brand
relationships can be repaired, together with the implications for stakeholders.
The review makes four contributions. First, it establishes through structured narrative synthesis and
vote-counting that greenwashing's damage to consumer trust is convergent and robust, whereas its
effect on purchase intention is predominantly indirect and conditional rather than uniformly negative.
Second, it consolidates the previously scattered evidence on mediating mechanisms and moderating
conditions into an ordered account of how that damage is transmitted and when it is buffered or
ampliﬁed. Third, and most substantively, it advances an integrative dual-pathway framework in which
greenwashing propagates through parallel cognitive and affective routes onto a behavioral escalation
continuum, formalized as testable propositions that reconcile the ﬁeld's divergent direct-effect ﬁndings.
Fourth, it complements the synthesis with a bibliometric mapping that situates the consumer-side
evidence within the broader intellectual structure of the ﬁeld, and on that basis it identiﬁes post-
greenwashing trust repair as the most consequential gap and the clearest priority for future research.
The synthesis is organized around ﬁve research questions and then integrated through the stimulus-
organism-response framework; the remainder of the article presents the review and bibliometric
methods, the results and discussion, and the conclusion with its theoretical, practical, and policy
implications.

2 Methodology
2.1 Research design and reporting standard
This study adopts a dual-method design that pairs a systematic literature review (SLR) as its primary
analytical engine with a complementary bibliometric analysis, an increasingly common combination in
business and management reviews. The SLR was designed and reported in accordance with the
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (Page
et al., 2021), and the bibliometric layer maps the intellectual structure of the surrounding ﬁeld. All stages
(identiﬁcation, screening, eligibility assessment, and methodological quality appraisal) were governed by
an a priori protocol so that the study-selection process is traceable and replicable. A review protocol
specifying the research questions, eligibility criteria, search strategy, and synthesis plan was developed a
priori and is available from the corresponding author on request; the review was not pre-registered in a
public registry such as PROSPERO or the Open Science Framework, which we note as a transparency
limitation.

2.3 Eligibility criteria
Inclusion and exclusion criteria were deﬁned before screening to preserve studies' relevance to the
research questions. A study was included if it met all of the following conditions:
greenwashing was the principal construct measured, manipulated, or systematically analyzed;
it measured at least one outcome from the trust cluster (trust, credibility, skepticism, or perceived
deception), the behavioral cluster (purchase intention, word of mouth, loyalty, boycott intention, or
actual behavior), or the brand cluster (brand equity, image, or attitude);
it positioned consumers, customers, or individual stakeholders as the unit of analysis;
it was an original empirical study with a quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods design; and
it was published in a reputable journal, or as a systematic review, in English within 2015–2026 with
accessible full text.
Conversely, a study was excluded if any of the following held:
greenwashing served only as background rather than the principal construct;
the unit of analysis was at the ﬁrm level, such as corporate disclosure, ﬁnancial data, or
organizational decision-making models;
the study was non-empirical or a secondary review; or
no consumer-level dependent variable was measured, or the unit of analysis was not the consumer
(for example, social-media content).

2.4 Study-selection process
Raw exports yielded 587 Scopus records and 500 Dimensions records, for 1,087 records in total. Before
screening, 39 records outside the 2015–2026 window, 73 non-article document types, and 167 cross-
database duplicates (identiﬁed by DOI matching, with no additional title-based duplicates) were
removed, leaving 808 unique records. To improve screening eﬃciency, an algorithmic pre-screen ﬂagged
articles that simultaneously satisﬁed three text-based criteria: the presence of greenwashing or an
equivalent term in the title, at least one trust- or behavioral-cluster outcome construct in the abstract,
and consumer-focused terminology in the abstract; this pre-screen ﬂagged 236 priority articles (29.2% of
the corpus) and was used solely to order the review, not to make inclusion or exclusion decisions. All 808
records were then screened manually at the title-and-abstract stage, and 750 were excluded as irrelevant
to the research questions. The full texts of all 58 remaining reports were retrieved (none was
unobtainable) and assessed against the eligibility criteria; at this stage ten reports were excluded (two
because greenwashing was not the principal construct, three because the unit of analysis was at the ﬁrm
level, two because they were non-empirical or review articles, and three because they measured no
consumer-level dependent variable or used social-media content as the unit of analysis), leaving 48
studies that passed full-text eligibility. The entire process is summarized in the PRISMA 2020 ﬂow
diagram (Fig. 1).
2.5 Methodological quality appraisal
Studies passing full-text screening were appraised for methodological quality with the Mixed Methods
Appraisal Tool (MMAT) version 2018 (Hong et al., 2018), which permits the simultaneous appraisal of
quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods designs. Each study was evaluated on two screening items
and ﬁve design-speciﬁc criteria, with each item scored as met (1.0), partially met (0.5), or not met (0.0),
for a maximum of seven. A conservative a priori inclusion threshold of four out of seven was applied;
one study fell below this threshold and was excluded, leaving 47 studies for the ﬁnal synthesis. Title-and-
abstract screening, full-text eligibility assessment, and the MMAT appraisal were conducted by the ﬁrst
author and independently veriﬁed by the three co-authors; any disagreements were resolved through
discussion until consensus was reached. Most included studies were non-randomized quantitative
cross-sectional survey designs, complemented by several quantitative experiments, one qualitative
study, and mixed-methods studies; the distribution of designs is summarized in Table 7.
2.6 Data extraction
Data were extracted from all 47 studies using a standardized form that captured study identity (authors,
year, title); methodological characteristics (research design, sample size, analysis method); the
conceptualization and operationalization of greenwashing; key ﬁndings together with effect sizes such
as standardized path coeﬃcients, reliability and validity indicators (Cronbach's alpha, composite
reliability, and average variance extracted), and per-path hypothesis-test outcomes; reported limitations;
and each study's relevance to the ﬁve research questions. Effect sizes were recorded as reported in the
Page 8/35

original studies (whether as standardized beta coeﬃcients, correlations, or experimental statistics such
as F and eta-squared) without further transformation.
2.7 Synthesis strategy
Given the heterogeneity of the corpus in research design, greenwashing operationalization, outcome
variables, and model speciﬁcation, meta-analytic pooling of effect sizes was deemed inappropriate,
because standardized coeﬃcients drawn from multivariate models with differing speciﬁcations are not
directly comparable. Synthesis therefore proceeded as a structured narrative that combined thematic
analysis for the conceptual and qualitative dimensions (particularly the questions concerning
greenwashing conceptualization, trust repair, and managerial implications) with vote-counting based on
the direction and statistical signiﬁcance of effects for the quantitative relationships, supplemented by a
descriptive presentation of the reported effect-size ranges. No pooled estimates were computed, and
every reported pattern was traced back to its source study to keep the synthesis transparent and
veriﬁable.
2.8 Bibliometric analysis
To complement the systematic synthesis, a bibliometric analysis of the de-duplicated identiﬁcation
corpus was conducted to map the intellectual structure and emergence of research themes. This
analysis followed the bibliometrix analytical framework (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017) and was run on the
combined Scopus-Dimensions corpus for 2015–2026. Performance analysis assessed annual scientiﬁc
production, the most relevant sources, the most productive authors, and the most inﬂuential documents
by document count and citations. Science mapping was performed through co-word analysis of author
keywords: an equivalence index between keyword pairs was computed, thematic clusters were formed
via community detection, and each cluster was positioned on the plane of Callon centrality and density
(Cobo et al., 2011), which was divided by median splits into motor, basic, niche, and emerging-or-
declining theme quadrants. It should be emphasized that the bibliometric layer covers the broader
identiﬁcation corpus, whereas the systematic synthesis covers only the 47 quality-appraised studies.

3.2.1 Conceptualization and operationalization of
greenwashing (RQ1)
Across the corpus, greenwashing was conceptualized and measured through six recurring approaches
(Table 8). The dominant approach, found in 37 of 47 studies, treats greenwashing as consumers'
perception of misleading environmental claims, captured through reﬂective, multi-item scales. A second
group manipulated greenwashing experimentally through advertising or product stimuli, enabling
stronger causal inference. A theoretically important distinction emerges in a third group that measured
awareness or recognition of greenwashing rather than perceived deception, a distinction that matters
because awareness does not behave identically to perceived deception, as the impact analysis shows.
The remainder treated greenwashing as a typology of violations (two studies), modeled it as an outcome
to be suppressed or through its converse such as authenticity and transparency, or constructed its
meaning inductively from consumer narratives in qualitative designs. This conceptual fragmentation is

3.2.2 Impact on trust, behavior, and brand (RQ2)
The most frequently estimated relationship is the effect of greenwashing on green purchase intention. A
vote-count of direct effects (Table 9) shows a dominant negative direction that is nonetheless far from
uniform. Nine studies reported a signiﬁcant negative direct effect: eight reported standardized
coeﬃcients from − 0.151 to -0.44, and one experiment reported η² = 0.33, a variance-explained statistic
that is not directly comparable to those coeﬃcients. Four studies found no signiﬁcant direct effect or no
direct path at all, and two reported a positive direct effect that reversed to negative once mediation or
total effects were taken into account. The core pattern is therefore that greenwashing's effect on
purchase intention is predominantly negative but frequently indirect or conditional: in six of the ﬁfteen
studies that estimated a direct effect the simple negative path did not hold (four null, two positive that
reversed through mediation), and the impact is instead channeled through intervening mechanisms,
most explicitly through advertising skepticism and brand credibility.

Beyond purchase intention, the corpus exhibits a highly consistent cognitive-affective signature.

heightens brand hate, which in turn drives boycott and sabotage intentions. Its effect on trust is
predominantly erosive (greenwashing, or the confusion it generates, lowers green and brand trust), while
for brand outcomes it reduces brand equity, image, and authenticity. A few exceptions are recorded as
reported, including one study in which perceived greenwashing was positively associated with trust
within an affective model, one whose anti-greenwashing construct was non-signiﬁcant for trust, and one
ﬁnding that greenwashing awareness reduced confusion, contrary to perception-based studies.
This pattern of indirect effects is precisely what explains why green marketing can backﬁre. When
environmental claims are read as non-credible signals, consumers interpret them through a signaling
lens, so that greenwashing lowers brand credibility and, in turn, purchase intention (Poulis et al., 2026;
Nguyen et al., 2026). Within an extended theory-of-planned-behavior framework, greenwashing tends to
weaken the attitude and self-eﬃcacy paths to intention rather than negate intention outright (Di Pillo et
al., 2025; Do et al., 2025), while persuasion-knowledge and elaboration accounts indicate that what
mediates evaluation is skepticism toward the advertisement, not perceived greenwashing alone
(Balaskas et al., 2025; Andreoli et al., 2024). In emerging markets characterized by high information
asymmetry, greenwashing aggravates uncertainty and gives rise to moral-hazard and adverse-selection
dynamics that depress trust even in genuinely sustainable products (Srisathan & Naruetharadhol, 2025;
Nguyen et al., 2026).

3.2.4 Trust repair, stakeholders, and managerial relevance
(RQ5)
The questions of trust repair and managerial implications are the least covered, by only seven of the 47
studies, making them the clearest gap in the corpus. Evidence on trust repair rests on a single
experimental study showing that the choice and sequencing of intervention strategies can restore green
trust after greenwashing, with cost-beneﬁt reconsideration as a mediator and consumers' implicit beliefs
as a moderator (Wang & Walker, 2023). A few other studies identiﬁed anti-greenwashing levers:
blockchain information and certiﬁcation, green demarketing, and the authenticity of green reporting
(Nygaard & Silkoset, 2023; Azazz et al., 2024; Nie & Zhou, 2026); one extended the scope to employees
as internal stakeholders (Mu & Lee, 2023); and one showed that an inoculation intervention improved
greenwashing detection but produced false positives toward genuinely honest advertising (Timmons et
al., 2024).
3.2.5 Cross-question synthesis and divergent ﬁndings
Taken together, the evidence base displays a convergent impact core (greenwashing consistently
increases skepticism, perceived risk, confusion, betrayal, and brand hate, and lowers trust, brand equity,
image, and authenticity), while its behavioral expression is predominantly indirect and conditional, since
the simple negative direct path to purchase intention held in only nine of the ﬁfteen studies that
estimated it, with the effect generally routed through mediators. Several divergent ﬁndings are retained
as substantive boundary conditions rather than dismissed as anomalies. The distinction between
awareness and perceived deception matters, because greenwashing awareness can raise purchase
propensity even when it is ultimately mediated negatively through confusion (Apostolopoulos et al.,
2025). Within an affective cognition-affect-behavior model, perceived greenwashing can be bundled with
other affective responses such that it is positively associated with trust (Haque & Lang, 2025). A positive
Page 19/35

direct effect can reverse to negative at the total level through dual routes (Balaskas et al., 2025); high
advertising skepticism in some consumers is associated with more favorable brand evaluations (Sailer
et al., 2022); and consumers often struggle to recognize greenwashing spontaneously, distinguishing it
only when the greenwashing category is explicitly activated (Fella & Bausa, 2024; Jugo et al., 2025). The
corpus is also relatively homogeneous methodologically, dominated by self-reported cross-sectional
designs that measure intention rather than behavior, and concentrated geographically and
demographically on emerging Asian markets and young consumers, a proﬁle that conditions the
generalizability of all of the above.
3.3 Strength of the evidence and risk of bias
The conﬁdence that can be placed in this synthesis depends on the methodological quality of its
constituent studies and on the structural features of the evidence base. All 47 included studies cleared
the MMAT threshold, with quality scores ranging from 5.0 to 7.0 and a mean of 6.0 out of 7, indicating a
generally sound body of work in which clearly stated research questions, adequate measurement, and
appropriate analysis were the norm rather than the exception. Quality was not uniform, however. The
lowest-scoring studies typically left their sampling strategy underspeciﬁed or did not address the
confounding that is endemic to cross-sectional designs, and one study was downgraded below the
inclusion threshold and removed.
Three structural features temper this otherwise favorable appraisal and shape how much weight each
type of conclusion can bear. First, the corpus is overwhelmingly correlational: 38 of the 47 studies are
cross-sectional surveys and only 8 are experiments, so the convergent ﬁnding that greenwashing erodes
trust and inﬂates skepticism rests far more on association than on manipulation. Second, the analytical
toolkit is highly homogeneous, with 20 studies relying on partial least squares structural equation
modeling and self-reported intention as the terminal outcome; shared method and measurement raise
the possibility that common-method variance inﬂates the mediated paths that dominate the ﬁeld. Third,
the literature is concentrated in a small number of outlets and within a recent, rapidly expanding
publication window, which makes selective reporting and publication bias plausible even though the
present review could not formally assess them through funnel-plot or trim-and-ﬁll procedures given the
heterogeneity of effect speciﬁcations. Taken together, these features justify treating the convergent
impact core as a high-conﬁdence ﬁnding, the inconsistent direct effect on purchase intention as a
genuine feature of the evidence rather than an artifact of underpowering, and any causal or behavioral
claim, particularly those concerning actual rather than intended behavior, as provisional. Although a
formal meta-analysis was precluded by this heterogeneity, the 15 studies that estimated the direct
greenwashing-to-purchase-intention path, most using comparable partial least squares speciﬁcations,
constitute a coherent subset on which a future meta-analytic synthesis could be built.
3.4 An integrative framework: dual-pathway processing
and the behavioral escalation continuum
Page 20/35

The synthesis above documents a recurring paradox: greenwashing reliably damages the consumer's
internal state yet translates only inconsistently into reduced purchasing, and the studies that estimate its
direct effect on purchase intention disagree, with a clear negative path holding in only 9 of the 15 studies
that tested it. Existing accounts, which treat greenwashing as a single stimulus acting on a single
outcome, cannot easily explain this divergence. We therefore advance an integrative framework that
organizes the evidence within a stimulus-organism-response architecture and resolves the paradox by
distinguishing the psychological route through which greenwashing is processed from the behavioral
response that each route produces. Figure 4 summarizes the framework.
The framework's ﬁrst claim is that greenwashing is not processed through one channel but two. A
cognitive route runs through green skepticism, perceived risk, and confusion, the appraisals a consumer
forms when an environmental claim seems unveriﬁable or misleading. An affective route runs through
perceived betrayal, cynicism, and distrust, the emotional reactions that follow when a claim is
experienced as a violation of good faith. Both routes are well represented in the corpus, but they have
largely been studied in isolation, which has obscured their distinct behavioral signatures.
Those signatures constitute the framework's central and most novel proposition. The cognitive route,
being evaluative and risk-oriented, predominantly drives passive disengagement: lowered purchase
intention, avoidance, and brand switching. The affective route, being a felt moral violation, predominantly
drives active retaliation: negative word of mouth, complaint, and boycott. Arranging these outcomes
along a single behavioral escalation continuum, from passive disengagement to active retaliation,
reframes the ﬁeld's apparently contradictory ﬁndings as observations taken at different points on one
underlying gradient. The inconsistency of the direct greenwashing-to-purchase-intention path then
becomes intelligible: where the effect is fully carried by the organismic routes, the residual direct path is
weak or null, which is precisely the pattern observed in the studies reporting non-signiﬁcant direct
effects.
The framework also reconciles the small number of studies in which greenwashing appears to raise
rather than depress purchase propensity. These cases typically measure awareness or recognition of a
green claim rather than perceived deception, and the framework treats awareness and perceived
greenwashing as distinct stimuli: an attended green claim may attract an environmentally motivated
consumer, whereas a claim experienced as deceptive repels. Finally, the framework positions the ﬁeld's
two families of moderators at different junctions. Brand-equity resources such as loyalty, credibility, and
authenticity operate early, buffering the stimulus-to-organism link and dampening both routes, whereas
consumer-engagement resources such as product involvement and perceived value operate later,
amplifying the organism-to-response link and steepening movement along the escalation continuum.
Trust repair, in turn, is modeled as a feedback path that can reverse organismic states, but one that the
evidence base has barely examined.
These relationships can be stated as testable propositions:
Page 21/35

P1. Perceived greenwashing activates two parallel organismic routes, a cognitive route comprising
skepticism, perceived risk, and confusion, and an affective route comprising betrayal, cynicism, and
distrust.
P2. The cognitive route predominantly drives passive disengagement (reduced purchase intention,
avoidance, switching), whereas the affective route predominantly drives active retaliation (negative word
of mouth, complaint, boycott); the two are ordered along a single behavioral escalation continuum.
P3. The direct effect of greenwashing on purchase intention is weak or null to the extent that it is fully
transmitted through the cognitive and affective routes, accounting for the non-signiﬁcant direct effects
observed in part of the corpus.
P4. Greenwashing awareness and perceived greenwashing are distinct stimuli: awareness of a green
claim can raise purchase propensity, whereas perceived deception lowers it.
P5. Brand-equity resources (loyalty, credibility, authenticity) moderate the stimulus-to-organism link,
buffering both routes.
P6. Consumer-engagement resources (product involvement, perceived value) moderate the organism-to-
response link, amplifying escalation toward active retaliation.
P7. Trust repair reverses organismic states and is gated by intervention sequencing and consumers'
implicit beliefs; absent such intervention, escalation along the continuum is path-dependent.
The framework is offered as an organizing scaffold rather than a ﬁnished model. Its value is twofold: it
renders the ﬁeld's divergent direct-effect ﬁndings mutually consistent, and it speciﬁes, in P1 through P7,
the relationships whose joint estimation would move the literature from documenting that greenwashing
harms to explaining how that harm is carried and when it escalates.

4 Conclusion
This review synthesized 47 empirical studies, complemented by a bibliometric mapping of 806 records,
to clarify how greenwashing affects consumer trust and behavior. The evidence aﬃrms a convergent
core: greenwashing consistently increases skepticism, perceived risk, confusion, and perceived betrayal
while eroding trust and damaging brand equity, image, and authenticity. Its effect on behavior, however,
is predominantly indirect and conditional, because direct effects are frequently null and the damage is
instead transmitted through intervening psychological mechanisms and shaped by brand and consumer
boundary conditions. Green marketing therefore backﬁres not through a single pathway but through a
network of mediated consumer responses, an inversion of its intended effect that is the review's central
message.
4.1 Theoretical and practical implications
Page 23/35

Theoretically, the synthesis shifts the question from whether greenwashing is harmful toward how, and
under what conditions, that harm materializes. The fragmentation in how greenwashing is
conceptualized (from perceived deception, experimental manipulation, and awareness measures to its
converse in authenticity and transparency) calls for standardized measurement so that ﬁndings can be
compared across studies (Tran et al., 2025; Nie & Zhou, 2026; Nygaard & Silkoset, 2023; Rahman &
Nguyen-Viet, 2023; Diaz-Bustamante-Ventisca et al., 2025). Across the corpus, the effects of
greenwashing have been read through several complementary lenses (signaling theory, the theory of
planned behavior, the persuasion-knowledge model, cognition-affect-behavior accounts, and
information-asymmetry perspectives), which jointly support a mechanism-centered rather than a direct-
effect reading of the phenomenon (Poulis et al., 2026; Do et al., 2025; Balaskas et al., 2025; Haque &
Lang, 2025; Srisathan & Naruetharadhol, 2025). Modeling the antecedents of perceived greenwashing
further shows that communication and brand relationships shape perceived greenwashing more strongly
than they directly build a green image (Tu et al., 2024). The reaﬃrmed role of mediators carries a
methodological implication: models that test only direct effects risk concluding that no effect exists
when, in fact, it operates through intervening paths (Natasya et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2026; Dudziak et
al., 2025).
For practice, the ﬁndings underscore that credible and veriﬁable sustainability communication is the key
to avoiding the boomerang effect (Nguyen et al., 2026). Brand credibility and loyalty act as buffers, so
investing in both reduces vulnerability to greenwashing accusations (Qayyum et al., 2023; Shi & Omar,
2024). Anti-greenwashing levers (blockchain-based information, third-party certiﬁcation, green
demarketing, and authentic green reporting) demonstrably lower perceived greenwashing (Nygaard &
Silkoset, 2023; Azazz et al., 2024; Nie & Zhou, 2026), and an authentic green brand can itself become a
source of competitive advantage when it is not accompanied by exaggerated claims (Fatimah et al.,
2026; Rahman & Nguyen-Viet, 2023). Where damage has already occurred, post-greenwashing trust
repair is possible through the appropriate choice and sequencing of intervention strategies (Wang &
Walker, 2023). At the policy level, educational interventions improve consumers' ability to detect
greenwashing but risk producing false positives toward honest claims, so system-level regulation
appears more effective than individual-level intervention (Timmons et al., 2024). Finally, because
greenwashing's impact extends to internal stakeholders such as employees, its managerial stakes are
broader than consumer-facing communication alone (Mu & Lee, 2023).
4.2 Limitations and future research directions
Several limitations qualify these conclusions. The evidence base is dominated by self-reported cross-
sectional designs that measure intention rather than actual behavior, which constrains causal inference
and behavioral validity (Apaolaza et al., 2023; Shojaei et al., 2024; Margariti et al., 2024). It is also
concentrated geographically on emerging Asian markets and demographically on young consumers,
limiting cross-cultural generalizability (Di Pillo et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Balaskas & Yfantidou, 2026).
Evidence on trust repair and on stakeholder-level impacts remains very limited, resting on a single
experimental study and a small number of related works (Wang & Walker, 2023; Mu & Lee, 2023). At the
Page 24/35

level of review procedure, Web of Science was not searched owing to a lack of institutional access; this
constraint is mitigated by the use of two complementary databases (Scopus and Dimensions) with
citation tracking, an a priori protocol, and a fully traceable extraction matrix. Future research should
expand experimental and longitudinal designs, measure actual behavior rather than mere intention,
broaden the diversity of contexts and consumer groups, standardize the measurement of greenwashing,
investigate why skepticism mediation is inconsistent across markets, and, most pressingly, build a
cumulative evidence base on post-greenwashing trust repair (Liao & Wu, 2024; Adil et al., 2024; Fella &
Bausa, 2024; Wang & Walker, 2023). Addressing these gaps would consolidate a still-fragmented ﬁeld
and clarify that the success of green marketing ultimately depends on credibility, not merely on
environmental claims.
