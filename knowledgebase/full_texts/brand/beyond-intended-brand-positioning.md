# Beyond Intended Brand Positioning

SCIENCE INTERNATIONAL EMPIRICAL REVIEW
Volume 2 - Issue 1 - 2026
Beyond Intended Brand Positioning
The Role of Brand Perception in Strategic Brand Positioning
Jens Schröder, brajns establishment, Liechtenstein
Abstract
Brand positioning is a central element of strategic Brand Management. Existing research
already incorporates Brand Identity, Brand Image, market orientation, stakeholder co-
creation, Identity-Image Alignment, perceptual analysis, and Brand Repositioning. The
theoretical challenge is therefore not that perception or diagnosis is absent from
positioning. Rather, perceptual information fulfils different functions across separate
research traditions and is not consistently integrated as a distinct diagnostic domain
before future positioning is defined. This conceptual article distinguishes Current Brand
Perception, Current Perceived Brand Positioning, and Intended Future Brand
Positioning and proposes four principles for integrating perception into strategic
positioning: Diagnostic Precedence, Separation of Perception and Strategic Intention,
Multi-Stakeholder Perception, and Iterative Positioning. The article argues that the
distinctive element of Perception Brand Positioning (PBP) is neither an inside-out nor
an outside-in orientation but a perception-based and stakeholder-sensitive logic.
Internal and external stakeholder perceptions can both provide evidence. Brand
Perception provides diagnosis, while Brand Strategy provides direction. The proposed
framework establishes the theoretical rationale for PBP as a strategic approach in which
systematic diagnosis and interpretation of Current Brand Perception become an explicit
evidence base for defining Intended Future Brand Positioning.
Keywords: brand positioning, brand perception, perception brand positioning, brand
identity, brand image, brand repositioning, stakeholder perception, strategic brand
management

1. Introduction
Brand positioning is fundamentally concerned with direction. Organisations define what
a brand should represent, how it should differ from relevant alternatives, which
associations it should establish, and why it should be meaningful to selected audiences.
Positioning therefore provides strategic orientation for future Brand Management and
guides decisions concerning communication, products, services, customer experience,
organisational behaviour, and other brand-related activities.
Positioning research has developed considerably since its early focus on competitive
differentiation and the deliberate establishment of a distinctive position in consumers’

Science International Empirical Review  21

minds (Aaker and Shansby, 1982). Contemporary approaches incorporate Brand
Identity, Brand Image, customer relevance, organisational resources, market structures,
competitive context, and stakeholder expectations. Positioning should therefore not be
reduced to communication. It is a strategic decision about what a brand seeks to stand
for and how that meaning should become relevant and differentiated.
At the same time, established brands do not enter positioning processes in a
perceptual vacuum. They already carry associations, experiences, evaluations, symbolic
meanings, reputational effects, and stakeholder-specific interpretations generated
through previous communication, products, services, behaviour, social interaction, and
cultural context. Before management defines where a brand should move, the brand
already exists within perception.
This does not mean that existing positioning theory ignores the external environment.
Market-oriented positioning explicitly incorporates customers, competitors, Brand
Image, market conditions, and other external information. Urde and Koch (2014), for
example, distinguish between market-oriented and brand-oriented schools of
positioning and demonstrate that positioning can emerge through both outside-in and
inside-out logics. PBP should therefore not be characterised as a new outside-in
approach.
The distinctive element of Perception Brand Positioning lies elsewhere:
The distinctive element of Perception Brand Positioning is not an outside-in
orientation, but a perception-based orientation.
The source of perception may be internal, external, or both. Employees, managers,
customers, partners, investors, intermediaries, communities, and other relevant
stakeholders may all hold perceptions of the brand. From a PBP perspective, the key
issue is not where information originates, but whether Brand Perception is
systematically diagnosed and interpreted before future positioning is defined.
This distinction becomes more important when Brand Perception is understood
broadly as the dynamic configuration through which relevant stakeholders experience,
interpret, associate, evaluate, and make sense of a brand. Current Brand Perception can
therefore include attributes and evaluations, associations, experiential meanings, values,
motivational relevance, symbolic and identity-related meaning, and stakeholder-specific
differences.
The strategic question is consequently broader than a general customer image survey:
How does the brand currently exist in the perceptions of the stakeholder groups
that matter for its future?
This question is related to several established research streams. Identity-Image
Alignment research demonstrates that organisational intention and external perception
can diverge (de Chernatony, 1999; Roy and Banerjee, 2014). Stakeholder and co-
creation research shows that Brand Meaning develops through interaction and is not
fully authored by management (Merz et al., 2009; Gyrd-Jones and Kornum, 2013;
Sarasvuo et al., 2022; Siano et al., 2022). Recent empirical evidence also shows that
marketers' intuitive judgements about brand identity elements can diverge substantially
from consumer association data (Brus et al., 2026). Brand Repositioning research
demonstrates that diagnosis can precede strategic change and that current and desired
positions can be distinguished (Serralvo and Furrier, 2008; Godsey et al., 2020).
The contribution of PBP therefore cannot be that external perception, diagnosis, or
current-versus-desired analysis is entirely new. The theoretical issue is more specific:
these perspectives remain distributed across different traditions and do not consistently

position Current Brand Perception itself as a distinct diagnostic domain that precedes
and informs Intended Future Brand Positioning.
The present article addresses the following research question:
How can Current Brand Perception be conceptualised as a distinct diagnostic
domain within strategic Brand Positioning, and how does this role extend existing
market-oriented, alignment-based, stakeholder, and repositioning perspectives?
The article contributes by distinguishing Current Brand Perception, Current Perceived
Brand Positioning, and Intended Future Brand Positioning; by conceptualising Brand
Perception as a distinct source of strategic evidence; by proposing four principles for
integrating perception into positioning; and by developing the theoretical rationale for
Perception Brand Positioning (PBP).
2. Brand Positioning and the Role of Perception
Strategic Brand Positioning sits at the intersection of organisational intention and
market interpretation. Early work emphasised differentiation and the selection of
meaningful competitive positions (Aaker and Shansby, 1982). Later approaches
connected positioning with Brand Identity, Brand Equity, market orientation, customer
relevance, and strategic brand resources (Keller, 1993; Urde, 1999).
2.1. Inside-Out, Outside-In, and Perception-Based Logic
Urde and Koch (2014) show that positioning theory already contains both brand-
oriented and market-oriented schools. Brand-oriented approaches emphasise Brand
Identity, organisational values, and internal strategic direction, whereas market-
oriented approaches give greater weight to customers, competitors, market structures,
and Brand Image. PBP does not replace one with the other. It can incorporate both,
because its defining criterion is not source but object: the systematic diagnosis of Brand
Perception across relevant stakeholders.
This distinction separates perception-based evidence from other strategic inputs.
Brand Identity, organisational strategy, capabilities, competitive context, future
ambition, category trends, and market development remain necessary. PBP adds a
disciplined perceptual evidence layer that can include internal and external
stakeholders alike.
2.2. Brand Identity, Brand Image, and Alignment
The distinction between Brand Identity and Brand Image provides an important
theoretical foundation. Brand Identity concerns the organisation-side understanding of
what the brand represents or seeks to represent, whereas Brand Image concerns how
the brand is represented in stakeholder cognition. de Chernatony (1999) and Roy and
Banerjee (2014) show that discrepancies between internally intended meaning and
externally perceived meaning can be analysed strategically.
These approaches establish that intention and perception are not equivalent, but their
principal analytical concern is alignment between current organisational meaning and
current external perception. PBP introduces an additional future-directed question:
what should the diagnosis of Current Brand Perception imply for the strategic definition

Science International Empirical Review  23

of Intended Future Brand Positioning? Alignment may be one finding, but it is not the
sole objective.
2.3. Stakeholder Meaning and Co-Creation
Stakeholder research further challenges a simple sender-receiver model of branding.
Merz et al. (2009) describe an evolution toward a stakeholder-oriented logic in which
Brand Value emerges through interactions among multiple actors. Gyrd-Jones and
Kornum (2013) show how Brand Meaning develops in multi-stakeholder ecosystems.
More recent reviews consolidate this development: Sarasvuo et al. (2022) systematise
co-creation research across 148 branding studies, while Siano et al. (2022) describe a
broader shift from brand control toward co-creation and increasingly open stakeholder
participation.
If Brand Meaning is partly co-created, management cannot assume that intended
meanings are reproduced uniformly. Internal and external stakeholders may hold
overlapping, divergent, or contradictory perceptions. Recent work on dynamic brand
positioning makes a related point by showing how firm-led and customer-led Brand
Meaning can interact in the evolution of a brand's position (He and Zhang, 2022). PBP
therefore does not introduce dynamism, customer participation, or co-creation into
positioning per se. Its proposed contribution lies in treating multidimensional Current
Brand Perception as an explicit diagnostic evidence domain before Intended Future
Brand Positioning is defined.
2.4. Repositioning and Diagnostic Precedents
Brand Repositioning is the closest conceptual predecessor to PBP because it necessarily
addresses an existing brand state. Serralvo and Furrier (2008) identify diagnosis,
decision, and implementation as recurring components of repositioning models. Godsey
et al. (2020) distinguish between current and desired brand positions and use
perceptual analysis to examine the distance between them.
These contributions establish that diagnosis before strategic change and current-
versus-desired comparisons are not novel in themselves. PBP therefore seeks not to
invent diagnosis, but to generalise the role of Current Brand Perception beyond specific
repositioning situations and treat it as an explicit diagnostic domain within strategic
Brand Positioning more broadly.
3. From Current Brand Perception to Future Positioning
A central distinction is required between Current Brand Perception, Current Perceived
Brand Positioning, and Intended Future Brand Positioning.
3.1. Current Brand Perception
Current Brand Perception is the broader configuration through which relevant
stakeholders presently experience, interpret, associate, evaluate, and make sense of the
brand. It can contain explicit evaluations, implicit associations, value-related meanings,

motivational relevance, symbolic interpretations, experiences, and stakeholder-specific
patterns. These elements do not automatically constitute a strategic position.
3.2. Current Perceived Brand Positioning
Current Perceived Brand Positioning is a strategically condensed interpretation of the
brand’s existing relative position: what does the brand currently stand for in relation to
relevant alternatives? It is an analytical output derived from the broader perceptual
configuration, not a substitute for it.
3.3. Intended Future Brand Positioning
Intended Future Brand Positioning is the strategically desired position that the
organisation seeks to establish. It is a future-oriented decision about what the brand
should stand for. Current perception can inform this decision, but cannot determine it
because future positioning also depends on Brand Identity, organisational strategy,
capabilities, competitive context, and future ambition.
Current Brand Perception → Diagnosis → Strategic Interpretation → Current
Perceived Brand Positioning → Intended Future Brand Positioning
The logic is interpretive rather than deterministic. Existing perceptions may be
outdated, fragmented, undesirable, weakly differentiated, or inconsistent with
organisational ambition. Conversely, they may contain perceptual assets that
management has underestimated. The purpose of diagnosis is not to reproduce the
current state but to understand the perceptual reality from which strategic movement
begins.
Brand Perception provides diagnosis; Brand Strategy provides direction.
4. A Diagnostic Logic for Strategic Brand Positioning
Four principles structure the proposed perception-based positioning logic.
4.1. Diagnostic Precedence
For established brands, Current Brand Perception should be diagnosed before Intended
Future Brand Positioning is finalised. This does not claim that diagnosis before change is
new; repositioning research already demonstrates otherwise. The extension lies in
treating Current Brand Perception itself as a distinct and explicit evidence domain.
Diagnostic Precedence is therefore a sequencing principle: perceptual evidence should
be available before the future position is defined, rather than being collected only
afterwards as a performance measure.
P1: For established brands, Current Brand Perception should be systematically
diagnosed as a distinct source of strategic evidence before Intended Future Brand
Positioning is defined.

4.2. Separation of Perception and Strategic Intention
Perception and strategic intention should remain analytically distinct. Collapsing them
creates two opposite risks: inside-out blindness, in which strategy is defined without
sufficient knowledge of perceptual reality, and perception determinism, in which
existing stakeholder perceptions are treated as instructions that strategy should simply
follow. PBP avoids both by inserting Strategic Interpretation between diagnosis and
future positioning.
P2: Current Brand Perception and Intended Future Brand Positioning should be
assessed separately and connected through strategic interpretation rather than
assumed to coincide.
4.3. Multi-Stakeholder Perception
Internal and external perceptions are not alternatives but complementary evidence
sources. Employees may perceive cultural authenticity, management may emphasise
strategic ambition, customers may focus on product experience, and partners may
interpret reliability or collaboration. Such differences can reveal strengths, blind spots,
or contradictions. Stakeholder groups should therefore be analysed separately before
shared and divergent Brand Perception Patterns are interpreted.
P3: Relevant internal and external stakeholder perceptions should be diagnosed
separately before common and divergent Brand Perception Patterns are
interpreted for positioning.
4.4. Iterative Positioning
Positioning does not end when Intended Future Brand Positioning has been defined.
Once translated into communication, behaviour, products, services, and experience, the
intended position enters stakeholder interpretation. Resulting Brand Perception may
therefore differ from managerial intention. Strategic positioning should consequently be
understood as an iterative relationship between diagnosis, strategic definition,
activation, and renewed perception.
P4: Strategic Brand Positioning should be treated as an iterative relationship in
which Resulting Brand Perception becomes evidence for subsequent diagnosis and
strategic interpretation.
5. Toward Perception Brand Positioning
The preceding analysis provides the theoretical rationale for Perception Brand
Positioning. PBP can be defined as a strategic approach in which the systematic
diagnosis and interpretation of Current Brand Perception across relevant stakeholder
groups constitutes an explicit evidence base for defining Intended Future Brand
Positioning.
This definition positions PBP neither as an inside-out nor as an outside-in school. It is
perception-based and stakeholder-sensitive. Internal stakeholders matter because they
possess perceptions of the brand, not simply because they are internal. External
stakeholders matter for the same reason. The common denominator is perception.

Science International Empirical Review  26

PBP therefore differs from conventional market analysis in object rather than merely
source. Market analysis may examine category structures, market size, trends,
competitors, customer behaviour, and unmet needs. These remain strategically relevant.
Brand Perception diagnosis asks a different question: how does the brand itself
presently exist in the perceptions of relevant stakeholders?
The present article deliberately stops short of specifying a complete operational
method. Measurement, Brand Perception Gap, stakeholder alignment, Brand Perception
Core, and longitudinal Brand Perception Change require separate theoretical and
empirical development. The contribution here is narrower: to establish why Brand
Perception deserves an explicit diagnostic role within strategic Brand Positioning and to
define the conceptual logic through which that role operates.
5.1. Integrated Diagnostic Logic
The four principles can be combined into a compact cycle: Current Brand Perception →
Diagnosis → Strategic Interpretation → Current Perceived Brand Positioning → Intended
Future Brand Positioning → Activation → Resulting Brand Perception → Renewed
Diagnosis. The purpose of the sequence is not to prescribe one rigid procedure but to
make the role of perception explicit within the strategic process.
Strategic Interpretation is the critical mediation point. Perceptual evidence is assessed
together with Brand Identity, organisational strategy, capabilities, competitive context,
market development, and future ambition. This prevents two opposite errors. The first
is to ignore existing perception when defining strategy. The second is to allow existing
perception to determine strategy automatically. PBP is perception-based, but it is not
perception-determined.
5.2. Theoretical and Managerial Implications
The theoretical contribution lies partly in the temporal positioning of Brand
Perception. In branding research, Brand Perception is often treated downstream as an
outcome of branding activity or comparatively as an alignment measure. PBP
additionally assigns it an upstream role as diagnostic evidence.
This does not duplicate dynamic positioning approaches that examine how firm-led
and customer-led meanings interact over time (He and Zhang, 2022). Rather, PBP
specifies a prior diagnostic requirement: the existing perceptual configuration should be
systematically understood before strategic direction is defined. Recent evidence that
marketer judgement may not accurately reproduce consumer association structures
further strengthens the practical rationale for such diagnosis (Brus et al., 2026).
At the same time, this upstream role does not eliminate the downstream function of
Brand Perception. After strategic positioning has been translated into communication,
behaviour, products, services, and experience, the resulting Brand Perception becomes
relevant again as feedback for subsequent diagnosis and strategic interpretation. The
relationship is therefore iterative:
diagnosis → strategy → activation → resulting perception → renewed diagnosis

A second theoretical implication concerns the distinction between perceptual breadth
and strategic condensation. Current Brand Perception may contain multiple
stakeholder-specific associations, evaluations, values, motivational meanings,
experiences, and symbolic interpretations. Current Perceived Brand Positioning, by

contrast, represents a strategically condensed interpretation of the brand’s relative
position. This distinction avoids reducing rich perceptual evidence to a single score
while still enabling strategic decision-making to work with a focused positioning logic.
For managers, the framework changes the sequence more than it changes the familiar
set of strategic inputs. Instead of beginning exclusively with the question of what the
brand should stand for, established brands first diagnose how they currently exist
within relevant stakeholder perceptions. This can reveal existing perceptual assets,
entrenched constraints, differences between stakeholder groups, contradictions
between intended and perceived meaning, or perceptual patterns with strategic
potential that are not yet reflected in formal positioning.
The resulting evidence does not determine strategy. It provides a more informed
starting point for strategic interpretation. Brand Identity, organisational strategy,
capabilities, competitive context, market development, and future ambition remain
essential inputs into Intended Future Brand Positioning.

This reinforces the central principle of PBP:
Brand Perception provides diagnosis; Brand Strategy provides direction.

5.3. Limitations and Future Research
The present article is conceptual and does not prescribe a single measurement system.
Future research should test which combinations of explicit, implicit, qualitative, value-
oriented, motivational, or projective methods provide reliable evidence for Current
Brand Perception in different contexts. Stakeholder selection and weighting also require
further development because not every stakeholder group is equally relevant to every
positioning decision.
Further work should examine how Current Brand Perception relates to Desired Brand
Perception, how a Brand Perception Gap can be conceptualised without duplicating the
Identity-Image Gap, and which perceptual elements form a Brand Perception Core
across heterogeneous stakeholders. Longitudinal research should test whether intended
positioning actually produces Brand Perception Change and which dimensions change
first or remain resistant.
Finally, the boundary between PBP and established market-led, identity-led, and
repositioning approaches should be examined empirically. Comparative studies could
test whether making Brand Perception an explicit diagnostic domain changes the
quality, distinctiveness, or implementation of positioning decisions. Such evidence is
necessary before PBP can be developed from a conceptual rationale into a fully validated
strategic framework.
6. Discussion and Conclusions
The framework contributes to Brand Positioning research by repositioning Brand
Perception within the strategic process. Existing research already treats perception as
Brand Image, market input, alignment measure, co-created meaning, performance
feedback, or repositioning reference. PBP integrates these insights by making Current
Brand Perception an explicit diagnostic domain prior to future positioning.

A second contribution is the distinction between Current Brand Perception and
Current Perceived Brand Positioning. The former is broad and multidimensional; the
latter is a strategically condensed interpretation of the brand’s relative position. A third
contribution is the explicit separation of perception source from perception logic. PBP
can integrate internal and external stakeholder perspectives without being classified as
either inside-out or outside-in. Finally, the iterative logic connects upstream diagnosis
with downstream feedback.
For managers, the framework suggests a simple sequence change. Instead of
beginning exclusively with “What should the brand stand for?”, established brands
should first ask “How does the brand currently exist within relevant stakeholder
perceptions?” This does not mean that strategy should follow existing perception
mechanically. Diagnosis reduces the risk of overlooking perceptual assets, misjudging
entrenched associations, or assuming that internal conviction is shared externally. The
managerial sequence can therefore be summarised as diagnose, interpret, define,
activate, reassess.
The article is conceptual and requires empirical validation across consumer, B2B,
corporate, service, employer, public, and place-brand contexts. Future research should
compare alternative measurement approaches, examine stakeholder selection and
weighting, test the relationship between Current and Desired Brand Perception, and
investigate Brand Perception Gap, Alignment, Core, and Change. Empirical comparison
with identity-led, market-led, and repositioning approaches would further clarify the
distinct contribution of PBP.
In conclusion, strategic Brand Positioning requires direction, but direction is defined
from an existing perceptual starting point. The contribution of PBP is not the
introduction of perception, external information, or diagnosis into positioning for the
first time. It is the explicit integration of Current Brand Perception as a distinct
diagnostic domain. PBP is neither inside-out nor outside-in; it is perception-based and
stakeholder-sensitive. Brand Perception provides diagnosis, while Brand Strategy
provides direction.
