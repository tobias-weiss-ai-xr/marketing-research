Abstract
Background:Global dietary public policies frequently rely on ﬁscal penalties ("sticks")—such as sugar-
sweetened beverage (SSB) taxes—to curb sugar consumption. However, the role of regulatory "carrots,"
speciﬁcally front-of-pack (FOP) relative nutrition claims, remains undervalued as an active policy
instrument.
Objective: This perspective paper evaluates how voluntary and regulated nutrition claims act as market-
driven incentives that transform technical product reformulation into a lucrative corporate opportunity.
Methods: Utilizing a comparative case-study analysis of structural policy frameworks across global
jurisdictions (the United Kingdom, the European Union, the United States, and Latin America), we
examine the speciﬁc threshold dynamics of "Less Sugar" labeling criteria.
Results: Our analysis reveals that the statutory minimum 25% sugar-reduction threshold required to
legally bear a "Reduced Sugar" claim acts as a precise engineering target for food scientists. By dropping
sugar levels incrementally (e.g., from 10.6 g to 7.5 g per 100 mL), beverage manufacturers successfully
bypass advertising restrictions and ﬁscal penalties while preserving brand equity and consumer ﬂavor
proﬁles.
Discussion: However, an inherent paradox persists: relative sugar claims often obfuscate absolute public
health risks. A 29% sugar reduction can inadvertently generate a "health halo" effect, masking the reality
that the reformulated product remains high-glycemic and sustains a biological baseline addiction to
extreme sweetness through the substitution of high-intensity sweeteners (HIS).
Conclusion & Policy Recommendations: Regulated FOP claims serve as a vital compliance ceiling that
rewards proactive corporate participation. To optimize public health outcomes, future regulatory
frameworks must couple relative claims with absolute sugar disclosures, preventing marketing rewards
from diluting comprehensive dietary policy goals.
1. Background and Rationale
The global epidemic of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), particularly obesity and type 2 diabetes,
has forced public health authorities to position sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) as primary targets
for intervention [1]. In Nigeria, where sub-Saharan Africa's rapid urbanisation is fueling an unprecedented
surge in cardiometabolic conditions, the Federal Government has aggressively turned toward legislative
mechanisms to curb liquid sugar consumption [2–3]. Historically, legislative efforts have leaned heavily
on ﬁscal penalties—the "sticks" of dietary public policy. This includes the foundational ₦10 per litre
excise duty introduced via the Finance Act [4], which the Nigerian Senate overwhelmingly voted to
escalate into an aggressive, retail price percentage levy under the Customs, Excise Tariff Amendment
(CETA) Bill [5–6]. SSB taxes, marketing prohibitions, and mandatory warning labels are widely deployed
to force manufacturers to reduce the sugar content of their portfolios [7].
While these punitive measures dictate the minimum regulatory ﬂoor, they frequently generate intense
corporate friction and economic resistance from bodies like the Manufacturers Association of Nigeria
(MAN) [8] due to escalating production costs [6]. Concurrently, sudden recipe overhauls frequently

trigger consumer rejection across the Nigerian market when swift alterations to traditional ﬂavor proﬁles
clash with localized sensory expectations [9–10].
An alternative, highly under-explored but effective mechanism operates through the regulatory "carrot":
permissible front-of-pack (FOP) nutrient comparative claims. Under the strict vetting mandates of Codex
Guidelines for Use of Nutrition and Health Claims, speciﬁc marketing allowances are legally unlocked
when a threshold is met [11]. For example, when a carbonated soft drink brand operating in Nigeria
reformulates a ﬂagship product from 10.6 g of sugar per 100 mL down to 7.5 g per 100 mL, it achieves a
29.2% relative reduction—surpassing the statutory 25% differential required to legally display a premium
FOP "Less Sugar" ﬂash [11–12].
This paper explores the structural thesis that while ﬁscal penalties dictate the ﬂoor of dietary public
policy in developing economies, regulated FOP nutrition claims serve as a vital ceiling, leveraging
market-driven incentives to turn technical reformulation into a proﬁtable corporate opportunity in
competitive consumer markets.
2. Methodology
This perspective paper employs a qualitative, multi-jurisdictional comparative policy analysis [13]. We
evaluate the legal architectures established by major global food authorities to determine how relative
sugar reduction thresholds are calculated, rewarded, and penalized.
The analytical matrix assesses four distinct policy paradigms:
1. The United Kingdom: The High Fat, Salt, and Sugar (HFSS) advertising restrictions [14–15].
2. The European Union: The voluntary, algorithm-driven Nutri-Score matrix [16].
3. The United States: The FDA’s revised criteria for "Healthy" claims and proposed FOP disclosures
[17].
4. Latin America (Chile/Mexico): The binary, mandatory black octagon warning labels [18–19].
Products exhibiting a model ~ 30% sugar reduction (sliding beneath common regulatory tax and warning
thresholds while claiming relative superiority) were used as conceptual case studies to map corporate
behavior against regulatory frameworks [20].

4. Discussion
4.1 The Psychology of the "Carrot" vs. the "Stick"
The commercial value of a front-of-pack (FOP) nutrition content claim lies in its structural ability to
transform an operational compliance burden into an active market differentiator [26]. Within dietary
public policy, ﬁscal penalties such as sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) taxes represent a recurring
ﬁnancial liability for manufacturers. These "sticks" either directly erode corporate proﬁt margins or force
a regressive inﬂation of shelf prices, a dynamic that risks alienating highly price-sensitive demographics,
particularly in developing economies like Nigeria [27-28].
In contrast, a legally permissible FOP relative claim—such as "Reduced Sugar" or "25% Less Sugar"—
operates as an appreciating marketing asset [29]. This regulatory "carrot" appeals directly to the rapidly
expanding global demographic of "wellness-oriented" or "sub-health" consumers. These individuals
actively seek healthier alternatives but consistently reject the sensory proﬁles, lingering aftertastes, or
perceived synthetic artiﬁciality of standard "Zero Sugar" or "Diet" variants that rely entirely on high-
intensity sweeteners (HIS) [30]. Consequently, rather than suppressing consumer demand, the carrot
utilizes market competition to pull the industry toward lower sugar formulations. Figure 1 shows a
conceptual framework contrasting industry behavioral pathways under punitive legislative mandates
versus promotional marketing incentives.
4.2 The Reformulation Paradox: Relative Triumphs vs. Absolute Risks
Despite the clear policy success of leveraging commercial incentives to drive volumetric sugar reduction,
a critical public health paradox emerges within the structural mechanics of relative claims:
The Health Halo Effect: By displaying a government-sanctioned "Less Sugar" ﬂash on the primary
packaging display panel, a product generates a powerful cognitive bias known as the "health halo"
[31]. Consumers routinely interpret relative improvements as indicators of absolute healthfulness,
misperceiving a "less unhealthy" beverage as inherently wholesome [32]. This cognitive distortion
can inadvertently trigger overconsumption via self-licensing behaviors, where an individual
consumes multiple servings of the modiﬁed product, ultimately matching or exceeding the absolute
sugar intake they would have sustained from a single standard beverage [33].
The Absolute Sugar Reality: The mathematical reality of a relative reduction often obscures a
deeply high-glycemic baseline. A carbonated soft drink variant engineered to drop from 10.6 g to 7.5
g of sugar per 100 mL achieves a statistically compliant 29.2% relative reduction, yet it remains
fundamentally dense in free sugars. A standard 330 mL single-serve can of this reformulated
product yields an absolute value of 24.75 grams of sugar. According to World Health Organization
(WHO) dietary guidelines, this single portion fulﬁlls—and virtually exhausts—the ideal conditional
recommendation limiting free sugar intake to 25 grams per day for an adult [34-36].
Sweetness Baseline Maintenance: To functionally compensate for the lost mouthfeel, viscosity, and
sensory satisfaction caused by removing 2.9 grams of sugar per 100 mL, manufacturers routinely

deploy advanced taste modulators and natural high-intensity sweeteners like stevia extracts
(rebaudiosides) [37]. While this successfully satisﬁes the chemical and ﬁscal parameters of sugar
reduction, it keeps the consumer’s biological palate ﬁrmly habituated to an artiﬁcially elevated
sweetness baseline [38]. This physiological habituation continuously sabotages broader public
health interventions aimed at down-regulating the population-wide sweet-taste preference, making it
progressively harder for individuals to enjoy the sensory proﬁles of unsweetened, whole foods [42].
5. Conclusions and Policy Recommendations
Regulated front-of-pack nutrition claims are highly eﬃcient policy instruments. They successfully
leverage corporate self-interest and competitive market dynamics to drive systemic, volumetric sugar
reduction across beverage portfolios. However, because these claims operate on relative rather than
absolute metrics, they risk institutionalizing high-sugar baselines under a state-approved health halo.
The carrot is necessary to foster proactive industry participation, but it must be calibrated precisely to
prevent the dilution of absolute public health goals.
To preserve the market-driven power of the "carrot" while neutralizing its deceptive health halo, global
food and health authorities should adopt the following structural policies:
Establish Absolute Ceilings for Relative Claims: Prohibit the use of any relative sugar reduction
claims (e.g., "Less Sugar") if the ﬁnal product's absolute sugar content remains above a designated
public health threshold, such as 5 g of total sugar per 100 mL.
Implement Mandatory Dual-Disclosure Front-of-Pack Labeling: Mandate that any voluntary relative
claim on the front of a package must be accompanied by an equally prominent, absolute metric
detailing the percentage of the WHO daily sugar limit contained within a single serving.
Adopt the Latin American Dual-Label Restriction Model: Legislate an absolute barrier specifying
that if a beverage triggers a mandatory "High Sugar" warning marker, it is strictly barred from
utilizing any positive relative nutritional assertions or wellness claims elsewhere on the packaging.
