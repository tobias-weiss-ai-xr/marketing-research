ABSTRACT

This study examines the associations of brand image, trust, and customer satisfaction with customer loyalty
among 96 Shopee -using students from the Faculty of Economics, Universitas Asahan, North Sumatra.
Respondents were recruited through accidental sampl ing. An a priori power analysis for multiple
regression with three predictors (α = .05, power = .80, f² = .15) indicated a minimum sample of 77. In the
original composite -score model, the predictors jointly explained 59.2% of loyalty variance, F(3, 92) =
44.436, p < .001; satisfaction was strongest (β = .576, p < .001), trust was positive (β = .199, p = .017),
and brand image was not statistically significant (β = .120, p = .134). Because satisfaction and loyalty
shared continued-use and advocacy content, respondent-level item scores from the thesis appendix were
reanalysed. With satisfaction restricted to expectation confirmation (P8) and loyalty to primary choice plus
retention (P11 + P12; α = .722), R² fell to .349. Satisfaction remained positive (β = .28 7, HC3 p = .019),
trust remained positive (β = .272, HC3 p = .010), and brand image remained non -significant (β = .180,
HC3 p = .167). The attenuation confirms that construct overlap inflated the original satisfaction –loyalty
estimate. Conclusions are restricted to this single-faculty convenience sample, and multi-homing remains
an unmeasured theoretical possibility.

1. INTRODUCTION
Indonesia’s e-commerce activity has expanded markedly. Bank Indonesia reports that national e -
commerce transaction value increased from about Rp205.5 trillion in 2019 to about Rp487 trillion in 2024
(Bank Indonesia, 2025 ). The Indonesian Internet Service Providers Association’s 2025 national survey
likewise identifies Shopee as the online-shopping platform most frequently accessed by respondents, with
53.22% reporting it as their most frequently accessed platform ( Asosiasi Penyelenggara Jasa Internet
Indonesia [APJII], 2025). These national indicators motivate the platform context; they do not imply that
the student sample analysed here represents Indonesian e-commerce consumers.
Marketplace applications generally impose low formal switching barriers: they are free to install,
do not require exclusivity, and allow users to compare alternatives quickly. These features make multi -
homing a plausible theoretical feature of online shopping. However, this study did not measure the number
of marketplace applications used, switching frequency, transaction shares across competing applications,
or relative brand image. Multi -homing is therefore treated only as a possible explanation to be tes ted in
future research, not as an observed mechanism in the present data.
This setting raises a narrower question for customer -based brand equity theory. The theory
proposes that strong, favourable, and unique brand associations can generate differential consumer
responses ( Keller, 1993 ), and prior work links brand image with loyalty ( Aaker, 1991 ; Chaudhuri &
Holbrook, 2001 ; Pappu & Quester, 2016 ). Indonesian studies also report positive brand -image
associations in coffee-chain and fixed-broadband contexts (Ramli et al., 2023; Suhandy & Widoatmodjo,
2022). Yet online marketplaces may differ because switching barriers can be low and transaction
experience may be repeatedly updated. Whether those features actually attenuate brand-image effects is an
empirical proposition rather than an assumption of this study.
Several theoretical considerations therefore suggest why a brand-image coefficient might become
smaller once trust and satisfaction are controlled. First, Keller’s (1993) framework requires distinctive
associations, so any convergence of associations across platforms would reduce discriminatory power.
Second, signalling theory implies that widely imitable cues may convey less information than costly or
difficult-to-copy cu es ( Connelly et al., 2011 ; Spence, 1973 ). Third, online -loyalty research emphasises
transaction experience, switching conditions, and purchase frequency as potentially important for
continued patronage (Reichheld & Schefter, 2000; Srinivasan et al., 2002; Watson et al., 2015). The present
survey does not directly measure these proposed mechanisms.
This study estimates the simultaneous associations of brand image, trust, and customer satisfaction
with loyalty among 96 Shopee-using students at the Faculty of Economics, Universitas Asahan, in Kisaran,
North Sumatra. Its purpose is to test whether brand  image retains a detectable partial association when
trust and satisfaction are included in the same regression. The study is intentionally framed as a local,
exploratory analysis. A non -significant brand -image coefficient is not treated as proof of a gene ral
boundary condition, and conclusions are not extended beyond the sampled population.

2. LITERATURE REVIEW AND HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT

2.1. Theoretical Framework
The primary lens is expectancy–disconfirmation theory. Oliver (1980) modelled satisfaction as the
outcome of comparing perceived performance against prior expectation: performance above expectation
yields positive disconfirmation, performance below it the reverse. Oliver (1999)  then distinguished
satisfaction from loyalty proper, defining loyalty as a deeply held commitment to rebuy a preferred offering
consistently despite situational influences and marketing efforts with the potential to cause switching. The
definition is exacting: it requires not merely repeat purchase but fortitude, the willingness to persist when a
competitor makes an attractive offer, and satisfaction is necessary but not sufficient for it.
The supporting lens is commitment –trust theory, in which relationship marketing succeeds
through the joint mediation of commitment and trust, trust being confidence in an exchange partner’s
reliability and integrity (Morgan & Hunt, 1994). Online marketplace transactions are the paradigm case of

exchange carrying unmonitorable risk: the buyer pays before inspecting, relies on a seller the platform vets
but does not employ, and depends on platform escrow and dispute resolution if the transaction fails
(Gefen et al., 2003; McKnight et al., 2002). Tr ust here is therefore substantially institutional rather than
interpersonal — confidence in structural safeguards — which matters when these results are compared
with findings from offline and regulated settings.
Customer-based brand equity provides the focal brand lens: brand knowledge can generate
differential consumer response when brand associations are favourable, strong, and distinctive ( Keller,
1993). Aaker’s (1991) related account likewise explains how associations can create value. The present
study uses these frameworks to motivate a test of brand image as a predictor; it does not presume in
advance that the marketplace setting constitutes a boundary condition for either theory (See Figure 1).
2.2. Brand Image and Customer Loyalty
The conventional prediction is straightforward: favourable associations can reduce perceived risk,
simplify choice under information overload, and confer expressive value, which may support repeat
patronage ( Aaker, 1991; Keller, 1993). Brand innovativeness has also been linked with loyalty through
perceived quality and self-image congruence (Pappu & Quester, 2016). Indonesian studies report positive
brand-image associations in fixed -broadband and coffee -chain contexts ( Ramli et al., 2023 ; Suhandy &
Widoatmodjo, 2022).
A plausible reason for attenuation in marketplace settings is that loyalty may depend on relative
rather than absolute attitude. Dick and Basu (1994)  argued that loyalty reflects the favourability of the
focal brand relative to salient alternatives. Conventional brand-image scales, including the one used here,
measure absolute perceptions. If users also hold favourable images of competing platforms, an  absolute
measure may have limited power to distinguish which platform receives the next transaction. This
possibility is theoretically relevant, but relative brand image and use of competing applications were not
measured in the present study.
The e -commerce loyalty literature offers related reasons for caution. Srinivasan et al. (2002)
emphasised site-experience antecedents of e-loyalty, Reichheld and Schefter (2000) highlighted fulfilment
and low switching friction, and Watson et al. (2015) showed that loyalty antecedents vary with switching
costs and purchase frequency. These studies support testing whether brand image retains a unique
association after experiential constructs enter the model. They do not establish that multi -homing is the
mechanism in this sample. Accordingly, H1 is retained as a conventional directional hypothesis rather than
as a test of a pre-specified boundary condition.
H1. Brand image is positively associated with customer loyalty.

2.3. Trust and Customer Loyalty
Commitment–trust theory predicts that trust sustains relationships by reducing the perceived risk
of continued exchange ( Morgan & Hunt, 1994 ). Mayer et al. (1995)  decomposed trustworthiness into
ability, benevolence, and integrity, adding willingness to be vulnerable as its behavioural expression — the
four facets the present measure uses. In electronic commerce these attach to platform mechanisms:
institution-based trust, comprising structural assurance and situational normality, is a distinct antecedent
of trusting intention online ( McKnight et al., 2002 ); trust shapes purchase intention partly through
perceived usefulness (Gefen et al., 2003); and brand trust translates into purchase and attitudinal loyalty
(Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001).
Trust is expected to show a positive association with loyalty, although its magnitude may vary
across contexts. Online trust can depend on platform safeguards, seller behaviour, and perceived reliability
(Gefen et al., 2003; McKnight et al., 2002). Prior Indonesian findings are mixed across settings (Febrianto
& Arini, 2022; Marlius & Wulandari, 2025; Sofian & Handoyo, 2025). The present model therefore tests
the trust–loyalty association without assuming a specific threshold process.
H2. Trust is positively associated with customer loyalty.

2.4. Customer Satisfaction and Customer Loyalty
Expectancy–disconfirmation theory makes satisfaction the proximate evaluative input to
repurchase ( Oliver, 1980 ). Cumulative satisfaction raises repurchase intention and reduces demand
elasticity (Anderson & Sullivan, 1993); it mediates between quality perceptions and loyalty in national index
models (Fornell et al., 1996); it raises willingness to pay along a non-linear function steepest in the middle
range (Homburg et al., 2005 ); and the behavioural consequences of service quality operate substantially
through judgements of this kind (Zeithaml et al., 1996).
Satisfaction is expected to relate positively to loyalty because repeated confirmation of expectations
can support continued patronage (Anderson & Sullivan, 1993; Oliver, 1980). However, the administered
satisfaction scale contains repurchase intention and willingness to recommend, while the loyalty scale also
contains repeat purchase and referral. This content overlap creates a measurement problem: the estimated
satisfaction–loyalty coefficient may partly reflect duplicated behavioural -intention content rather than a
clean association between distinct constructs. H3 is therefore tested in the original model for transparency,
but its magnitude must be interpreted provisionally and checked with non-overlapping indicators.
H3. Customer satisfaction is positively associated with customer loyalty.
2.5. Joint Effect
Brand image, trust, satisfaction, and loyalty are theoretically distinct, but the administered
satisfaction and loyalty indicators are not fully distinct empirically. The original composite-score model is
retained to reproduce the study as conducted. A red uced-overlap sensitivity specification is also defined
below so that the satisfaction –loyalty relationship can be re -estimated after removing duplicated
repurchase and referral content.
H4. Brand image, trust, and customer satisfaction jointly account for a significant share of variance in
customer loyalty.

3. METHOD

3.1. Research Design and Setting
The study used a cross-sectional, explanatory survey design with self-administered questionnaires
on a single occasion. The setting was the Faculty of Economics of Universitas Asahan in Kisaran, a
secondary regional city rather than a metropolitan centre — a distinction that matters because Indonesian
consumer markets vary considerably across such settings in logistics coverage, delivery times, and retail
alternatives (Kuncoro & Resosudarmo, 2023). Fieldwork was conducted between January and April 2026.

3.2. Population, Sample, and Sampling Procedure
The population comprised Shopee users among students of the faculty; its size was unknown,
since platform use is not recorded in enrolment data. Sampling was accidental — convenience sampling
in which any member of the population encountered by the researc her and judged suitable is enrolled
(Sugiyono, 2019). Respondents had to be at least 17 years of age, current Shopee users, and to have
completed more than one purchase. The last criterion secures the transaction history the satisfaction and
loyalty constructs depend on, but also restricts the sample to us ers of the focal platform, a restriction
addressed in the discussion.
Sample adequacy was evaluated for the planned multiple -regression model rather than with a
formula for estimating a population proportion. An a priori power analysis for three predictors, α = .05,
target power = .80, and a medium effect size of f² = .15 indicated a minimum total sample of 77 (Cohen,
1988; Lakens, 2022). The realised sample of 96 exceeds this benchmark and provides approximately .89
power for f² = .15. It does not provide high power for small incremental effects; for example, detecting f²
= .02 with the same specifications would require approximately 550 observations.
Power analysis addresses statistical detectability, not representativeness. Because respondents were
recruited through accidental sampling from one faculty, the inferential population is restricted to Shopee-
using students of the Faculty of Economics, Universitas Asahan who met the eligibility criteria. The sample
does not justify generalisation to Indonesian marketplace consumers or to Indonesian university students
generally.
Questionnaires were administered through Google Forms and distributed via WhatsApp and
Instagram; participation was voluntary and responses anonymous.

3.3. Measures
All constructs were measured with closed -ended items scored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5
(strongly agree). Brand image used three items on feature completeness relative to competitors, ease of
recalling Shopee for online shopping, and the recognisability/distinctiveness of Shopee’s appearance and
concept. Trust used four items on Shopee’s effort to provide its best service, transaction safety and
convenience, honesty and transparency of product information, and respondents’ feeling of safety when
transacting through Shopee. The original satisfaction scale comprised expectation confirmation (P8),
continued-use intention following satisfaction (P9), and willingness to recommend (P10). The original
loyalty scale comprised Shopee as the respondent’s primary o nline-shopping choice (P11), continued use
despite competing marketplaces offering similar products (P12), and positive word -of-mouth about
Shopee shopping experiences (P13). P9 and P10 therefore overlap conceptually with loyalty continuity
and advocacy. A ppendix A reproduces the complete administered questionnaire wording and maps the
sensitivity treatment (See Table 1).

3.4. Analytical Procedure
Hypotheses were tested by ordinary least squares regression in a single model:
CLᵢ = β₀ + β₁BIᵢ + β₂TRᵢ + β₃CSᵢ + εᵢ
where CL is the original three-item customer-loyalty score, BI brand image, TR trust, and CS the
original three-item satisfaction score for respondent i. Partial associations (H1 –H3) were evaluated with
two-tailed t tests and the joint model (H4) with the F statistic. Standardised coefficients are reported to
aid comparison, but they are not treated as causal importance weights because predictors differ in reliability
and the satisfaction and loyalty composites share item content (Hair et al., 2019). Exact p values and 95%
confidence intervals are emphasised (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016).
Assumption checks comprised residual normality, multicollinearity, and error-variance diagnostics.
Tolerance and variance inflation factors were evaluated against conventional thresholds ( Ghozali, 2021).
HC3 heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors were used as a robustness check because visual residual
diagnostics alone are informal ( White, 1980 ). The original composite -score model is reported for
reproducibility. The reduced-overlap sensitivity model was then estimated from the respondent-level thesis
data using P8 as satisfaction and P11 + P12 as loyalty; P11 and P12 were also modelled separate ly to
examine the primary-choice and retention facets.

3.5. Common Method Variance
The design is single -source, single-occasion, and entirely self -report, which is the configuration
under which common method variance is most likely to distort observed relationships ( Podsakoff et al.,
2003). Procedural remedies present in the design were limited but not absent: participation was anonymous
and voluntary, which reduces evaluation apprehension and socially desirable responding; the questionnaire
was self-administered without an interviewer pre sent; and predictor and criterion items were separated
into labelled construct blocks. Remedies that were not implemented include temporal, proximal, or
methodological separation of predictor and criterion measurement, and the inclusion of a marker variable
or negatively worded items permitting a statistical estimate of shared method variance.
Common method bias cannot be assigned a known direction in this design. Shared response
format and same-occasion self-report may inflate some correlations, while other forms of method variance
may attenuate them (Podsakoff et al., 2024). Accordingly, neither the significant coefficients nor the brand-
image null is treated as protected from common -method concerns. Procedural anonymity helps reduce
evaluation apprehension, but future research should add temporal or source separation, marker variables,
or behavioural loyalty data.

4. RESULTS

4.1. Respondent Profile
The realised sample comprised 96 Shopee -using students from the Faculty of Economics,
Universitas Asahan. Seventy-five respondents were female (78.1%) and 21 were male (21.9%). Almost all
were aged 21 –25 (93 respondents, 96.9%); three were aged 17 –20 (3.1% ). The sample is therefore
demographically narrow and institutionally local. These characteristics are descriptive of the realised
sample only and are not used to infer the profile of Indonesian marketplace users.

(r = .482, p < .001). Its smaller partial coefficient indicates that much of this association is shared
statistically with trust and satisfaction. Importantly , brand image also remains non -significant in the
reduced-overlap model, although its point estimate increases somewhat; thus the null is not solely an
artefact of the duplicated satisfaction–loyalty items.

4.4. Robustness, Measurement Sensitivity, and the Brand-Image Null
Standard regression diagnostics do not materially change the original inference. Residual normality
was supported by the Kolmogorov –Smirnov test (D = 0.088, p = .064), VIF values were low, and HC3
robust standard errors yielded p = .232 for brand image, p = .025 for trust, and p < .001 for satisfaction.
These checks address error structure and collinearity, not construct validity.
Power also limits the interpretation of non -significance. The a priori analysis shows that n = 96
provides adequate power for a medium overall regression effect but not for small incremental effects. The

5. DISCUSSION

5.1. The Brand Image Null
Brand image was not statistically significant after trust and the original satisfaction composite were
included, and it remained non-significant in the reduced-overlap model (β = .180, HC3 p = .167). This is
a conditional, sample-specific null rather than evidence that brand image has no role in marketplace loyalty.

Priviet Social Sciences Journal
682
Volume 6, Issue 7, available at https://journal.privietlab.org/index.php/PSSJ
The positive zero -order correlations show that favourable brand perceptions and loyalty co -vary; the
multivariable models indicate only that an independent brand -image association was not detected at the
conventional significance level once the other predictors were included.
Multi-homing offers one theoretical explanation for this attenuation, but it was not measured. The
questionnaire did not record how many competing applications respondents used, how often they
switched, how transactions were allocated across platforms, or how Shopee’s image compared with the
next-best alternative. Consequently, low switching barriers, relative attitude, and multi -homing should be
treated as hypotheses for future research rather than as an established boundary condition in these data
(Dick & Basu, 1994; Watson et al., 2015).
Other explanations remain viable. Brand -image scores were compressed toward the favourable
end, which can restrict observable covariance, and the study has limited power for small partial effects.
Measurement overlap was another plausible explanation, but the reanalysis shows that removing the
duplicated continued-use and advocacy content does not make brand image statistically significant. Its
reduced-model confidence interval still includes zero and a modest positive association, so the evidence
remains inconclusive rather than negative.
The difference from positive brand-image findings in other Indonesian settings (Ramli et al., 2023;
Suhandy & Widoatmodjo, 2022) is therefore best read as a context-dependent empirical discrepancy rather
than proof that marketplace architecture overturns customer -based brand equity. Future studies should
directly measure multi-homing intensity, switching frequency, competing applications, and relative brand
image and test their moderating roles.

5.2. Trust
Trust showed a positive association with loyalty in both specifications: β = .199 in the original
model (p = .017; HC3 p = .025) and β = .272 in the reduced -overlap model (HC3 p = .010). This is
consistent with commitment–trust theory, which links confidence in an exchange partner’s reliability and
integrity with relationship continuity (Morgan & Hunt, 1994), and with online-trust research (Gefen et al.,
2003; McKnight et al., 2002 ). The facet checks sharpen this pattern: trust predicted the
retention/switching-resistance item P12 (β = .356, HC3 p = .001) but not the primary-choice item P11 (β
= .120, HC3 p = .365). Because each facet is represented by a single self-report item, these differences are
exploratory rather than evidence of stable subscales.
The honesty and transparency item had the lowest trust mean (M = 3.938), suggesting a possible
diagnostic area for future platform research. The present data do not establish that trust operates as a
threshold or that platform safeguards are equivalent across competitors; those explanations require direct
comparative measurement.

5.3. Customer Satisfaction
Customer satisfaction had the largest coefficient in the original composite-score model (β = .576,
p < .001), consistent in direction with expectancy –disconfirmation theory and prior research linking
satisfaction with continued patronage (Anderson & Sullivan, 1993; Oliver, 1980; Fornell et al., 1996). The
reduced-overlap reanalysis cuts that coefficient approximately in half (β = .287, HC3 p = .019) and reduces
R² from .592 to .349. Thus, the reviewer’s concern is empirically consequential: duplicated continued-use
and advocacy content inflated the original association. At the same time, expectation confirmation retains
a positive relationship with the cleaner two -item loyalty score, so the result is not entirely an artefact of
overlap.
The facet models further indicate that loyalty is not well represented as a single homogeneous
outcome. Expectation confirmation predicted the primary-choice item P11 (β = .287, HC3 p = .009) but
not the retention item P12 at α = .05 (β = .222, HC3 p = .102), whereas trust showed the reverse pattern.
P11 should be understood only as a behavioural -intention or choice proxy, not observed repeat
purchasing; P12 reflects attitudinal retention or switching resistance. Positive word-of-mouth (P13) is best
treated as an advocacy outcome rather than merged automatically with these facets.

5.4. Theoretical Implications
The theoretical implication is a testable proposition rather than an established boundary condition.
Customer-based brand equity suggests that distinctive associations matter for differential response (Keller,
1993). In platform settings where consumers use several applications and perceive little relative image
differentiation, the incremental brand-image–loyalty association may be weaker after transaction -specific
evaluations are controlled. The fact that brand image remains non-significant after the overlap correction
is compatible with that possibility but does not test it because multi -homing intensity and relative brand
image were not measured.
The stronger contribution is methodological. The reanalysis demonstrates that satisfaction and
loyalty should not be operationalised with duplicated continued -use and advocacy indicators: removing
those overlaps reduced the satisfaction β from .576 to .287  and reduced model R² by 24.3 percentage
points. The facet checks also show different predictor patterns for primary choice and retention,
reinforcing the need to distinguish behavioural-intention, attitudinal commitment, and advocacy outcomes
rather than forcing them into one loyalty score.
Finally, a non -significant coefficient should be interpreted through its confidence interval,
measurement quality, and statistical power rather than converted into evidence of absence (Wasserstein &
Lazar, 2016). This study therefore treats the brand-image result as a local empirical finding that motivates
a more direct future test.

5.5. Practical Implications
The cross-sectional regression does not compare spending effectiveness, causal returns, or the
return on investment from brand versus operational expenditure. It therefore does not support a
recommendation to shift budgets from brand building to fulfilment or other operational activities. For the
sampled students, the positive associations of trust and the original satisfaction composite with loyalty
suggest that platform managers may continue monitoring transaction reliability, information transparency,
and expectation fulfilment alongside brand metrics.
Brand investment should likewise not be judged ineffective from a non -significant partial
coefficient. Brand image may operate earlier in the customer journey, influence platform consideration, or
have small effects that this study cannot detect. A credibl e allocation decision would require longitudinal
or experimental evidence linking specific investments to behavioural outcomes and costs.
For campus -oriented research and practice, a more defensible next step is measurement: track
actual repeat transactions, platform switching, share of orders across competing applications, and relative
brand evaluations. Those data would allow the proposed multi-homing explanation and any managerial
trade-offs to be tested rather than assumed.

5.6. Limitations and Future Research
Measurement remains the principal limitation. Trust reliability is marginal (α = .677). The original
satisfaction and loyalty scales were below .70 and overlapped conceptually; the reduced analysis improves
separation but necessarily uses a single expectation-confirmation item for satisfaction and only two loyalty
items (α = .722). The separate primary-choice and retention models are therefore single-item facet checks,
not fully validated behavioural and attitudinal loyalty scales. Future work should use lo nger validated
satisfaction scales, observed repeat -purchase or transaction -share measures for behavioural loyalty, and
multi-item commitment/switching-resistance scales for attitudinal loyalty (McNeish, 2018).
Sampling also limits generalisability. Accidental sampling of 96 Shopee -using students from one
faculty at Universitas Asahan does not represent Indonesian marketplace consumers, Indonesian students,
or Shopee users nationally. The conclusions therefore apply only to the sampled student group and require
replication across faculties, universities, regions, age groups, and platforms.
The design is cross-sectional, single-source, and self-report. It cannot establish causal direction or
returns to managerial investment, and common method variance remains a concern ( Podsakoff et al.,
2003, 2024). Behavioural data such as verified order histories, transaction shares, or supervisor -
independent platform logs would provide stronger loyalty measures than intentions alone.

Several theoretically important variables were omitted. Multi -homing intensity, switching
frequency, use of competing applications, relative brand image, promotion exposure, price sensitivity,
service quality, delivery performance, habit, and prior experie nce were not measured. In particular, the
proposed multi-homing explanation should be tested directly as a moderator rather than inferred from the
non-significant brand-image coefficient.
Finally, the realised sample is adequately powered for a medium overall regression effect but not
for small incremental effects. Larger, probability -based or stratified samples would provide tighter
confidence intervals and permit reliable tests of moderation and multidimensional loyalty.

5.8. Reduced-Overlap Sensitivity Specification
Reduced-overlap sensitivity specification and result. Satisfaction was rescored as P8 only
(expectation confirmation), and loyalty was rescored as P11 + P12 after removing the positive -word-of-
mouth item P13. The two-item loyalty score had α = .722. In the re-estimated model, R² = .349 (adjusted
R² = .328), F(3, 92) = 16.465, p < .001. HC3 inference gave brand image β = .180, p = .167; trust β =
.272, p = .010; and satisfaction β = .287, p = .019. Separate facet checks found that satisfaction predicted
P11 primary choice (β = .287, p = .009), whereas trust predicted P12 retention (β = .356, p = .001). These
facet results are exploratory because each outcome is a single item.

6. CONCLUSION

This study examined brand image, trust, and customer satisfaction as correlates of customer loyalty
among 96 Shopee -using students from the Faculty of Economics, Universitas Asahan. In the original
composite-score model, the predictors jointly explained 59 .2% of loyalty variance; trust and satisfaction
were positive, while brand image was not statistically significant after the other predictors were controlled.
Reanalysis of the thesis item-level data after removing direct overlap reduced explained variance to 34.9%.
Satisfaction remained positive (β = .287, HC3 p = .019), trust remained positive (β = .272, HC3 p = .010),
and brand image remained non-significant (β = .180, HC3 p = .167).
The reanalysis materially qualifies the original satisfaction result. Its standardised coefficient falls
from .576 to .287 after the continued -use and advocacy overlaps are removed, showing that part of the
original relationship was measurement -induced. Th e facet checks also suggest different antecedent
patterns for primary choice and retention. Separately, the brand -image null does not establish multi -
homing, switching, or image convergence as mechanisms because those constructs were not measured; it
indicates only that an independent brand-image association was not detected in these local models.
Accordingly, the contribution is exploratory and sample -specific. Future research should use
conceptually distinct and more reliable satisfaction measures, distinguish observed behavioural loyalty
from attitudinal commitment and advocacy, directly measure multi-homing and relative brand image, and
replicate the analysis with broader probability-based samples and behavioural outcomes.
