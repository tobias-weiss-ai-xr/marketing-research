ABSTRACT
Value creation is a core function in the marketing management process, and the relationship between
marketing and brand equity can be found in literature. Yet, the effects of value creation on brand equity
have not been sufficiently addressed. Scholars suggest that another critical dimension should be added as a
predictor of brand equity, which is ambidextrous leadership, as it supports marketing strategy
implementation and value creation. The purpose of this study is to investigate the relationship between
value creation, marketing strategy, and ambidextrous leadership, and how these factors affect brand equity.
The literature review focused on theories, definitions, concepts, and dimensions of the variables under
study. A quantitative methodology was chosen wherein quantitative data were collected and analyzed using
statistical tools and interpreted to test the 4 hypotheses. The measurement and conceptual model to depict
the hypothesized relationships were evaluated based on 150 hospital managers in Indonesia, using a cross-
sectional survey design. Through empirical analysis, the study showed the significant effects of value
creation (VC) and marketing strategy (MS) on brand equity as moderated by ambidextrous leadership (AL).
The proposed model represents a  valuable contribution to the subject of brand equity. The findings from
the study show that value creation is a well -integrated and strategic component of healthcare providers.
Healthcare provider managers demonstrate high levels of adoption of marketing strategies. Furthermore,
healthcare provider managers view their institutions as having stronger brand equity in terms of quality
perception and awareness. Overall, the healthcare providers' brand equity has a significant collective effect
on their value creation efforts.
Keywords: Brand Equity, Value Creation, Ambidextrous Leadership, Marketing Strategy

INTRODUCTION
Brand equity has been recognized by scholars as an intangible asset that contributes
to organizational success. However, studies on brand equity have mostly focused on
brands that produce tangible goods. There is a dearth of literature discussing brand equity
in the context of services.
As the economy transitions to a service economy, it is important to understand how
the concepts of brand identity, value creation, and leadership play their roles in an
organization to achieve competitive advantage.

This research aims to address this gap and attempt to provide seminal knowledge
on the perception of brand equity among the management of private hospital
organizations.
The landscape of modern business management is increasingly determined by
intangible assets, with brand equity emerging as a critical factor in sustaining competitive
advantage. Brand equity is currently considered to be a key driver for achieving
competitive advantage (Rua & Santos, 2022). The higher the brand equity of an
organization, the higher the brand preference in the consumer's mind, thus leading to
higher market share and financial performance.
Traditional brand equity models proposed by Aaker and Keller define brand equity
as the value that a brand adds to a firm based on the perception of brand association, brand
loyalty, perceived quality, and brand awareness.
The study of brand equity in different types of organizations has gained momentum
recently. These studies, however, have mainly focused on products and merchandise
sectors, with fewer empirical studies conducted at the healthcare organizational level. On
one hand, scholars posit that there is incoherence in the conceptualization of brand equity
itself in the service sector. Some scholars have also argued that the brand equity model
for tangible products should not be applied to service products.
Further, despite the considerable body of knowledge on brand equity, there remains
a notable dearth of literature exploring how value creation, which is closely tied to
marketing functions, directly influences brand equity, which is a vital asset of any
organization.
Value creation has been understood as the process of enhancing the worth of a firm's
market offer with the goal of shaping customer perception and driving loyalty. However,
the explicit mechanism of how value creation affects brand equity is underexplored in the
literature. This is a significant gap to address, considering that value creation forms the
foundation upon which strong brands are built.
Adding to this complexity is the role of marketing strategy in shaping brand equity.
Previous studies have shown how marketing strategies positively influence brand equity
(Keller, 2013). Yet, investigation into the relationship between how marketing strat egy
affects brand equity in the context of healthcare remains limited. The purpose of this study
is to measure brand equity, value creation, and marketing strategy among managers of
Adventist hospitals in Indonesia and determine what type of strategic approach would be
recommended.
Kim et al. pioneered hospital brand equity (HBE) research by showing that trust,
satisfaction, relationship commitment, loyalty, and awareness —developed through
CRM—positively shape hospital image and brand equity; its strengths are a clear service-
specific HBE path model and strong managerial implications. Yet it is dated, context -
bound (Korean hospitals with strict advertising limits), cross -sectional, and largely
patient/relationship-marketing centric. It does not unpack how internal levers —brand
identity, leadership, and value -creation processes —cohere to build equity from the
management side or in private hospital systems in emerging markets.

Chahal and Bala (2012) advance the service HBE literature by evidencing that
perceived quality and loyalty drive brand equity in healthcare, with brand image acting
indirectly; this contributes a clean construct structure and usable measures. However, the
study relies on convenience sampling in one Indian city, keeps the lens on patient
perceptions, and does not theorize how marketing strategy and value creation inside the
organization translate into equity —precisely where many private hospitals struggle to
architect differentiation.
This research aims to (i) measure brand equity, value creation, and marketing
strategy as perceived by managers of Adventist hospitals in Indonesia; (ii) test how brand
equity, value-creation practices, and strategic marketing jointly predict brand equity in a
services (hospital) setting; and (iii) derive a recommended strategic approach for
strengthening competitive advantage. The expected benefits include a management -side
diagnostic of brand equity drivers tailored to private hospitals, actionable levers  for
aligning leadership, value creation, and marketing investments, and a service -specific
framework that extends product -oriented equity models to healthcare while offering
administrators evidence-based priorities for building preference, loyalty, and fi nancial
performance.

RESEARCH METHODS
This chapter presents the research methods used in this study. Included herein are
the methodological choice, research design, population, sampling procedures, research
instrument, validity test, reliability test, rubric (rating scale), ethical considerati ons, and
statistical tests.
The methodological choice in this study is quantitative. This aligns with the
objective of the study, which is to improve existing theories by providing empirical
evidence and facilitating the testing of hypotheses (Saunders et al., 2011). A quantitative
approach measures variables and tests relationships between them to reveal correlations
or causal links (Blanchflower, 2018). In this study, quantitative data was collected,
analyzed using statistical tools, and interpreted to test the individual hypotheses.
The design of this study is a cross -sectional survey design, which, according to
Bryman (2012), assesses the prevalence of certain characteristics, behaviors, or opinions
within a population and identifies associations between variables at a single point in time.
The study is both correlational and descriptive. The descriptive aspect relates to the extent
to which hospital managers’ value creation and marketing strategies influence brand
equity.
The study sought to determine causal relationships among the variables. Thus, a
deductive approach was adopted, whereby hypotheses were developed and outcomes
examined to confirm or reject them, followed by testing against theories to generate
generalizations. The study required the collection of quantitative data using a structured
questionnaire. The data was analyzed both descriptively and inferentially, leading to the
conclusions. The field survey employed a questionnaire designed to address the research
questions.

To test the hypotheses, a survey was designed using a questionnaire to collect a
sizeable amount of quantitative data from the study population in a cost -effective way.
The survey described the perceptions of the population sample. A cover letter
accompanied the survey, including instructions to complete it within three days using an
online form accessed through a unique Google Form link. The link was distributed to the
representative sample. The questionnaire was translated from English into Bahasa
Indonesia, the official language of all participants. The researcher carefully reviewed the
translation and made revisions to ensure completeness and accuracy relative to the
original English version. English variable names were retained in the translated
questionnaire to aid participants’ understanding of terms. The questionnaire was
administered online to guarantee anonymity, with data accessible only to the researcher.
Population, Sampling, and Sampling Procedures
The population in this study included all managers of the Adventist hospital system
in Indonesia. The combined manager population was 150, and a margin of error of 5%
was assumed in the sampling.
Considering the finite and relatively small population, Cochran’s formula was used
to determine the sample size, yielding an approximate n of 109 (see Appendix for
calculation). The questionnaire link was sent to 150 hospital managers in July 2024, and
97 responses were received, resulting in a response rate of 58%. A response rate of 51%
or higher is considered acceptable in organizational management research (Anseel et al.,
2010). Therefore, an acceptable response rate was achieved.
The hospital industry was chosen because of its labor -intensive and capital -
intensive nature, combined with intense private sector competition. Another
consideration was the feasibility of collecting data in a cost-effective manner.
Convenience sampling was applied to select the hospitals under study. Convenience
sampling is a non -random approach in which participants are chosen based on
accessibility and willingness to respond. In this study, the selected hospitals were part of
the Adventist denomination healthcare system with which the researcher had connections.
Thus, the organizations permitted their employees to participate after obtaining ethical
clearance and approval from relevant offices. The questionnaires were then distribut ed
randomly among willing respondents.
Validity Test
The content validity of the instrument was assessed through theoretical grounding
and expert review by two PhD holders in business, one of whom specialized in research
methodology. For face validity, three experts with master’s degrees in marketing and over
20 years of professional experience conducted evaluations. Careful attention was given
to ensure that questionnaire items elicited respondents’ perceptions of specific details,
compensating for the absence of access to hard data from their workplaces.
To test the validity of the questionnaire, responses from five non -sample
respondents were analyzed using a one -sample t-test. If the test was not significant, the
questionnaire was considered valid. If significant, the problematic items were simplified
and retested with the five non-sample respondents.

Based on the pilot test results, several items were rephrased or removed, reducing
the questionnaire from 66 to 52 items. This ensured that the first significant digit in
Cronbach’s Alpha for each scale was not reduced.
Reliability Test
To assess reliability, the questionnaire was administered to five non -sample
respondents. Their responses were tested using Cronbach’s α. A value of at least 0.7 was
required to pass the reliability threshold. If α was below 0.7, items with significant
differences were simplified, and the revised questionnaire was re-administered.
Research Instrument
The instrument used to collect data was a structured survey questionnaire. Some
items were adapted from prior studies, while others were developed by the researcher
based on existing theories.
Rubric
Items measuring perceptions were rated using a 5 -point Likert scale. This method
assigns one scale value to each possible response. The questionnaire included multi-item
scales ranging from 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = not sure, 4 = agree, and 5 =
strongly agree, allowing respondents to describe their workplace perceptions.
The questionnaire was divided into sections:
• Section I: Demographic profile (age, gender, position, educational level)
• Section II: Value creation
• Section III: Positioning
• Section IV: Marketing strategy
• Section V: Brand equity
A total of 97 items were used to measure all variables. The scales employed in this
study were adopted and modified for the research. Cronbach’s Alpha for each scale was
established during the pilot study.

Table 4 presents the regression analysis for the effect of hospital managers’ brand
equity comprising brand loyalty, brand association, brand quality perception, and brand

the combined effect of the four brand equity components. After adjusting for the number
of predictors and sample size, the adjusted R square is 0.191, indicating that 19.1% of the
variance in value creation is attributed to brand equity.
The regression model is statistically significant as shown by the ANOVA F-test (F

contribute to the model in explaining value creation.

components of brand equity significantly predict value creation at the 0.05 level. Thus,
while brand equity as a whole contributes to value creation, specific elements may need
to be further strengthened or targeted for more direct impact.

The derived conceptual framework posits a direct relationship between Value Creation and

Marketing Strategy as a discrete construct in the framework has been retained with the
following key observations:
1. Conceptual Overlap: Several marketing strategy components —such as unique proposition
and STP tools—are conceptually integrated within value creation and positioning dimensions.
2. Empirical Simplicity and Parsimony: The possibility of simplifying the model enhances its
clarity and empirical testability, especially in contexts where firms embed strategic marketing
decisions directly within their value delivery systems rather than as standalone processes.
This approach aligns with emerging views in service-dominant logic and customer-centric
models, which emphasize co-created and embedded value as key drivers of brand strength.

CONCLUSION
The concluding chapter presents an assessment of the factors affecting brand equity
among hospital managers, emphasizing their strong engagement in value creation,
marketing strategy implementation, and perceptions of brand equity. The findings reveal
that managers prioritize patient -centered practices, emotional engagement, and strong
internal alignment in their marketing strategies. Statistical analyses confirm that brand
equity dimensions—such as loyalty, association, quality perception, and awareness—are
generally perceived positively, although areas such as customer retention and top-of-mind
recall require further strategic attention. The study highlights the integration of value
creation and marketing strategies within a Balanced Scorecard framework, linking these
practices to financial outcomes and customer satisfaction. To strengthen brand equity, the
researcher proposes a strategic approach that includes enhancing value creation
initiatives, optimizing marketing strategies, reinforcing b rand equity d imensions, and
building strategic capacity through investment in marketing capabilities and innovation.
This comprehensive strategy is designed to ensure sustainable performance and secure a
competitive advantage for private healthcare providers.
