Abstract

Viticulture represents a commercially intensive form of horticultural enterprise in which profitability is influenced by a combination
of production efficiency, varietal choice, technological adoption, post -harvest management, logistics, quality assurance and access
to appropriate markets. Consequently, the economic performance of a grape enterprise extends considerably beyond vineyard -level
production. The present study focused on the Business Model Canvas (BMC) as a strategic framework for examining the manner in
which value is generated, transferred and monetised across the viticulture business. The analysis considered the nine elements of the
BMC, namely key partners, key activities, key resources, value propositions, customer relationships, customer segments, chann els,
cost structure and revenue streams, and related them to the operational characteristics of the grape business. The analysis h ighlights
the importance of an interconnected business ecosystem comprising growers, Farmer Producer Organisations, grower associations ,
research institutions, variety developers, exporters, importers, packhouses, logistics providers, government agencies and retailers.

1. Introduction

The role of agriculture in the economy is gradually shifting from a production -centred activity towards a market -responsive and
commercially integrated enterprise. In high -value horticultural commodities, farm profitability is increasingly determined by the
interaction of production decisions with technology, market information, logistics and consumer demand. Table grapes provide a
particularly relevant example because their commercial success depends not only on obtaining adequate yields but also on
producing the right variety, meeting quality and food -safety standards and reaching markets at the appropriate time (Ministry of
Food Processing Industries, 2011). Recent evidence from grape marketing in India further indicates that marketing -channel
structure, marketing efficiency and stakeholder constraints are important components of the commercial organisation of grape value
chains (Dhoriyani & Prajapati, 2024).  At the production level, constraints affecting the adoption of recommended grape cultivation
practices can also shape growers’ capacity to align farm operations with wider commercial requirements (Raut et al., 2026).

The grape industry comprises a diverse network of participants operating at different stages of the value chain. Growers are
connected with input suppliers, Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs), grower associations, research institutions, variety
developers, packhouses, exporters, importers, logistics companies, retailers and consumers. Marketing studies conducted in gr ape-
producing regions such as Nashik have identified several channels through which grapes move towards domestic, export and
processing markets. The presence of these interconnected actors demonstrates that the competitiveness of a grape enterprise c annot
be adequately assessed solely in terms of farm-level productivity (Pawar et al., 2015).

The business -model perspective provides an opportunity to examine these relationships more systematically (George & Bock,
2011). Teece (2010) conceptualised the business model as the mechanism through which an enterprise organises the creation and
delivery of value and subsequently derives economic returns. Osterwalder and Pigneur (2010) translated this concept into the
Business Model Canvas, which organises a business into nine interconnected components: key partners, key activities, key
resources, value propositions, customer relationships, customer segments, channels, cost structure and revenue streams. Rathe r than
examining these components separately, the BMC allows their interdependence to be considered within a single strategic
framework. Within viticulture, these relationships are particularly important because market expectations can influence varie ty
selection, production practices, post-harvest operations and even the choice of business partners. For example, demand for specific
seedless or premium grape varieties can influence planting decisions, while export -market requirements can affect crop -
management practices, residue management, traceability and packaging. Similarly, investments in post -harvest infrastructure can
influence the markets that an enterprise is capable of serving. Recent agrifood research further emphasises that value creation occurs
across networks of chain and non -chain actors, highlighting the importance of examining relationships among multiple participants
rather than focusing on a single enterprise in isolation (Sadovska et al., 2020; Schoneveld & Weng, 2023).  Research on agri -food
business-model innovation also shows that digital technologies can reconfigure mechanisms of value creation and value capture,
reinforcing the relevance of analysing business -model components as interdependent elements (Mancuso et al., 2023; Tell et al.,
2016).

In parallel, recent work on agricultural data value creation identifies information-related mechanisms that can support organisational
performance and business -process improvement, which is consistent with the growing importance of market intelligence and
traceability in contemporary agribusiness (Uyar et al., 2024). Despite the relevance of these interdependencies, an integrate d
application that simultaneously maps partners, activities, resources, value propositions, customer relationships, customer se gments,
channels, costs and revenue mechanisms within the viticulture business remains insufficiently articulated in the context addr essed
by this study.

Accordingly, the objective of this study was to adapt the Business Model Canvas to the viticulture business and use its nine
components to examine how value is created, delivered and captured across the grape enterprise.

2. Background of Study

The Business Model Canvas developed by Osterwalder and Pigneur (2010) provides a structured method for examining how an
enterprise organises its activities and resources to satisfy customer needs and generate economic returns. Its nine component s
collectively show the major relationships between an organisation, its partners, resources, activities, customers, distributi on
mechanisms, costs and sources of income. When applied to viticulture, these components require adaptation to reflect the biological,
seasonal and market -dependent characteristics of agricultural production. A grape enterprise is not simply a unit that converts
agricultural inputs into harvested produce; rather, it operates within a network in which market requirements, production dec isions,
post-harvest systems and commercial relationships influence one another (Zhou et al., 2015).

The adapted framework begins with customer and market requirements. Preferences concerning flavour, appearance, seedlessness,
berry size, colour, shelf life, food safety and traceability can influence the choice of cultivars and production strategies (Wang et al.,
2017). These requirements are transmitted through exporters, importers, FPOs, retailers and technical organisations to grower s and

3. Methods of Study

3.1 Methodology

The study adopted a descriptive and exploratory framework -based research design to examine the structure, functioning and
strategic characteristics of the viticulture business. This design was considered appropriate because the study sought to des cribe the
major operational and commercial dimensions of viticulture enterprises and to explore how different business components inter act
within the value chain. Rather than focusing exclusively on production -related variables, the analysis considered the viticulture
enterprise as an integrated business system involving production, partnerships, markets, customers, supply chains, costs and
revenue-generating activities.

The Business Model Canvas (BMC ) developed by Osterwalder and Pigneur (2010) was used as the principal analytical framework
for organising and interpreting the studied parameters. The nine components of the BMC were adapted to the specific characteristics
of the viticulture sector. Accordingly, the parameters examined were key partners, key activities, key resources, value propo sitions,
customer relationships, customer segments, channels, cost structure and revenue streams. Each parameter was assessed in relation to
its role in supporting the establishment, operation, competitiveness and long-term sustainability of a viticulture enterprise.

The key partners parameter focused on the organisations and stakeholders that contribute technical, commercial, financial, lo gistical
or institutional support to viticulture businesses. These included plant breeders, nurseries, input suppliers, research organ isations,
marketing agencies, exporters, logistics providers, certification agencies, producer organisations and other strategic collab orators.
Particular attention was given to the contribution of partnerships to cultivar development, access to planting materials, tec hnology
transfer, quality assurance, export-market compliance and the coordination of supply-chain activities.

The key activities parameter covered the principal operations required for the production and commercialisation of grapes. Th ese
activities included vineyard establishment, variety selection, canopy and crop management, irrigation, nutrient management, p est
and disease management, harvesting, post -harvest handling, grading, packaging, storage, transportation and marketing. Activities
related to quality control, traceability, certification, market intelligence and coordination with buyers were also considere d because
these functions directly influence product quality, market acceptance and commercial performance. The key resources component
was used to identify the physical, biological, human, technological and organisational resources required for successful viti culture.
The studied resources included suitable land, soil and climatic conditions, irrigation facilities, grape varieties and planting materials,
farm machinery, trellising systems, post -harvest infrastructure, cold -chain facilities, skilled labour, technical expertise, market
information and business networks. Intellectual resources such as improved varieties, production knowledge, traceability syst ems
and branding were also considered because of their increasing importance in competitive domestic and international markets. T he
value proposition parameter examined the specific benefits offered by viticulture enterprises to their customers. These benef its
included fruit quality, varietal characteristics, freshness, appearance, taste, nutritional value, food safety, consistent su pply,
traceability and compliance with market or export standards. The analysis also considered differentiation strategies based on
specialised cultivars, premium-quality grapes, sustainable production practices, efficient post-harvest management and the ability to
meet the specifications of particular market segments. The customer segments parameter was used to identify the different groups of
buyers served by the viticulture business. Depending on the nature of the enterprise, these segments may include local consum ers,
wholesalers, retailers, supermarkets, processors, institutional buyers, exporters and overseas importers. The analysis consid ered the
differences among these customer groups in terms of quality expectations, quantity requirements, packaging specifications,
purchasing behaviour and market standards. The customer relationships component examined the approaches used by producers and
agribusiness organisations to establish, maintain and strengthen relationships with buyers. Parameters considered under this
component included direct interaction with customers, contractual arrangements, buyer -producer coordination, responsiveness to
quality requirements, after-sales communication, long-term supply relationships and mechanisms for receiving market feedback. In
organised supply chains, traceability, certification and consistent compliance with buyer specifications were treated as impo rtant
mechanisms for strengthening customer confidence. The channels parameter examined the pathways through which grapes and
related information move from producers to end users. These channels included direct farm sales, local markets, wholesale mar kets,
organised retail chains, producer organisations, exporters, importers and other supply -chain intermediaries. Distribution and
communication channels were considered together because market access depends not only on the physical movement of produce
but also on the efficient transmission of information concerning prices, quality standards, demand, production schedules and
traceability. The cost structure component was used to identify the major expenditure categories associated with viticulture
production and marketing. The studied cost parameters included vineyard establishment, planting materials, trellising, irriga tion
infrastructure, fertilisers, crop -protection inputs, labour, machinery, harvesting, grading, packaging, storage, transportation,
certification, marketing and other supply-chain expenses. Consideration of these cost components enabled the study to illustrate how
different operational decisions and market requirements may influence the overall economic structure of a viticulture enterprise. The
revenue streams parameter examined the principal sources through which value generated by the enterprise is converted into
income. Revenue may be generated through the sale of fresh grapes to local or organised markets,

4. Results and Discussion

4.1 Business Model Canvas of the Viticulture Business

4.1.1 Key Partners

The commercial functioning of the grape industry depends on collaboration among a wide range of stakeholders. At the producti on
level, growers provide the foundation for production, while FPOs and grower associations provide collective action, technical
training and market coordination. Research institutions contribute scientific and technical knowledge, whereas variety develo pers
and licensing organisations provide access to improved genetic material. The importance of specialised variety organisations is
becoming increasingly evident in the modern table -grape industry. Organisations such as Grapa Global and SNFL represent the
growing role of breeding, varietal innovation and intellectual -property-based business arrangements. Similarly, industry
organisations such as the South African Table Grape Industry (SATI) and the Australian Table Grape Association demonstrate th e
contribution of coordinated industry institutions to technical development, export preparedness and market development.

Other important partners include exporters, importers, packhouses, transport and logistics providers, certification agencies,  testing
laboratories and government institutions. These actors collectively provide access to infrastructure, compliance systems, mar ket
networks, technical knowledge and commercial opportunities. Thus, partnerships within viticulture constitute a broader agribusiness
ecosystem rather than a simple supplier–farmer relationship.

4.1.2 Key Activities

The activities undertaken within the viticulture business encompass the entire chain from vineyard establishment to final pro duct
distribution. At the beginning of the chain, cultivar selection, variety evaluation, breeding and licensing influence the bio logical and
commercial characteristics of the crop. Production activities subsequently include vineyard planning, planting, canopy management,
irrigation, nutrient management, crop protection, harvesting and other crop-management operations.

Farmer training and technical advisory services are also significant because production practices must be aligned with the
requirements of the intended market. Following harvest, grapes undergo procurement, aggregation, sorting, grading and packagi ng.
Maintaining product quality may require pre-cooling, cold storage and controlled transportation.

Export-oriented operations involve additional activities such as residue monitoring, quality inspection, traceability, phytosanitary
documentation, certification and international logistics. Market development, buyer coordination and demand assessment are
equally important because consumer and retailer requirements can change the commercial attractiveness of particular varieties  and
markets.

Consequently, the activity structure of contemporary viticulture extends well beyond crop cultivation and incorporates produc tion
management, quality management, post-harvest handling and market development as interconnected business functions.

4.1.3 Key Resources

The resources required for successful viticulture comprise physical, biological, human and intangible assets. Physical resour ces
include vineyards, irrigation infrastructure, machinery, packhouses, grading and sorting equipment, pre -cooling facilities, cold -
storage systems, transportation facilities and packaging infrastructure. Biological resources include planting material, impr oved
cultivars and other genetic resources. Human resources are equally important and include growers, agronomists, field technici ans,
quality-control personnel, procurement staff, packhouse workers, logistics managers, marketing professionals and export personnel.
Their knowledge and experience influence both production quality and commercial performance.

In addition to these conventional resources, the modern grape business increasingly relies on intangible assets. Variety righ ts,
licensing arrangements, brand identity, market intelligence, farmer networks, buyer relationships and traceability informatio n can
generate strategic advantages that are difficult for competitors to replicate. This suggests that the resource foundation of modern
viticulture is expanding from the traditional combination of land, labour and physical inputs towards genetics, technology,
information, infrastructure, intellectual property and relationships.

4.1.4 Value Propositions

The value offered by a viticulture business differs according to the stakeholder involved (Osterwalder et al., 2014). For gro wers, the
business proposition may include access to improved cultivars, technical guidance, production technologies, organised procure ment
and reliable market linkages. Exporters and importers, in contrast, place greater emphasis on consistent quality, adequate vo lumes,
traceability, regulatory compliance and dependable supply. Retailers require grapes that satisfy specifications concerning
appearance, uniformity, shelf life, packaging and food safety. Consumers generally evaluate the product in terms of taste, fr eshness,
appearance, seedlessness, berry characteristics, convenience and confidence in product safety.

The increasing importance of improved varieties and traceability demonstrates that the value proposition of grapes is no long er
restricted to the physical product. The activities of organisations such as SNFL and Grapa highlight the commercial significa nce of
differentiated cultivars, while integrated grape enterprises such as Sahyadri Farms illustrate the role of traceability and p roducer-
level information in strengthening product transparency.

4.1.5 Customer Relationships

Relationships with growers and buyers form an important foundation of the grape business. On the supply side, enterprises may
maintain farmer relationships through technical recommendations, training, production planning, variety guidance, input suppo rt,
quality specifications and procurement arrangements. Such relationships help coordinate production with anticipated market
requirements.

On the demand side, exporters, importers, wholesalers and retailers require regular communication regarding product specifications,
volumes, delivery schedules, quality standards and traceability. Long -term commercial relationships can provide greater visibility
regarding future demand and enable producers to align production accordingly.

The two sides of relationship management are interconnected. Effective relationships with growers can strengthen consistency and
supply reliability, while stable relationships with buyers can improve market information and reduce uncertainty. Customer
relationship management therefore becomes a coordination mechanism connecting production decisions with market expectations.

4.1.6 Customer Segments

The grape business encompasses multiple customer groups operating through a predominantly B2B2C structure. These include
farmers and producer organisations on the supply side and exporters, importers, wholesalers, distributors, processors, foodse rvice
operators, supermarket chains, retailers and final consumers at subsequent stages. Every customer group has distinct requirem ents.
Exporters and importers are particularly concerned with regulatory compliance, quality consistency, volume and reliability.
Retailers emphasise uniformity, packaging, shelf life, presentation and consumer appeal. Consumers generally evaluate grapes
according to taste, freshness, appearance, seedlessness, safety and convenience.

The diversity of customer requirements also means that the same production system may not be suitable for every market. Marke t
segmentation can therefore assist grape enterprises in identifying appropriate cultivars, quality standards, packaging format s and
distribution channels for particular market opportunities.

4.1.7 Channels

Grape distribution commonly involves a sequence of intermediaries through which the physical product moves from production to
consumption. A representative channel can be expressed as:

Farmer → Aggregator → Packhouse → Exporter → Importer → Wholesaler/Retailer → Consumer

However, the role of these channels extends beyond physical movement. Information concerning market prices, consumer
preferences, retailer specifications, quality problems and demand conditions also travels through the value chain. Consequent ly,
channels serve both logistical and informational functions. International trade exhibitions, buyer meetings, trade missions a nd
export-promotion programmes can supplement conventional distribution arrangements by creating opportunities for business
development and market intelligence. Integrated enterprises may also combine several functions, including production coordination,
packing, wholesale, retail, foodservice and processing.

An effective channel system in viticulture must therefore ensure both physical product flow and timely information flow.

4.1.8 Cost Structure

The cost structure of viticulture includes expenses incurred at several stages of the value chain. At the farm level, major e xpenditure
categories include planting material, labour, fertilisers, crop -protection inputs, irrigation, vineyard maintenance and harvesting.
Additional expenditure is incurred during procurement, aggregation and transportation.

Packhouse operations generate costs associated with grading, sorting, labour, packaging, electricity, quality assessment and
handling. Post -harvest management may further require expenditure on pre -cooling and cold storage. For export -oriented
businesses, costs may include freight, documentation, insurance, phytosanitary certification, port handling, compliance testi ng and
other logistics -related charges. Marketing and commercial -development activities can generate additional expenditure through
branding, trade fairs, buyer interactions and promotional activities. Premium cultivars may also involve licensing or royalty
payments.

4.1.9 Revenue Streams

Fresh table grapes constitute the principal source of revenue for most grape enterprises. The value realised from fresh grape s is
influenced by production volume, cultivar, grade, quality, harvest timing, destination market and prevailing prices. Premium
cultivars provide an opportunity for higher -value realisation when their characteristics are recognised by consumers, retailers and
other buyers. Differentiation based on colour, taste, seedlessness, berry size, shelf life or other attributes can allow ente rprises to
target specialised market segments.

Additional revenue opportunities may arise from branded grapes, private -label arrangements, processing, raisins, grape juice and
other value -added products. Licensing and royalty arrangements associated with proprietary varieties represent another potential

4.1.10 Interrelationship among the Business Model Components

The nine elements of the Business Model Canvas are closely connected in the viticulture business. Market requirements influen ce
the desired value proposition, which in turn affects customer segmentation, variety selection and production planning. These
requirements are transmitted through commercial and institutional partners to growers, technical teams and variety developers .
Production decisions subsequently determine the resources and activities required to deliver the product. Once grapes are har vested,
procurement, aggregation, grading, packaging, pre-cooling, storage, traceability and logistics determine whether the desired product
quality can be maintained until it reaches the customer.

The overall business process can therefore be represented as:

Market Requirements → Value Proposition → Variety and Production Decisions → Resources and Activities →
Strategic Partnerships → Post-Harvest Management → Distribution Channels → Customers → Revenue Generation →
Reinvestment

5. Conclusion

The Business Model Canvas provides a comprehensive strategic lens for examining viticulture as an interconnected agribusiness
rather than simply as a crop production activity. The analysis demonstrates that the commercial performance of grape enterpri ses
depends on the coordinated functioning of partners, activities, resources, value propositions, customer relationships, market
segments, channels, costs and revenue mechanisms. The resource requirements of contemporary viticulture increasingly extend
beyond land, labour and conventional farm inputs. Improved genetics, precision technologies, post -harvest infrastructure, market
intelligence, traceability, intellectual property and long -term business relationships have become important sources of competitive
advantage. At the same time, the value offered by grapes increasingly incorporates characteristics such as quality, different iation,
food safety, consistency, traceability and supply reliability.

The analysis consequently suggests that the future competitiveness of viticulture will depend on the ability of enterprises to integrate
production capability, market orientation and value-chain coordination. By mapping these relationships, the Business Model Canvas
provides a practical framework for identifying opportunities to strengthen market access, improve value capture, diversify re venue
and build long-term competitiveness in the table-grape industry.

6. Limitation

This study is descriptive and exploratory and applies the Business Model Canvas to viticulture using illustrative industry ex amples
rather than statistically representative observations. Accordingly, it provides a structured interpretation of relationships among
business components but does not estimate the magnitude of their effects or compare performance across enterprises, regions,
cultivars or market channels. The findings should therefore be interpreted as a conceptual representation of the viticulture business
rather than as evidence of causal relationships or generalised quantitative performance.
