Beyond Human Audiences: Machine-Facing Branding and Algorithmic Legibility in the
Era of Agentic AI

Abstract
The emergence of agentic artificial intelligence is transforming the processes through which
brands are discovered, interpreted, evaluated, compared, and recommended. Branding theory
has historically prioritized human audiences, emphasizing consumer cognition, emotional
engagement, symbolic meaning, brand identity, and customer experience. However, intelligent
agents are increasingly operating as marketplace intermediaries that can interpret consumer
goals, evaluate alternatives, coordinate decisions, and undertake authorized purchase-related
actions. This development creates a dual-audience environment in which brands must remain
meaningful to humans while becoming accurately interpretable and recommendable to
machines. This theory-development paper introduces Machine-Facing Branding (MFB) and
proposes the Algorithmic Brand Legibility Framework (ABLF) . Drawing on signaling
theory, information-processing perspectives, brand equity theory, knowledge representation,
source-credibility research, human-AI trust, and Generative Engine Optimization, the
framework identifies eight antecedents of algorithmic brand legibility: semantic clarity,
structured knowledge representation, entity identity, contextual consistency, digital authority,
verifiable trust signals, transparency, and technical accessibility. Algorithmic brand legibility
is proposed to influence agent interpretation quality, recommendation confidence, AI-mediated
brand consideration, consumer trust calibration, brand preference, and brand equity. The paper
extends branding theory by conceptualizing AI systems not merely as communication tools but
as interpretive and decision-shaping market actors. It concludes with propositions, managerial
implications, ethical considerations, measurement directions, and a research agenda for testing
machine-facing branding across agent-mediated marketplaces.
Keywords: Agentic AI; Machine-Facing Branding; Algorithmic Brand Legibility; AI-
Mediated Marketing; Generative Engine Optimization; Knowledge Representation; Brand
Equity; Digital Trust; Agentic Discoverability.

1. Introduction
Branding has traditionally been conceptualized as a human-facing activity. Organizations
create names, symbols, narratives, reputational associations, customer experiences, and
emotional meanings in order to establish distinctive positions in consumers’ minds. Whether
viewed through identity, equity, personality, authenticity, relationship, or experience
perspectives, conventional branding models generally assume that human beings encounter
brand information, interpret its meaning, evaluate alternatives, and make consumption
decisions.
Digital marketing altered the channels through which this process occurred but did not
fundamentally eliminate the human consumer from the decision path. Search engines, social
networks, digital advertising platforms, recommendation systems, and electronic marketplaces
reorganized access to brand information, yet marketers continued to optimize communication
primarily for human attention, engagement, recall, and persuasion.

Agentic artificial intelligence introduces a more fundamental change. Agentic systems may
interpret goals, decompose tasks, compare alternatives, coordinate actions, negotiate
conditions, and execute authorized marketplace decisions with varying levels of human
involvement. Consumer-side agents may search markets and compare offerings, while brand-
controlled agents may manage personalization, service, relationship development, and
recovery. The special issue itself identifies delegated consumer agency, machine-facing
branding, algorithmic legibility, brand discoverability, agent- to-agent commerce, trust
calibration, and automation bias as relevant areas of inquiry.
Under these conditions, the path between a brand and a consumer increasingly contains an
interpretive computational layer as explained in figure 1 below.

AI intermediation does not mean that human meaning, emotion, identification, and experience
become irrelevant. Rather, brands must now address two interconnected audiences. The human
audience evaluates meaning, relevance, trust, identity, and experience, while the machine
audience retrieves, structures, reconcile, compares, and represents brand information. A brand
may therefore remain culturally meaningful yet become weakly represented in AI-generated
consideration sets if its identity, offerings, evidence, and relationships are difficult for
intelligent systems to interpret.
This paper argues that such a shift requires an extension of branding theory. Brands must
become not only salient and meaningful but also algorithmically legible. The paper introduces
Machine-Facing Branding as a strategic domain and develops the Algorithmic Brand Legibility
Framework to explain how machine-readable brand signals influence AI interpretation,
recommendation confidence, consumer consideration, trust, preference, and brand equity.
Research Objective
The objective of this paper is to conceptualize Machine-Facing Branding and develop an
integrated theoretical framework explaining how brand information becomes interpretable,
credible, discoverable, and recommendable within agentic AI ecosystems.
Research Questions
RQ1: What constitutes Machine-Facing Branding in AI-mediated marketplaces?
RQ2: Which factors determine the algorithmic legibility of a brand?

RQ3: Through which mechanisms do algorithmic brand legibility influence AI
recommendations and consumer responses?
RQ4: What organizational, technological, market, and ethical conditions may strengthen or
weaken these relationships?
Contribution Statement
This paper makes four principal contributions. First, it expands the assumed audience of
branding from human consumers to a dual audience comprising people and intelligent agents.
Second, it introduces algorithmic brand legibility as a distinct construct rather than treating AI
visibility merely as a technical search-optimization outcome. Third, it connects machine-facing
antecedents with established branding outcomes through agent interpretation and
recommendation confidence. Fourth, it establishes a multidisciplinary research agenda linking
marketing, information systems, human-AI interaction, information retrieval, communication,
and brand management.

2. Why Traditional Branding Is No Longer Sufficient
Traditional brand-management approaches were developed in an environment where
consumers performed most of the interpretation, comparison, and evaluation involved in a
purchase. Marketers attempted to enter consumers’ awareness and consideration sets through
distinctive brand assets, persuasive communication, distribution, service experience, social
influence, and reputation.
Agentic AI compresses and redistributes these activities. An intelligent agent may synthesize
product specifications, expert commentary, customer reviews, organizational policies, pricing,
availability, and consumer constraints before presenting a shortlist. The consumer may begin
the visible decision journey only after an algorithmic system has already retrieved, excluded,
compared, and prioritized competing alternatives.
Evidence from contemporary practice supports the importance of this shift. BCG (Boston
Consulting Group 2026) describes agentic commerce as an environment in which AI agents
become active participants in the journey from consumer intent to purchase and argues that
marketers increasingly need to account for two decision makers: humans and the AI systems
influencing their choices. It also emphasizes the need to align brand promises with observable
customer experience because AI systems can aggregate reviews, complaints, service
interactions, and other market signals.
Research on Generative Engine Optimization (Aggarwal et al. 2024) similarly recognizes that
generative engines synthesize information from multiple sources instead of simply displaying
ranked links. The foundational GEO study describes the diminished control of content creators
over when and how their information appears in generated responses and proposes visibility
optimization for such environments.
Consequently, traditional branding remains necessary but becomes insufficient on its own.
Human memorability without machine interpretability may limit agentic discoverability.
Conversely, technically structured information without human meaning may produce visibility
without emotional attachment, distinctiveness, or loyalty. Machine-facing branding must
therefore complement rather than replace consumer-facing branding.

3. Literature Review
3.1 Agentic AI and Delegated Consumer Decision-Making
Generative AI initially expanded the production and interpretation of content. Agentic AI
extends these capabilities toward goal-directed action. Agentic systems may evaluate
alternatives, sequence activities, interact with other systems, and perform predefined actions
with limited intervention. Within marketing, this alters who conducts the search, who forms
the shortlist, and who exercises influence over final selection.
The special issue frames agentic AI as a redistribution of decision rights, informational power,
and accountability among consumers, brands, platforms, marketers, service employees, and AI
agents. It also recognizes potential benefits such as reduced information-processing costs and
improved decision support, together with risks including restricted consideration sets,
unauthorized preference inference, commercially motivated steering, and reduced
transparency.
Delegation may occur across a continuum. At the lowest level, AI retrieves information while
the consumer retains evaluation and choice. At an intermediate level, AI compares alternatives
and recommends a shortlist. At higher levels, the system may negotiate conditions, select an
offering, or execute an authorized purchase. Machine-facing branding becomes progressively
more consequential as the level of delegation increases because the agent has greater influence
over the consideration set and decision sequence.
This development raises a new marketing question. Instead of asking only how brands
influence consumers, researchers must examine how brand signals are interpreted by systems
acting on behalf of consumers. The issue is not that an AI agent becomes a biological consumer.
Rather, the agent becomes an influential information processor and decision intermediary
whose representation of the brand can affect subsequent human judgment.

3.2 Branding in AI-Mediated Ecosystems
Digital branding literature has examined websites, social platforms, electronic word of mouth,
online communities, personalization, algorithms, and customer experience. Nevertheless,
much of this literature continues to focus on how digital communication affects human
perceptions and behavior.
AI-mediated ecosystems add a computational interpretation layer. Brand information may be
extracted, categorized, summarized, reconciled with other sources, and transformed into a
recommendation. This means that the brand communication received by a consumer may be
neither the organization’s original message nor a conventional advertisement. It may instead
be an AI-generated representation assembled from owned, earned, transactional, reputational,
and contextual evidence.
BCG argues that AI increasingly shapes product discovery, option evaluation, purchasing
decisions, and transactions. In this environment, AI can expose inconsistencies between brand
claims and customer experience because it can synthesize evidence across the wider
information ecosystem.

Accordingly, brand stewardship must extend beyond controlling messages. Organizations also
need to manage the clarity, structure, consistency, credibility, and verifiability of the digital
evidence from which AI-generated representations may be constructed. Machine-facing
branding therefore concerns the brand’s interpretable evidence environment , not merely
technical metadata or content optimization.

3.3 Algorithmic Visibility and Generative Engine Optimization
Traditional search optimization generally seeks higher placement in ranked search results.
Generative engines instead create synthesized answers based on retrieved sources. Visibility
consequently becomes multidimensional. It may involve whether a brand is mentioned, how it
is described, which claims are attributed to it, which sources support the description, whether
it is included in a shortlist, and whether it is recommended for a particular need.
Aggarwal et al. (2024)  introduced Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)  as one of the
earliest systematic frameworks designed to help content creators improve their visibility within
AI-generated responses. Using GEO-Bench, a large-scale benchmark comprising diverse user
queries across multiple domains, the authors demonstrated that specific optimization
techniques, such as incorporating citations, statistics, and authoritative references, could
improve content visibility in generative-engine outputs by up to 40%. However, their findings
also revealed that the effectiveness of these strategies varies considerably across domains,
highlighting the need for context-specific approaches to AI-mediated discoverability and
recommendation.
However, GEO and Machine-Facing Branding should not be treated as identical. GEO
principally concerns the visibility of content in generative responses. Machine-Facing
Branding is broader. It includes the management of brand identity, entity relationships,
evidence, trust, authenticity, consistency, governance, product representation, and the
conditions under which an agent can appropriately recommend. GEO may therefore be
conceptualized as one operational capability within Machine-Facing Branding (figure 2).

3.4 Knowledge Representation and Brand Entities

AI systems require more than isolated keywords. Brands exist within networks of entities and
relationships involving organizations, people, products, locations, certifications, categories,
audiences, claims, policies, reviews, and external sources.
Adobe (2026) defines a knowledge graph as a structured representation of information that
organizes content into interconnected entities (such as products, services, people, and
concepts) and the relationships between them. According to Adobe, knowledge graphs help
search engines, AI assistants, and large language models connect information across a brand’s
offerings, services, content, and expertise, enabling a more coherent and accurate interpretation
of the brand. This structured architecture enhances machine understanding, improves content
discoverability, and contributes to greater visibility and trustworthiness within AI-powered
search environments (Adobe, 2026).
From a branding perspective, knowledge representation creates three important capabilities:
1. Entity recognition: The system can identify the brand as a distinct entity.
2. Entity disambiguation: The system can distinguish the brand from similarly named
organizations or products.
3. Relational understanding: The system can connect the brand to relevant categories,
products, services, audiences, evidence, and contexts.
Theoretical caution is nevertheless required. Knowledge graphs should not be presented as a
guaranteed route to recommendation. They can support structured understanding, but inclusion
in a particular AI output can also depend on the agent’s architecture, retrieval sources, task
constraints, user preferences, platform incentives, available evidence, and model behavior.

3.5 Source Credibility, Digital Authority, and Trust Signals
Signaling theory explains how actors communicate otherwise difficult- to-observe qualities
through visible indicators. In conventional branding, signals may include warranties,
certifications, endorsements, pricing, reputation, service commitments, and consistent
experience.
In AI-mediated environments, the visibility and verifiability of signals become important. A
statement appearing only on a brand-controlled page may be interpreted differently from a
claim supported by independent sources, regulatory records, professional evidence, customer
experience, or recognized certifying bodies.
Digital authority in the ABLF does not simply mean a high volume of online content. It refers
to the quality, relevance, consistency, independence, and verifiability of the evidence associated
with the brand. Authority may therefore be strengthened through legitimate external
recognition, named expertise, transparent methodology, traceable claims, and congruence
between organizational promises and stakeholder experience.
This distinction protects the framework from degenerating into a manipulation model.
Machine-facing branding should not encourage organizations to manufacture artificial
authority. Instead, it should make authentic evidence easier for both humans and machines to
locate, interpret, and verify.

3.6 AI Recommendations, Trust, and Consumer Judgment

AI-mediated recommendations can reduce the cognitive effort required to search and compare
offerings. However, trust in AI is not necessarily uniform, stable, or appropriate. Consumers
may under-rely on accurate recommendations, over-rely on inappropriate recommendations, or
fail to verify an AI-generated shortlist.
BCG’s (Boston Consulting Group 2026) consumer research reports growing use of generative
AI within shopping journeys and describes characteristics of consumers value in AI-assisted
shopping, including directness, perceived objectivity, transparency, and personalization. The
underlying research involved large multi-country consumer samples and qualitative research.

1. Trust in the Brand
Trust in the brand refers to a consumer's confidence that the organization is reliable, honest,
competent, and capable of consistently delivering promised value. This form of trust develops
through reputation, customer experience, product quality, service performance, brand
authenticity, and long-term stakeholder relationships.
Example
A consumer may trust: Apple, Toyota, Microsoft, Rolex because of years of perceived quality
and reliability.
However, even if the consumer trusts the brand, they may still be skeptical of an AI
recommendation involving that brand.

2. Trust in the AI Agent

Trust in the AI agent refers to confidence in the system itself rather than the brands it evaluates.
Consumers ask:
• Is the AI objective?
• Is it unbiased?
• Does it understand my needs?
• Is it technically competent?
Example
A consumer may trust ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, Claude, or another agent to compare options
accurately.
In this case:
Trust AI Agent = High
But the consumer may still not trust the recommended brand.

3. Trust in the Recommendation
This refers specifically to confidence in the recommendation generated by AI.
This is different from trusting the AI generally.
Example
A consumer may trust ChatGPT extensively but still question a recommendation for a brand
they have never heard of.
Trust in AI = High
Trust in Recommendation = Moderate
Consumers often ask:
• Why was this recommended?
• Were alternatives considered?
• Is this really the best option?
Recommendation trust is therefore situational and recommendation specific.

4. Trust in Supporting Evidence
Trust in supporting evidence refers to confidence in the quality and credibility of the
information used by AI.
Consumers increasingly examine:
• Customer reviews
• Certifications

• Research evidence
• Media references
• Expert opinions
• Regulatory approvals
Example
An AI may recommend a university.
The consumer may ask:
• Are the rankings credible?
• Are the reviews genuine?
• Is the information current?
Even if the AI recommendation appears reasonable, weak evidence can reduce trust.

5. Confidence in the Consumer's Own Decision
This represents the final psychological outcome.
Consumers often ask: "Have I made the right decision?"
This confidence may be influenced by:
• Brand trust
• AI trust
• Recommendation trust
• Evidence trust

The consumer feels comfortable proceeding with the purchase or action.

Trust in Brand + Trust in AI Agent + Trust in Recommendation + Trust in Supporting Evidence
→ Decision Confidence → Brand Preference → Brand Equity
Within the Algorithmic Brand Legibility Framework (ABLF), trust is conceptualized as a
multidimensional phenomenon rather than a single construct. Consumers may simultaneously

evaluate trust in the brand, trust in the AI agent, trust in the recommendation provided, and
trust in the supporting evidence on which the recommendation is based. These trust dimensions
collectively influence the consumer's confidence in their final decision. Consequently,
successful machine-facing branding requires not only enhancing brand credibility but also
facilitating accurate AI interpretation, transparent recommendations, and verifiable evidence
that enable consumers to make informed and confident choices.
Algorithmic brand legibility may improve interpretation, but it should not automatically be
assumed to produce justified consumer trust. Appropriate trust depends on recommendation
accuracy, transparent reasoning, source quality, consumer awareness, the consequences of the
decision, and the opportunity to contest or verify the output.

3.7 Brand Authenticity and Representational Fidelity
Brand authenticity concerns whether a brand is perceived as genuine, consistent, credible, and
aligned with its declared identity and values. AI-mediated brand representations create a new
authenticity risk because algorithmically generated descriptions may simplify, distort,
generalize, or incorrectly attribute brand meaning.
Machine-Facing Branding must therefore pursue representational fidelity, defined in this
paper as the extent to which an AI-generated brand representation accurately reflects the
brand’s verifiable identity, offering, capabilities, values, limitations, and stakeholder
experience.
Representational fidelity prevents algorithmic visibility from becoming the sole objective. A
brand not only should be mentioned frequently. It should be represented accurately and
contextually. Incorrect inclusion, exaggerated recommendations, or misattributed capability
can damage trust even when short-term visibility increases.

5. Definitions of Key Constructs
5.1 Machine-Facing Branding
Machine-Facing Branding is the strategic design, structuring, verification, distribution, and
governance of brand information and evidence to facilitate accurate recognition, interpretation,
comparison, and contextual appropriate recommendation by AI systems and intelligent agents.
Machine-Facing Branding does not mean replacing human-centered branding. It creates a
complementary layer through which brand meaning and evidence can traverse machine-
mediated environments with reduced ambiguity and distortion.
5.2 Algorithmic Brand Legibility

Algorithmic Brand Legibility is the degree to which an AI system can accurately identify a
brand, distinguish it from other entities, interpret its value, proposition and evidence, connect
it to relevant contexts, and represent it appropriately during retrieval, comparison, or
recommendation.
Legibility is distinct from visibility. A brand can be visible but inaccurately understood. It can
also be understandable but excluded from a particular recommendation because it does not
satisfy the task or user’s constraints.
5.3 Agent Interpretation Quality
Agent Interpretation Quality refers to the accuracy, completeness, consistency, contextual
relevance, and evidential grounding of an AI agent’s internal or expressed representation of a
brand.
5.4 AI Recommendation Confidence
AI Recommendation Confidence refers to the degree of certainty with which an intelligent
agent includes, ranks, or recommends a brand for a defined task, user need, or decision context.
Recommendation confidence is a proposed theoretical construct. It should not be equated
automatically with model-generated probability unless an empirical study operationalizes it
that way.
5.5 Agentic Discoverability
Agentic Discoverability is the likelihood that a brand will be retrieved and considered by an AI
agent when the brand is relevant to a user’s goal, constraints, context, and authorized task.
5.6 AI-Mediated Brand Consideration
AI-Mediated Brand Consideration is the inclusion of a brand within a consumer’s effective
consideration set because of retrieval, comparison, summarization, or recommendation
performed by an AI system.
5.7 Representational Fidelity
Representational Fidelity is the correspondence between the brand’s verifiable identity and
performance and the representation produced or communicated by an AI system.
5.8 Machine-Interpretable Brand Equity
Machine-Interpretable Brand Equity refers to the strategic value arising from the accurate,
credible, and favorable representation of a brand across AI-mediated discovery and decision
environments.
This proposed construct supplements rather than replaces consumer-based or financial brand
equity.

6. Expanded Theoretical Foundations
6.1 Signaling Theory
Signaling theory explains how observable signs reduce information asymmetry between
parties. In Machine-Facing Branding, organizations make quality, identity, legitimacy, and trust
signals available within digital environments. AI agents may retrieve these signals when

evaluating alternatives, but the value of a signal depends on its clarity, consistency, credibility,
and verifiability.
Signaling theory supports the inclusion of digital authority, verified claims, transparent
policies, certifications, credible reviews, and source corroboration within ABLF. Where signals
conflict, AI interpretation may become uncertain or rely on alternative sources. Accordingly,
MFB depends on coherent evidence rather than promotional assertion alone.
6.2 Information-Processing Perspective
Human consumers have limited time, attention, and cognitive capacity. AI agents may reduce
part of that burden by retrieving and comparing information. Yet the agent’s ability to perform
such functions depends on whether brand information can be extracted, reconciled,
contextualized, and associated with the correct entity.
The information-processing perspective therefore supports semantic clarity, accessibility,
structured representation, and contextual consistency as antecedents of algorithmic legibility.
6.3 Brand Equity Theory
Brand equity theories explain how brand awareness, associations, perceived quality, loyalty,
and other assets contribute to marketplace value. ABLF extends this reasoning by introducing
an agent-mediated route through which brand information enters consumer awareness and
consideration.
The framework does not suggest that machine legibility directly creates brand equity. Instead,
machine legibility may affect brand equity through interpretation quality, recommendation
confidence, consideration, trust, preference, and experience. Actual product and service
performance remain indispensable.
6.4 Source-Credibility Theory
Source credibility is relevant because AI-generated brand representations may synthesize
multiple sources. The perceived or algorithmically assessed expertise, trustworthiness,
independence, and consistency of those sources may influence the resulting answer.
ABLF consequently distinguishes first-party information from external corroboration. Both
may be valuable, but unverified self-claims should not be treated as equivalent to independently
substantiated evidence.
6.5 Knowledge Representation
Knowledge representation explains how entities, attributes, and relationships can be organized
in ways that support machine interpretation. Brand names, products, services, leaders,
locations, certifications, categories, policies, and audiences become intelligible when they are
represented as connected entities rather than isolated phrases. Adobe’s description of
knowledge graphs similarly emphasizes entities and relationships as the elements through
which information becomes connected and interpretable.
6.6 Human-AI Trust and Trust Calibration
Trust calibration concerns the correspondence between reliance and actual system capability.
Within ABLF, consumers should neither blindly follow nor reflexively reject agentic
recommendations. Transparent sourcing, explanations, disclosure of uncertainty, contestability,
and opportunities for verification can support appropriate reliance.

6.7 Institutional and Legitimacy Perspectives
Some brands gain visibility because they are recognized by institutions, regulators,
professional bodies, credible publishers, or established stakeholder communities. Legitimacy
perspectives help explain why digital authority and external corroboration influence how a
brand is represented.
These theories also reinforce an ethical requirement. Algorithmic brand legibility should reflect
genuine legitimacy rather than simulated authority or manipulated evidence.

7. Revised Algorithmic Brand Legibility Framework
Traditional branding models were originally designed for human audiences. However, the
growing adoption of generative AI, conversational search engines, and autonomous
recommendation systems has created a new environment in which brands must communicate
simultaneously with human consumers and machine agents.
The growing significance of machine-facing branding is further reinforced by emerging
scholarship on agentic artificial intelligence. The Future Business Journal  special issue on
Agentic AI in Marketing highlights a fundamental shift in market interactions, where AI agents
increasingly act on behalf of consumers by searching, evaluating, comparing, and
recommending products and services. The call for papers emphasizes new challenges relating
to delegated consumer agency, brand stewardship, algorithmic contestability, and sustainable
value creation in AI-mediated markets (Future Business Journal, 2026).
To address these emerging challenges, this article proposes the Revised Algorithmic Brand
Legibility Framework, which explains how organizations can enhance machine interpretability,
algorithmic visibility, recommendation confidence, and trustworthiness in AI-mediated
decision environments.
The Revised Algorithmic Brand Legibility Framework offers a comprehensive view of the
mechanisms through which brands become understandable, verifiable, and recommendable
within AI-driven ecosystems, as summarized in figure 3 below.

Framework Explanation
The framework begins with eight Machine-Facing Branding capabilities. These capabilities
increase the possibility that an AI agent can identify and contextualize the brand accurately.
Together, they form algorithmic brand legibility.
Legibility is proposed to improve agent interpretation quality. An agent with a more complete
and evidence-grounded brand representation may be better positioned to evaluate whether the
offering satisfies a user’s stated requirements. Where the interpretation is favorable and
relevant, recommendation confidence may increase.
Recommendation confidence influences whether the brand is included within an AI-mediated
consideration set. However, consumer response is not automatic. Consumers may accept,
verify, challenge, or reject the recommendation depending on AI literacy, perceived risk, trust,
explanation quality, brand familiarity, and the consequences of the decision.
Brand preference and equity emerge only when machine-generated representations are rein forced by
credible evidence and satisfactory stakeholder experience. Algorithmic legibilit y should therefore be
understood as an enabling condition, not a substitute for product quality or ethical conduct.

Interpretive Paragraph
The transition toward Machine-Facing Branding should not be interpreted as a complete
replacement for earlier branding paradigms. Product performance remains necessary, human
consumers continue to construct meaning, and digital platforms remain influential. The
emerging paradigm adds a machine-mediated layer through which brands are retrieved,
interpreted, and recommended. Contemporary brand management must therefore coordinate
product truth, human meaning, digital experience, platform visibility, and algorithmic
legibility.

9. Expanded ABLF Dimensions
9.1 Semantic Clarity
Semantic clarity is the consistency and precision with which a brand explains its identity,
offering, audience, capabilities, and value proposition. Ambiguous terminology, unexplained
acronyms, conflicting descriptions, and generic claims can weaken interpretation.
9.2 Structured Knowledge Representation
Structured knowledge representation refers to the coherent organization of brand information
into identifiable attributes and relationships. It includes appropriately structured product
information, entities, metadata, relationships, and accessible factual content.
9.3 Entity Identity and Disambiguation
Entity identity concerns whether the brand can be recognized as a distinct organization or
offering. Disambiguation becomes important where similar names, subsidiaries, markets, or
products could be confused.
9.4 Digital Authority
Digital authority reflects the credibility and relevance of the evidence associated with a brand
across owned and independent sources. It includes expert authorship, legitimate institutional
recognition, high-quality publications, credible media coverage, verified profiles, and authentic
stakeholder evidence.
9.5 Verifiable Trust Signals
Trust signals include substantiated certifications, transparent policies, service guarantees,
verified reviews, professional qualifications, regulatory information, and documented
performance claims.
9.6 Contextual Consistency

Contextual consistency is the alignment of brand facts, descriptions, claims, identity, and
positioning across websites, social channels, directories, publications, commerce platforms,
and external sources.
9.7 Transparency and Disclosure
Transparency concerns accessible information about ownership, methods, policies, pricing
where relevant, limitations, AI use, data practices, conflicts of interest, and the basis of claims.
9.8 Technical and Retrieval Accessibility
Technical accessibility refers to the extent to which legitimate brand information is available
for indexing, retrieval, parsing, and interpretation. It can include well-organized page
structures, accessible text, stable URLs, and appropriate structured representation.

11. Discussion
11.1 From Brand Communication to Brand Interpretability
The ABLF changes the focal question in brand communication. Traditional branding asks how
a brand can attract attention, create meaning, stimulate memory, and build an emotional
relationship. Machine-Facing Branding adds the question of whether an intelligent system can
correctly interpret the brand’s identity, evidence, suitability, and relationships.
This shift does not reduce branding to metadata. Machines encounter brands through
information ecosystems composed of websites, product databases, external publications,
customer reviews, regulatory information, media narratives, structured data, and platform
interactions. Machine-facing brand strategy must therefore coordinate the entire evidence
environment.
11.2 Distinguishing Visibility from Legibility
One of the paper’s important theoretical distinctions is between visibility and legibility.
Visibility concerns whether the brand appears. Legibility concerns whether the brand is
understood accurately. A visible but poorly interpreted brand may be associated with the wrong
category, geography, audience, capability, or value proposition. Conversely, a well-defined
brand may remain absent from an output if the agent cannot retrieve relevant evidence or if the
offering does not meet the user’s constraints.
This distinction extends GEO research. GEO addresses inclusion and visibility in generated
responses, whereas ABLF explains the brand-level capabilities that support accurate
interpretation and contextually appropriate recommendation.

11.3 Brands as Dual-Audience Systems

ABLF introduces dual-audience brand management. Human audiences seek relevance,
meaning, emotion, authenticity, identity, and experience. Machine audiences require clarity,
structure, evidence, relationships, consistency, and accessibility.
The two audiences should not be treated as opposing priorities. Human-centered brand
narratives can be supported by structured factual explanations. Distinctive brand identity can
coexist with precise product specifications. Emotional value can be accompanied by
transparent evidence. The strategic task is to preserve human meaning while enabling machine
interpretation.
11.4 Recommendation Confidence as an Intermediary Mechanism
The framework proposes recommendation confidence as a bridge between interpretation and
consumer consideration. An intelligent system may recognize a brand but remain unable to
recommend it because relevant information is missing, contradictory, unsupported, outdated,
or unsuitable for the specified task.
Recommendation confidence therefore depends not merely on being known but on being
sufficiently understood and evidenced within the decision context. This offers a theoretical
explanation for why content volume alone may be inadequate. More information does not
necessarily produce greater clarity if the information is repetitive, generic, contradictory, or
weakly substantiated.
11.5 Representational Fidelity and Authenticity
Representational fidelity connects Machine-Facing Branding with authenticity. A brand may
optimize its information structure, but the resulting AI representation should remain faithful to
actual identity and performance. Where algorithmic representation exaggerates brand
capability, fails to disclose limitations, or reproduces inaccurate claims, short-term visibility
can create long-term trust damage.
ABLF therefore rejects the premise that successful MFB means manipulating AI systems into
recommending the brand. Responsible MFB means enabling agents to represent the brand
clearly and appropriately when it genuinely fits the user’s needs.
11.6 The Role of Consumer Agency
Agentic intermediation can improve convenience but may also narrow consumer exposure. If
an agent presents only a small number of alternatives, exclusion from the machine-generated
set may have greater consequences than a lower position in a conventional search result.
Consumers require the ability to understand why an offering was recommended, which criteria
were applied, which sources supported the recommendation, and whether commercial
relationships shaped the result. The special issue similarly emphasizes delegated agency,
autonomy displacement, restricted consideration sets, behavioral steering, and algorithmic
contestability.

12. Managerial Implications
12.1 Conduct an Algorithmic Brand Legibility Audit
Organizations should evaluate whether AI systems can identify the organization correctly,
distinguish it from similarly named entities, describe its offerings accurately, associate it with
appropriate audiences, and support statements with credible sources.
The audit should examine:
• Brand-name consistency
• Product and service descriptions
• Entity ambiguity
• Outdated facts
• Contradictory claims
• Missing policies

• Unverified statements
• Weak external corroboration
• Accessibility of essential information
• AI-generated misrepresentations
12.2 Develop a Canonical Brand Knowledge Base
Organizations should maintain an approved source of core brand facts covering identity,
ownership, offerings, audiences, locations, leadership, policies, certifications, terminology, and
evidence. This knowledge base should support consistency across human- and machine-facing
channels.
12.3 Align Brand Promise with Observable Performance
Agentic systems may synthesize brand-controlled claims with customer reviews, complaints,
service records, media coverage, and other evidence. Organizations should therefore ensure
that communications are aligned with product and service delivery. BCG similarly emphasizes
the need to align what brands say, what they do, and what customers experience.
12.4 Move from Keyword Ownership to Entity Clarity
Brand managers should supplement keyword strategies with clear entity relationships. The
organization should be consistently connected with its:
• Products and services
• Founders and experts
• Categories and sectors
• Locations
• Certifications
• Publications
• Policies
• Audiences
• Authorized partners
• Original frameworks or intellectual property
12.5 Optimize for Answers Without Sacrificing Meaning
Content should include concise definitions, structured explanations, verifiable evidence,
appropriate citations, comparison criteria, limitations, and clearly identified authorship.
However, organizations should avoid producing generic content designed solely to attract
algorithmic attention.
12.6 Monitor AI-Mediated Reputation
Traditional media and search monitoring should be complemented by periodic evaluation of:
• Brand mentions in generated answers

• Accuracy of brand descriptions
• Sources used
• Context of recommendations
• Competitor inclusion
• Misattribution
• Missing or outdated information
• Variability across agents and prompts
No single generated answer should be treated as a definitive reputation score because outputs
may vary by system, prompt, location, context, and retrieval conditions.
12.7 Establish Algorithmic Brand Governance
Organizations should define responsibility for:
• Canonical brand facts
• Structured information
• Claims verification
• AI-use disclosure
• Correction of inaccuracies
• Brand-response protocols
• External-source monitoring
• Data ethics
• Agentic-commerce participation
• Human escalation
12.8 Preserve Human Distinctiveness
Machine readability should not lead to standardized, undifferentiated branding. The brand’s
purpose, values, voice, culture, customer understanding, and creative expression remain
central. MFB should protect distinctive meaning while improving factual clarity.

13. Ethical and Policy Implications
Machine-Facing Branding creates risks of optimization without accountability. Organizations
may be tempted to manufacture apparent authority, flood digital environments with repetitive
content, conceal sponsorship, manipulate reviews, or structure ambiguous claims to influence
AI recommendations.
Responsible MFB should therefore follow six principles:
1. Truthfulness: Claims should be accurate and supportable.
2. Traceability: Important evidence should be attributable.

3. Transparency: Relevant commercial, AI, and data practices should be disclosed.
4. Contestability: Incorrect representations should be correctable.
5. Fairness: Optimization should not rely on discriminatory exclusion or manipulated
evidence.
6. Human agency: Users should retain meaningful oversight for consequential decisions.
The objective of MFB is not to make agents recommend a brand regardless of suitability. It is
to enable accurate brand interpretation and appropriate inclusion when the brand genuinely
meets the user’s needs.

16. Recommended Limitations Section
This paper is conceptual and therefore does not provide empirical validation of the proposed
constructs or causal relationships. Machine-Facing Branding and algorithmic brand legibility
are newly proposed concepts whose dimensionality, discriminant validity, and predictive value
require systematic testing.
The framework also simplifies a technically heterogeneous environment. AI assistants,
generative search systems, recommender systems, shopping agents, and autonomous
transaction agents may rely on different data, retrieval architectures, models, platform rules,
and commercial incentives. A brand that is legible to one system may not be interpreted
identically by another.
Furthermore, the internal processes through which proprietary AI systems retrieve information
and produce recommendations are not always transparent. The relationship between particular
brand signals and recommendation outputs may therefore be probabilistic rather than
deterministic.
Finally, the framework primarily examines legitimate organizational efforts to improve
interpretability. It does not fully resolve risks involving synthetic authority, manipulated
reviews, content flooding, platform self-preferencing, algorithmic discrimination,
misinformation, hallucination, privacy, and the exclusion of smaller or less digitally resourced
brands.

17. Revised Conclusion
Agentic AI marks a fundamental transition in the organization of marketplace information and
consumer choice. Brands are increasingly encountered not only through direct communication,

human search, advertising, and lived experience, but also through AI-generated interpretations,
comparisons, summaries, and recommendations.
This paper introduced Machine-Facing Branding as the strategic management of brand
information and evidence for accurate machine recognition, interpretation, comparison, and
contextual appropriate recommendation. It also proposed the Algorithmic Brand Legibility
Framework, which connects semantic clarity, structured knowledge representation, entity
identity, contextual consistency, digital authority, verifiable trust signals, transparency, and
technical accessibility with agent interpretation quality and AI recommendation confidence.
The framework does not suggest that brands should prioritize machines over people. Instead,
it presents contemporary branding as a dual-audience responsibility. Brands must preserve
human meaning, authenticity, emotion, and experience while making their identities and
evidence sufficiently clear for machine-mediated environments.
Algorithmic visibility without authenticity may generate exposure but weaken trust. Human
salience without algorithmic legibility may preserve brand meaning but reduce inclusion in
agent-mediated decisions. Sustainable brand advantage will therefore depend on the integration
of consumer-facing distinctiveness, verifiable organizational performance, machine-
interpretable evidence, and responsible AI governance.
Machine-Facing Branding represents not simply another digital marketing tactic but an
emerging theoretical domain. Its development invites marketing scholars to reconsider brand
audience, intermediation, decision rights, visibility, trust, and equity in markets increasingly
shaped by intelligent agents.
