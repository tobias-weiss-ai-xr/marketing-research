Inclusive
sensory design in phygital retail is increasingly relevant as retail
environments combine physical and digital service interactions that rely on
coordinated sensory conditions. Anchored in the European Accessibility Act
(EAA), applicable from 28 June 2025, this chapter argues that compliance focused
primarily on digital interfaces and self-service touchpoints is necessary but
insufficient when environmental sensory conditions – such as lighting, sound, scent,
visual density, and crowd dynamics – limit participation in retail services. The
chapter integrates a critical literature review across multisensory retail
environments, servicescape research, human-centered design, and accessibility and
neurodiversity studies, together with documentary case studies of sensory-inclusive
retail practices. Based on this synthesis, it proposes performance-based regulatory
guidelines capable of preserving brand expression while ensuring choice,
controllability, predictability, and phygital equivalence in access to services. The
chapter introduces a sensory-accessibility taxonomy, a comparative case analysis
template, a governance checklist for designers and retailers, and a Sensory
Accessibility Statement template designed to support transparent compliance and
inclusive innovation in multisensory retail environments.
Keywords: phygital retail, inclusive sensory design, European Accessibility Act,
brand experience, sensory accessibility1.
IntroductionContemporary
retail environments increasingly operate as phygital service
systems, in which physical and digital layers interact to shape how customers
perceive, navigate, and participate in retail experiences. Within these environments,
sensory conditions – such as lighting, soundscapes, scent, materiality, and dynamic
media – are orchestrated alongside digital touchpoints, including mobile interactions,
digital signage, and self-service terminals. Consequently, sensory design cannot be
understood merely as an esthetic layer. Instead, it functions as operational 1

infrastructure
that supports orientation, legibility, reassurance, and memorability
within brand experiences.
The influence of physical surroundi
ngs on behavior and service outcomes has long been recognized in the servicescape
tradition, which conceptualizes the service environment as shaping both customer and
employee responses and, ultimately, service performance [1]. In retail contexts,
environmental cues also function as informational signals through which customers
infer service quality and store image [2]. Building on these perspectives, this chapter
proposes a conceptual model describing how sensory conditions intersect with
accessibility governance in phygital retail environments (Figure 1).
In phygital retail settings, this relationship becomes particularly significant
because sensory conditions directly influence the usability of digital touchpoints
embedded within physical environments. Excessive glare may undermine the
readability of screens, ambient soundscapes may mask audio instructions, and visual
clutter may increase cognitive load during service interactions. When such
conditions occur, sensory design ceases to be a matter of atmosphere and becomes
a determinant of effective access to retail services.
This issue becomes increasingly salient as accessibility regulation expands beyond
public-sector contexts into private-sector services. The European Accessibility Act
(Directive (EU) 2019/882) harmonizes accessibility requirements for specific
products and services across the European Union and will apply from 28 June 2025
[3]. Its scope is directly relevant to phygital retail environments because it includes

mobile applications, digital signage, and automated payment systems. This
integration creates retail environments in which physical and digital interactions
occur simultaneously and influence the overall accessibility of services.
1.1 Research problem and questions
Accessibility compliance in retail environments is frequently interpreted through an
interface-centered perspective, while sensory conditions are often treated as matters of
atmosphere or individual preference. In phygital retail environments, however, this
distinction becomes increasingly untenable. Environmental sensory conditions can
directly enable or disable access to digital touchpoints and core service interactions.
This chapter, therefore, addresses the following research questions:
a. How can sensory design in phygital retail be conceptualized simultaneously as
a driver of brand experience and as accessibility infrastructure?
b. Which provisions within the European Accessibility Act already imply multisen -
sory access and alternatives to single-modality interaction, and where do gaps
remain regarding environmental sensory conditions in retail contexts? [ 3, 4]
c. What recurring patterns in documented retail cases demonstrate inclusion through
sensory modulation and sensory augmentation without erasing brand expression?
[5–9]
d. What forms of performance-based regulatory guidance and governance instru -
ments could support both policy development and design practice in multisensory
phygital environments?

Methodology2.1
Research design
This chapter adopts a qualitative, non-interventionist research design, combining
two complementary approaches: (1) a critical narrative literature review aimed at
conceptual consolidation and gap identification, and (2) documentary case studies
based on publicly available sources.
This methodological structure aligns with the chapter’s regulatory and governance-
oriented objectives. Rather than generating new experimental measurements or
behavioral effect sizes, the research seeks to translate existing empirical and conceptual
knowledge into performance-based guidance capable of informing accessibility
governance and design practice in multisensory retail environments.
The approach, therefore, emphasizes interpretive synthesis and regulatory
translation, positioning design knowledge as a bridge between academic evidence
and policy-oriented implementation.

The first stream concerns service environments and servicescapes, which establish
how environmental conditions shape behavior, perceptions, and service outcomes
[1]. The second stream addresses retail atmospherics and multisensory store
environments, consolidating evidence that coordinated sensory variables influence
customer evaluations and behavioral responses [ 10, 11].The

2.3 Documentary case studies: Selection and sources
To complement the conceptual synthesis provided by the literature review, the
chapter analyzes a set of documentary case studies illustrating inclusive sensory
interventions within retail environments.
Cases were selected according to three criteria. First, each case needed to
demonstrate an explicit sensory intervention within the retail environment. Second,
the intervention had to involve a clear accessibility or inclusion objective, either
explicitly stated or strongly implied by the implementation. Third, the intervention
needed to relate to phygital retail interactions, particularly situations where
environmental sensory conditions intersect with digital or service touchpoints.
Based on these criteria, four cases were selected:●
Tesco’s “Quiet Hour” initiative, representing scheduled sensory modulation in
supermarket environments [5];●

inclusive navigation tools [ 8];●
Mastercard’s Touch Card, developed in collaboration with accessibility organi-
zations enables tactile differentiation of payment cards and increases payment
independence [9, 17].
These cases collectively illustrate different strategies for addressing sensory
accessibility, ranging from stimulus modulation to sensory augmentation and
multimodal interaction design.

3.1 Phygital retail as a multisensory service system
Servicescape theory establishes that environmental dimensions – ambient
conditions, spatial layout, and social context – shape cognition, affect, and behavior
within service environments, ultimately influencing satisfaction and service outcomes
[1]. Within retail contexts, environmental cues also function as informational signals
through which customers infer service quality and store image [2].
Environmental psychology provides further explanation for why sensory intensity
and unpredictability may influence behavior in these environments. The PAD
framework, for instance, links environmental stimuli to emotional states that shape
approach–avoidance responses [12]. In retail environments, such responses can translate
into reduced time spent in-store, diminished willingness to engage with self-service
technologies, and lower levels of perceived autonomy during service interactions.
Evidence from retail atmospherics research reinforces the multisensory nature of
these dynamics. Reviews of experimental studies demonstrate that environmental
variables influence shopping behavior through multiple mechanisms, including
attentional processes, affective responses, and interpretive meaning-making [ 10].
From a multisensory perspective, store atmospherics therefore emerge not from
isolated stimuli but from the coordination of multiple sensory variables operating
simultaneously within the retail environment [ 11].
Taken together, these findings support an important interpretive shift. Sensory
design should not be treated solely as a matter of atmosphere or esthetic
enhancement; rather, it constitutes a functional layer of service performance and
brand experience. When sensory conditions interfere with the completion of access-
critical tasks – such as navigation, product information retrieval, or payment
interactions – they become directly relevant to accessibility governance.
3.2 Human-centered design and accessibility as operational requirements
Human-centered design frameworks reinforce the importance of understanding
the conditions under which interactions occur. ISO 9,241-210 emphasizes that
effective design depends on analyzing users, tasks, and the context of use within real-
world environments [13].
In phygital retail settings, the context of use necessarily includes sensory
conditions such as glare, soundscape fluctuations, information density, and crowd The

These factors determine whether interactions with digital touchpoints –
such as kiosks, payment terminals, or mobile interfaces – are practically achievable

From a regulatory perspective, the European Accessibility Act provides the principal
legal framework governing accessibility requirements for a range of products and
services across the European Union, with implementation beginning on 28 June 2025
[3]. For ICT products and services, accessibility requirements are commonly
operationalized through standards such as EN 301 549, which specifies accessibility
criteria and evaluation approaches widely used in European compliance contexts [14].
However, a critical challenge emerges in phygital retail environments. Even
when digital interfaces conform to accessibility standards, effective accessibility may
still fail if environmental conditions undermine usability at the point of interaction.
Excessive glare, high ambient noise, or visually saturated environments may
render accessible interfaces difficult – or impossible – to use in practice.
3.3 Dissecting the European accessibility act through a sensory lens
Although the European Accessibility Act is frequently interpreted as a regulatory
framework primarily concerned with digital accessibility, its provisions already embed
a multisensory logic that is highly relevant to physical–digital service environments. In
particular, Annex I of the Directive, which defines the functional accessibility
requirements that products and services must fulfill, includes provisions stating that
information necessary for operation, control, and orientation should be available
through more than one sensory channel and that alternatives should exist when
interactions depend on specific modalities such as vision, auditory perception, speech,
or tactile interaction [3, 4]. The Directive also specifies that situations requiring timed
responses should provide alerts through multiple sensory channels [3, 4].

3.4 Sensory variability, neurodiversity, and inclusion beyond “quiet”
Evidence addressing autism and retail environments indicates that sensory
triggers frequently include bright lighting, loud or unpredictable noise, strong
smells, and rapidly changing environmental conditions. These findings suggest that
inclusive retail environments require more than the provision of occasional “quiet
hours” alone [15]. Rather than assuming that stimulus reduction is universally
sufficient, inclusive sensory design must address a broader range of sensory needs
through mechanisms such as choice, predictability, controllability, and redundancy
across sensory channels.
Design-oriented frameworks developed for autism-inclusive built environments
further reinforce this perspective. For example, the ASPECTSS framework
highlights the importance of acoustic control, sensory zoning, transition spaces, and
opportunities for sensory relief within built environments [ 16]. These approaches
indicate that inclusion depends not only on reducing environmental stimuli but also
on structuring environments in ways that allow users to regulate their sensory
exposure. As a result, inclusive sensory design in retail contexts requires governance
mechanisms capable of addressing environmental conditions systematically, rather
than relying on isolated adjustments.
Neurodivergent conditions, such as autism spectrum conditions or attention
deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), may involve heightened sensitivity to
sensory stimuli, including lighting intensity, sound levels, visual complexity, or
sudden environmental changes. In retail environments, these sensitivities can affect
orientation, comfort, and the ability to complete service interactions. Recognizing
these variations reinforces the importance of providing sensory choice,
predictability, and controllability within multisensory retail environments [ 5].

The first principle, meaningful stimulus, emphasizes that sensory stimuli should
serve explicit experiential or functional purposes rather than generating unnecessary
sensory intensity. In retail environments, sensory variables such as lighting, sound,
or dynamic media often contribute to brand expression and spatial orientation;
however, these stimuli should not interfere with access-critical tasks such as
navigation, product information retrieval, or payment interactions.
The second principle, choice and controllability, requires that equivalent
pathways exist for accessing core retail services. Customers should be able to
complete essential interactions through different modes, including assisted service
pathways, moderated sensory modes, or alternative interaction channels.
The third principle, predictability and transparency, highlights the importance of
communicating sensory conditions and accessibility options in advance. Providing
clear information about sensory environments – such as quieter time periods,
sensory zoning, or alternative service options – allows customers to plan their visits
and select environments that better suit their sensory preferences.

not undermine the effective accessibility of digital touchpoints embedded in physical
retail environments. Even when digital interfaces comply with accessibility
standards, environmental factors such as glare, excessive noise, or visual saturation
may compromise usability in practice. Ensuring phygital equivalence therefore,
requires evaluating accessibility under realistic environmental conditions rather than
solely through interface-level compliance.

Discussion5.1
Sensory design as brand value and access infrastructure
Research on service environments demonstrates that physical settings influence
customer behavior, perceptions, and service outcomes [ 1]. Within retail contexts,
environmental cues also shape quality inference and store image, reinforcing the
interpretation of sensory environments as functional components of brand
experience [2].
Building on these foundations, this chapter argues that sensory design in
phygital retail should also be understood as access infrastructure. When
environmental conditions support orientation, comprehension, and interaction,
they enable customers to complete service tasks independently. Conversely,
when sensory environments interfere with perception or interaction,
they may undermine the usability of digital touchpoints embedded in the
retail environment.
5.2 What the EAA already implies, and what remains under-specified
The multisensory provisions embedded in Annex I of the European
Accessibility Act require that information for operation, orientation, and control
be available through multiple sensory channels, and that alternatives exist for
modality-dependent interaction [3, 18]. These requirements demonstrate that
accessibility regulation already recognizes the importance of multimodal
communication.
However, the environmental contexts in which these interactions occur remain
under-specified. In phygital retail environments, sensory conditions such as glare,
background noise, or visual clutter may effectively negate the intended accessibility
modality. Addressing this gap, therefore, requires governance approaches that
extend accessibility considerations beyond interface design to include
environmental sensory conditions.
5.3 Case patterns: Modulation and augmentation without erasing brand
expression
The documentary cases analyzed in this chapter reveal two complementary
strategies for inclusive sensory design. The first involves sensory modulation,
implemented through predictable and scheduled adjustments to environmental
stimuli, as illustrated by Tesco’s Quiet Hour and Walmart’s Sensory-Friendly
Hours [6–8].
The second involves sensory augmentation, where additional sensory channels are
introduced to support interaction and orientation, as demonstrated by IKEA’s tactile Inclusive

Together, these strategies demonstrate that inclusive sensory design does not require
eliminating sensory richness or brand expression. Instead, it involves governing
sensory conditions in ways that allow customers to access services through multiple
sensory pathways.
5.4 Why performance-based regulation is appropriate
Retail environments vary widely in spatial configuration, brand identity, and
experiential design strategies. Prescriptive regulatory approaches risk homogenizing
retail environments and constraining creative expression.
Performance-based regulation provides a more flexible alternative by specifying
accessibility outcomes rather than prescribing exact environmental conditions. As
shown in Table 4, the principles proposed in this chapter link accessibility objectives
to auditable evidence and governance mechanisms while allowing variability in
sensory design.
5.5 Methodological reflections and limitations
Because this chapter relies on literature synthesis and documentary case
evidence, it does not propose universal numerical thresholds for environmental
variables such as lux levels, decibel limits, or scent intensity. Instead, the framework
prioritizes governance instruments and performance outcomes that can be adapted
across diverse retail contexts.
In this sense, the chapter should be understood primarily as an explanatory
and interpretive contribution. Its purpose is not to prescribe fixed sensory standards
but to clarify how sensory conditions interact with accessibility requirements in
phygital retail environments. By articulating concepts from multisensory design,
accessibility regulation, and retail practice, the framework highlights the role of
sensory environments as operational conditions that can enable or constrain
participation in retail services.
Consequently, the proposed framework emphasizes governance
mechanisms, transparency instruments, and performance-based
principles rather than fixed environmental thresholds. This approach allows
accessibility considerations to be integrated into diverse retail environments
while preserving variability in spatial configuration, brand expression, and
experiential design.6.
