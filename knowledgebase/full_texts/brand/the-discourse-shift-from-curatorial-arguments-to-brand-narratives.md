# The Discourse Shift from Curatorial Arguments to Brand Narratives

Academic Journal of Art and Design
ISSN: 3080-9924 (Print), ISSN: 3080-9932 (Online) | V ol. 1, No. 3, 2026

1
The	Discourse	Shift	from	Curatorial	Arguments	to
Brand	Narratives
Chenxiao Du
School of Nuova Accademia di Belle Arti milan Italy

Abstract: Determine if, after transitioning  from art sponsorship to exhib ition production, the curatorial discourse in brand
museums has shifted from a curation-centric mode to a branding- led narrative system. Museum branding and brand museums
are not the same phenomenon; therefore, this study focuses on brand-founded exhibition spaces that have become cultural agents
for a commercial brand. According to Paul O'Neill's idea of the curatorial turn, the paper defines curatorial argument as the body
of knowledge claims that can be produced through selection, organisation and space in an exhibition, and exhibition narrative is
regarded as a way to present such a claim to the audience. WA VES at Casa Sanlorenzo in Venice and Sueño Perro at Fondazione
Prada in Milan will be selected as the subjects for a comparati ve case study. Based on the above analysis, WA VES generally
creates an aesthetically pleasing environment in line with the luxury concept of identity, whereas Sueño Perro shows stronger
characteristics of archival and media specificity. The paper does not think that brand narrative will automatically substitute for a
curatorial argument; instead, it demonstrates that when aesthetic unity is emphasized over critical propositions, brand narrative
may stand out.
Keywords: Brand museum; curatorial argument; exhibition narrative; luxury brand; Casa Sanlorenzo; Fondazione Prada; Paul
O'Neill.

1. Introduction
The top-tier luxury brands are gradually moving away from
endorsements and charitable foundations to renovations,
culture spaces, self-operated exhibition areas, etc. If the brand
leads at the exhibition, the curatorial text may move away
from showcasing art and focus instead on promoting the
brand's cultural legitimacy and public awareness.
The subject of this study is the brand museum or the brand-
established cultural institu tion; it is a place where a
commercial brand serves as a cultural subject. Therefore, the
research problem is as follows: When a luxury brand switches
from being a sponsor of an exhibition to the subject of
exhibition creation, does the curatorial text change from
presenting an argument for selection to building a brand
narrative?
To address the above problem, this paper compares
WA VES at Casa Sanlorenzo in Venice and Sueño Perro:
Instalación Celuloide by Alejandro G. Iñárritu at Fondazione
Prada, Milan. Both are related to the cultural platforms of
luxury brands and use immersive spatial language, but their
language strategies are not the same. WA VES creates a soft
sensory world for the wave, and Sueño Perro is organised
around archival footage, 35mm materiality, and the memory
of Amores Perros.
Section 2 shows the theoretical differences among
curatorial argument, exhibition narrative and brand narrative.
Section 3 Introduces the selected cases and analysis methods.
Section 4 introduces the two exhibitions. Section 5 is the
implication for Brand Museums. Section 6 concludes that the
main problem is not whether a brand has been mentioned, but
whether the exhibition still makes a claim that goes beyond
what the brand has stated itself.
2. Theoretical Framework: Curatorial
Argument, Exhibition Narrative and
Brand Narrative
2.1. Curatorial argument
Paul O'Neill's talk of the curatorial turn is good: exhibitions
are not neutral; they also have their own talk. Curation is not
merely the selection of works; it is the construction of an idea
by choosing some works and not others, arranging them in
space, and guiding the audience' s experience of them [1].
O'Neill's later works have also pointed out that curators are
now visible producers of culture rather than hidden custodians
of collections [2]. Curatorial argument refers to the main
claim or critical proposition that an exhibition puts forward in
this paper. The supporting materials for the evidence include
written text, objects, space-tim e sequence, material contrast,
light and shadow, sound and rhythm, etc. Why are these works
displayed together, and what problem does the exhibition
hope to address? Therefore, the definition does not need to be
a general mood. A single show does not need to be a lecture.
It is argumentative when the relationship among the works
raises a question, a tension or a position that can be discussed
outside of aesthetic appreciation.
2.2. Exhibition narrative
Exhibition narrative is not the same as curatorial argument,
but they are related. If curatorial argument is the main idea,
then exhibition narrative is the space and language that help
visitors experience this idea. According to Mieke Bal's idea
of exhibition as a series of encounters, meaning is produced
through movement, alteration and changes in the perception
of objects experienced by visitors [3]. Macdonald and Basu
also believe that exhibitions gather people, objects, ideas,
texts, spaces and media to create a special knowledge
situation [4].
The narrative of the exhibition includes wall texts, captions,

2
the arrangement of galleries, spatial division, sound design,
colour, light and visual dialogue, etc., all of which attract the
public's attention. If it only c reates emotions, lifestyles or a
sense of belonging, the exhibition will be understandable but
lack critical depth.
2.3. Brand narrative and brand museums
A brand story is also a type of speech. It does not usually
ask what the work is about; instead, it seeks to determine how
the institution or brand should be known. Research on
corporate museums has found that these spaces help organise
the company's memory, identity and selective history [5].
Research on the brand identity  of art museums shows that
institutional identity can be built through products, services,
symbols, people and organisation characteristics [6].
Curatorship in the luxury sector is also employed to build an
all-encompassing brand experience and goes beyond
traditional museum work [7].
Museum branding and a brand museum are not the same
thing. The start of museum branding was a wish for public
recognition. A brand museum begins with a brand and creates
cultural legitimacy through museum-like forms. Cultural
heritage and organisational memory can be turned into
valuable brand assets in such places [8]. Recently, scholars of
brand museums have proposed that they can commodify and
disseminate organisational heritage through historical
artifacts, interactive displays and narratives of innovation, as
well as visitor co-creation [9].
The brand's story is not inherently negative. A brand
museum can offer resources, exposure and space for
experimentation. The problem is that the exhibition's
argument is reduced to a demand for brand consistency; if the
works serve mainly to reinforce the brand's atmosphere,
values or lifestyle image, the curatorial discourse becomes
mere support for brand narrative.
3. Case Selection and Analytical
Approach
This paper will use a qualitative comparative case study.
The two cases are luxury brand cultural institutions that differ
in the structure of their discourse. Sanlorenzo Arts presented
WA VES at Casa Sanlorenzo in Venice from May 6 to June 28,
2026, for the 61st International Art Exhibition of La Biennale
di Venezia. Sergio Risaliti and Cristiano Seganfreddo were
the curators of the group exhibition, and works by Alexander
Calder, Lucio Fontana, Fausto Melotti, Tony Cragg, Christine
Safa, Marcello Maloberti and Friedrich Andreoni from
different sections of Casa Sanlorenzo [10] were shown in the
interior, garden and bridge areas. Almine Rech's exhibition
information also lists WA VES as a group exhibition supported
by the science of Ersilia Vaudo Scarpetta and a dialogue
among modern masters and contemporary artists [11].
Analysis of WA VES also incorporates the author's own
exhibition experiences and observations at the scene to
describe things such as the mood in a certain place,
relationships among works, and visitors' emotions at Casa
Sanlorenzo.
The second is Sueño Perro: Celuloide Installation by
Alejandro G. Iñárritu: Shown at Fondazione Prada in Milan
from September 18, 2025, to February 25, 2026. Fondazione
Prada has referred to it as a world-class multi-sensory
exhibition at the intersection of cinema and visual arts,
designed for the 25th anniversary of Amores Perros, and
based on never-before-seen footage in the film archives of the
National Autonomous University of Mexico [12]. LACMA's
page for the later Los Angeles screening lists that the
exhibition was jointly organised by LACMA and Fondazione
Prada and presents film as livi ng material made up of time,
light and space [13].
As with WA VES, the analysis of Sueño Perro also utilizes
the author's own visit and on-site observation at Fondazione
Prada to present a spatial sequence, as well as the materiality
of projection and the responses of visitors that institutional
texts fail to document.
The four comparison criteria for the two exhibitions are
agency, object-text relations, spatial narratives and audience
address. Agency refers to who is speaking in the exhibition
text. Object-text relations refer to whether a particular work
is interpreted as a sign of extended identity. Spatial narrative
refers to whether a place generates a sense of crisis or is more
atmospheric. Audience address refers to whether a visitor is
in a state of deep observation, actively engaging in sense-
making, or a member of a brand community.

4. Case Analysis
4.1. WA VES at Casa Sanlorenzo: the wave as
perceptual brand environment
WA VES is not a direct presentation of yachts and corporate
history. It does not have a strong brand story. The official
language presents the exhibition in waves, frequencies,
movements, changes, arts, sciences and the sea. Metaphor is
flexible, and thus it can connect Venice with water, cosmic
energy, sound, modern sculpture, and the architectural
environment of Casa Sanlorenzo [10].
A curatorial argument should s pecify what the concept of
waves enables us to explore critically or in art history. Does it
reconsider sculpture as vibration? Connecting modern
abstraction with ecology: sound and cosmology? Is it in
conflict with the association of Venice and luxurious mobility
with environmental instability? The exhibition raises these
problems, but the language used by the public is generally
limited to an accessible system. Interior space, garden, bridge,

3
artworks, sound and brand atmosphere flow together to create
a unified sensory environment.
This way is reflected in the organisation of the works.
Calder's mobile can be employed to demonstrate a problem of
motion, stability and air. Fontana's cuts can be regarded as
Spaces rather than flat pictures. Andreoni's sound installations
can make low-frequency waves tangible, and Tony Cragg's
sculptures can also give a sense of altered materials [11]. The
above connections could provide the material for a curatorial
argument on sculpture as energy and unstable space. However,
all of these works fall under the general category of WA VES
and are thus flattened; their individual tensions are reduced to
a single, shared atmosphere.
Therefore, the exhibition function is that of a brand
museum and does not take the form of a traditional company
museum. It does not have a brand archive. In turn, it will build
a brand world for the public to enjoy. All these are cultural
symbols that create a sense of luxury for Sanlorenzo: the sea,
movement, fine materials, soft light, and controlled space.
Therefore, the difference between a sponsor and a curator has
begun to disappear. Sanlorenzo no longer shows only foreign
works of art; it has created a place to experience such art.
WA VES is better for viewers to compare specific works of
art through the lens of curatorial discourse, such as a moving
Calder and an opened Fontana. It is weaker when the
exhibition language uses the wave as a universal connector.
At that time, the curatorial argument will likely be a brand
narrative of fluidity, elegance and resonance. The exhibition
does not aim to promote the brand but to offer a cultural form
of brand reassurance.
4.2. Sueño Perro at Fondazione Prada:
archival argument within a branded
institution
Sueño Perro is also a cultural institution for a high-end
brand, but its discourse work is different. Fondazione Prada's
text focuses on the artist, the cinematic medium and the
archival conditions of the material. Although it is related to
Amores Perros, the exhibition is not solely based on the
famous movie. It shows new pictures that were previously cut,
stored and later released after 2 5 years [12]. Therefore, the
exhibition has put forward a specific curatorial proposal: a
film is not a closed system, and its components can be
arranged in various other ways by means of cinema and space.
The first kind of the official account is shown in 35mm film.
Grain, flicker, warmth, projection and sound are not for
decoration; they serve to show that cinema can be
experienced as living material rather than just storytelling
[12]. LACMA's later presentation makes this argument even
more explicit by asking how many films exist within a film
and describing film as composed of time, light and space [13].
It was not dark at the construction site; it was rather close t o
being so. The visitor saw the archival fragment directly and
did not know that it belonged to a particular brand.
The Brand's Story Lives On. Fondazione Prada's
institutional identity also includes cross-disciplinary
experimental exhibitions of art, film, architecture and theory.
The cultural capital of the foundation will be increased by the
works of a foreign film director. However, the exhibition text
does not need to impress the viewer with the world of Prada.
It shows parts of life in Mexico City in 2000, including love,
betrayal, violence and the socio-political environment [12].
Thus, the brand identity supports the platform; the problem
the exhibition raises falls outside the scope of the brand. The
two are not the same. Sueño Perro is immersive but still has a
problem. A visitor's path of projections, darkness, sound and
archival images can be used to make the material history of
cinema tangible. Thus, the exhibition narrative serves to
mediate the curatorial argument rather than substitute for it.
5. Comparative Discussion
5.1. From curatorial proposition to
atmospheric coherence
The two cases differ in the extent to which the curatorial
proposal aligns with the atmosphere. O'Neill's curatorial turn
can help us understand this better: an exhibition becomes
discursive when it takes a stand through the arrangement of
works and public awareness of mediation [1, 2]. This location
is relatively exposed in Sueño Perro. The exhibition proposes
that the remains of a film can generate another kind of filmic
and spatial reality; the archival fragment is not a secondary
source but the object of inquiry itself.
The proposal is less specific in WA VES. The exhibition has
a good narrative of the display but a weak curatorial argument.
Its objects are spatially effec tive, but the wave metaphor is
too general; it may explain everything and thus fail to move
people. As Bal mentions in his talk on exhibition narration,
movement can also be a kind of affect [3]. Therefore, WA VES
is relatively convenient to add to a system of atmospheric
expressions.
5.2. Brand museum, not museum branding
Both exhibitions are brand museums in the sense defined
above; they both start with a brand and build cultural
legitimacy through a museum-like form, rather than gathering
public collections to gain recognition. However, what the two
cases show is that this common institutional form does not
determine the discursive outcome. WA VES takes the brand-
museum concept to the extreme and dissolves the claim of
exhibition into the atmosphere of fluidity and resonance of the
brand. Sueño Perro is also in the same category of brand-
founded cultural institutions, but it has shown that the form
does not automatically reduce cu ratorial discourse to brand
narrative. Museum branding and brand museums are thus
different concepts that specify institutions; they do not
indicate whether a curatorial argument will be effective. That
result is not determined by who established the space, but by
how the language of exhibition organises agency, objects,
space and address.
5.3. Does curatorial text degenerate into brand
narrative?
It may not be the case. Curatorial text is reduced to brand
narrative when the three signs appear together. First, the
exhibition concept is now a value word rather than a problem.
Secondly, the object-text relationship turns particular works
into evidence of the pre-existing brand atmosphere. Thirdly,
most of the visitors are desired to feel a sense of belonging,
refinement or immersion, rather than to face a critical
question. Only some of these signs are displayed in WA VES.
A curatorial text should be appealing to the audience and
does not need to be intellectually demanding. The required
friction may be due to missing archives, a lack of historical
materials, contradictory objects or a spatial sequence that is
not entirely uniform. Sueño Perro creates such friction by
using cut footage, cinematic materiality and the violence of
the source film; the branded institutional frame is visible, but

4
the text still exceeds institutional self-description.
In terms of theory, brand narrative and curatorial argument
do not need to be in opposition and can coexist. The brand
supplies the funds and promotional support; curation
produces knowledge. The fault is at the top level. If the brand
narrative is the destination and curatorial language is merely
decoration along the way, the exhibition will be a polished
extension of the brand. Conversely, if the curatorial argument
only sets out the problem and brand narrative does not shape
access, there will be no exhibition.
6. Conclusion
Determine whether the curatorial discourse in brand
museums has shifted from curatorial arguments to brand
narratives since luxury brands started being displayed in
museums rather than being sponsored by them. By comparing
WA VES at Casa Sanlorenzo and Sueño Perro at Fondazione
Prada, the paper shows that brand museums do not
automatically exclude curatorial argument. Organise the
display of agency, objects, s pace and audience in exhibition
language.
WA VES shows how a brand museum can use artworks as
components of an atmospheric brand environment. Although
the wave metaphor connects sc ulpture, sound, Venice and
space, it is also relatively general and can be applied to the
language of luxury, fluidity and resonance. Therefore, the
curatorial discourse is no longer a critique but a type of
cultural comfort for the brand.
Sueño Perro shows a different possibility. Although it is
under the auspices of a high-end fashion company, it focuses
on old films, uses 35mm film, and is inspired by Amores
Perros, so a sense of history can be felt. The exposition's story
aims to make readers think more profoundly about cinema,
memory and material culture, and thus departs from the
conventional exhibition of Prada's brand image.
Thus, the problem is not whether a brand is present, but
whether the exhibition can still present a claim beyond the
brand's own description. When the brand's story is merely the
destination and the curatorial language only decorates the
road, the exhibition is an elegant extension of the brand.
