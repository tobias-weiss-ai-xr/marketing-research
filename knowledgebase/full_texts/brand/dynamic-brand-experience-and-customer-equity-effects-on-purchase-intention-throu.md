Purpose: With the digitally immersive brand environment, brand-consumer
engagements have transformed from rigid brand-focused interactions into
fluid technology-enriched encounters. The present research focuses on the
impact of Dynamic Brand Experience (DBE), with AI-powered personalisation,
AR/VR interfaces, and frictionless omnichannel integration, on consumer
behavioural outcomes in India’s beauty industry. It particularly assesses how
DBE helps develop customer equity elements and how it combines with
Brand Relationship Quality (BRQ) in shaping purchase intention.
Design/methodology/approach: A cross-sectional descriptive study design was
adopted, collecting information from 413 Indian beauty product consumers
who had already experienced brand technologies. Purposive sampling was
employed to make the participants’ experiences meaningful. A structured
questionnaire was administered, and the data were analysed using partial least
squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) in SmartPLS 4, assessing the
measurement model (reliability, convergent and discriminant validity), the
structural model (R-square, effect sizes, and bootstrapped path significance),
and specific indirect effects, with common method bias evaluated through
Harman’s single-factor test. The Value Equity scale did not meet reliability
standards and was excluded from the model.
Findings: Dynamic Brand Experience shapes purchase intention chiefly by
building relationship equity (β = 0.493), which is by far the strongest
antecedent of Brand Relationship Quality (β = 0.731; f² = 1.150), and BRQ is the
dominant driver of purchase intention (β = 0.597). The specific indirect effect
RE → BRQ → PI (β = 0.437) is the single most influential pathway, with the
serial route DBE → RE → BRQ → PI (β = 0.215) also substantial. Brand Equity is
behaviourally negligible, and the Value Equity scale could not be validly
measured and was excluded; the study therefore advances no claim regarding
value equity. Moderation by income and gender is identified as a direction for
future empirical research.
Originality/value: This research contributes to theory by bringing together
Customer Equity Framework and Service-Dominant Logic in a digital brand
setting, and providing empirical evidence for the asymmetrical effects of
customer equity dimensions on consumer intention. It highlights the move
from symbolic brand heritage to value-based, emotionally resonant
experiences, especially in digitally engaged emerging economies. By explicitly
delimiting the scope of the current empirical investigation to mediation, the
study also delineates a clear agenda for future moderation research involving
income and gender as boundary conditions.

Research limitations/implications: A cross-sectional study design restricts
longitudinal insight into changing brand-consumer relationships. Experimental
or longitudinal study designs, or cross-cultural settings, in future research can
increase generalisability and identify dynamic behavioural trends. Additionally,
the moderation effects of income and gender on the BRQ–purchase intention
pathway, while theoretically conceptualised in this study, were not empirically
tested and represent a key limitation of the current work. Additionally, some
contextual data cited in this study (e.g., Deloitte, 2023; KPMG, 2020) originate
from industry reports rather than peer-reviewed sources. While these are used
solely to establish market context, future studies should anchor contextual
claims more firmly in peer-reviewed empirical literature on Indian digital
consumer behaviour.
Practical implications: This research suggests the importance for beauty retailers
to make digitally immersive experiences like AR try-ons, AI-powered
consultations, and personalised recommendation engines the focal point to tap
into consumer value and participation. Old brand equity strategies based on
heritage should give way to adaptive, personalised branding campaigns. Since
Brand Relationship Quality cannot equally mediate consumer responses,
retailers should actively seek to establish trust, satisfaction, and emotional
attachment. Firms should consider adopting a two-pronged strategy in digitally
fragmented markets like India: high-tech, immersive technologies for urban
consumers and mobile-optimized, utility-based solutions for mass segments.
Interactive, ongoing conversations through influencers and user-generated
content are among the key drivers of brand visibility and consumer co-creation
in digitally fragmented markets.

1 Introduction
The digital transformation of consumer markets has
significantly altered the nature of consumer–brand interactions,
rendering traditional models of brand experience increasingly
inadequate. Historically, brand experience has been
conceptualised around sensory and transactional dimensions,
with emphasis placed on physical stimuli and satisfaction
derived post-purchase (Brakus, Schmitt, and Zarantonello,
2009). However, the convergence of advanced technologies most
notably Artificial Intelligence (AI), Augmented Reality (AR),
Virtual Reality (VR), and real-time algorithmic personalisation
has redefined brand experience as an evolving, immersive, and
co-created process (Hollebeek, Srivastava, and Chen, 2019;
Lemon and Verhoef, 2016). This shift marks a transition from a
linear, firm-driven communication model to a two-way,
dynamically consumer-oriented ecosystem characterised by
emotional resonance, contextual relevance, and real-
time adaptability.
Within this paradigm, Dynamic Brand Experience (DBE) has
emerged as a critical framework for examining consumer-brand
engagement in digitally augmented environments. DBE extends
traditional brand experience by incorporating real-time
interactivity, AI-facilitated personalisation, and multimodal
connectivity across platforms and devices (Chung, Wedel, and
Rust, 2019; Poncin and Mimoun, 2014). These experiences span
functional, emotional, and cognitive domains, and are delivered
via synchronised, omnichannel touchpoints including AR/VR-
enabled product visualisation and personalised digital storefronts
resulting in elevated consumer involvement, trust, and loyalty
(Verhoef et al., 2015). Consistent with the scope of advertising
and marketing communication research, this study frames
Dynamic Brand Experience as a form of technology-mediated
marketing communication in which immersive, co-created
brand encounters shape consumer response, rather than as a
matter of personality or social-psychological disposition.
Analysing a decade of award-winning campaigns, Allagui (2026)
shows that effective branded communication increasingly
succeeds through immersive, authentic brand experiences built
on narrative cohesion, cultural relevance, and platform-specific
tailoring. Related work in the same journal demonstrates that
emotional storytelling and culturally resonant visual
communication strengthen brand recall, engagement, and
purchase-relevant responses (Limna, Shaengchart, and
Suradinkura, 2026; Mohamed, 2025).
Despite the increasing salience of DBE in contemporary
marketing practice, academic investigations remain fragmented.
Prior studies have often examined constructs such as brand
equity, perceived value, or customer engagement in isolation,
neglecting the interactive nature of these elements within an
integrated experiential system (Keller, 2013; Zarantonello and
Schmitt, 2010). While conceptual frameworks such as the
Customer Equity Model (Rust, Lemon and Zeithaml, 2004) and
Service-Dominant Logic (Vargo and Lusch, 2004) offer robust

2 Conceptual background and
theoretical framework
2.1 Dynamic brand experience (DBE)
Brand experience has traditionally been understood as a four-
dimensional construct containing sensory, emotional, cognitive,
and behavioural components (Brakus, Schmitt, and
Zarantonello, 2009). With the emergence of digital technology,
however, brand experience has been revolutionized into a
dynamic, adaptable, and immersive one. The dynamic brand
experience (DBE) concept encapsulates this shift with an
emphasis on AI-based customization, real-time adaptability, and
platform consistency (Chung, Wedel, and Rust, 2019; Hollebeek
and Andreassen, 2018).
It is important to distinguish DBE from the broader construct
of brand experience as traditionally conceptualised. Brakus et al.
(2009) defined brand experience as a relatively static, firm-
stimulated construct comprising sensory, affective, intellectual,
and behavioural dimensions elicited by brand-related stimuli.
DBE extends this by introducing three distinguishing features:
(a) real-time adaptability, whereby the brand interaction evolves
responsively to consumer behaviour and context rather than
being pre-scripted; (b) co-creation, whereby the consumer is an
active participant rather than a passive recipient of brand
stimuli; and (c) technological embeddedness, whereby the
experience is mediated by AI, AR/VR, and omnichannel
platforms rather than physical or static media. These
distinctions render DBE a distinct theoretical construct, not
merely a digital variant of traditional brand experience, and
justify its separate operationalisation in empirical research
(Hollebeek and Andreassen, 2018; Lemon and Verhoef, 2016).
DBE is characterized by its capacity for adaptation and
contextual pertinence. Emerging technologies, such as
augmented reality and virtual reality (AR/VR), enhance sensory
immersion further, while artificial intelligence-based
recommendation systems provide for personalised interaction
that significantly boosts perceived relevance and consumer
involvement (Poncin and Mimoun, 2014). Moreover, Lemon
and Verhoef (2016) argue that these technologies make seamless
omnichannel experiences possible, which have become the
consumer expectation standard. Whereas the growing strategic
importance of DBE has not been matched by academic research
in its full extent, especially in the emerging economies where
digital uptake is increasing.
A further conceptual boundary requires clarification,
namely the distinction between DBE and the broader notion
of digital branding or digital marketing communica tion.
Digital branding refers to channel-lev el tactics such as social
media presence, targeted advertising, and e-commerc e
storefronts through which firms disseminate brand messages
across digital media (Lemon and Verhoef, 2016). DBE is
conceptually distinct from these tactics. It is an experiential
and co-crea tive process in which value emerges from the real-
time integra tion of consumer and brand resources at the
point of interaction (Chung, Wedel, and Rust, 2019; Vargo
and Lusch, 2008). Such immersive, technology-mediated value
creation is increasingly evident in emerging retail and
metaverse contexts, where perceived relative advantage and
co-crea ted value shape adoption (Singh and Gupta, 2025),
and where technology-acceptance mechanisms continue to
structure consumer engagement with digital retail (Gupta,
Singh, and Prashar, 2025). Digital branding is therefor e better
understood as the infrastructure that may host DBE
rather than as DBE itself, because a brand can maintain an
extensiv e digital presence without delivering a dynamic,
adaptiv e, and co-crea ted experience. This distinction is
important to isolate the experiential mechanism examine d in

2.2 Customer equity dimensions
The Customer Equity Framework by Rust, Lemon and
Zeithaml (2004) categorises customer value determinants into
three interconnected elements: Value Equity, Brand Equity,
and Relationship Equity. Each element has a distinct
contribution towards shaping the behaviour of consumers and
is particularly applicable in measuring the impact of DBE.
While these dimensions have been widely studied
independently , their interaction with dynamic, immersive
branding environments remains underexplored. DBE has the
potential to activate all three equity dimensions by enriching
the customer journey across rational and emotional planes
(Verhoef et al., 2015)
2.2.1 Value equity
Value equity refers to the logical evaluation of utility by the
customer on the basis of price, quality, and convenience
perceptions. Value equity is reinforced in digitally immersive
situations by AI-driven personalization, which maximizes
functional relevance as well as perceived service effectiveness
(Choi and Lee, 2019; Kumar and Reinartz, 2016). Personalized
skincare advice based on machine learning, for example, not
only enhances perceived product fit but also boosts satisfaction
and trust. Hollebeek, Srivastava, and Chen (2019) presume that
value co-creation via digital experiences significantly contributes
to perceived value equity in high-involvement product categories
such as beauty. However, in the present study, the Value Equity
scale failed to meet acceptable internal consistency standards
(Cronbach’s α = 0.365), indicating that the items did not form a
coherent reflective construct in this sample; accordingly, Value
Equity is excluded from the empirical analysis and no claim is
advanced regarding this dimension (see Section 8.1).
2.2.2 Brand equity
Brand equity consists of the cognitive and affective links that
consumers form with a brand (Aaker, 1991; Keller, 2003). While
brand equity has been a dominant predictor of customer loyalty
and buying behaviour for many years, its influence is ever more
reliant on the creation of compelling experiences (Zarantonello
and Schmitt, 2010). In virtual environments, the brand story
and symbolic value need to be bolstered by interactive content
and experiences that are emotionally compelling. Kervyn, Fiske,
and Malone (2012) argue that if this is not done, the influence
of brand equity on consumer action may be severely eroded.
2.2.3 Relationship equity
Relationship equity captures the intensity and duration of the
affective connection between a brand and consumers. Loyalty
programs, personalised interaction, and community engagement
drive it (Hollebeek et al., 2022; Verhoef, 2003). AR/VR and
chatbot-enabled delivery of services are some of the technologies
facilitating personalization of the customer experience, thereby
increasing emotional engagement and long-term loyalty. In the
beauty retailing environment where identity expression and
emotional attachment are paramount, relationship equity is a
relevant concept in capturing how DBE is converted into loyalty
and advocacy (Dessart, Veloutsou, and Morgan-Thomas, 2016).
2.3 Brand relationship quality (BRQ) as a
mediator
Brand Relationship Quality (BRQ) is an important mediating
construct that enables a connection between brand experience and
behavioural consequences. Comprehended by constructs like trust,
satisfaction, and commitment (Fournier, 1998; Morgan and Hunt,
1994), BRQ is particularly relevant in digitally immersive
environments where traditional cues are replaced with virtual
interactions and co-created experiences. Immersive brand
experiences construct BRQ through engaging and customised
experiences that enhance transparency, satisfaction, and
emotional engagement (Lemon and Verhoef, 2016). Augmented
reality product trials or artificial intelligence-based consultations,
for example, can increase consumer confidence and satisfaction,
thus enhancing trust and commitment towards the brand.
Hollebeek et al. (2019) point out that the depth of emotion
provided by immersive branding considerably reinforces the
psychological foundation underlying BRQ. Existing studies
suggest that brand experience significantly shapes BRQ, which
in turn influences behavioural outcomes such as loyalty and
purchase intention (Dwivedi, 2015; Kim and Ko, 2012).
However, few studies have examined BRQ as a mediating
mechanism between customer equity and behavioural intention
within a technology-rich, immersive brand environment
(Dessart et al., 2016). This gap is particularly relevant in high-
involvement sectors like beauty and fashion, where emotional
engagement plays a central role in shaping purchase decisions,
therefore this study addresses this gap by positing BRQ as a
mediator that explains how customer equity dimensions
enhanced through DBE translate into purchase intention.
A central proposition of this study is that BRQ does not mediate
the customer-equity dimensions uniformly, but operates selectively
as a relational conduit that most strongly transmits relationship-
based engagement. This anticipated asymmetry rests on the
psychological distinction between cognitive appraisal and affective
bonding. Relationship equity is an affectively grounded construct
built from trust, loyalty, and sustained interaction (Rust, Lemon
and Zeithaml, 2004). Trust-formation theory holds that
behavioural commitment is motivated once relational trust and
satisfaction are internalised (Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman, 1995;
Morgan and Hunt, 1994). BRQ, which comprises trust,
satisfaction, and commitment, is therefore the natural channel
through which relationship equity is converted into intention,
because the two share the same affective-relational substrate. It
functions as the mechanism through which a favourable but
affectively neutral value appraisal acquires the relational weight
needed to shape purchase intention (Fournier, 1998). This
conditional, mediated logic is consistent with recent moderated-
mediation evidence in marketing and management research
(Singh and Parayitam, 2025).

The value-based and symbolic components of customer equity,
by contrast, are cognitively or representationally charged rather
than relationally grounded, and they engage the trust-and-
satisfaction processes that constitute BRQ only weakly. A utilitarian
appraisal of price, quality, and convenience shapes preference at a
cognitive level (Park, MacInnis, Priester, Eisingerich, and Iacobucci,
2010; Thomson, MacInnis, and Park, 2005), and consistent with
affect-as-information reasoning it does not readily convert into the
felt relational commitment that drives action (Schwarz and Clore,
1983). Symbolic and identity-based brand associations similarly
operate at a representational rather than a relational level (Keller,
2003), so in the absence of continuous experiential reinforcement
they may not activate the relational mechanism at all (Kervyn,
Fiske, and Malone, 2012). This reasoning leads to the a priori
expectation that BRQ most strongly mediates the relationship-
equity to purchase-intention path, while transmitting the value-
based and symbolic components of customer equity only weakly.
This study tests the proposed asymmetry directly.
2.4 Purchase intention
Purchase intention has been identified for decades as a strong
predictor of consumer behaviour and is a critical outcome measure
for the field of experiential marketing research (Ajzen, 1991;
Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975). In the context of immersive branding,
purchase intention is influenced not only by functional aspects
such as price and product features but also by the quality of the
experiential and relational journey (Nadeem et al., 2021). There is
evidence across a number of studies for the propositions that
perceived value and affective attachment often enhanced through
interactive digital engagement are strong purchase intention drivers
(Hollebeek, Clark, and Macky, 2022; Kumar and Reinartz, 2016).
The link between symbolic brand meaning and buying behaviour
is, however, more and more moderated by the degree of
interactivity and personalization sensed by the consumer
(Zarantonello and Schmitt, 2010). This further underscores the
need to incorporate immersive, emotionally engaging, and
functionally relevant brand experiences into strategic marketing
campaigns, particularly where technology is driving industry, as in
the beauty retailing industry. Enhanced brand experiences
especially those leveraging technology to personalise and immerse
are likely to foster stronger intentions to purchase by elevating
emotional engagement and perceived relevance.
2.5 Theoretical anchors: customer equity
theory and service-dominant logic
Customer Equity Theory, proposed by Rust, Lemon and
Zeithaml (2004), provides a strategic and financial perspective
on customer management by asserting that a firm’s long-term
value is derived not from isolated transactions or static brand
metrics, but from the cumulative value of its customer base.
This theory identifies three key drivers of customer equity: value
equity (the customer’s objective assessment based on quality,
price, and convenience), brand equity (emotional and symbolic
associations with the brand), and relationship equity (the
strength of loyalty and engagement built over time through
trust, commitment, and brand-related relational efforts). These
dimensions function not in isolation but interactively,
influencing customer lifetime value. In high-involvement,
experience-centric sectors like beauty retail, immersive digital
experiences enabled by AI-driven personalisation, AR/VR tools,
and omnichannel platforms reshape how consumers evaluate
product value, perceive brand meaning, and build relational
bonds, thereby directly influencing all three dimensions of
customer equity. The Service-Dominant (S-D) Logic, developed
by Vargo and Lusch (2004, 2008), represents a fundamental
paradigm shift in marketing theory. It is critical to note,
however, that S-D Logic encompasses a broader set of principles
beyond value co-creation alone. Its foundational premises
include: (a) service as the fundamental basis of exchange,
whereby all economic actors exchange service for service, with
goods serving merely as distribution mechanisms; (b) operant
resources as the primary source of competitive advantage,
whereby knowledge, skills, and relational capabilities matter
more than physical assets; (c) the customer as always a co-
creator of value, not merely a passive recipient; and (d) value as
always uniquely and phenomenologically determined by the
beneficiary, meaning value is context-dependent and cannot be
pre-determined by firms (Chandler and Lusch, 2015; Vargo and
Lusch, 2008). This richer conception of S-D Logic has direct
implications for immersive branding research. When a
consumer interacts with an AI-powered skincare
recommendation tool or an AR try-on feature, they are not
simply receiving a service they are integrating the brand’s
operant resources (algorithms, data, interfaces) with their own
operant resources (skin knowledge, aesthetic preferences,
purchase history) to co-determine value. The resulting value is
therefore phenomenological and personal, not standardised.
Under this logic, value is not embedded in products or delivered
unilaterally by firms but is instead co-determined through
ongoing, resource-integrating interactions between customers
and brand ecosystems (Vargo and Lusch, 2004). In the context
of digitally enhanced brand experiences, constructs like
customer equity and Brand Relationship Quality (BRQ) serve
not only as outcomes of these co-creation processes but also as
accumulating resources that deepen subsequent interactions and
sustain long-term engagement. While distinct in focus,
Customer Equity Theory and Service-Dominant Logic are not
merely complementary but mechanistically integrated within this
study. S-D Logic provides the processual logic: it explains how
and why value is co-created through ongoing consumer-brand
interactions, positioning DBE not as a firm-delivered stimulus
but as a collaborative exchange enabled by AI, AR/VR, and
omnichannel platforms (Vargo and Lusch, 2008). Customer
Equity Theory then provides the metric architecture: it
operationalises the outcomes of this co-created value into
measurable dimensions value equity, brand equity, and
relationship equity that capture how the consumer evaluates,
accumulates, and sustains value across brand touchpoints (Rust
and Huang, 2014). In this study, S-D Logic explains the
mechanism (how DBE generates value through co-creation),
while Customer Equity Theory explains what form that value
takes (which equity dimension is activated) and how it translates
into BRQ and purchase intention. This integration is not
parallel but sequential and causal: S-D Logic’s co-creation

3 Research gap analysis
3.1 Theoretical incongruence in
conceptualizing brand experience
The Brakus, Schmitt, and Zarantonello (2009) early work
defined brand experience as a four-dimensional construct
comprising sensory, affective, intellectual, and behavioural
elements but one that is predominantly static and essentially
comprising stimuli created by the firm. Today, however, consumer
and brand interactions take place in dynamic, technology-facilitated
environments where experiences are co-created, dynamic, and
emotionally resonant (Hollebeek and Andreassen, 2018; Lemon
and Verhoef, 2016). Despite conceptualization of Dynamic Brand
Experience (DBE) as a theoretical framework, a significant gap in
empirical research, especially in knowing the mechanisms through
which DBE enables real-time consumer interaction on multiple
omnichannel, AI-driven, and AR/VR-mediated touchpoints
(Chung, Wedel, and Rust, 2019; Hilken et al., 2017), serves as an
essential reminder to advance the theoretical framework of brand
experience to incorporate its evolving digital aspects.
3.2 Fragmented examination of customer
equity dimensions
Yet another shortcoming of the existing literature pertains to
the disjointed exploration of the components constituting
customer equity i.e., value equity, brand equity, and relationship
equity. Although the Customer Equity Framework (Rust, Lemon
and Zeithaml, 2004) provides a theoretically sound methodology
to address the determinants of consumer value, remarkably little
research investigates the synergic relationships between these
dimensions in engaging digital environments (Kumar and
Reinartz, 2016). Existing research often measures these variables
independently, resulting in a failure to capture the intricate
dynamics that drive purchase behaviour, especially for high-
involvement product classes like beauty. In addition, relative
strength between each of the equity components may vary in
digital-rich settings. Symbolic links that historically create brand
equity, for instance, may become ineffective if not reinforced by
experiential or personal contact (Kervyn, Fiske, and Malone,
2012; Zarantonello and Schmitt, 2010).
3.3 Underutilization of brand relationship
quality as a mediator
Brand Relationship Quality (BRQ) comprising trust,
satisfaction, and commitment (Fournier, 1998; Morgan and Hunt,
1994) has been well proven to be a significant driver of consumer
behaviour and loyalty (Kim and Ko, 2012). However, few
empirical researches have positioned BRQ as a mediator within
the DBE-customer equity-purchase intention chain, even though
it has been theoretically relevant in relationship marketing and
value co-creation literature (Hollebeek, Srivastava, and Chen,
2019). The exclusion of BRQ as an intermediary process is likely
to hide the entire process by which experiential brand
involvement manifests in behavioural responses. This omission
calls for a more holistic model one which includes BRQ not just
as a byproduct but as a relational vehicle of experiential value.
3.4 Contextual myopia: overemphasis on
luxury and global brands
A core restriction to existing literature is its contextual bias
towards global or luxury brands, with scant regard for digitally
evolved, emerging markets like India. In accordance with
Deloitte (2023) and KPMG (2020), India’s beauty retail market
is rapidly digitalizing, with complex consumerism and
technological uptake. Empirical research on immersive
branding in this dynamic market, however, is in its early
stages. Moreover, most research uses single-brand case
methodology or investigates monolithic market segments,
thus unable to generalize findings across brand positioning
tiers luxury, premium, and mass-market. More sophisticated
research spanning brand categories is needed to
derive contextually specific findings relevant to various
brand strategies.
3.5 Methodological underdevelopment in
mediation testing
Although Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) is widely
applied to test sophisticated theoretical models, in mediation
analysis of immersive branding studies, its application is
significantly limited. Specifically, application of advanced
methodologies like user-defined estimands in platforms like IBM
AMOS is not commonly found in spite of their capacity to
handle complicated indirect effects in a simplified manner
(MacKinnon, Fairchild, and Fritz, 2007). Such methodological
limitations result in reductionist understanding of how relational
constructs like Brand Relationship Quality (BRQ) mediate the
effect of Dynamic Brand Experience (DBE) on behavioural
outcomes. Incorporation of strict mediation testing in empirical
frameworks would provide more informative findings on causality
and effect size, hence enhancing both scholarly quality and
practical utility.

4.1 Immersive branding
The immersive branding concept, as conceptualized in this
study, is a revolutionary brand management paradigm, grounded

in consumer engagement facilitated by technologically mediated,
richly emotional, and contextually flexible interactions. Compared
to traditional brand communications that rely largely on static
advertisement and limited consumer touchpoints, immersive
branding employs technologies like artificial intelligence,
augmented reality/virtual reality, and omnichannel platforms to
construct inclusive and experiential brand worlds. This
conception distinguishes immersive branding from conventional
digital branding through its emphasis on real-time adaptability,
consumer co-creation, and technological embeddedness. These
immersive worlds build richer consumer-brand connections by
intensifying sensory engagement, facilitating emotional resonance,
and allowing personalised value co-creation. In India’s beauty
retail market a category that is marked by high digital adoption
and a growingly complex consumer base immersive branding
becomes especially critical. It not only aids in differentiation for
brands in a densely populated market but also builds lasting
relational equity. By incorporating immersive branding into the
Dynamic Brand Experience (DBE) framework and linking it to
customer equity and brand relationship quality, this research
provides a holistic, empirically based understanding of how
immersive strategies shape consumer purchase behaviour in
highly advanced digital markets.
4.2 Research problem and motivation
Brand communication within the current hyper-digital
business ecosystem has evolved from passive, one-way
communication to active, participative, and interactive
experiences. Consumers are now more actively engaging with
brands via artificial intelligence-pow ered personalization,
virtual and augmented reality platforms, and integrated
omnichannel platforms, which all redefine the value perception
and relationship building (Verhoef, Kannan, and Inman, 2015).
Even with such a shift, branding research has not yet
transitioned to include these new realities. Most existing
frameworks are still based on heritage frameworks that do not
capture the live, technology-enabled experiences currently
shaping consumer behaviour. The lack of integrative
frameworks that incorporate DBE, customer equity metrics,
and BRQ as a mediator hinders our ability to comprehend the
experiential branding strategies’ role in influencing purchase
intentions, particularly in emerging economies such as India.
Hence, the study seeks to address these gaps by creating an
analytical framework that integrates DBE, customer equity
dimensions, and BRQ in driving purchasing behaviour. To
achieve this, it extends branding literature’s theoretical
contribution as well as marketers’ strategic toolkit in today’s
digitally changing consumer contexts.
4.3 Conceptual model development
The evolution of branding during the digital age has
revolutionized consumer perception, evaluation, and
engagement with brands. The fixed brand experience, where
there are reduced sensory or transactional interactions, has
been replaced by what the scholarly literature calls Dynamic
Brand Experience (DBE) a type of consumer-brand interaction
that is experiential, dynamic, and technology-mediated. The
theory is based on the work of Brakus, Schmitt, and
Zarantonello (2009) and extended by Hollebeek and
Andreassen (2018). DBE includes artificial intelligence-enabled
real-time personalizatio n, immersive realities enabled by
augmented and virtual reality, and omnichannel engagement.
These features not only increase consumer engagement but
also construct customised emotional connections, setting up a
new benchmark against which brand engagement is evaluated.
Borrowing from the theoretical Customer Equity framework
outlined by Rust, Lemon and Zeithaml (2004), DBE is expected
to impact the three most important pillars of customer equity:
value equity, addressing perceived utility and value; brand
equity, addressing cognitive and emotional connections; and
relationship equity, addressing interaction, trust, and loyalty.
For instance, AI’s functionality for personalizatio n provides
value equity through product relevance and service
effectiveness enhancement (Chung, Wedel, and Rust, 2019). In
addition, AR/VR technology’s functionality for interactivity
enhances sensory appeal and creates emotions, thus positively
impacting both brand equity and relationship equity (Poncin
and Mimoun, 2014). It should be noted that while value equity
is theoretically included in the conceptual model, the Value
Equity scale could not be validly measured in the present
sample and is therefore excluded from empirical estimation;
the findings reported in Section 8 pertain to brand equity and
relationship equity only. Prior research also highlights the
importance of Brand Relationship Quality (BRQ) as an
outcome of successful brand experiences. Defined by Fournier
(1998) and elaborated by Morgan and Hunt (1994), BRQ
includes trust, satisfaction, and commitment, and has been
shown to mediate the relationship between branding strategies
and consumer behaviour (Hollebeek, 2011; Kim and Ko, 2012).
Dynamic interactions whether through virtual try-ons, AI-
enhanced customer service, or personalised messaging have
been proven to elevate BRQ by fostering transparency,
enhancing satisfaction, and deepening emotional commitment
(Lemon and Verhoef, 2016). At the behavioural end of this
framework lies purchase intention a well-documented proxy
for consumer decision-making. The Service-Dominant Logic
(Vargo and Lusch, 2004) reinforces the idea that consumers
co-create value through interactive brand encounters, and this
co-created value significantly shapes their willingness to
purchase. As such, it is postulated that DBE shapes purchase
intention not directly but indirectly, by influencing the
customer-equity dimensions of brand equity and relationship
equity, which in turn affect purchase intention both directly
and through Brand Relationship Quality as a
mediating mechanism.

7 Research methodology
This study adopts a cross-sectional descriptive research design
to examine customer perceptions and preferences regarding
immersive branding practices in the Indian beauty retail sector.
Data were collected from a sample of 413 consumers who had
engaged with beauty brands in Karnataka, India. The
respondents were selected using purposive sampling to ensure
relevance to the research objective, specifically targeting
individuals familiar with digital or immersive brand experiences.
The data collection was conducted via an administered
structured questionnaire over a five-month period, from January
to May 2025. This study employed purposive sampling rather
than a probability-based method, and the reason was
substantive. Dynamic Brand Experience can be meaningfully
evaluated only by respondents who have direct experience with
immersive brand technologies such as AR try-ons, AI-enabled
recommendation systems, and omnichannel platforms.
A general-population probability sample would have included a
large proportion of respondents without such experience, whose
responses to the DBE items would carry low construct validity
and would add measurement error rather than
representativeness. Purposive sampling therefore functioned as
an eligibility criterion that matched the sample to the theoretical
requirements of the model (Etikan, Musa, and Alkassim, 2016).
Respondents were recruited through online beauty-consumer
communities and university networks across Karnataka between
January and May 2025, and were screened with a filter question
confirming prior experience with at least one immersive brand
technology (AR try-on, AI-based recommendation, or
omnichannel platform); only those confirming such experience

than a definitive test; the procedural remedies applied during
data collection assured anonymity, confidentiality, and voluntary
participation, serve as the primary safeguard against common
method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Out of the 413
respondents, 58.3% were female and 41.7% male. In terms of
age, the majority fell in the 25–34 years bracket (47.2%),
followed by 35–44 years (31.6%), with the remainder above 45

above ₹15 lakh per annum, and educational backgrounds
spanning undergraduate to postgraduate level. This profile
corresponds to the intended population of digitally engaged
Indian beauty consumers and provides an appropriate basis for
testing the proposed model.

8.7 Research findings
As reported in Sections 8.4 and 8.5, the structural and
mediation analyses were conducted to test how Dynamic Brand
Experience (DBE) and the retained customer-equity dimensions
shape Purchase Intention (PI), and whether Brand Relationship
Quality (BRQ) transmits those effects. The evidence converges
on a single relational mechanism, which is presented below,
concentrating on the hypotheses that carry the substantive
findings. The three brand-equity direct paths — H1 (DBE →
BE, β = −0.070), H3 (BE → BRQ, β = 0.075), and H5 (BE → PI,
β = 0.039) — were all statistically supported but negligible in
magnitude (f² ≤ 0.012) and are therefore not examined
individually; their limited behavioural role is taken up in the
theoretical implications (Section 9.1). H6 (RE → PI, β = 0.171)
was likewise supported and is addressed within the H9
discussion, where it forms the direct component of the total RE
→ PI effect (β = 0.608).
H2: DBE positively influences Relationship Equity (RE). This
hypothesis was strongly supported (β = 0.493, t = 37.44,
f² = 0.321). The result confirms that immersive, technology-
mediated encounters: AR try-ons, AI-based personalisation, and
frictionless omnichannel integration operate primarily by
deepening the consumer’s relational bond with the brand rather
than by signalling symbolic prestige. This aligns with Service-
Dominant Logic, in which value is co-created in interaction
(Vargo and Lusch, 2004), and supports the view that in digitally
native categories relational engagement is built experientially
rather than inherited from brand heritage.
H4: Relationship Equity positively influences BRQ. This was the
strongest path in the entire model (β = 0.731, t = 92.12, f² = 1.150, a
very large effect). It establishes relationship equity as
overwhelmingly the dominant antecedent of relationship quality:
when consumers feel loyalty, recognition, and personalised
treatment, trust, satisfaction, and affective commitment follow
almost directly. For managers, this indicates that relationship
quality is not a diffuse by-product of “good branding” but a
specific, buildable consequence of relationship-equity investments.
H7: BRQ positively influences Purchase Intention. This
hypothesis was supported, with BRQ emerging as the dominant
proximal driver of intention (β = 0.597, t = 37.83, f² = 0.359;
large). Once relationship quality is established, it converts into
behavioural intent more powerfully than any customer-equity
dimension acting alone, positioning BRQ, consistent with
Fournier’s (1998) relationship framework, as the psychological
gateway between brand experience and purchase.
H8: BRQ mediates Brand Equity (BE) → PI relationship. This
hypothesis was technically supported but negligible in magnitude.
The specific indirect effect BE → BRQ → PI was statistically
significant (β = 0.045, t = 6.54) largely because of the large
sample and tight bootstrap intervals; its practical contribution is
trivial (all BE paths f² ≤ 0.012), and immersive experience does
not build Brand Equity (DBE → BE = −0.070). Substantively, the
cognitive/symbolic brand-equity dimension is behaviourally inert
in an immersive setting—echoing arguments that perception-
based brand associations do not translate into behaviour unless
embedded in emotionally rich, interactive experience (Kervyn,
Fiske, and Malone, 2012; Zarantonello and Schmitt, 2010).

9.1 Theoretical implications
This study extends the Customer Equity Framework and
Service-Dominant Logic into digitally immersiv e
consumption environments. The empirical validat ion of
Dynamic Brand Experience (DBE) as a strong antecedent of
relationship equity (β = 0.493) underscores the evolving role
of technology-enabled brand interactions as co-crea tors of
relational value. In this sample, DBE operated through the
relational rather than the symbolic route: it did not
meaningfully build the retained Brand Equity, and value
equity could not be validly measured and is therefore
excluded from interpreta tion. These findings lend support to
reconceptualising brand experience not merely as a symbolic
interface but as a relationship-gener ating mechanism through
which functional, emotional, and contextual value are
embedded within digital brand touchpoints.
Further, the negligible behavioural contribution of Brand
Equity qualifies prevailing brand-centric paradigms that
emphasise legacy and symbolic associations. Although the
Brand Equity paths were statistically significant given the large
sample and tight bootstrap intervals, their practical magnitude
was trivial (f² ≤ 0.012; indirect β ≤ 0.045), indicating that
perception-based brand associations did not translate into
purchase intention in this immersive setting unless carried by
relational quality. This pattern is consistent with a shifting
emphasis, whereby consumers in highly digital contexts may
increasingly prioritise immediacy, relevance, and interactive
personalisatio n over static brand heritage. Accordingly, the
findings invite further examination of how symbolic brand
value operates within digitally native markets, particularly
among segments characterised by high digital fluency and
dynamic expectations.

identifying and explaining an asymmetry in how Brand
Relationship Quality mediates the customer-equity to purchase-
intention relationship. Rather than functioning as a universal
mediator across all consumer-brand interactions, BRQ operated
selectively, and this pattern was borne out empirically. Its
mediating role is concentrated in the relational pathway: the

9.2 Managerial implications
The research provides multiple managerial implications for
retail beauty practitioners, especially those dealing with
digitalization and omni-channel integration. In light of the
evidence that DBE builds purchase intention chiefly by
strengthening relationship equity, beauty retailers should
prioritise digitally immersive technologies like AR try-on
capability, virtual consultations, and interactive product
experiences their top priority. These technologies must be
optimized for interaction, as well as for the provision of
concrete consumer utility and emotional connection. The
negligible behavioural contribution of the symbolic Brand
Equity suggests a strategic imperative for beauty brands to shift
resources away from heritage, traditional messaging towards
more forward-looking, relationship-centred efforts in building
the brand. This includes the development of adaptive,
contextual, and personalised brand experiences. AI-based
technologies such as skin diagnostic tools, personalised
recommendation systems, and chatbot-driven support should be
systematically integrated into the customer journey. All these
technologies have direct influences on perceived value and
satisfaction, particularly when they are linked to individual
consumer profiles and behavioural patterns. Since Brand
Relationship Quality in itself does not automatically serve as an
experiential consequence mediator, it is required that brand
strategies be developed with the aim of strengthening some
relational aspects like trust, satisfaction, and affective
commitment. Thus, beauty retailers need to concentrate on
experiential aspects that build these psychological factors rather
than expecting loyalty to result directly from experience. In
countries like India with expanding economies, where consumer
digital literacy as well as digital infrastructure is extremely
diverse, managers will need to take a two-pronged strategy.
Digitally native city consumers are likely to be attracted towards
larger-immersion solutions (e.g., AR/VR-based solutions), while
lower-tech, mobile-optimised interventions need to appeal to the
utilitarian demands of less digitally literate audiences. The
results call for a move away from linear, campaign-based
marketing to continuous, interactive patterns of engagement.
Beauty companies have to invest in influencer partnerships, live-
streaming frameworks, and user-generated content strategies
that maintain brand visibility through participatory co-creation.
10 Conclusion and future research
directions
This study provides empirical validation for an integrated
model that links Dynamic Brand Experience (DBE), Brand
Equity (BE), Brand Relationship Quality (BRQ), and Purchase
Intention (PI) within the digitally evolving Indian beauty sector.
The results confirm that Dynamic Brand Experience primarily
strengthens relationship equity, which is by far the strongest
antecedent of Brand Relationship Quality, and that BRQ is the
dominant driver of purchase behaviour. However, the relatively
weak or non-significant role of brand equity across several
pathways points to a possible shift in consumer decision-
making, from reliance on symbolic brand cues toward an
emphasis on functional value and emotional engagement. This
pattern is consistent with the emerging view that, in highly
digital contexts, consumers may be less persuaded by legacy
brand associations and more responsive to immersive,
meaningful, and technologically enriched brand interactions.
Moreover, the mediation analysis identifies RE → BRQ → PI
as the single most influential indirect pathway, with a substantial
serial route running from Dynamic Brand Experience through
relationship equity and BRQ to purchase intention. Brand
Equity contributes only negligible indirect effects, and value
equity is not interpreted because it could not be validly
measured. These insights qualify oversimplified assumptions in
the branding literature that emphasise linear and hierarchical
relationships among constructs. Instead, they point to more
fluid and conditional mechanisms in which emotion-laden
experiences and functional utility act simultaneously but
asymmetrically on purchase behaviour. From a managerial
standpoint, this calls for recalibrating branding strategies,
moving beyond equity-building campaigns toward more
continuous, adaptive, and value-anchored relationship strategies.
This study has several limitations related to its sampling and
design. First, the use of purposive sampling, although
appropriate for reaching respondents with genuine experience of
immersive brand technologies, may introduce self-selection bias.
Respondents who are already familiar with, and favourably
inclined toward, these technologies may report more positive
perceptions, which could inflate the observed effects of Dynamic
Brand Experience and limit the generalisability of the findings
to the wider consumer population. Second, the cross-sectional
design measures all constructs at a single point in time and
therefore cannot establish causal direction. Future research using
probability-based sampling, longitudinal data, or experimental
designs would strengthen both the representativeness of the
sample and the basis for causal inference.
Future research could extend this study in several directions.
First, the moderating effects of income and gender on the BRQ to
purchase intention pathway constitute a priority agenda for
future empirical investigation, for which multi-group SEM or
interaction-term analysis would be suitable. Second, a
longitudinal research design would offer deeper insight into
how consumer perceptions of DBE and relationship quality
evolve over time and shape brand loyalty and lifetime value.
Third, experimental designs could isolate the effects of specific
immersive technologies, such as AR try-ons compared with AI-
