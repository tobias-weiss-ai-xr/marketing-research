# Beyond Conscious Brand Perception

SCIENCE INTERNATIONAL EMPIRICAL REVIEW
Volume 2 - Issue 1 - 2026
Beyond Conscious Brand Perception
A Multi-Dimensional Approach to Understanding Brand Perception
Jens Schröder, brajns establishment, Liechtenstein
Abstract
Brand perception is central to strategic brand management, yet branding research
addresses its components across partly separate traditions. Brand image and
associations explain consciously reportable representations, while research on implicit
attitudes, automatic processing, personal values, motivation, and symbolic meaning
demonstrates that not all brand-related responses are equally accessible to deliberate
articulation. This conceptual article integrates these perspectives by distinguishing two
dimensions of Brand Perception: accessibility and content. Accessibility describes the
degree to which brand-related representations can be consciously accessed and
expressed, ranging from explicit evaluations to less directly accessible and automatic
processes. Content describes what is represented in relation to the brand, including
attributes and evaluations, associations and meaning, values, motivational relevance,
and symbolic or identity-related meaning. The framework avoids treating values or
motives as fixed levels of consciousness and instead conceptualises Brand Perception as
a configuration of content across varying degrees of accessibility. This distinction
clarifies why explicit and implicit findings can provide complementary rather than
competing evidence and offers a differentiated basis for Brand Perception diagnosis in
strategic Brand Management. The article concludes that understanding what
stakeholders perceive and how consciously those perceptions are accessible are distinct
questions that should be analysed separately but interpreted jointly.
Keywords: brand perception, brand image, brand associations, implicit brand attitudes,
brand values, motivation, symbolic meaning, strategic brand management
JEL codes: M31
1. Introduction
Understanding how people perceive brands is fundamental to Brand Management.
Brands derive strategic value not only from what organisations intend them to
represent, but also from the meanings, associations, evaluations, expectations, and
experiences that develop among those who encounter them. Branding research has
therefore produced a broad range of constructs concerned with the external existence of
brands, including Brand Image, Brand Associations, Brand Attitudes, Brand Personality,
Self-Congruity, symbolic consumption, and Brand Meaning (Keller, 1993; Aaker, 1997).

Much of this research relies on information that people can consciously access and
communicate. Respondents can be asked which characteristics they associate with a
brand, whether they view it as innovative or trustworthy, how positively they evaluate
it, or how well it corresponds with their self-concept. Such measures are indispensable
because they reveal reflected and socially communicable perceptions. The difficulty
arises only when conscious articulation is treated as equivalent to the entirety of Brand
Perception.
Research in consumer psychology has repeatedly shown that evaluations, memories,
associations, and behavioural responses may also be influenced by processes that are
less consciously accessible. Studies of implicit Brand Attitudes, implicit memory,
priming, automatic categorisation, and nonconscious consumer behaviour indicate that
brand-related responses can emerge without being fully represented in deliberate
verbal evaluation (Maison et al., 2004; Brunel et al., 2004; Yoo, 2008; Vriens and Alves,
2017). Explicit and implicit approaches therefore provide potentially different windows
on brand-related cognition rather than mutually exclusive accounts of a single “true”
perception.
Accessibility, however, is only one part of the problem. Brand-related perceptions also
differ in content. A brand may be perceived through functional attributes, broader
associations, personal or cultural values, motivational relevance, or symbolic and
identity-related meaning. Research on Self-Congruity, personal values, symbolic
consumption, and consumer identity demonstrates that such meanings can influence
evaluation and choice (Sirgy, 1982; Belk, 1988; Aguirre-Rodriguez et al., 2012; Michel et
al., 2022).
This produces a conceptual problem. A simple distinction between conscious and
unconscious Brand Perception risks mixing two different questions: how accessible is a
perception, and what does the perception contain? Values, for example, may be
consciously endorsed, implicitly activated, or only partly available to articulation. The
same is true of motivational and symbolic meanings. The “depth” of content therefore
should not automatically be equated with the “depth” of consciousness.
The present article addresses this problem by proposing a multi-dimensional
perspective that separates accessibility from content. The central research question is:
How can Brand Perception be conceptualised as a multi-dimensional
phenomenon that distinguishes between the accessibility and the content of
brand-related perceptions?
The article makes three contributions. First, it integrates established branding
research on Brand Image and Brand Associations with findings from implicit cognition,
values, motivation, and symbolic Brand Meaning. Second, it proposes accessibility and
content as analytically distinct dimensions of Brand Perception. Third, it develops a
compact framework showing how different forms of perceptual content may occur with
different degrees of conscious accessibility. The objective is not to privilege implicit over
explicit measures, but to clarify when different forms of evidence may complement each
other in strategic Brand Management.
2. Brand Perception in Existing Research
Brand Perception is not represented by one single construct in branding literature.
Instead, different traditions explain different aspects of how people experience,
interpret, evaluate, and assign meaning to brands. Brand Image provides one of the most

established foundations. Keller (1993) defines it through perceptions reflected by
associations held in consumer memory. These associations vary in strength,
favourability, and uniqueness and contribute to customer-based Brand Equity.
This association-based perspective establishes a key principle: brands exist not only
as organisational identities or market offerings, but also as cognitive representations
held by individuals. Other constructs extend this view. Brand Personality addresses
human characteristics attributed to brands (Aaker, 1997), while Self-Congruity research
examines relationships between consumer self-concepts and symbolic brand
characteristics (Sirgy, 1982; Aguirre-Rodriguez et al., 2012). Symbolic consumption
research similarly shows that brands can acquire personal, social, and identity-related
significance beyond functional attributes (Belk, 1988).
2.1. Explicit and Implicit Brand Responses
Explicit measures ask respondents to consciously access and report an attitude,
evaluation, association, or preference. Implicit approaches seek to infer associative or
evaluative tendencies without relying solely on deliberate self-report. Maison et al.
(2004) and Brunel et al. (2004) showed that implicit measures can provide information
that differs from, and may complement, explicit consumer responses. More recently,
Wörfel (2021) reviewed 446 articles on implicit consumer cognition and identified
several partly separate research streams, reinforcing the need for greater integration
across research on implicit attitudes, memory, automatic processing, and related
consumer-cognition phenomena.
Vriens and Alves (2017) further demonstrated that implicit measures may produce
explanatory patterns that differ from explicit measures and may be less susceptible to
some response-style effects. These findings do not establish implicit measures as
inherently superior. Rather, they support a more cautious conclusion: explicit and
implicit approaches can provide access to different aspects of brand-related cognition
and evaluation. The strategic task is therefore not to search for one supposedly 'true'
measure, but to match methods to the accessibility and content of the perceptual
question being investigated.
2.2. Automatic and Nonconscious Processing
A related stream concerns automatic and nonconscious processing. Yoo (2008) showed
that advertising exposure can influence Brand Attitudes through implicit memory under
conditions of limited conscious processing. Such work demonstrates that deliberate
reflection is only one route through which brand information can affect responses. For
conceptual clarity, however, implicit, automatic, and nonconscious should not be treated
as synonyms. Implicit measurement is primarily methodological, automaticity concerns
limited deliberation or control, and nonconscious processing refers more specifically to
processes occurring without awareness. The framework developed here therefore uses
accessibility as a higher-order analytical dimension rather than proposing a strict
psychological hierarchy.
2.3. Values, Motivation, and Symbolic Meaning
Brand-related meaning can also involve values. Values concern broader conceptions of
what is personally or socially desirable and can shape the meanings consumers attach to

brands. Michel et al. (2022), drawing on Schwartz’s value theory, show that
relationships between personal values and brand values influence consumer responses
and may also involve aspirational or self-expansive meanings. Values therefore
constitute an important content domain of Brand Perception, but they should not
automatically be labelled subconscious.
Motivational relevance is similarly distinct from consciousness. Self-Congruity theory
suggests that consumers respond more favourably when brand characteristics
correspond with relevant dimensions of the self (Sirgy, 1982). The meta-analysis by
Aguirre-Rodriguez et al. (2012) supports the importance of Self-Congruity and identifies
cognitive and motivational mechanisms underlying these effects. A brand may support
belonging, distinction, achievement, security, stimulation, or self-expression. Some of
these motives can be articulated; others may influence responses without being
spontaneously recognised.
Brands also operate as symbolic resources. Possessions and brands can contribute to
identity construction and expression (Belk, 1988). Symbolic meanings may be highly
explicit, such as deliberately choosing a brand to signal prestige or sustainability, but
they may also operate through learned cultural associations or identity relationships
that are difficult to verbalise fully. These examples reinforce the need to distinguish
what a perception contains from how consciously it is accessible.
3. Why a Conscious-Unconscious Dichotomy Is Insufficient
A binary conscious-unconscious distinction is attractive because it appears to provide a
simple ordering principle for Brand Perception. For strategic Brand Management,
however, the distinction is too coarse. Conscious accessibility is not necessarily all-or-
nothing. Brand-related representations can vary in the ease, immediacy, confidence, or
deliberation with which they are retrieved and expressed. A respondent may be able to
report an association after reflection but not spontaneously, while another response
may be activated rapidly without a clear verbal explanation. Treating both as either fully
conscious or fully unconscious obscures these differences.
The dichotomy also risks attaching particular types of content to presumed levels of
consciousness. Values, motives, and symbolic meanings are often described as deeper
than functional attributes, but depth of meaning is not equivalent to lack of awareness. A
person may consciously identify security, achievement, sustainability, or status as
important aspects of a brand, while another may show similar relevance only indirectly.
Conversely, apparently simple evaluations such as liking, familiarity, or category fit can
be activated automatically. Content and accessibility therefore vary independently.
A third limitation concerns interpretation. If less consciously accessible evidence is
automatically treated as more authentic, Brand Management may overvalue indirect
measures and undervalue articulated meanings that are socially and strategically
consequential. Explicit perceptions influence word of mouth, justification of choice,
recommendation, organisational discourse, and stakeholder expectations. Implicit or
automatic processes may add information, but they do not invalidate what people can
consciously report. The more defensible position is therefore complementary: different
forms of accessibility can reveal different aspects of the perceptual configuration.
This argument shifts the conceptual task away from locating one true level of Brand
Perception and toward mapping the relationship between what the brand means and
how accessible that meaning is. The framework developed below follows this logic by

separating accessibility from content and treating their intersection as the relevant unit
of analysis.
4. A Multi-Dimensional Model of Brand Perception
The literature suggests that Brand Perception should be conceptualised through two
intersecting dimensions rather than a hierarchy of progressively deeper levels:
Accessibility of Brand Perception and Content of Brand Perception.
4.1. Dimension I: Accessibility
Accessibility refers to the extent to which brand-related representations can be
deliberately accessed, reflected upon, and communicated. Three broad reference points
are useful. Explicit or consciously accessible perceptions can be recalled and verbalised
directly. Less directly accessible or implicit perceptions may influence responses
without being immediately available for self-report. Automatic or nonconscious
processing describes effects that occur with little or no deliberate conscious processing.
These categories are analytical reference points, not fixed neurological compartments.
Accessibility is better understood as a continuum from more consciously accessible to
less consciously accessible.
4.2. Dimension II: Content
Content concerns what is represented in relation to the brand. Five broad domains
capture the main streams reviewed above: attributes and evaluations; associations and
Brand Meaning; values; motivational relevance; and symbolic or identity-related
meaning. The domains are deliberately broad and partially overlapping because the
purpose is not to create five new measurement scales, but to separate substantive
meaning from conscious accessibility.

5. Strategic Implications
5.1. Methodological Fit Rather Than Methodological Maximalism
The model implies that no single method can be assumed to capture Brand Perception in
its entirety. Direct questioning remains essential for consciously accessible meanings,
while indirect approaches may provide additional evidence where articulation is
difficult or where automatic responses are strategically relevant. The appropriate
question is therefore not “Which method measures Brand Perception?” but “Which
methods fit the perceptual dimensions relevant to the strategic problem?” This principle
avoids the opposite error of assuming that every brand study requires implicit or
projective methods.
5.2. Complementary Evidence and Divergence
Different dimensions can provide different forms of strategic information. Explicit
perception may reveal what stakeholders recognise and communicate; associative
analysis can identify meaning networks; value-oriented analysis can reveal broader
orientations; motivational analysis can indicate why meanings matter; and symbolic
analysis can show how brands connect with identity. These findings should not be
collapsed prematurely into one overall score.
Explicit and less directly accessible findings may also diverge. A brand may be
described consciously as innovative while more automatic associations remain linked to
familiarity or tradition. Such differences should not automatically be treated as
measurement error, nor should the less conscious response be declared more
“authentic.” The strategic task is to interpret why different perceptual forms coexist and
what that coexistence implies for future Brand Management.
5.3. Brand Perception as a Configuration
The proposed model is best understood as a configuration rather than as a stack of
progressively deeper layers. A hierarchical metaphor can be attractive because it
appears to order conscious, subconscious, and unconscious phenomena neatly. Yet such
an order implies more psychological certainty than the reviewed branding literature
supports. The same substantive content may be consciously accessible in one context
and less directly accessible in another. A stakeholder can explicitly describe a brand as
secure or prestigious, while another may show the same relationship only indirectly.
The distinction therefore belongs to accessibility, not to the substantive meaning itself.
A configurational view also avoids the assumption that Brand Perception can be
reduced to one global score. Strategic relevance often lies in patterns across dimensions.
A brand may receive strong explicit evaluations while showing little distinctive value or

identity meaning. Another may perform modestly on functional differentiation but
display strong motivational or symbolic relevance. A third may evoke favourable self-
reports while automatic associations point toward an older or different category
meaning. Such profiles are strategically more informative than a single overall attitude
because they reveal where perceptual strengths and tensions are located.
This perspective also provides a more disciplined relationship between theory and
method. Direct surveys, qualitative interviews, association tasks, value-oriented
measures, implicit procedures, and projective approaches can all contribute to Brand
Perception diagnosis, but none should be treated as privileged access to a uniquely
authentic level. Each method produces evidence about particular aspects of content and
accessibility. The task of Brand Management is therefore to interpret convergent and
divergent evidence rather than to rank methods by presumed psychological depth.
5.4. From Measurement to Strategic Interpretation
The distinction between measurement and interpretation is especially important for
strategic application. Collecting more dimensions does not automatically produce better
strategy. A diagnosis becomes useful only when perceptual patterns are connected with
the strategic questions the organisation actually needs to answer. This requires
decisions about which stakeholder groups matter, which content domains are relevant,
and which differences are meaningful enough to influence positioning, communication,
experience design, or organisational behaviour.
Several recurring pattern types can be strategically relevant. Reinforcement occurs
when explicit and less directly accessible evidence points in the same direction.
Contradiction occurs when different forms of evidence diverge. Absence describes
strategically desired meanings that are not perceptually established. Dominance occurs
when one meaning overwhelms other desired associations. Fragmentation describes
inconsistent perceptions across stakeholder groups or content domains. Hidden
potential refers to positive meanings that are perceptually present but underused in
formal strategy. These pattern types are not new constructs; they are interpretive
categories that illustrate why a multidimensional diagnosis can matter.
The framework therefore remains intentionally non-prescriptive about a single ideal
research design. In some contexts, explicit Brand Image and association measures may
be sufficient. In others, strategic questions about values, motivation, identity, or
automatic evaluation may justify additional methods. The principle is proportionality:
methods should be selected because they answer a relevant diagnostic question, not
because they appear methodologically sophisticated.
5.5. Limitations and Future Research
The framework has several limitations. First, accessibility is presented as an integrative
analytical dimension, but explicit, implicit, automatic, and nonconscious processes have
distinct theoretical foundations. Future research should therefore avoid treating the
continuum as evidence of one unified psychological mechanism. Second, the proposed
content domains are broad and partly overlapping. Values, motivation, symbolic
meaning, and identity are theoretically related and may not always be empirically
separable.
Third, indirect measurement does not prove unconscious processing. Likewise, self-
report does not necessarily capture every consciously available perception. Future

studies should therefore distinguish carefully between the method used, the construct
inferred, and the level of awareness claimed. Fourth, the relative strategic importance of
content domains is likely to vary across consumer, B2B, corporate, service, employer,
public, and place brands. Comparative studies across contexts are needed before any
general hierarchy of diagnostic relevance can be proposed.
A useful empirical programme would compare Brand Perception profiles generated
by combinations of explicit, implicit, value-oriented, motivational, and qualitative
approaches and examine whether these profiles explain strategically relevant outcomes
beyond conventional Brand Image measures. Longitudinal research could further test
whether changes in accessibility and content occur together or follow different
trajectories after brand interventions. Such work would provide an empirical bridge
from the present conceptual model toward later research on Brand Perception Change
and Perception Brand Positioning.
6. Discussion and Conclusions
The central contribution of this article is the separation of accessibility from content.
Without this distinction, values, motives, or symbolic meaning can easily be
misclassified as levels of consciousness. Yet there is no necessary one-to-one
relationship between the nature of Brand Meaning and its accessibility. Value-based
content may be consciously articulated; functional attitudes may operate automatically;
identity meanings may be both deliberate and partly implicit. Treating the two
dimensions as intersecting rather than hierarchical therefore provides a conceptually
cleaner account of Brand Perception.
The framework also rejects the search for one “true” Brand Perception. Explicit
perception is not merely a distorted surface beneath which a more authentic implicit
perception resides. Consciously articulated meaning is strategically consequential
because stakeholders communicate it, use it to justify decisions, and incorporate it into
social discourse. Less consciously accessible processes may nevertheless add
information that self-report does not fully capture. Their value lies in complementarity,
not superiority.
The model is conceptual and requires empirical validation. Future research should
examine the boundaries among accessibility categories, test whether the proposed
content domains are sufficiently distinct, and compare Brand Perception profiles
produced through combinations of explicit, implicit, value-oriented, motivational, and
qualitative approaches. The strategic relevance of different dimensions is also likely to
vary across consumer, B2B, corporate, employer, service, and other brand contexts.
In conclusion, Brand Perception should not be equated solely with what stakeholders
can consciously articulate. At the same time, deeper substantive content should not
automatically be labelled subconscious. What stakeholders perceive and how
consciously those perceptions are accessible are different questions. Separating them
provides a more differentiated theoretical foundation for Brand Perception research and
a more disciplined basis for subsequent work on perception-based diagnosis and
Perception Brand Positioning.
