Abstract. Despite decades of development, the fast food industry still shows considerable
room for expansion. Market resources are becoming increasingly concentrated among
leading brands, while local enterprises are also rising quickly by relying on cultural
adaptation and regional advantages. Alongside internationally recognized companies such as
McDonald's and KFC, Japanese restaurant brands including Yoshinoya and Ajisen Ramen
have established a strong presence in East Asian markets. At the same time, Chinese brands
such as Mixue Ice Cream & Tea and Tastien have accelerated their overseas expansion and
gradually strengthened their influence in Southeast Asia. This study adopts case analysis and
literature review as its primary research approaches to investigate how consumer preferences
differ under various cultural backgrounds and how fast food enterprises coordinate global
brand identity with localization strategies during international expansion. Through the
discussion of cross-cultural marketing practices in the catering industry, the paper aims to
enrich existing theoretical research and provide practical references for the international
development of other service-oriented industries.
Keywords: fast food, dietary culture, Hofstede's Cultural Dimension Theory, Cross-Cultural
Marketing, Perceived Value Theory
1. Introduction
1.1. Research background
With the continuous advancement of global economic integration, enterprises are no longer
concerned solely with product innovation and quality improvement. Increasing attention has also
been given to how brands position themselves within different cultural environments in international
markets. As a result, cross-cultural marketing has gradually become an important area of research
and practice in international business. In essence, cross-cultural marketing refers to the process by
which companies adjust their marketing strategies according to the social values, consumer
behaviors, and cultural characteristics of specific regions. For the fast food industry, cultural
adaptation is especially important because eating habits and taste preferences are deeply influenced
by local traditions, religious beliefs, and geographical environments. When expanding into overseas

1.2. Research questions
Drawing on brand narrative theory, cultural dimension theory, and perceived value theory, this study
discusses how fast food enterprises develop international marketing strategies under different
cultural contexts. The paper mainly focuses on two research questions.
First, how can fast food brands establish effective international marketing systems by considering
the characteristics of target markets and consumer demand, while maintaining a balance between
global brand identity and localization strategies in order to strengthen international competitiveness?
Second, when entering markets with different levels of cultural distance from their home
countries, how should fast food brands adjust their localization approaches and brand storytelling
strategies to better connect with local consumers?
2. Literature review
2.1. Cross-cultural marketing research
This study takes the Chinese and American markets as core research scenarios and selects Starbucks'
social media platforms (Sina, Weibo in China, Instagram in the US) as carriers to conduct a
comparative study of brand narrative communication and consumer perceived value in cross-cultural
contexts. The findings reveal significant differences in value perception dimensions of brand
narrative between Chinese and American consumers:
The Chinese market prefers collective, festival-oriented, and relationship-focused narratives,
which significantly boost emotional value and social identity value.
The American market is more receptive to individualistic, personalized, and value-rational
narratives, prioritizing functional value and self-expression value.
Furthermore, the localization adaptation of brand narrative directly affects consumer engagement
and perceived value scores. Chinese users show significantly higher participation in localized
interactions than American users, with a positive emotion rate of 61.8% (vs. 54.2% in the US),
confirming that culturally adapted narratives effectively bridge cultural gaps and strengthen
consumer identification.
The study further argues that cross-cultural marketing of global fast food brands is essentially a
cultural translation of brand narrative and a localized reconstruction of perceived value. Expansion
in China requires weakening Western standardized narratives and strengthening local cultural
symbols, dietary customs, and emotional bonds. For Chinese brands going global (e.g., Yangguofu)
entering the US market, it is necessary to balance Chinese cultural core and American consumer
values to avoid cultural discount and build a value system with both cultural uniqueness and market
adaptability. This conclusion provides direct theoretical and empirical references for optimizing
localized narratives of McDonald's, KFC, and Yoshinoya in China, as well as cross-cultural value
construction for Chinese fast food brands going global [1].

2.2. The promoting role of perceived value theory in fast food localization
Consumer perceived value is the core logic that drives transnational fast food brands to attract local
consumers. Its essence is the trade-off made by consumers between functional benefits, emotional
benefits, and social benefits on the one hand, and monetary costs, time costs, and psychological
costs on the other. In the context of transnational marketing, fast food brands need to adapt their
standardized products to local price levels, maintain their advantages of convenience, efficiency, and
controllable quality to ensure high cost-performance, and reduce consumers' decision-making risks.
By integrating the international brand image with local culture, brands can activate emotional value;
for instance, the international brand identity can highlight fashion value, and proper localization can
enhance consumers' sense of belonging to the brand. Research shows that consumers have a certain
emotional attachment to local food. Therefore, launching localized products by international brands
may not directly increase product sales, but will strengthen local consumers' identification with the
brand and boost their repurchase intention for the fast food [2]. International brands provide
consumers with social value, as the global brand image helps to demonstrate personal identity and
improve consumers' social recognition.
2.3. Hofstede's Cultural Dimension Theory
Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions Theory measures the cultures and values of different countries
across five distinct dimensions. This paper makes a comparative analysis of the differences between
the Chinese market and the American market as examples. China is a highly collectivist society,
while the United States emphasizes individualism. China values respect for elders and has an
obvious family hierarchy, while American families advocate equal communication and parents need
to listen to children's opinions, so China features a high power distance, and the United States
features a low power distance. Third, in terms of uncertainty avoidance, Chinese society is
characterized by high uncertainty avoidance, which is relatively conservative and needs time to
adapt to new things, while the United States is more open with low uncertainty avoidance. Chinese
society tends to be feminine, which values family relations and parent-child companionship, while
the United States tends to be masculine, which values efficiency, speed, and cost-performance.
China is a long-term oriented society that values traditional concepts and family inheritance, while
the United States is a short-term oriented society that focuses on the present and immediate pleasure
and satisfaction. Therefore, American advertisements can focus on individual efficiency, while
advertisements adapted to China should refer more to family and collective values [3].
2.4. Challenges of fast food brands in cross-cultural markets
International brands face a variety of challenges when entering overseas markets in the initial stage.
In terms of products, standardized original fast food items often fail to match local dietary tastes and
eating habits, making it difficult to satisfy local consumers' flavor preferences and even violating
dietary taboos. Second, there are issues related to consumption level and brand positioning. A brand
positioned as affordable, mass-market fast food in its home country may be regarded as a high-end
and novel consumption venue in emerging markets, resulting in low public penetration and low
repurchase rates. In terms of marketing communication, advertisements from the brand's home
country cannot be directly used, while advertisements designed for the target market are prone to
cultural and value conflicts, which may easily cause misunderstanding among local consumers [4].

The adopted research methods include case study, literature analysis, and comparative analysis.
3.2. Case selection and rationale
Case Selection and Rationale: KFC, McDonald's, Yoshinoya, Yangguofu Spicy Hot Pot
KFC: A highly successful case of deep localization, currently having the largest number of stores
among overseas fast food brands in China.
McDonald's: Low degree of localization at the initial entry into the Chinese market, and has
promoted its localization process with the economic development of China.
Yoshinoya: A Japanese fast food brand with a similar dietary culture, adopting differentiated
strategies in response to competition from local brands.
Yangguofu Spicy Hot Pot: A model case of Chinese local brands going global.
In the Chinese market, KFC initially positioned itself as a high-end Western fast food brand and
gradually penetrated the mass market through localization transformation. Having shifted its focus
from first‑tier and second‑tier cities to full coverage in third‑tier and fourth‑tier cities, KFC has
become a national Western fast food brand. McDonald's has maintained a mid‑to‑high‑end
positioning, mainly targeting young students and urban white‑collar workers in first‑tier and
second‑tier cities. Yoshinoya positioned itself as a Japanese casual dining, mainly for office workers,
and attracted a large consumer group with its image of convenience and healthy light meals. When
entering overseas markets, Yangguofu Spicy Hot Pot promoted the concept of "meat and vegetable
combination with customizable selection". In terms of brand narrative, KFC and McDonald's have
deeply integrated traditional Chinese culture, festivals, and social hot issues to build localized brand
narratives. They have launched festival advertisements and promotional activities during traditional
festivals such as the Spring Festival, National Day, and Mid‑Autumn Festival to reduce the cultural
gap as a foreign brand. Yoshinoya focuses on the combination of minimalist Japanese culture and
urban life in China, attracting contemporary young people with fast casual meals that fit their
lifestyle. Yangguofu Spicy Hot Pot takes Chinese dietary culture, Sichuan‑style cuisine, and
self‑selected dining culture as its core narrative to attract a large number of overseas consumers. In
terms of emotional value, all fast food brands need to establish emotional connections and reduce
the sense of alienation as foreign brands through localized festival marketing, warm brand stories,
and childhood nostalgia marketing [5]. McDonald's and KFC have become common venues for
young people's socializing, dating, and parent‑child dining, endowing consumption with social
attributes; Yoshinoya has built a minimalist workplace lifestyle label, and Yangguofu Spicy Hot Pot
has created a healthy light meal label, which is in line with the mainstream trend of healthy
consumption overseas to satisfy consumers' social value [6]. In summary, the strategies adopted by
McDonald's, KFC, and Yoshinoya to enter China include product localization, marketing
localization, and positioning localization, reducing cross‑cultural barriers through cultural
integration, ensuring quality through standardization, and enhancing consumer perceived value
through localization. The core logic of Yangguofu's global expansion is cultural differentiation:
retaining the core characteristics of Chinese products with minor product improvements, and
attracting recognition from overseas consumers through multicultural appeal [7].

3.3. Analytical dimensions
This paper systematically analyzes different brands from four dimensions: product localization

4. Case analysis of fast food brand marketing strategies
4.1. KFC: deep localization strategy in China
Differences in dietary culture are often the most critical issue that fast food brands must consider
when entering a new market. For the Chinese market, Western fast food brands need to address
greater disparities in taste preferences and dietary customs. While retaining their core Western
identity, they must also make localized adjustments based on local eating habits. Among them, KFC
has carried out particularly prominent localization reforms. Since KFC entered the Chinese market
in 1987, KFC has actively studied local raw materials, consumer preferences, and other local market
conditions, and established a product research and development team and test kitchen in the 1990s.
While maintaining the novelty of Western fast food, KFC has launched many products that align
with local dietary culture and foster a sense of belonging among consumers, such as Old Beijing
Chicken Roll, Fresh Shrimp Spring Rolls, and Portuguese Egg Tarts, as well as various Chinese-
style breakfast items, including congee, soy milk, and fried dough sticks [8]. Furthermore, in the
process of deep Sinicization, KFC has continuously refined its offerings and launched products with
distinctive Chinese regional flavors from Beijing, Sichuan, Guangdong, and other areas, such as Old
Beijing Chicken Roll, Sichuan Spicy Crispy Chicken, and Old Duck Soup [8]. Its high degree of
localization has enabled it to surpass McDonald's, Pizza Hut, and other brands to become the largest
foreign Western fast food brand in China.
4.2. McDonald's localization process
Different from KFC's radical localization strategy, McDonald's expansion in the Chinese market has
generally been based on steady and cautious attempts. McDonald's entered the Chinese market in
1990, three years later than KFC. Unlike KFC, which opened its first restaurant in Beijing,
McDonald's set its headquarters in Hong Kong and opened its first mainland store in Shenzhen. It
was not until 2002 that McDonald's opened its first restaurant in Beijing, and it moved its
headquarters to Shanghai in 2004 [9]. Later, McDonald's opened stores in many large first‑tier and
second‑tier cities in China with certain consumption power [10]. After KFC's Old Beijing Chicken
Roll became a hit in 2002, McDonald's launched the Five‑Colored Tender Chicken Spinach Roll as a
reference. Following this first attempt, it gradually introduced Chinese‑style breakfast items such as
soy milk and fried dough sticks. Different from KFC's deep localization, McDonald's still focused
on its standardized burgers, fried chicken, and other Western fast food products. It can be seen that
although McDonald's and KFC are both influential Western fast food brands, McDonald's has been
relatively slow in localization and store expansion. KFC has opened a large number of stores in
third‑ and fourth‑tier cities and successfully integrated into grassroots consumers in China, while
McDonald's still focuses on first‑ and second‑tier cities with strong consumption power. With the
continuous development of China's economy and the expansion of McDonald's stores, the brand has
tentatively launched some localized products. Although its sales volume and store expansion results
are not as good as KFC's, its steady promotion of localization has still made it the second‑largest
international Western fast food brand in China.

4.3. Yoshinoya: cultural similarity and competitive pressure
Unlike the other two relatively successful Western fast food brands, Yoshinoya, a Japanese fast food
brand, is now facing considerable difficulties in the Chinese market. Yoshinoya entered the Chinese
mainland in 1992 and opened its first store in Wangfujing, Beijing. In the early stage of China's
reform and opening-up, Chinese consumers held high expectations for foreign brands. Influenced by
*Tokyo Love Story* and *Norwegian Wood* at that time, Japanese culture enjoyed considerable
popularity in China. As a result, Japanese fast food naturally became synonymous with high-end
Japanese cuisine in people's minds. Moreover, similar eating habits and dietary culture enabled
Yoshinoya to integrate into Chinese society relatively quickly. Japanese fast food, represented by
Yoshinoya, soon won a large number of post-80s and post-90s consumers. However, with the
development of the times, Yoshinoya's marketing strategies have encountered several problems.
First, Yoshinoya has a limited product range and lacks new product research and development,
which has made its original loyal customers feel bored. Second, as a fast food brand, its product
prices are too high. The signature beef rice, originally priced at 18 yuan per bowl, has now risen to
24 yuan, and the low cost-performance has discouraged some customers. Facing competition from
cheap and diverse local fast food brands such as Shaxian Snacks, Yoshinoya has gradually lost its
competitiveness. About 30 Yoshinoya stores closed in 2022, and its subsidiary brand Hanamaru
Udon completely withdrew from the Chinese market. In response to the rise of local brands and the
loss of consumers in the Chinese market, Yoshinoya has also made attempts at differentiation. It
launched limited-edition Pokémon tableware, renovated its restaurants into café-style venues, and
introduced Japanese anime culture to attract young people. Nevertheless, these attempts achieved
limited effects and failed to reverse Yoshinoya's difficult situation in the Chinese market [11].
4.4. Yangguofu Malatang: localization and Chinese brand globalization
When China became the second-largest economy, its market now influences the world and has
become more and more important. Chinese consumers no longer hold strong admiration for foreign
brands, and local Chinese fast‑food brands have gradually come into the spotlight. A representative
example of such brands that have expanded domestically and gained prominence overseas is
Yangguofu Malatang. By the end of 2025, the brand had nearly 200 overseas stores covering more
than 20 countries and regions. Taking South Korea as an example, the average daily revenue per
store reaches approximately 20,000 RMB. After its early rapid expansion, the brand has placed
greater emphasis on balancing Chinese malatang elements with local tastes and innovating dishes
with localized characteristics. It has shifted from catering to the demand for Chinese flavors among
overseas Chinese to attracting more local non‑Chinese consumers. While actively expanding into
overseas markets, Yangguofu has also strengthened its brand narrative. In South Korea, which shares
a similar dietary culture within the East Asian cultural sphere, Yangguofu adopted a differentiated
positioning by associating malatang with health preservation. It launched a herbal bone broth
containing more than 20 kinds of oriental botanical spices, including 16 herbal ingredients. Taking
the concept of "food therapy and health preservation, where medicine and food share the same
origin" as its new differentiating edge, Yangguofu has built a unique market position overseas [12].

6. Conclusion
Based on an analysis of the overseas marketing cases of four distinct brands and the perceived value
theory, the following conclusions can be drawn. Brands that differ greatly from the local original
culture should focus on localization, develop products with local characteristics to enhance local
consumers' sense of identity, and launch limited-time packages and promotional activities during
local traditional festivals to improve local consumers' favor and trust in the brand and increase their
emotional value. When entering markets with similar dietary cultures, brands should pursue
differentiation while expanding and strengthening their own brand narratives. While ensuring
product quality, brands should highlight their unique features to differentiate themselves from
similar local products and explore the space for cultural premium. Taking their own characteristics
as the entry point to lead the dietary trend and enhance local consumers' sense of social identity.
When fast food brands expand into overseas markets, they need to balance localization and brand
characteristics. Brands with obvious dietary cultural differences can promote localization to build
emotional identity among local consumers. Brands with similar dietary cultures need to build their
own brand narratives, innovate and launch characteristic dishes in line with their brand narratives,
lead the dietary trend of local consumers, and strengthen their social identity.
