Abstract
This article develops a theoretical  approach to explain the success of Specialized Tourism
Communities (CTEs) by modeling the relationship between government marketing, governance, and
innovation as an integrated mechanism rather than as separate domains. Building on place branding
and public management scholarship, the argument is that territorial brands become consistent and
durable only when they are embedded in multi-actor coordination, institutional decision-making, and
sustained innovation. The model sets three “hinge” mechanisms to verify the brand–performance link.
First is organizational innovation. This uses internal redesign and cross -sector coordination to turn
the brand into action. The second key element is adaptive capability, this includes the ability to sense
change and learn from it, the group then adjusts resources. The third element is social innovation, it
involves community plans that share benefits. These plans manage conflict and build legitimacy. This
paper views branding as more than simple communication, it reflects a changing institution. This
system learns and validates itself over time. This approach offers a structured framework for
understanding why some Creative Tourism Enterprises (CTEs) achieve competitiveness and
sustainability, while others remain hindered by fragile narratives.
Keywords: Specialized Tourism Communities, Government Marketing, Governance, Organizational
Innovation, Dynamic Capabilities.

1. Introduction
In local tourism contexts, a territorial brand is not an image exercise but an institutional
arrangement revealing how a place competes, coordinates, and governs. This article argues that the
relationship between government marketing, governance, and innovation explains why some
Specialized Tourism Communities (CTEs) build credible, durable public promises while others
remain trapped in fra gile narratives lacking implementation capacity. The discussion rests on three
premises: government marketing works as a public management technology producing value and
legitimacy; governance describes public action as coordination among interdependent ac tors; and
innovation in tourism is a structural condition that sustains differentiation and prevents obsolescence
in volatile markets.

The paper combines these premises into a verifiable model for CTEs, in which a destination
brand becomes solid only through three concrete mechanisms: organizational innovation, the internal
capacity to coordinate tasks; dynamic capabilities, the ability t o learn and adjust; and social
innovation, the community's capacity to maintain trust, share benefits, and manage conflict. This
framework organizes the literature and offers a strict diagnostic tool for identifying risks —shallow
marketing, hollow governance, and innovation that favors only visitors—while enabling comparison
across cases, explanation of deficiencies, and more accurate prediction of durable trajectories for
tourism development.

2. Government Marketing and Place Branding
2.1 Government Marketing
Government marketing is a strategic public management tool that strengthens the relationship
between institutions and citizens through communication, public value, and legitimacy. It differs from
political marketing, which targets acquiring power in electoral contexts (Maarek, 2012 [2007]);
government marketing instead operates after the election to susta in governability, legitimacy, and
implementation capacity, since democracies require social consent to execute policies (Lees -
Marshment ed., 2014). Marketing thus shifts from electoral persuasion toward relationship
management, communicating policies and building the consensus and cooperation implied by Kotler
and Levy's (1969) expansion of marketing into public and non-profit fields.
Wiebe (1951) first argued that public “products” —state services and programs —need
strategies comparable to commercial marketing to generate adoption, use, and support. Kotler and
Levy (1969) extended this logic to public and non -profit organizations genera lly, and Mokwa and
Permut (1981) coined the term “government marketing,” marking a shift from merely informing the
public toward building bonds of recognition and appreciation between citizens and government.
Spanish-speaking scholars affirm that government marketing serves public management and
legitimation (Asensio, 2008; Valdéz, 2012; Aguilar Miranda, 2017), though it is often confused with
institutional advertising or propaganda under a tradition that treat s propaganda as forced ideology
(Domenach, 2015). The key distinction is that propaganda indoctrinates and creates dependency,
whereas government marketing is justified within contexts of political competition and pluralism,
since governmental continuity d epends on generating trust, explaining decisions, and mobilizing
support (Wiebe, 1951; Lees-Marshment ed., 2014).
From a power -analytic perspective, government marketing functions as a technology of
governance that shapes behavior subtly through shared understandings and social agreement rather

coordinate diverse groups, fitting a collaborative, multi -partner governance model (Eshuis & Klijn,

institutional framework, political culture, and participatory openness.

pluralism and accountability, beyond simply winning elections (Maarek, 2012 [2007]; Lees -
Marshment ed., 2014). This approach aims to bridge governmental goals and perceived public value,
translating societal demands into workab le decisions through communicative, relational, and
coordination capabilities.

2.2 Place Branding and Related Concepts
Place branding—along with city branding, territorial branding, and city marketing—sits at the
intersection of strategic marketing, urban planning, and public policy. Although the term city
marketing became established in the 1990s, competition among cities  for visibility, population,
commerce, and prestige is far older. Huberman (1997 [1936], pp. 40 –51) describes how European
cities, from the Middle Ages onward, projected themselves along commercial routes to attract labor
and economic activity, confirming that territorial visibility is a structural, not merely modern,
necessity.
Urban spaces are designed with deliberate intent, and a city's appeal and quality of life depend
on how residents perceive the environment (Lynch, 1960), a shared perception that also builds
reputation and distinctiveness (Anholt, 2007). Modern city market ing emerged from late-twentieth-
century deindustrialization and budget crises, which forced cities to compete for capital and skilled

labor. Ashworth and Voogd's Selling the City (1990) marked this shift, linking marketing principles
to public planning and demanding tailored, non-standardized strategies for each city.
The field later moved toward place branding, a broader approach than mere promotion. This
shift coincides with a changing state role in urban governance and the construction of territorial
identity, shaped jointly by government, business, and citizens (Kavaratzis, 2004), driven by growing
global competition for investment, tourism, and cultural consumption. As Molotch (2006) notes,
places effectively become products within this competitive system. Local governments therefore
actively craft territorial visions through narratives, symbols, and policy. Branding thus ceases to be a
minor communication tool and becomes a governance instrument that organizes coalitions, aligns
interests, and sets development priorities (Lichrou et al., 2010; Eshuis & Klijn, 2012).
Kotler, Haider, and Rein (1993) define the modern meaning of place marketing as a diagnosis
and strategy—not a campaign —that identifies an economic role, incorporates social participation,
and aligns infrastructure, regulations, and incentives with a share d vision. Ashworth and Tunbridge
(2000), in The Tourist -Historic City, deepen the link between urban identity, tourism, and local
government, showing how historic cities convert heritage into destinations while officials must
simultaneously protect history , sustain residents' quality of life, and remain attractive to tourists.
Smyth (2016 [1994]) observes a similar dynamic in post -industrial cities, where urban marketing
relies on public–private coalitions rather than traditional advertising to attract capital and host major
events.
By the twenty -first century, scholars agreed that a place brand exceeds logos or slogans.
Kavaratzis (2004) described territorial identity as expressed through a region's actions, statements,
and physical traits—architecture, services, institutional behavior, and media images. Govers and Go
(2009) located identity where physical, virtual, and imagined worlds meet, while Anholt (2007)
termed this “competitive identity,” requiring strategic alignment of economic, cultural, and symbolic
policies.
Successful place branding requires combining strategic planning with local engagement.
International case studies (Dinnie ed., 2011) underscore participation's role in cultivating distinct,
credible place attributes, while Campelo ed. (2014) examines colla borative dynamics among
institutions, businesses, and communities in cultural identity formation. De-San-Eugenio and Ginesta
(2020), drawing on Catalonia, view brand governance as an instrument for aligning expectations and
projects; Pérez Heredia (2022) a dds that, in Murcia, the brand must remain on the development
agenda independent of electoral cycles. Pasquinelli (2015) shows how SMEs produce images

The shift from place and city marketing to place branding marks a major conceptual change,
moving beyond resource attraction toward actively building identity and strengthening governance
for development. This is valuable for local tourism environments, allowing analysis of branding as a
coordination tool and its relation to organizational and social innovation capacities, explored in later
sections.

3. Governance and Local Public Administration
3.1 Governance
Understanding governance as a contemporary logic of public action requires beginning with
the structural transformation of the state following the 1970s crisis of the bureaucratic and welfare
models. Rising fiscal pressures, globalization, and complex publ ic problems made hierarchy -based
coordination increasingly inefficient (CLAD, 1998). New Public Management was the first major
response, importing market tools and management practices into the public sector; Hood (1991) and
Osborne and Gaebler (1992) called for government to “reinvent” itself around results, efficiency, and
performance. Yet this change preserved a centralist premise: the state remained the dominant actor,
and society participated only marginally, as users or clients rather than co-producers of decisions.
The academic discussion shifted in the 1990s toward the governance model, which holds that
the state cannot organize everything through top -down commands; public action instead occurs in
networks where public, private, and social actors share resources, in formation, legitimacy, and
abilities. Rhodes (1996) and Stoker (1998) argue that governing means coordinating relationships
rather than issuing orders; Kooiman (2003) adds that public steering blends hierarchical, market, and
network methods. Stoker (1998)  lists defining features —multiple actors, mutual resource

dependence, and autonomous yet steerable networks —making coordination inherently political,
involving power, priorities, and the distribution of costs and benefits.
Bevir (2013) cautions against treating governance as a single “recipe,” defining it instead as
a broad category of practices spanning state, market, and society —a caution particularly relevant in
Latin America, where state capacities, inequality, and institutional distrust shape cooperation. Aguilar
Villanueva (2022) sees governance as the capacity to produce collective direction: designing
inclusive policies, managing conflict as routine, and generating decisions citizens accept. Without
romanticizing collaboration, local governments face real implementation limits absent coordination
and legitimacy; governance thus shifts practice from command -and-control toward managing
alignments through shared objectives, rules, deliberative arenas, and co-responsibility.
This approach directly affects territorial branding projects in tourism: rather than treating
cities and regions as administrative units or products to be sold, governance requires seeing them as
political communities where meanings, identities, and develo pment trajectories are negotiated.
Kavaratzis (2004, 2012) understands place branding as the management of meanings and
relationships rather than logo production. A local brand works when a governance architecture aligns
government, firms, and citizens aro und a shared narrative and coherent project portfolio (De -San-
Eugenio & Ginesta, 2020). Govers and Go (2009) show that brand value depends on lived experience,
requiring inter -organizational coordination and openness to participation; Baker (2019), studyin g
smaller destinations, links brand strength to the ability to sustain a credible promise.
Territorial branding functions as a governance instrument in tourism, ordering agendas,
prioritizing investments, and activating alliances that clarify a territory's direction. Pérez Rojas (2023)
frames city branding as a governance strategy that balances interests and aligns development policies,
reinforced by cooperation and innovation; coordination among government, private sector, academia,
and citizens explains why some destinations advance while others remain trapped in rhetoric. The
triple helix (Etzkowitz & Leydesdorff, 2000), later expanded into the quadruple helix by Carayannis
and Rakhmatullin (2014) to include civil society, frames this connection.
Government marketing and territorial branding must be framed within governance, which
determines how coalitions form and investment decisions are made. Government marketing is central
to local governance —generating information, shaping public narratives, a nd managing citizen
participation—thereby establishing political legitimacy. Where local authorities harmonize
narratives, initiatives, and regulations, strong unified brands emerge; where genuine coordination is
replaced by propaganda and visibility is mistaken for transformation, places fail to progress.

3.2 Local Governments and Public Policies for Tourism
As an illustrative frame of reference, consider Mexico, where local government is
constitutionally structured around the municipio libre (free municipality). Article 115 of the Mexican
Constitution grants municipalities legal status and authority to manage local finances and services,
allowing them to set territorial priorities. Mexico City's alcaldías (boroughs) act similarly but under
greater centralized constraints: formal autonomy exists technically, yet finance and programs remain
centrally controlled, reducing local government power —a limitation especially visible in tourism, a
sector requiring constant coordination.
Classical theory views local government as a site of institutional leadership; Selznick (1984
[1957]) argued that leading a public organization involves protecting values, building purpose, and
maintaining coalitions beyond mere administration. Local gover nment has become a vital platform
for development and competitiveness rather than a secondary entity, with coalitions essential for
project continuity. Osborne and Gaebler (1992) supported “entrepreneurial” government, gathering
resources and building alliances with private companies and citizens to solve real public problems—
applicable to tourism policy and territorial branding, where a local government's capacity to
coordinate and negotiate matters more than promotional campaigns alone.
Public policy is the tool that translates leadership and governance into action. Lasswell and
Lerner (1968) linked knowledge to collective decision -making, while Kingdon (1984) described
policy formulation as the confluence of problems, solutions, and poli tics, with entrepreneurs
exploiting windows of opportunity. Ospina and O'Sullivan (2007) later argued that policy analysis
requires rigorous evidence and a governance focus—vital given varying institutional capacities across
municipalities and boroughs. Local public policies are thus structured decisions that deploy financial,
regulatory, administrative, and symbolic resources to address concrete problems, not mere
bureaucratic steps.
Coordinating policy within the municipal cycle is difficult: local governments depend on
external funding, lack technical skills, and face short political terms, weakening consistency and
evaluation. Differing demands from residents, businesses, community groups, and the tourism sector
add complexity. Tourism -specific public policies are nonetheless vital frameworks, guiding
investment, land use, heritage protection, and visitor-flow management while shaping the destination
experience. These policies simultaneously generate a public narrative about the place—one that must
remain credible for both residents and visitors.
Municipal tourism policy balances economic gain against environmental protection and
resident quality of life. UNWTO (2022) frames such policy as a strategic guide directing investment

and supporting long -term health beyond simple regulation. Effective local plans combine
infrastructure, clear service standards, and shared marketing platforms into a unified tourism model,
with a consistent brand tying these elements together so that poli cy becomes a governing tool rather
than a set of separate projects. Ashworth and Tunbridge (2000) support this link between heritage,

Local government occupies a foundational role in effective territorial branding. Pasquinelli
and Kavaratzis (2025) argue that brand viability depends on local governments proactively
harmonizing stakeholders, setting clear strategic priorities, and maintai ning long-term consistency

to electoral change and to gaps between public image and reality. Branding thus becomes a vital
public asset rather than merely a sales tool, requiring managers to prioritize the general interest while
fostering participation and innovation.
Pérez Rojas (2024) compared public policies for developing tourism city brands in Barcelona,
Pátzcuaro, Hamburg, and Edmonton, showing that success depends on deliberate effort, authentic
attributes, and agreement among government, the private sector, and community—not accident.
Cities must balance visitor attraction with resident quality of life, relevant to Mexico's Pueblos
Mágicos program and its move toward community-centered models. Municipal governments should
lead in defining the tourism model and es tablishing regulations that ensure equitable distribution of
benefits and minimization of costs.
Mexican local governments face strict financial, legal, and environmental constraints that
reduce flexibility, making tourism policy and regional branding top priorities. Strong governance
strengthens these efforts, improving competitiveness, fostering innovation, and building public trust.
Government marketing, crucially, is not mere advertising but a core institutional skill: officials use it
to gather data, build authentic narratives, encourage participation, and coordinate diverse groups —
work that goes beyond communication to become central to how localities manage tourism,
generating lasting results and political support.

4. Innovation Systems and Clusters
4.1 Systemic and Sectoral Logic
Innovation and local/regional tourism development are structurally linked: in the
contemporary economy, a regional tourism brand is not an aesthetic exercise but a competitiveness
device requiring sustained innovation. In today's volatile environments —underscored by the post -

pandemic world (World Economic Forum, 2021) —innovation is essential for maintaining
competitiveness. Within tourism, a purchase is fundamentally an experiential promise; failure to
innovate and update the value proposition compromises that promise, inevita bly eroding brand
credibility.
Understanding this relationship requires moving beyond the traditional Innovation Systems
(IS) framework toward Innovation Ecosystems (IE). Where IS prioritized structure —macro actors
like firms, government, and research centers —IE offers a dynamic lens fo r tourism destinations,
viewing actors, activities, and artifacts as an evolving mix shaped by cooperation, competition, and
substitution (Granstrand & Holgersson, 2020) rather than a static snapshot. This shift matters because,
in tourism, artifacts such as products, services, and experiences are not peripheral—they are the core
of the brand promise and visitor evaluation.
The ecosystem's analytical value lies in revealing how brand management navigates
simultaneous cooperation —generating mutual stakeholder value —and competition for resources,
attention, positioning, and benefit capture. Granstrand and Holgersson (2020, p. 3 ) identify
continuous innovation in experience design and strategic deployment of tangible and intangible
assets, such as stakeholder engagement or ICT, as crucial differentiators. The ecosystem approach is
also compatible with transitions literature: a re silient territorial brand persists when its network of
actors can adapt to technological, regulatory, and socio-environmental transitions (Markard, 2020).
Tourism additionally requires incorporating the symbolic character of knowledge
underpinning visitor and resident experience. The differentiated knowledge bases perspective shows
that innovation varies across sectors according to distinct modes of knowledg e (Asheim & Coenen,
2005; Martin et al., in Isaksen, Martin, & Trippl, eds., 2018), and the symbolic knowledge base is
especially prominent where tourism overlaps with creative and cultural sectors —focused on
meaning-making and storytelling through aesthet ics, symbols, images, and narrative structures
(Lichrou et al., 2010). These symbolic layers are not decoration but the main source of value for
tourism products, conveying identity, distinctiveness, and the promise of a singular experience.
Place branding relies on this symbolic dimension: destination brands function as physical and
sensory experiences that build deep emotional connections (Eshuis & Ripoll González, 2025).
Symbolic knowledge is embedded in specific cultures, reflecting local norms and practices;
incorporating it into the brand supports authenticity by foregrounding heritage and history as
competitive assets (Kotler, Haider, & Rein, 1993). When brand strategy consistently communicates
these intangible but decisive emotions, it can reduce uncertainty and perceived risk in visitors'
decision-making (Hernández Mogollón et al., 2020).

The structural competitiveness approach ties together innovation, branding, and territorial
performance. Competitiveness derives not solely from firms' internal practices but from sectoral
structure, institutional framework, and governmental action (Porter , 2017 [1985]; Puccio & Grana,
2008, 2020). Tourism innovation can be defined as introducing new or significantly improved
products, processes, organizational methods, or market strategies that generate material and
immaterial advantages for tourism agents and host communities (Castillo & Cruz, 2022). Under this
lens, the brand is sustained not by communication but by territorial capacities that avoid obsolescence
amid environmental constraints and an increasingly informed, connected tourist.
This is why innovation policies and instruments specifically adapted to tourism matter.
Innovative Business Groups (AEIs) strengthen collaboration and collective learning (Ivars et al.,
2014), while the Smart Tourism Destination (DTI) model integrates tech nology, data, and
participatory management to enhance experience and sustainability (Forés & Fernández -Yáñez,
2020)—both expressions of a systemic logic translated into concrete sectoral capabilities.
The sectoral innovation approach specifies how systemic logic materializes: each sector
combines its knowledge base, demand patterns, business networks, regulation, and institutional
arrangements uniquely, generating differentiated trajectories (Malerba, 2 002). In tourism, this
appears through territorial clusters and coordination among local governments, firms, universities,
and communities, where innovation becomes translated into routines, standards, and operational
agreements. The destination thus operates as a relatively autonomous tourism system whose rules and
practices shape performance (Pasquinelli & Kavaratzis, 2025).
The literature on trajectories reinforces the importance of strategic direction: technology
determines shared trajectories (Dosi, 1982), and a tourism brand risks stagnation by clinging to
outdated models that ignore the destination's actual issues. Echoing Kotler, Haider, and Rein (1993),
territorial rescue rarely comes from central governments given resource scarcity, so cities and citizens
must cultivate their own competitive strengths—directing innovation toward organizational structure,
commercial strategies, goal-setting, and consensus -building, all of which rely on coordination and
governance (Eshuis & Klijn, 2012; Castillo & Cruz, 2022).
Tourism functions as a distinct sector where innovation happens incrementally, with less R&D
intensity than manufacturing (Castillo & Cruz, 2022). The strategic goal is not constant breakthroughs
but accelerating diffusion, learning, and consistency, since  technology advances unevenly across
regions, shaping convergence or divergence (Kuei-Kuei et al., 2023). Destinations must raise quality
and service standards so that reputation, accessibility, or infrastructure issues do not block
competitiveness.

An operational thesis follows: the brand is not built through ephemeral campaigns; its
perception results from sustained, shared, cumulative effort (Dosi, 1982). Place branding research
shows success depends more on long -term management than disruptive adv ertising (Puig, 2009;
Dinnie ed, 2011). Especially in historic urban areas, innovation proves valuable when aligned with
local character, enhancing a distinctive value proposition rooted in the place's genuine particularities
(Ashworth & Tunbridge, 2000).
A tourism brand functions as an integrated product, incorporating attractions, services, and
infrastructure (Smyth, 2016 [1994]), viewable as an assembled system (Stephan et al., 2017) whose
performance depends on sectoral configuration. Coordination, tech nological alignment, and
experiential coherence are essential, since the system spans accommodation, transport, heritage,
digital technologies, and public services; configurational diversity demands political coordination
beyond conventional technology-push or demand-pull approaches (Stephan et al., 2017, p. 33), while
digitalization reconfigures capabilities through the co -evolution of technologies, innovation, and
skills (Ciarli et al., 2021; Kuei-Kuei et al., 2023). In sum, sectoral innovation keeps the brand promise
viable in a market where differentiation demands continuous adaptation (Forés & Fernández-Yáñez,
2020; Dosi, 1982; Ciarli et al., 2021).

4.2 Tourism Competitiveness through Clusters
Moving from innovation systems and sectors to the operational terrain of tourism localities,
the cluster perspective is decisive: it shows how innovation relationships become territorialized in
geographic concentrations of firms and institutions that coope rate and compete simultaneously
(Porter, 1998). A tourism cluster drives innovation by uniting specialized production, service
networks, and institutions under a shared territorial identity that defines the value proposition.
Place branding today functions as a measure of organizational capacity, showing whether a
territory truly works as a creative cluster capable of sustaining uniqueness over time; building a
destination brand requires more than a logo and slogan (Govers & Go, 2009)—it demands structural
ability to compete through continuous improvement of offers, systems, and connection with people
(Puccio & Grana, 2020; Díaz & Ruiz, 2025). Schumpeter's (1934) theory holds that development
stems from “new combinations” that t ransform production and society; in tourism, this implies
constant renewal of the traveler's experience without damaging the core symbols anchoring
destination identity (Smyth, 2016 [1994]), a balance that prevents the place from becoming outdated
(Baker, 2014).

Competitiveness scholarship argues that natural or cultural attractions alone are insufficient;
competitive advantage stems from the business and institutional environment's ability to innovate,
improve, and differentiate (Porter, 2017 [1985]). If a territ ory fails to convert resources into
memorable experiences backed by coherent services, infrastructure, and rules, branding becomes
mere rhetoric (Govers & Go, 2009, p. 264). Tourism innovation takes multiple forms —product,
process, organizational, marketin g, institutional —though incremental innovations from collective
learning and territorial networks tend to predominate (Hjalager, 2010), consistent with regional
innovation systems where competitiveness emerges from interactions among firms, knowledge,
policy, and culture (Cooke, 2003; Asheim & Coenen, 2005).
Place branding is the set of perceptions and expectations tied to a location (Ashworth &
Tunbridge, 2000; Govers & Go, 2009; Puig, 2009). Because identity, experience, and image are
linked, brands cannot be invented from scratch—they must be rooted in genuine identity and shown
through consistent experiences for residents and visitors alike (Govers & Go, 2009). Innovation
systems add discipline here, supporting coordinated growth of the offer, organizational learning, and
public policies that allow testing and adaptation.
Puccio and Grana (2020) identify three capacities sustaining destination brands:
differentiating products and services with evidence rather than promises; managing territorial
complexity through governance and institutions; and adapting technology and narr atives to
environmental and social demands without lowering experiential quality —consistent with the Oslo
Manual's expanded definition of innovation, which recognizes organizational, marketing, and
institutional innovation beyond industrial firms (OECD/Eurostat, 2018). In tourism brands, decisive
changes often occur through interaction among governments, operators, residents, and visitors; Puig
(2009) suggests the city brand can simultaneously serve as a governance instrument and a device for
public innovation.
The most tangible contribution of innovation systems to branding is the tourism cluster: a
geographically concentrated network of tourism firms and related organizations, articulated through
cooperation and competition, that increases a destination's competitive advantage and innovativeness
(Porter, 1998; Kachniewska, 2013; Pérez Cárdenas & Paredes, 2024). Proximity facilitates
knowledge transfer with measurable performance effects (Quatraro & Usai, 2016), while social
capital—trust, networks, cooperation —provides the soft foundation for generating ideas and
competing better (Putnam, 1993), building operational reputation in branding terms.
Cluster collaboration links intrinsically to the triple helix model, where interaction among
universities, the state, and industry drives knowledge production, technology development, and

innovation (Sábato, 1975; Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 2000). This coordination is fundamental to
destination governance: brand value strengthens when institutional actions are coherent, reinforcing
legitimacy (Smyth, 2016 [1994]; Puig, 2009; Baker, 2014). A ccordingly, several authors emphasize
place branding as a governance mechanism, not merely a promotional technique (Govers & Go, 2009;
Campelo ed, 2019 [2017]).
Current theorists view innovation, governance, and sustainability as deeply connected
(Pasquinelli & Kavaratzis, 2025). Innovation builds adaptive skills —the ability to reorganize
resources and strategies during unstable times (Teece et al., 1997)—and government marketing serves
as a system for environmental scanning, multi -stakeholder dialogue, and institutionalizing
participatory channels. This requires transcending bureaucratic fragmentation by aligning
traditionally siloed domains —tourism, culture, econ omic development, communication —within a
unified territorial vision.
The Smart Tourism Destination model emphasizes quality, excellence, and the simultaneous
attainment of economic, social, and environmental sustainability (Forés & Fernández-Yáñez, 2020).
Branding strengthens when it integrates eco -innovation and circular e conomy practices into the
cluster's value proposition (Pérez Cárdenas & Paredes, 2024), reorienting the tourist –resident
relationship toward balance and heritage valorization.
In sum, the literature converges on a strategic message: territorial branding rests on the
articulation between innovation and governance, not on graphic solutions or isolated campaigns
(Kotler, Haider, & Rein, 1993; Ashworth & Tunbridge, 2000; Govers & Go, 2009; Puig, 2009; Dinnie
ed, 2011; Baker, 2014; Smyth, 2016 [1994]; Madden, 2018; Campelo, 2019 [2017]). In execution,
marketing and communication should be the closing phase: after defining the destination concept,
building real agreements, allocating responsibilities, and aligning policies and investments, only then
can the brand be innovative, legitimate, and sustainable —and the tourism cluster translate identity
into verifiable competitiveness.

5. Toward an Integrative Model of Specialized Tourism Communities
5.1 Requirements of an Integrative System
The articulation between government marketing, governance, and innovation explains why
some tourism communities (CTEs) in the State of Mexico build consistent territorial brands while
others remain trapped in fragile narratives. In these localities, the br and cannot be reduced to a
campaign: it requires multi-actor coordination, conflict management, and adaptive capacity, aligning

with Ashworth and Tunbridge's (2000) finding that destination competitiveness and sustainability
depend on institutional arrangements and policy decisions, not ornamental communication.
Previous sections established the relationship between innovation and territorial
competitiveness (ecosystems, clusters, symbolic knowledge); closing the causal circuit between
marketing and governance requires three “hinge” concepts operating at the local  and organizational
level: organizational innovation, dynamic capabilities, and social innovation. These are necessary,
not cosmetic, because they show the tangible link between brand and performance and guarantee
sustained operation —without them, claims t hat marketing “coordinates” or governance “aligns”
remain unproven.
Government marketing establishes a public commitment and reinforces shared understanding,
promoting cooperation between residents and businesses. Its value lies in building relationships of
recognition with citizens (Mokwa & Permut, 1981), also linked to t rust and social cohesion in the
Spanish-speaking context (Valdez, 2012; Otero -Ortega & Otero -Romero, 2021). From a place
branding perspective, local government formulates a destination value promise and delimits strategic
publics by integrating the expectations of residents, firms, social organizations, and visitors (Kotler,
Haider, & Rein, 1993; Govers & Go, 2009; Dinnie, ed., 2011); in CTEs, government marketing thus
converts a territory's identity and strategic vision into a practical, debatable narrative.
Public action in tourism is not unilateral command but an interdependent network with
distributed resources, information, and veto power (Rhodes, 1996; Kooiman, 2003). Local
municipalities build credible brands by orchestrating rather than broadcasting, co nnecting interests
and managing trade-offs concerning public space, overcrowding, heritage protection, and regulation.
Aguilar Villanueva (2022) notes that governing means steering collective action toward legitimate
decisions and visible results, not imposed direction.
In CTEs, a territorial brand functions as a symbolic representation of governance, integrating
internal agreements, political priorities, and operational rules into a unified narrative; a successful
national narrative requires both domestic credibility and  international appeal (Anholt, 2007). A
brand's fragility becomes evident when its external projection lacks alignment with internal
consensus, since the brand is essentially a promise that governance must coordinate. A destination's
true performance depen ds on three mechanisms operating within both the administrative structure
and the broader social environment:
(a) Organizational innovation: the internal mechanism that makes the brand executable
In CTEs, taking territorial branding seriously forces local government to redesign how it
works. The Oslo Manual defines organizational innovation as a major change in management

practices requiring shifts in internal structure, coordination, or external relations to improve outcomes
(OECD/Eurostat, 2018). Branding often remains a superficial discourse detached from needed
internal change, yet a genuine brand promise forces destina tions to reorganize, creating cross -
functional teams that coordinate typically separate sectors—tourism, economic development, culture,
communication, mobility, security, and public services —since research shows these areas must be
aligned to deliver a consistent brand (Govers et al., 2015; Madden, 2018).
Organizational innovation is thus paramount to sustaining a municipal branding strategy
beyond a mere collection of campaigns: organizational capacity is a clear prerequisite for effective
public branding, since defining an identity is insufficient if the organization itself is not built to
execute it.
(b) Dynamic capabilities: the adaptive mechanism that prevents destination obsolescence
Tourism operates in a fast -changing environment marked by shifting regulations, online
reputation, and technology, alongside evolving visitor needs and social patterns. Teece et al. (1997)
defined dynamic capabilities as the ability to rearrange skills and  resources as the environment
moves—a concept that explains why some local governments sustain a strategic path and learn, while
others merely react and miss new opportunities.
Government marketing acts here not merely as speech but as a system for listening, watching,
and learning: municipalities gather feedback from visitors, providers, and residents to monitor
saturation, safety perceptions, housing pressure, and online reputa tion, using this information to
update communications, rank investments, and adjust rules. Networks —tourism clusters, brand
roundtables, agreements with key actors —process this adaptation, connecting with innovation -
ecosystem logic that integrates governme nt, firms, universities, and civil society (Carayannis &
Rakhmatullin, 2014), particularly relevant in cluster -based destinations where hospitality,
gastronomy, transport, culture, and commerce meet (Porter, 1998; Kachniewska, 2013).
Adaptive capabilities thus bridge governance and innovation, explaining how a destination
stays consistent under change, adapting without breaking identity or losing legitimacy.
(c) Social innovation: mechanism of legitimacy and sustainability
Social innovation is the third crucial element, essential because tourism generates externalities
and conflicts unresolved by administrative improvements or visitor -focused digital tools alone.
Mulgan et al. (2007) define social innovation as new ideas that meet social needs and improve quality
of life; as tourism grows in CTEs, it often worsens conflicts over public space, housing costs, and
cultural friction, triggering public discussion about who benefits and who bears the burdens (Castillo

Ferráez & Valverde, 2018). Branding that ignores these problems may raise short -term demand but
destroys social agreement over time.
Social innovation builds institutional and community structures that share power and benefits
fairly—citizen brand councils, participatory budgeting, visitor codes of conduct, secure route
systems, and certification programs supporting local enterprises linked to distinct identity, alongside
mechanisms ensuring shared responsibility for heritage preservation. Government marketing can
catalyze this by making successful practices visible, producing local pride, and positioning
collaboration as a distinctive destination feature (Bassano et al., 2019; Campelo ed, 2019 [2017]); in
sum, social innovation is the legitimacy component ensuring the brand is an appropriated project, not
an imposed promise.

5.2 From the Public Promise to Territorial Tourism Performance—and Its Risks
With these three pieces in place, the marketing –governance–innovation relationship can be
modeled as a verifiable circuit in Specialized Tourism Communities (CTEs):
Promise and alignment (government marketing + governance): local government identifies
icons, attributes, and a value proposition, then consolidates them through collaborative arenas where
the tourism model, priority publics, rules, and investment portfolio are negotiated. The brand, at this
point, consolidates the agreement—an external commitment supported by internal alignment (Eshuis
& Klijn, 2012; De-San-Eugenio & Ginesta, 2020; Madden, 2018; Pasquinelli & Kavaratzis, 2025).
Execution and internal redesign (organizational innovation): the agreement needs structure.
Municipalities create units or roles, change rules, and coordinate across sectors —establishing
connections with the cluster, as outlined in the Oslo Manual (OECD/Eu rostat, 2018) —thereby
transforming a “brand” into an active capability (Govers et al., 2015; Madden, 2018).
Lasting adaptation (dynamic capabilities): a municipality must show resilience and continuity,
learning as the environment changes. Adaptive capacity involves spotting environmental signs,
reallocating resources, adjusting the public narrative, and updatin g policy priorities (Teece et al.,
1997); in tourism clusters, this process is amplified by leveraging existing networks and platforms
for experimentation (Porter, 1998; Carayannis & Rakhmatullin, 2014).
Governance and marketing must collaborate to produce concrete social innovations, including
eco-innovation, circular economy models, digital destination management, specialized training,
thematic tourism routes, and community -based tourism projects (Forés & Fernández-Yáñez, 2020;
Pérez Cárdenas & Paredes, 2024). When residents recognize that benefits align with their

19

community's identity, social capital and cooperation strengthen (Putnam et al., 1993), providing
stability for both the brand and the cluster.
This circuit clarifies why the brand should not be analyzed as a “communicative outcome.”
Marketing innovation, per the Oslo Manual, is an institutional arrangement that evolves and gains
legitimacy over time, arising from new methods for research, positio ning, or managing user
relationships—sustained only when supported by corresponding innovations within the organization
and community (OECD/Eurostat, 2018).
Typical risks must be taken seriously. Superficial marketing —focused on logos and
campaigns rather than organizational change and social agreements —generates visibility without
genuine capacity, leading to a shaky reputation. Performative governance, where talk of cooperation
masks the dominance of powerful stakeholders or hidden agreements, causes a negative shift in
tourism advantages. Innovation centered only on the visitor favors tourism technology while ignoring
local inequality and distributional disputes, eroding legitimacy. This model evaluates whether CTEs
avoid these drifts: whether marketing functions as a coordination technology, whether governance
produces real agreements, and whether organizational, adaptive, and social innovation translate int o
performance and well-being.
Within specialized tourism communities, government marketing, governance, and innovation
are deeply interconnected rather than separate. Marketing establishes and reinforces the public
commitment; governance sets rules, builds coalitions, and determines pr iorities; organizational
innovation facilitates execution; dynamic capabilities ensure ongoing adaptation; and social
innovation secures legitimacy and long -term sustainability. Under this architecture, the territorial
brand becomes evidence of an innovative governance process that competes without tearing the social
fabric—the guiding criterion for evaluating CTEs: whether the brand expresses a learning,
coordinating, distributing institutional arrangement, or remains a competitive narrative without
transformation (Pasquinelli & Kavaratzis, 2025).

6. Conclusions
First conclusion: in CTEs, government marketing and place branding are not mere
communication layers but governance infrastructure that structures a credible public promise,
stabilizes shared meanings, and —when designed as verifiable dialogue —becomes a coo rdination
mechanism aligning expectations, reducing uncertainty, and increasing the political feasibility of
local decisions. A brand's value lies in its role as a symbolic expression of governance, consolidating

a territory's rules, priorities, and trade-offs into a coherent narrative that must be legitimate internally
while remaining competitive externally.
Second conclusion: tourism brands compete through innovation rather than campaigns.
Innovation networks, clusters, and symbolic knowledge sustain the brand promise, since visitors
judge tourism artifacts —services and experiences —through the meanings, stori es, and physical
experiences that make the difference stick. Clusters and helix -style coordination function as the
region's engine, converting identity into real experiences through shared learning and clear systems;
the destination's genuine organizational capability is mirrored in the strength of its brand.
The third conclusion links marketing and governance through three mechanisms:
organizational innovation, dynamic capabilities, and social innovation, which convert brand promises
into measurable results and prove the brand acts on its word. Organizational change makes promises
real through new structures and teamwork; dynamic capabilities prevent decline by enabling the
destination to notice environmental change, learn, and adapt; and social innovation earns the
community approval tourism requires, through feedback channels, fair distribution of benefits, and
dispute resolution. Judged against these tools, CTEs risk failure through shallow marketing, hollow
management, or innovation privileging only visitors —each flaw representing a circuit break that
reduces branding to fragile, ineffective rhetoric.
