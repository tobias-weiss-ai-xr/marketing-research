Abstract
This study examines the concept of Zeitgeist — the prevailing cultural spirit of an era — as a
theoretical lens for understanding brand identity transformation in the context of new media.
Drawing on Herder’s and Hegel’s philosophical foundations, Krause’s  (2019)
operationalisation of period -specific cultural patterns, and Holt’s (2004) cultural branding
framework, the paper develops an original typology of brand metamorphosis comprising three
strategic orientations: reactive, proactive, and performative. The Dove Real Beauty Campaign
(2004–2024) is examined as the primary illustrative case for proactive metamorphosis through
a multi -method analysis combining documentary analysis, MAXQDA qualitative content
analysis of campaign texts across four phases (133 coded segments, 18 sub -codes). The
analysis suggests how a brand may sustain identity-stable instrumental metamorphosis across
successive Zeitgeist waves — from industrial beauty standards through digital filter culture,
social media comparison anxiety, and AI-generated imagery — while navigating the structural
paradoxes of commodity feminism and corporate -level misalignment. This study aims to
contribute to cultural branding theory by proposing an empirically grounded typology of brand
metamorphosis and offering what appears to be one of the first MAXQDA-assisted longitudinal
analyses of a femvertising campaign’s discursive evolution across four distinct Zeitgeist
configurations.
Keywords: Zeitgeist, Brand metamorphosis, Cultural branding, New media.

Digital Zeitgeist: Brand Metamorphosis in the Axis of New

Introduction
In the contemporary digital environment, brands face a recurring challenge: the need
to realign their identities with rapidly shifting cultural climates. While brand
management literature acknowledges the importance of cultural relevance, it has yet
to fully theorise the mechanisms through which brands reconfigure identity narratives
in response to such transformations — particularly as digital infrastructures compress
the temporal frames within which cultural trends emerge and dissipate (Castells, 2009;
Vredenburg et al., 2020).
To address this challenge, this study adopts the philosophical notion of Zeitgeist
— literally “spirit of the times” — as a conceptual lens. Originating in Johann Gottfried
Herder’s emphasis on historical-cultural specificity (Herder, 2002) and later refin ed in
Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel’s account of consciousness unfolding within distinct
historical contexts (Hegel, 1807/1977), Zeitgeist denotes the prevailing collective
mentality that both shapes and is shaped by symbolic production. Within this
framework, brands are understood not merely as market entities but as cultural
artefacts embedded in, and responsive to, the broader socio -cultural climate. Their
narratives participate in an ongoing dialogue between evolving societal values and
identity construction at individual and collective levels.
Holt’s (2004) model of cultural branding offers the central theoretical framework
for exploring these dynamics. At its core is the concept of “identity myths”: narrative
constructs through which brands respond symbolically to tensions inherent in a given
Zeitgeist. These myths emerge from widespread anxieties, desires, or contradictions,
providing audiences with story -based resolutions that confer meaning and
differentiation. Further work by Holt and Cameron (2010) elaborates this process within
competitive myth markets where authenticity and cultural resonance determine brand
longevity. However, while cultural branding theory explains how brands craft myths
aligned with prevailing conditions, it does not systematically account for how such
myths are transfo rmed across successive shifts in Zeitgeist — especially under

conditions of accelerated change characteristic of digital media ecologies (Castells,
2009; Jenkins, 2006; van Dijck, 2013).
The present study aims to extend Holt’s framework by developing a typology of
brand metamorphosis — reactive, proactive, and performative — grounded in Krause’s
(2019) operationalisation of Zeitgeist as a period-specific configuration of socio-cultural
forces. Reactive metamorphosis captures narrative adjustments prompted directly by
emergent cultural tensions or reputational crisis; proactive metamorphosis refers to
anticipatory positioning based on forecasted Zeitgeist shifts; performative
metamorphosis i nvolves surface -level symbolic alignment without substantive
organisational change, a pattern described in the brand activism literature as “woke
washing” (Vredenburg et al., 2020). By mapping these modes onto specific instances
of brand evolution, this st udy offers an analytical tool that may complement extant
theories and address some of their limitations regarding temporal change rates.
Theoretical Framework
Zeitgeist: Philosophical and Cultural Foundations
Johann Gottfried Herder argued that each cultural epoch possesses distinct collective
modes of thinking arising from the intertwining of historical, political, economic, and
geographic circumstances (Herder, 2002). Cultural production is not an abstract entity
but a living expression of prevailing collective cognition: art, literature, and symbolic
representation are shaped by, and reciprocally contribute to, their socio -historical
contexts.
The transition from medieval symbolism to Renaissance humanism illustrates this
principle with particular clarity. Medieval artistic practice was anchored in
transcendental symbolism, with creative works reflecting a worldview organized
around divine order  rather than individual achievement. As economic structures,
geographic knowledge, and religious ideology underwent transformation, collective
cognition altered accordingly. Renaissance humanism infused creative production with
individuality, secular inquiry, and anthropocentric values. Such shifts confirm Herder’s
central claim: each epoch contains distinct conceptual laws governing perception,
creativity, and symbolic expression (Neves, 2024).

Critically for the purposes of this study, Herder’s framework resists the
assumption of a singular, universal Zeitgeist. His sensitivity to cultural pluralism
suggests that different societies produce different forms of collective thought
simultaneously — a point of considerable relevance when examining how brands
navigate multicultural markets and heterogeneous audiences. Brands functioning as
cultural artefacts must adapt their identity myths for heterogeneous audiences if they
aim for resonance without erasing local particularities (Kipnis et al., 2019).
Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel, writing in the Phenomenology of Spirit  (1807),
extended and systematized the concept of Zeitgeist within a broader account of the
dialectical unfolding of collective consciousness across historical periods. For Hegel,
the spirit of a given age is not merely descriptive; it is constitutive. Philosophical works,
artistic productions, and social institutions are all concrete embodiments of Spirit’s
current shape, expressing the contradictions and resolutions particular to their
historical moment. Crucially, Hegel argues that no individual can surpass the spirit of
their own time, for the spirit of the age is also their own spirit.
This dialectical dimension carries direct implications for the analysis of brands as
cultural actors. Where Hegel views artistic or philosophical works as concrete
embodiments of Spirit’s current shape, Holt (2004) interprets brand narratives —
particularly “identity myths” — as vehicles for addressing the contemporary cultural
contradictions that define a given period’s Zeitgeist. When inherited symbolic forms no
longer mediate effectively between subjective aspirations and objective social
conditions, a c risis of relevance occurs. New symbolic forms must emerge — or, in
Hegelian terms, be sublated — to reflect the transformed Zeitgeist. Brands that fail to
rearticulate their symbolic content during such moments of disruption risk becoming
obsolete, superseded by those more attuned to the emergent spirit of the times.
Furthermore, the acceleration of communicative infrastructures in the digital age
introduces what may be understood as a fragmentation of the Zeitgeist itself. Digitally
mediated disruptions — viral discourse, platform-driven opinion shifts, algorithmicall y
amplified cultural moments — compress the temporal scales on which symbolic
reconfiguration historically occurred, demanding a speed of adaptation previously
unnecessary.
From a sociological perspective, the concept of Zeitgeist encompasses the
collective consciousness, cultural symbolism, and ideological formations that both

reflect and structure social relations in a given historical period. This approach draws
on semiotic and cultural analysis to examine how prevailing currents of meaning are
produced, sustained, and contested across different social groups. Roland Barthes’
formulation of myth as a second -order semiological system is directly relevant here:
signs — combinations of signifiers and signifieds in the first order — can become
signifiers in a broader mythic discourse, thereby transmitting ideologies beneath their
surface meaning. In this sense, Zeitgeist is not a neutral “spirit of the age” but an arena
in which hegemonic forces negotiate symbolic control.
Krause (2019) proposes that Zeitgeist be understood not as a unified spirit of an
age but as a hypothesis for a pattern in meaningful practices specific to a historical
period, linking different realms of social life across geographical contexts. Criticall y,
multiple Zeitgeists may coexist at any given moment, varying in duration and scope.
Products and brands become symbols within this field: prevailing cultural currents tend
to shape material preferences by embedding them within identity discourses tied t o
local or global orientations (Sulhaini, 2021).
Media platforms such as social networks disseminate symbolic messages rapidly
across diverse geographies. This acceleration compresses the traditional temporalities
assumed in earlier sociological accounts; shifts in collective sentiment or ideological
emphasis may occur in days rather than years (Castells, 2009). Contemporary media
institutions contribute actively to shaping collective identities by filtering information
streams, framing public discourse, and offering content designed to reinforce socio -
cultural symbols. Sociological interpretations emphasise that control over media
production allows certain narratives, and thus certain interpretations of reality, to
dominate — a dynamic that brand managers operating within Holt’s (2004) cultural
branding framework must actively engage with.
Anthropological approaches to cultural epochs treat the concept of Zeitgeist as a
reflection of the collective imagination and cognition operating within a society’s
material and symbolic production. In such perspectives, artistic forms, narratives, and
ritual practices are examined not as isolated creations but as interconnected
expressions of societal modes of thought that evolve with historical transformations.
The anthropological emphasis lies in relating these broad shifts to concrete artefacts,
seeing them as microcosms of their era’s worldviews (Neves, 2024).

Cultural Branding and Identity Myths
Brands conceived within the cultural branding framework operate as more than
transactional vehicles; they become symbolic artefacts deeply embedded in the
cultural milieu from which they emerge. The model proposed by Douglas Holt (2004)
positions brands wi thin a “myth market,” where the competition is anchored not in
functional differentiators but in their capacity to convey identity myths that resonate
with prevailing cultural contradictions. These myths are constructed from anxieties,
desires, and tension s circulating within the collective consciousness, allowing the
brand to link personal identity narratives to shared societal symbols. When such myths
are perceived as authentic, drawing upon what Holt calls “populist worlds,” they acquire
legitimacy and the ability to orient consumer meaning-making.
Iconic brands recognised within this model demonstrate sensitivity to disruption;
when key cultural changes occur, they must reinvent their myths to avoid a decline in
relevance (Holt, 2004). In Spain, brands such as Campofrío and IKEA have been
identified as successfully embedding campaigns within social tensions felt during
2008–2018, including feminist movements, brain drain, and political polarisation, by
aligning messages with relevant socio -historical disruptions (Sanz -Marcos, 2019).
Cultural fusion strategies add further complexity by combining traditional elements with
contemporary features. Chinese “Guochao” fashion branding blends recognisable
heritage symbols with modern lifestyle cues, creating rich symbolic ecosystems that
appeal to audiences in habiting multiple overlapping cultural affiliations (Dai et al.,
2024). Brands unable to contextualise identity myths appropriately risk misalignment
and diminished resonance across heterogeneous audiences (Kipnis et al., 2019).
Douglas Holt’s model of cultural branding, developed most fully in How Brands
Become Icons  (2004), places “identity myths” at the centre of how brands operate
within the symbolic economy. These myths are narrative constructs designed to
address tensions and contradictions perceived in the collective consciousness. Holt

frames these contradictions as emerging from what he terms populist worlds  —
culturally potent spaces whose ideologies are regarded by audiences as authentic, and
which provide the raw material from which brand narratives are constructed.
Within this framework, value is not confined to utilitarian product attributes. It
derives from a brand’s capacity to absorb and reflect prevailing cultural tensions,
resolving them symbolically through stories that consumers experience as personally
meaningful. Brands compete not in a conventional product marketplace but in a myth
market, where the currency is identity rather than utility (Holt, 2004).
When the Zeitgeist shifts, established myths lose traction unless brands reinvent
their narratives — a requirement that mirrors Hegel’s dialectical logic: outdated
symbolic forms must be replaced by those congruent with the emergent cultural
moment. Failure to maintain this alignment produces symbolic obsolescence (Kipnis
et al., 2019).
As noted above, digital infrastructures have compressed the temporal dynamics
through which Zeitgeist operates, so that symbolic reconfiguration once measured in
decades now unfolds over days or weeks (Castells, 2009).
Empirical literature on digital media’s relation to sense of place shows that virtual
spaces coexist with real -world geographical interactions. Residents share stories,
imagery, and commentary online, enhancing emotional resonance with local
communities. S uch practices integrate brand perceptions with specific locations
through social media and virtual experience technologies, linking commercial symbols
to environmental and cultural attributes (Dai & Liu, 2024). For brands, this means that
symbolic identity  is continuously co -constructed across physical and digital
environments, demanding strategies that account for both registers simultaneously.
For brands operating within Holt’s (2004) cultural branding framework, this
acceleration introduces a structural challenge. The iterative myth renewal that Holt
describes as essential to maintaining iconic status must now occur within dramatically
shortened timeframes. Brand narratives that lag behind the speed of cultural sentiment
risk irrelevance not over years, but over a single news cycle. The Zeitgeist, in the digital
age, no longer waits.

conditions where local and global identities often interact dynamically, sometimes

but maintaining authenticity while appealing broadly requires careful calibration lest
hybrid forms lose connection with their originating communities. Successful myth
construction depends on reading these intersecting forces accurately within agile

New media platforms have altered the structural relationship between brands and
consumers in ways that are immediate and profound. Social media, algorithmic content
distribution, and participatory culture have replaced one -directional brand
communication w ith multi -directional interactions where meaning is collaboratively
constructed. The affordances of platforms like TikTok, Instagram, and X/Twitter give
audiences not merely interpretive latitude but direct channels for content creation and
dissemination.
Jenkins’ (2006) concept of participatory culture captures this shift succinctly. He
describes environments in which consumers are not passive recipients of media but
active contributors whose creative participation — whether through remixing,
commentary, o r original production — shapes the cultural artefacts themselves. In
such contexts, brand communication increasingly depends on user-generated content
that may extend, subvert, or reframe official narratives. This decentralisation of
meaning-making forces brand managers to reckon with an expanded set of authorship
claims over brand identity.
Van Dijck’s (2013) work emphasises that platforms are not neutral arenas: their
algorithms mediate visibility and determine which brand narratives gain traction.
Castells’ (2009) concept of the network society adds that power accrues to those able
to navigate information flows across interconnected nodes. Brands unable to insert
themselves effectively into these flows risk marginalisation. This transformed

Brand Metamorphosis: A Typology
Brand metamorphosis may be defined as the deliberate transformation of a brand’s
identity, core narrative, and symbolic positioning in response to, or anticipation of, shifts
in prevailing cultural currents. To understand what such a transformation entails , it is
first necessary to clarify the nature of the Zeitgeist itself. As established in Section 2.1,
Krause (2019) frames Zeitgeist not as a unified spirit of an age but as a period-specific
pattern in meaningful practices, with multiple such patterns coexisting at any moment;
a brand therefore navigates a field of overlapping and sometimes competing patterns
rather than a single coherent spirit (Krause, 2019). Within Holt’s (2004) cultural
branding paradigm, brand metamorphosis constitutes a recalibration of identity myths
in response to shifts within this field — an attempt to reconnect the brand’s symbolic
positioning with the dominant or emergent patterns of its era. This study proposes a
three-part typology to distinguish the principal strategic orient ations through which
such recalibration occurs: reactive metamorphosis, proactive metamorphosis, and
performative metamorphosis. Reactive metamorphosis describes crisis -driven
transformations undertaken when an existing brand myth has been rendered
incompatible with contemporary ideological climates — such changes are typically
compressed in timing and motivated by reputational threat. Proactive metamorphosis
comprises anticipatory transformations aimed at leading cultural shifts rather than
following them;  campaigns such as Dove’s Real Beauty advocacy or Nike’s
engagement with athlete activism exemplify how brands can seed new populist worlds
before they fully saturate mainstream discourse (Holt, 2004; Holt & Cameron, 2010).
Performative metamorphosis, the third type, refers to surface -level
transformations in which brands adopt the symbolic markers of a prevailing Zeitgeist
without substantively modifying their institutional commitments or operational
practices. Vredenburg et  al. (2020) formally define this pattern as inauthentic brand
activism: a condition arising when activist marketing messaging is detached from a
brand’s purpose, values, and corporate conduct — a practice they term “woke
washing.” Authenticity, in their fr amework, requires the alignment of four mutually
reinforcing characteristics: the brand must be purpose - and values -driven; it must
address a controversial, contested, or polarising sociopolitical issue; it must adopt a

discernible stance on that issue; and it must contribute to the cause through both
messaging and tangible corporate practice (Vredenburg et al., 2020). When this
alignment is absent — when brands adopt activist messaging as a response to market
pressures without the supporting infrastructure of practice and values — the resulting
performative gesture risks not only brand equity damage but the broader
delegitimisation of authentic brand activism itself. From Holt’s (2004) perspective, such
gestures produce only cosmetic overlay on existing myth structures; because populist
worlds confer authenticity through perceived ideological stability, performative shifts
expose the dissonance between representation and reality, triggering consumer
scepticism. The distinc tions among these three metamorphic types are analytically
significant along three axes — timing, intentionality, and depth of integration into Holt’s
identity myth framework — and it is through this lens that the case studies examined
in the following section will be evaluated.
It is important to distinguish brand metamorphosis, as theorised here, from
adjacent constructs in the branding literature. Corporate rebranding models classify
change by its visible magnitude — Muzellec and Lambkin (2006), for instance, contrast
evolutionary rebranding (gradual, barely perceptible adjustments) with revolutionary
rebranding (wholesale repositioning, typically marked by a change of name). Such
frameworks are concerned primarily with the identity signifiers themselves. The
present typology di ffers in its organising principle: it classifies transformation not by
magnitude but by its temporal and motivational relation to the prevailing Zeitgeist —
whether repositioning follows a cultural rupture (reactive), anticipates it (proactive), or
merely simulates alignment with it (performative). It likewise departs from
transformative branding (Spry et al., 2021), which treats the brand as a macro -level
agent seeking to reshape the dominant social paradigm; metamorphosis, by contrast,
is a firm -level ada ptive response through which an identity myth is recalibrated to
shifting cultural conditions, whether or not the brand aspires to change those
conditions. What the typology contributes is thus a Zeitgeist -relative account of why
and when identity myths ar e reconfigured, complementing rather than duplicating
existing magnitude- and agency-based schemes.

Methodology
Research Design and Case Selection
This study adopts a qualitative, theory -illustrating case study design grounded in
documentary analysis. The primary theoretical contribution lies in the typology of brand
metamorphosis developed in Section 2; empirical cases serve as illustrative exemplars
that make the typology’s categories analytically visible. Following Eisenhardt (1989)
and Yin (2014), cases are selected and analysed for their capacity to illuminate the
mechanisms of reactive, proactive, and performative metamorphosis through
analytical rather than statistical generalisation. The study adopts an interpretivist
framework: brand texts are read as socially situated artefacts whose meanings are
constructed in relation to the prevailing Zeitgeist (Krause, 2019; Castells, 2009).
Case selection follows a purposive strategy ensuring representation of all three
metamorphosis types. Cases are selected on two criteria: (i) identifiable, documented
shifts in brand identity corresponding to changes in socio -cultural climate; and (ii)
sufficient documentary evidence to reconstruct the metamorphosis trajectory within
Holt’s (2004) cultural branding framework. The Dove Real Beauty Campaign (2004 –
2024) serves as the primary case for proactive metamorphosis.
The primary MAXQDA document set — detailed in Table 1 (Section 3.2) — is
supplemented by secondary interpretive sources comprising academic literature,
industry analyses, and corporate reports (principally Unilever, 2024). The triangulation
logic recommended by Yin (2014) is observed throughout: convergence of evidence
from distinct source types reduces single -source bias and strengthens the plausibility
of interpretive claims.

are explicitly foregrounded, on the grounds that the limits of a typological category are

Findings: Dove Real Beauty Campaign (2004–2024)
The Dove Real Beauty Campaign is selected as the primary illustrative case for
proactive metamorphosis within the typology proposed in this study. Spanning two
decades, it represents the most sustained and analytically rich example of a brand
continuously reconfiguring its identity myth in anticipation of — rather than in reaction
to — successive Zeitgeist shifts. Unlike reactive cases driven by crisis or performative
cases driven by market opportunism, Dove’s trajectory exemplifies what this study
terms identity-stable instrumental metamorphosis: the brand’s core value proposition
— authenticity and self-esteem — remained constant across twenty years, while the
medium through which that proposition was expressed, and the cultural threat to which
it responded, evolved with each new Zeitgeist wave. The case is examined through
documentary analysis combined with MAXQDA qualitative content analysis of official
campaign texts across four phases.

In 2004, Dove launched the Campaign for Real Beauty in response to a foundational

a semiotic rupture: it disrupted the sign system through which beauty advertising had
naturalised a narrow and commercially produced ideal.

Typology Classification and Critical Paradoxes
Within the typology proposed in Section 2, Dove’s trajectory exemplifies proactive
metamorphosis — specifically the threat-responsive variant. Dove identifies emergent
cultural threats to its founding value proposition and reconfigures the delivery
mechanism of its identity myth before threats reach full cultural saturation. This pattern
is consistent across all four phases: the brand does not wait for industrial beauty
standards, digital filter culture, social media comparison anxiety, or AI -generated
imagery to become dominant public concerns before responding; it positions its
response at the moment of emerging cultural salience, claiming cultural thought -
leadership rather than merely cultural relevance. This is consistent with Holt and
Cameron’s (2010) a ccount of cultural strategy: the brand positions itself as a
countercultural voice against successive waves of ideological distortion of female
beauty, each time reconfiguring not its values but its adversary.

The sustained alignment between Dove’s identity myth and its corporate conduct
appears to satisfy Vredenburg et al.’s (2020) criteria for authentic brand activism
across all four phases. The criteria require the alignment of purpose, values,
messaging, and prosocial corporate practice. On this framework, Phase 4 represents
the strongest case: the Real Beauty Pledge is a binding institutional commitment; the
Real Beauty Prompt Playbook is a concrete technical tool freely distributed to other
brands and creators; the campaign’s research is published in full; and the commercial
outcomes (€6 billion revenue, 100 million young people educated) indicate that, in this
case, activist positioning and financial performance were not in zero-sum tension. The
OI category’s growth from 7 total codes in Phase 1 to 24 in Phase 4 quantifies precisely
this deepening of the authenticity infrastructure over time: each phase adds a new
layer of evidential legitimation to the campaign’s activist claim.
Three structural paradoxes nuance this classification, supported by the MAXQDA
analysis and prior empirical research. First, the commodity feminism paradox: Dove’s
campaign appropriates feminist discourse in service of commercial objectives,
reconstructing self-esteem as achievable through brand consumption rather than
through collective political action (Johnston & Taylor, 2008). The campaign
simultaneously challenges the conditions that produce low self -esteem and deepens
consumers’ identification with a beauty product brand — a dynamic in which the
emancipatory narrative and the commercial one are structurally intertwined rather than
merely co -present. This tension surfaces in consumer communities, where
oppositional readings cite the Axe connection as co rporate insincerity (Feng et al.,
2019), and is registered in the MAXQDA data through the OI-4 (Paradox Signal) code’s
co-occurrence with DS-1 at the network’s centre.
Second, the Unilever paradox: the parent company simultaneously markets Axe,
whose advertising historically relies on hypersexualised female imagery directly
contradictory to Dove’s stated values. This is a failure of alignment at the corporate
rather than brand level (Vredenburg et al., 2020), and it cannot be resolved by the
brand’s own discursive strategies. Third, the naive integration paradox: Dove’s
depictions of “real” women remain sufficiently proximate to conventional beauty ideals
to achieve mains tream acceptance, suggesting the campaign challenges narrow
standards without fully dislodging them. In Holt’s (2004) terms, the populist world Dove

radical enough to destabilise it.
These paradoxes are not incidental limitations of an otherwise straightforward
case; they are constitutive features of the Dove brand’s cultural position and, as the
MAXQDA data reveals, they are embedded in the campaign’s own discursive
architecture. Yet they do not invalidate the typological classification. What the Dove
case ultimately demonstrates is that proactive metamorphosis, even in its most
commercially successful and institutionally committed form, operates as a practice of
productive tension rat her than resolution: the brand does not eliminate the
contradictions of the beauty industry Zeitgeist but navigates them in ways that support
both cultural resonance and commercial durability.  The 20-year trajectory from a 2%

the more fully documented instances of proac tive brand metamorphosis available in
the contemporary branding literature. Its analytical value lies precisely in the fact that
it is neither a simple success story nor a simple critique, but a case that holds both
simultaneously.
Comparative Illustration: Reactive and Performative Metamorphosis
While the Dove case anchors the proactive mode in depth, the typology posits two
further orientations whose contours are best clarified through brief contrastive
illustration. These sketches are not offered as equivalently evidenced case analyses
but as boundary markers that make the discriminant logic of the typology visible; each
is read through the same three axes — timing, intentionality, and depth of integration
into Holt’s (2004) identity-myth framework — used to classify the Dove trajectory.
Reactive metamorphosis. Uber’s post -2017 repositioning exemplifies the
reactive mode. As Howcroft and Leaver (2025) document, the #DeleteUber campaign,
workplace harassment allegations, and intensifying regulatory and legal challenges
rendered Uber’s founding narrative — the disruptive, consumer-championing “sharing
economy” brand — a moral-legitimacy liability almost overnight. In response, incoming
CEO Dara Khosrowshahi sought to reposition the company as a responsible corporate
citizen, publicly stressing that Uber needed to act with integrity and demonstrate that
it was a genuinely responsible company contributing to society, not merely a successful

product (cited in Howcroft & Leaver, 2025). In typological terms, the transformation is
crisis-driven rather than anticipatory: its timing is compressed and externally forced, its
intentionality defensive, and its repositioning initiated only after reputat ional damage
had crystallised. This contrasts sharply with Dove, which reconfigures its identity myth
before a threat reaches saturation rather than after it has become a liability.
Performative metamorphosis. Pepsi’s 2017 Kendall Jenner advertisement is
the paradigmatic performative case. The spot appropriated the visual grammar of
contemporary protest movements — echoing Black Lives Matter imagery — while
remaining detached from any corresponding brand purpose , values, or prosocial
corporate practice, and was withdrawn within days amid near -unanimous criticism
(Vredenburg et al., 2020). Measured against the four authenticity conditions, the
gesture fails on the decisive one: alignmen t between activist messaging and
substantive corporate conduct. Its timing is opportunistic, its intentionality reputational
rather than transformative, and its depth of integration effectively nil — a cosmetic
overlay on an unchanged myth structure. The case illustrates precisely the dissonance
that Dove’s sustained investment in pledge language and concrete corporate action
(the OI code family) is calibrated to avoid.

Discussion
The findings suggest that the typology’s three categories — reactive, proactive, and
performative — correspond to meaningfully distinct patterns of brand adaptation,
distinguishable both at the level of strategic intent and discursive structure. Dove’s
trajectory suggests that proactive metamorphosis may function not merely as a
branding posture but as a sustained discursive practice requiring continuous
recalibration of the threat object, communicative medium, and legitimation strategy
while preserving a stable identity myth at its core. The MAXQDA data offers indicative
evidence: DS-1 (Authenticity) registers the highest frequency across all phases while
ZT, MR, and OI categories register systematic transformation with each Zeitgeist shift.
The MAXQDA findings are consistent with existing research on audience
reception. Feng et al. (2019) found that 71.04% of YouTube commenters engaged
affirmatively with the Real Beauty Sketches campaign, while approximately 20%
engaged critically — invoking commodity feminism and corporate hypocrisy arguments
that align directly with the OI -4 (Paradox Signal) code’s co -occurrence pattern in the
present analysis. This correspondence between the brand’s discursive self-positioning
and documented audience recept ion provides corroborating evidence for the
typological classification.
The typology’s broader implications extend beyond the Dove case. In an era
characterised by what Castells (2009) describes as the network society’s compression
of communicative temporality, brands face Zeitgeist shifts that operate on timescales
previously unimaginable in branding strategy. A single viral moment can render an
existing brand myth obsolete; a single cultural controversy can transform a
performative gesture into a reputational crisis. The analysis suggests that the capacity
to detect, interpret, and respond to emerging cultural configurations before they reach
mass saturation is not merely a tactical advantage but a structural feature of brand
longevity within digital media ecologies. The Dove AI pledge and Prompt Playbook

represent an early -stage institutional response to this challenge; their long -term
efficacy in shaping the generative image ecology remains to be assessed.
Conclusion
This study set out to theorise how brands reconfigure their identity narratives across
successive cultural climates, and to render that process analytically tractable. Its
principal contribution is conceptual: a Zeitgeist -relative typology of brand
metamorphosis — reactive, proactive, and performative — distinguished along the
axes of timing, intentionality, and depth of myth –practice integration. The Dove Real
Beauty Campaign served as the primary illustrative case for the proactive mode, with
the reactive and performative modes clarified through brief comparative illustration; the
MAXQDA analysis lends indicative empirical support by showing a stable authenticity
core alongside systematic transformation of threat, medium, and legitimation across
four phases. Taken together, the typology offers a vocabulary for describing not merely
that brands change, but how their change relates to the cultural moment it answers to.
The study has several limitations. First, the case selection is deliberately singular:
the Dove case was chosen for its analytical richness and evidential depth rather than
as a representative sample of brand metamorphosis patterns. The reactive and
performative categories of the typology remain to be examined through cases that
instantiate those modes with equivalent empirical depth. Second, the analysis is limited
to official brand texts; incorporating third-party media coverage or audience reception
data across all four phases would strengthen the cross-phase comparison.
Future research could extend this framework in several productive directions.
Cross-industry comparative analysis of brand metamorphosis patterns would allow the
typology to be stress -tested against cases where institutional, regulatory, or
competitive con straints limit adaptive capacity. Finally, the interaction between
generative AI and brand identity construction represents a frontier that branding theory
has only recently begun to address. An emerging body of work examines how
generative systems reshape consumer behaviour and the production of brand meaning
(Hermann & Puntoni, 2024) and finds that AI-generated content can diminish perceived
brand authenticity (Brüns & Meißner, 2024) — precisely the tension Dove’s Phase 4
response engages. Systematic theo rising nonetheless remains at an early stage: as
AI tools increasingly mediate the production, distribution, and reception of brand

symbolism, the mechanisms through which identity myths are constructed and
contested will require fundamental theoretical revision. The Dove case, in committing
to shape generative AI’s representational standards rather than merely avoiding its
worst outputs, offers a provisional model — productive tension rather than resolution
— for how brands might navigate this emergent terrain.
