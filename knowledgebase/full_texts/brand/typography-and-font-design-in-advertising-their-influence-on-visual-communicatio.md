The growing body of evidence demonstrates that typography should be understood as a form of
visual language. Consumers do not encounter advertising text as neutral information. They
interpret its shape, weight, spacing, orientation, case, and overall composition alongside the literal
meaning of the words. Typography therefore contributes to the construction of meaning and can
influence the relationship between consumers and brands. The issue is particularly relevant in
contemporary digital advertising. Brands increasingly communicate through small screens and
rapidly changing visual environments in which users may encounter an advertisement for only a
few seconds. Under such conditions, effective typography must attract attention while
maintaining legibility, hierarchy, brand consistency, and emotional relevance.
This study therefore examines the influence of typography and font design on three closely related
areas: visual communication, consumer perception, and brand identity. Particular attention is given
to recent empirical research in order to identify areas of agreement , disagreement, and
development within existing scholarship.

Statement of the Problem
Despite the importance of typography in advertising, font selection is sometimes treated as a
secondary aesthetic decision rather than a strategic communication decision. Advertisements may
contain excessive numbers of typefaces, inadequate contrast, inappropriate font sizes, poor
spacing, or typefaces that conflict with the personality of the brand. Such problems can weaken
communication. A visually attractive typeface m ay fail if it is difficult to read. Similarly, a highly
legible font may be inappropriate if its personality conflicts with the advertised product.
Consequently, successful typography requires a balance between aesthetic expression,
readability, psychologi cal meaning, brand positioning, and contextual appropriateness. Another
problem is that some discussions of “font psychology” present typeface meanings as universal
rules. Recent research, however, suggests a more complex picture. Typography interacts with
product category, brand personality, message purpose, consumer characteristics, and other visual
elements. For instance, handwritten typography may communicate connectedness in charitable
advertising, while machine-like typography may be more appropriate in another context.
There is therefore a need for a contemporary synthesis that moves beyond the simple claim that
“serif means traditional” or “sans -serif means modern.” This study addresses that need by
reviewing recent research and comparing findings ac ross different advertising and branding
contexts.

Aim and Objectives
The main aim of this study is to examine the influence of typography and font design on visual
communication, consumer perception, and brand identity in advertising.
The specific objectives are to:
• examine the role of typography in advertising and visual communication;
• analyses how font characteristics influence consumer perception;
• examine the relationship between typography and advertising effectiveness;

Gestalt Theory
Gestalt principles explain how viewers organize visual elements into meaningful wholes. Principles
such as proximity, similarity, continuity, figu re-ground relationships, and visual hierarchy are
directly relevant to typography. In advertising, appropriate grouping and hierarchy enable
consumers to understand the relationship between headlines, images, body text, prices, and calls
to action.

AIDA Model
The AIDA model Attention, Interest, Desire, and Action provides a useful framework for
understanding advertising persuasion.
Typography can contribute to each stage:
Attention: Bold, large, or distinctive type attracts initial attention.
Interest: Clear and readable typography encourages continued engagement.
Desire: Typography that communicates appropriate emotional and brand associations can
strengthen product appeal.
Action: Clearly presented calls to action can encourage behavioural responses.
However, recent research suggests that the process is not strictly linear. Typographic effects can
depend on the product, brand, audience, and communication context.

Semiotic Theory
Semiotic theory explains how signs communicate meanings beyond their literal content.
Typography operates as a visual sign because typeface characteristics can suggest qualities such
as tradition, modernity, friendliness, authority, nostalgia, or technological sophistication. Recent
studies on vintage typography, letter case, and s erif/sans-serif differences provide empirical

Typography and Font Design
Typography refers to the organization and visual treatment of written lang uage. It includes
typeface selection, font size, weight, spacing, alignment, line length, hierarchy, contrast, and
composition. Font design concerns the visual construction and characteristics of letterforms and
their systematic use across different contex ts. Typography therefore involves much more than
selecting a font from a computer menu. It determines how information is organized and how visual
emphasis is established. In advertising, typography can distinguish a headline from supporting
copy, emphasize  a price or promotional offer, identify a brand name, and direct the audience
toward a desired action. Contemporary typography also includes responsive and digital
applications. Advertisements must operate across different screen sizes and environments,
requiring designers to consider legibility, scaling, contrast, and adaptability.

Typography as Visual Language
Typography functions as a visual language because letterforms carry both linguistic and visual
meaning. Consumers interpret not only what a word says but also how it appears. This principle is
supported by recent empirical research. Kulczynski and Hook (20 24), for example, examined
vintage typography and found that typographic style can influence consumers' associations with
nostalgia and perceptions related to product safety. Their work demonstrates that typography can
activate culturally and emotionally m eaningful associations beyond the literal information
conveyed by words. This finding is significant because it demonstrates that typography
participates in meaning -making. A typeface can establish a historical atmosphere, communicate
modernity, create familiarity, or suggest authenticity. Thus, typography can become a persuasive
component of advertising rather than simply a container for language.

Typography and Visual Hierarchy
Visual hierarchy refers to the organization of design elements according to t heir relative
importance. Typography is one of the primary tools used to create hierarchy. Large and bold
headlines generally attract attention before smaller body text. Changes in font weight, size,
spacing, alignment, and colour can establish relationshi ps among different levels of information.
Effective hierarchy enables viewers to identify the central message rapidly. In digital advertising,
this function is especially important because consumers frequently scan rather than read
advertisements in detail . Typography must therefore facilitate rapid visual processing while
retaining sufficient information for consumers who choose to engage more deeply.

relates more specifically to the clarity of individual characters and letterforms. Effective advertising

Typography and Consumer Perception
Consumer perception involves the processes through which consumers select, organi ze, and
interpret information. Typography contributes to this process through visual associations,
emotional responses, expectations, and perceived brand characteristics. Recent research suggests
that consumers respond to typographic features in systematic  but context -dependent ways.
Zhang et al. (2025) provide particularly useful evidence. Their five -study investigation found that
serif and sans-serif typefaces generated different perceptions of brand characteristics. Serif logos
increased perceptions of a ctivity, while sans -serif logos increased perceptions of potency.
Importantly, the researchers also found that design freedom and brand value moderated these
relationships.
This finding challenges simplistic font -psychology classifications. Rather than cla iming that serif
fonts are universally “better” or that sans -serif fonts are universally “modern,” the research
indicates that the effectiveness of a typeface depends on the desired brand meaning. Similarly,
research by Kulczynski and Hook (2024) demonstra tes that vintage typography can influence
associations involving nostalgia and product safety. This indicates that consumers may interpret
typography through cultural memory and learned associations.
Typography can therefore influence:
• perceived product quality;
• brand personality;
• credibility;
• emotional response;
• attention;
• memory;
• brand attitude;
• purchase intention; and
• willingness to engage.

However, these effects should not be interpreted as automatic. The relationship between
typography and consumer response is moderated by context.

Typography and Advertising Effectiveness
Advertising effectiveness refers to the degree to which an advertisement achieves its
communication and marketing objectives. These objectives may include attention, recall,
engagement, attitude formation, purchase intention, website visits, or other behavioural
responses. Kim et al. (2021) demonstrated that typefaces can influence psychological perceptions
and advertising responses, while also showing that typeface effects may inter act with the
perceived power of a communicator in an advertisement. This finding supports a congruence
perspective in which typography should be evaluated in relation to other elements of the
advertisement rather than in isolation.
Mead, Richerson, and Li (2020) similarly demonstrated that dynamic right -slanted fonts can
increase behavioural intentions in promotional retail advertising when used with promotional
offers. Their studies showed effects on retailer visit intentions, purchase intentions, and email click-
through behaviour. The researchers proposed that the slanted typography created mental imagery
associated with movement and limited promotional time.
The importance of this finding is that typography can communicate temporal and behavioural cues.
A dynamic typographic treatment may be appropriate for a limited -time promotion but
inappropriate for a formal corporate report. Thus, typographic effectiveness depends partly on
whether the visual characteristics of the font corresp ond with the communication objective.
Research on computational advertising also demonstrates that advertising text itself should be
matched to the stage of consumer decision -making. Recent research found that more subjective
and personalised advertising t ext could increase click -through rates, while more objective and
brand-consistent text could improve conversion performance. Although this study focuses on
textual style rather than typeface alone, it reinforces the broader argument that communication
form should correspond with the intended consumer response.

Typography and Brand Identity
Brand identity represents the visual and conceptual characteristics through which an organization
presents itself to consumers. Typography can become a central component  of this identity
because it is repeated across logos, advertising, packaging, websites, social media, signage, and
other communication channels. Recent research provides empirical evidence that typographic
design affects brand-related outcomes. A 2022 study examining sound symbolism and typography
found that congruence between the auditory image of a brand name and the visual image
communicated by its typography increased brand appeal, perceived quality, and memory. This
finding extends earlier theories of  brand identity by showing that typography can interact with
other sensory and symbolic cues. A successful brand identity is therefore not simply a collection of
attractive visual elements; it is a coordinated system in which typography, language, imagery,
colour, sound, and other signals reinforce one another. Letter case can also become a brand
identity variable. Research published in the Journal of Retailing  found that uppercase brand
presentation increased perceptions of brand premiumness, particularly for products with high
social visibility.

Research Design
This study adopted a qualitative narrative literature review design . The approach was sele cted
because the purpose of the study was to synthesize and critically compare existing research on
typography, advertising, consumer perception, and brand identity rather than to collect primary
data from participants.

Literature Search and Selection
Relevant academic literature was identified through searches of scholarly databases and publisher
platforms, including ScienceDirect, Taylor & Francis Online, Wiley Online Library, and SAGE
Journals. Search terms included combinations of typography and advertising, typeface and
consumer perception , font and brand identity , typography and brand attitude , typeface
advertising effectiveness , and font consumer behaviour . Priority was given to peer -reviewed
research published between 2020 and 2025 , with parti cular emphasis on empirical studies
examining the psychological and behavioural consequences of typographic choices. Earlier
foundational literature was retained where necessary to explain established concepts and
theoretical frameworks. Sources were selec ted when they directly addressed at least one of the
study's central themes: visual communication, consumer perception, advertising effectiveness, or
brand identity.

Data Analysis
The selected literature was analyzed thematically. Findings were organized into four principal
themes:
i. typography as visual communication;
ii. typography and consumer perception;
iii. typography and advertising effectiveness; and
iv. typography and brand identity.

The studies were then compared according to their research objectives, methodo logies,
typographic variables, consumer outcomes, and contextual conditions. The review does not claim
to be a systematic meta -analysis. Instead, it provides a critical synthesis of recent scholarship and
identifies areas of convergence, divergence, and research opportunity.

Discussion
The reviewed literature strongly supports the central argument that typography influences
advertising communication and consumer response. However, comparison of recent studies
reveals that the influence is more complex than  traditional descriptions of “font psychology”
suggest.

Agreement among Existing Studies
Several recent studies agree that consumers respond to typography beyond the literal meaning of
words.
Kim et al. (2021) found that typefaces generate psychological associations relevant to advertising
effectiveness.
Kulczynski and Hook (2024) demonstrated that vintage typography can activate associations
involving nostalgia and product safety.
Chu (2023) found that handwritten typefaces can increase con sumer connectedness in charitable
advertising, particularly among consumers with weaker existing attachment to the brand.
Together, these studies demonstrate that typography can function as an emotional and symbolic
cue rather than simply a mechanism for displaying language.

Differences in Research Findings
Despite this general agreement, the studies differ in the specific psychological mechanisms they
identify.
Chu (2023) emphasizes connectedness. The handwritten appearance of the message can simulate
interpersonal communication and encourage consumers to feel closer to the sender.
Kulczynski and Hook (2024), in contrast, emphasize semantic and cultural associations ,
demonstrating that vintage typography can activate nostalgic meanings and affect perceptions of
product safety.
Zhang et al. (2025) focus on brand trait perceptions, finding different effects for serif and sans-serif
typefaces on activity and potency.
These differences are not necessarily contradictory. Rather, they suggest that typography
operates through multiple psychological pathways.

Typography Is Context-Dependent
One of the most important conclusions from the recent literature is that no typeface is universally
effective.
Mead et al. (2020) found that right -slanted fonts were particularl y effective in promotional
advertising when a limited-time offer was present.
This means that the same typeface that successfully communicates urgency in a retail promotion
might be inappropriate for a bank, university, hospital, or luxury brand.
Similarly, Zhang et al. (2025) found that serif and sans-serif effects were moderated by brand value
and design freedom.
This extends earlier typographic theories by demonstrating that designers should not select fonts
solely according to generalised personality ca tegories. Instead, the typeface should be evaluated
against the brand's positioning and communication objective.

Typography and Consumer Trust
Typography can also contribute to perceived credibility. However, recent evidence suggests that

communicate warmth and personal connection in a charitable campaign, whereas a highly formal
typeface may communicate competence in a financial or professional context. The effectiveness
of either approach depends on the ty pe of relationship the organization seeks to establish with
consumers. The 2024 study of typeface use in brand -failure apologies provides a particularly
interesting example. It found that handwritten and machine -written typefaces could produce
different forgiveness effects depending on whether consumers perceived the failure as moral or
competence-related. This finding significantly expands the discussion of typography because it
demonstrates that typeface effects can depend not only on brand personality bu t also on the
nature of a specific communication problem.

Implications for Nigerian Advertising
The implications are particularly relevant to Nigerian advertising and graphic design practice.
Nigeria has a highly diverse linguistic and cultural environment, while businesses increasingly
communicate through social media, mobile platforms, print advertising, signage, and digital
marketing. Recent Nigerian research on typeface preferences has demonstrated the importance
of reading platform and familiarity when evaluating typography.
This suggests that Nigerian advertisers should consider:
• language and cultural context;
• screen versus print environments;
• audience familiarity with typefaces;
• readability;
• brand personality;
• local cultural associations;
• accessibility; and
• consistency across communication platforms.

The widespread use of mobile phones also makes responsive typography particularly important. A
typographic layout designed for a large printed poster may not communicate effectively when
reduced to a smartphone screen.

Balancing Aesthetics and Readability
Designers must balance visual originality with functional communication. Highly decorative fonts
may attract attention but reduce readability.

Typeface Overuse
Using too many fonts can produce visual confusion and weaken brand recognition. A controlled
typographic system is generally more effective than excessive variation.

Cultural Interpretation
Typography can carry cultural meanings that differ among audiences. Designers working acro ss
Nigerian ethnic and linguistic contexts should therefore consider local interpretation rather than
relying exclusively on assumptions developed in Western design environments.

Digital Adaptation
Typography must remain effective across different screen sizes and resolutions. This requires
responsive layouts and careful consideration of font size, weight, spacing, and contrast.

Accessibility
Designers should ensure that advertisements remain readable for people with different visual
abilities. Sufficient contrast, appropriate sizing, and clear letterforms are important aspects of
inclusive advertising.

Variable Typography
Variable fonts allow multiple stylistic dimensions to be incorporated within a single font system.
This provides greater flexibility for digital advertising and responsive design.

Artificial Intelligence
AI-assisted design systems can recommend typeface combinations, generate typographic layouts,
and adapt designs for different platforms. However, human designers remain essential for cultural
interpretation, strategic decision-making, and creative judgment.

Motion Typography
Animated text is increasingly used in social media, video advertising, and digital displays. Motion
can add emphasis and communicate ideas such as speed, urgency, or transformation.

Personalized Typography
Digital advertising makes it increasingly possible to adapt messages to specific audiences. Future
advertising systems may use consumer data to modify typographic presentation according to
audience preferences, device characteristics, or communication objectives.

Multisensory Branding
Recent research connecting typography with sound symbolism demonstrates the potential for

Conclusion
Typography and font design are strategic components of advertising that influence visual
communication, consumer perception, advertising effectiveness, and brand identity. The literature
reviewed in this study demonstrates that typefaces communicate meaning beyond the literal
content of words and can affect attention, emotion, brand associations, memory, engagement,
and behavioural intentions. The most important conclusion emerging from the comparison of
recent research is that typography does not have a universal psychological effect. Instead, its
influence is contextual. Serif and sans -serif typefaces may produce different brand pe rceptions;
handwritten typography may strengthen feelings of connectedness in some situations; vintage
typography may activate nostalgia; and dynamic typography may increase responses to
promotional messages.
Recent research therefore moves the field away from simplistic assumptions about font
personality and toward a more nuanced understanding based on congruence, context, audience,
brand positioning, and communication objectives. Typography should consequently be considered
at the strategic planning stage  of an advertising campaign rather than added after other design
decisions have been made. Effective typographic design requires consideration of readability,
emotional meaning, brand personality, cultural context, platform, accessibility, and the
relationship between typography and other advertising elements. For Nigerian advertisers and
graphic designers, this approach is particularly valuable because advertising communication
operates across diverse cultural, linguistic, and technological environments. A  successful
typographic strategy should therefore combine international design knowledge with sensitivity to
local audiences and communication practices.

Recommendations
Advertising agencies and graphic designers should:
1. treat typography as a strategic co mmunication element rather than merely a decorative
component;
2. select typefaces according to brand positioning, audience, product category, and
communication objectives;
3. test typography with representative audiences before large-scale campaigns;
4. priorities readability and accessibility across print and digital platforms;
5. avoid excessive use of unrelated typefaces within a single advertisement;
6. establish clear typographic guidelines for consistent brand communication;
7. consider cultural and linguist ic meanings when developing advertising typography for
Nigerian audiences;

Limitations of the Study
This study is based on a narrative literature review rather than primary con sumer research.
Consequently, it does not generate new statistical evidence from Nigerian consumers. In addition,
the reviewed studies were conducted in different cultural, technological, and advertising
environments, meaning that their findings should not  automatically be generalized to every
audience. Future research should therefore employ experimental and survey methods with
Nigerian consumers to examine how typography affects brand trust, advertising recall, purchase
intention, and brand preference across different product categories.
