Abstract. Under the Mobile Internet, short-video algorithms are now the primary modes of
change for culture creation. The two effects of algorithmic recommendations on the logic of
content creation and the public's aesthetic demands are examined here. Based on a review of
the literature and case studies, and using the algorithmic transformation of Douyin and
Kuaishou in 2016-2017 as examples, this paper finds that algorithms employ metrics such
as completion rates to create a "traffic pool competition" mechanism. Although it will
increase the distribution efficiency in the short run, there will be a loss of content diversity
and depth over time. Algorithms limit the scope of users with "cocoons", lower aesthetic
standards, and thus reduce interest. Algorithms enhance the segmentation of aesthetics and
social groups. Therefore, this paper proposes a four-dimensional optimization system:
rearrangement of algorithm parameters, construction of private-traffic brand moats,
strengthening platform-based aesthetic guidance, and promotion of public media literacy.
This paper will investigate the cultural changes brought about by the algorithmic era and
offer some suggestions for platform management, creator development, etc.
Keywords: Short-video Platforms, Algorithmic Governance, Cultural Production, Aesthetic
Stratification, Informational Cocoons
1. Introduction
1.1. Research background
With the spread of mobile Internet, short-video platforms have become a popular source of
information and entertainment for the public. The basic algorithm mechanism for extending users'
stay, predicting their interests based on behaviour data, and constantly recommending similar
content has altered the entire process of content production and consumption [1]. Algorithms are
now the "invisible gatekeepers" to content creation, and most editorial and distribution decisions are
made automatically based on quantitative indicators such as watch time and interaction rate;
therefore, the visibility of content has been declining [2]. Algorithms have constructed an
information environment for public aesthetics that is "what is seen is what is desired". Platforms
collect, spread and strengthen users' aesthetic demands to build a cycle of similar content

1.2. Literature review
Existing literature studies algorithms for recommendation in the intersection of content creation and
aesthetic experience. According to the theory of "cultural cocoon", a preference-based algorithm
may only present users with similar materials, thus restricting their aesthetic horizons and
information sources [5, 6]. Algorithms are the core expressions of platform power; they have
replaced cultural judgments with commercial goals and caused creators to alter their production
plans according to the performance of parameters [7]. According to the theory of cultural
reproduction, algorithmic distribution promotes aesthetic stratification by exposing lower-class users
to sensory content and directing knowledge-oriented materials to the elite [8]. Concurrently, real-
time feedback mechanisms and emotional labour pressures drive creators toward high-intensity,
homogenized outputs, exacerbating aesthetic fatigue and creative burnout [9, 10].
Most existing research has studied the impact of algorithms' recommendations on only one
aspect. Some have focused on "the narrowing of audience cognition caused by information
cocoons", and others have concentrated on "the distortion of creator behaviour driven by traffic-
based logic".On the other hand, relatively few scholars have investigated content creation and public
aesthetics in the same analytical system or systematically studied how short-video platform
algorithms simultaneously change the creative logic on the supply side and the aesthetic structure of
demand on the demand side. Previous research has either attributed the changes in aesthetic taste to
a "decline in users' tastes" without considering the active influence of algorithms on this
phenomenon or blamed content homogeneity on "a lack of originality among creators" and failed to
examine the system of rewards and punishments implemented by algorithms.
1.3. Research framework
At present, most studies focus on the cognitive effects of information cocoons and disregard traffic-
driven creator distortions. Few studies have explored the simultaneous reshaping of supply-side
creative logic and demand-side aesthetic structures by algorithms through content creation and
public aesthetics. Existing research has often attributed the aesthetic change to declining user tastes
or reduced creator originality and failed to explore algorithmic penalties on diversity and the closed-
loop process of parameter settings, creator adaptation, content homogenization, and audience
conditioning. Personalized recommendations, although expanding content diversity compared with
random distribution, may also deepen the self-isolation of narrow-interest users; thus, scholars
should avoid technological determinism.

2. Case context and industry evolution
Since 2016, the recommendation algorithms of short-video platforms have been changed to avoid
the previous system that relied on manual editing and follower distribution. Platforms are collecting
behaviour data on how long users watch, like, comment on, share, and scroll past content, etc., to
train models that can predict which other content other users will be interested in. The goal of
creators has shifted from content creation to "giving the audience what they want", and now
clickbait titles and "golden hooks" in the first three seconds, as well as placing emotional climaxes
at the beginning, are standard templates. At the level of consumers, a new consumption behaviour
has emerged: to like what is displayed in the feed and to be shown more of the same; aesthetic
preferences are continually validated and reinforced by algorithms. Current situation: short-video
platforms have completed the transition from "social distribution" to "algorithmic distribution", with
the content ecosystem characterized by "high adaptability but high homogenization". Public
aesthetics are undergoing a structural shift amid sensory stimulation and the narrowing of
information exposure, while creators as a group are increasingly caught in the survival dilemma of
"without data, there is no traffic; with data-driven content, creative value is diminished" (Table 1).

3. Dual impact analysis: mechanisms and challenges
3.1. Positive externalities: efficiency and democratization
3.1.1. Democratization of content distribution channels
In the time of traditional media, editors, producers and platform operators controlled the distribution
of content, and it was difficult for grassroots creators to gain attention. Short-video algorithms
distribute the recommendation traffic according to the performance of the content, not the creator's
reputation. Videos by users without a large number of followers can access the higher-tier traffic
pool if they meet the requirements for completion and interaction. Change in the old model of
information dissemination, algorithms now help groups such as rural artisans, grassroots musicians
and ordinary teachers spread their works to millions online. The democratization of content
distribution has also created a platform for folk aesthetics and marginalized forms of culture that
have received little attention from mainstream media, thus adding to the diversity of popular culture.

3.1.2. Optimization of information retrieval and consumption efficiency
Algorithms predict users' interests based on their past behaviour, skip irrelevant content, and directly
recommend videos that users are likely to enjoy to reduce the cost of information search
significantly. Given that modern people have limited leisure time and are in urgent need of
entertainment, providing high-quality content upon launching the application will be more
convenient for them to enjoy. Precise matching can also reach niche interests, such as woodworking,
seal carving and dialect preservation; therefore, they will not be ignored due to a lack of a large
mass market for long-tail content. The resulting improvement in consumption efficiency has helped
short-video platforms gain attention in the attention economy and become a popular form of daily
entertainment for the public.
3.1.3. Data-driven creative iteration and feedback loops
After uploading a video, the creator can see how many people have watched it, when they stopped
watching, and which parts were liked by many viewers to learn what people enjoyed and did not
enjoy. Data feedback has partially replaced the older models of preview screenings and focus groups
to alter the speed, content and form of expression.A fast "publish-observe-adjust" cycle reduces the
cost of creative trial and error, and high-quality creators can find their own style and target audience
at a lower cost. Such iterative mechanisms are necessary but relatively unknown accelerators to the
overall improvement of quality in short-video content.
3.2. Negative externalities: homogenization and aesthetic degradation
3.2.1. Algorithmic incentives and the erosion of creative depth
In order to boost the completion rate, algorithms will frequently add some conflict or suspense in the
first few seconds of a video to attract people's attention. When the creators learned that the formula
of "clickbait title + climax in the first three seconds + emotional provocation" could draw attention
from the public, all sectors in China began to apply this model. Knowledge-based content is now
fast-food style, with a "punchline every three seconds"; narrative content has dropped exposition and
gone straight to conflict; and in-depth analysis has been condensed to "conclusion first + omitted
reasoning".The reward and punishment mechanism of the algorithm will systematically penalize
slow-burning content, long-narrative development, and information-dense videos. Such content
generally has a lower completion rate and will not be further promoted even if it has a relatively
high number of likes. Algorithms have solved the problem of selection by providing entertainment
options, and as a result, the ecosystem of content has shifted from depth and complexity to sensory
stimulation and emotional impact.
3.2.2. Sensory overload and the shift towards superficial aesthetics
Under algorithmic recommendation, audiences are continuously exposed to content with high levels
of sensory stimulation, including rapid editing, strong colour contrasts, sudden sound effects, and
exaggerated facial expressions. The brain's response threshold to such stimulation is continually
raised. When audiences return to content that requires gradual narrative development, subtle
expression, deliberate omission, and sustained reflection, they may experience a physiological sense
of "not being able to sit through it". Aesthetic patience can gradually shift from being able to watch
an entire documentary to swiping away whenever a video fails to provide a striking moment within

fifteen seconds. More subtly, algorithms do not promote content that requires audiences to think
before appreciating it, because such content often generates lower completion rates. As a result,
aesthetic capacities may gradually deteriorate in an environment where audiences are rarely
challenged. The fundamental problem is that algorithms train aesthetic preferences toward "instant
gratification", while the public's capacity to engage with complex forms of art is systematically
weakened by the technological mechanism.
3.2.3. Algorithmic cocooning and the entrenchment of aesthetic stratification
Algorithms divide the content into categories according to the behaviour of the users. Users who are
interested in knowledge-based content will continue to receive more knowledge-based materials,
and those who like sensory stimulation will be repeatedly shown low-information-density content.
Therefore, the two groups are in different information environments, and their aesthetic horizons
gradually differ. Less privileged users may be exposed to an information system full of suggestive,
sensational or prank-oriented content, and as a result, their sense of aesthetics will gradually be
altered. The privileged group may be in a state of "information isolation" and therefore fail to gain
knowledge or feel empathy about popular culture. Algorithmic recommendation, therefore, creates a
perpetual "technological structure" of aesthetic stratification rather than a "social phenomenon".
People from different social groups consume different content, and as a result, they may be unable to
experience the content enjoyed by other groups; thus, a shared public space for aesthetic dialogue is
lost.
3.2.4. Datafication anxiety and the erosion of creative autonomy
Many creators now have to focus on improving their performance indicators daily due to the
instability of view counts and changes in engagement rates; as a result, they often lose their original
style, copy successful content models, and stop creating content they are passionate about in favour
of topics more likely to perform well according to the data. Thus, the internal reasons for creative
motivation (such as the desire for self-expression and art) will be replaced by external motivations
(such as traffic data and platform rewards/penalties). Data anxiety can also lead to too many posts.
Algorithms are favouring active accounts more and more; therefore, some creators have had to post
several times a day to remain popular, and as a result, quality has been reduced in favour of quantity.
Algorithms have reduced creators to "data workers" by limiting their creative freedom in response to
the demands of the platform at the bottom.
4. Strategic interventions and governance mechanisms
4.1. Algorithmic rebalancing through multi-dimensional metrics
Too much emphasis on completion and participation has resulted in a decline of aesthetic depth for
some works. Therefore, adjust the weight given to different factors by an algorithm. Add an
"Aesthetic Diversity Index" and a "Content Depth Coefficient" to the existing traffic pool
competition mechanism. In addition to the above real-time feedback indicators, algorithms should
also consider other attributes of quality, such as how many times users have rewatched the material,
the proportion of content fully consumed, and how widely different modules of the site are explored.
At the same time, the platform can build a "high-quality content pool" and provide an initial traffic
incentive for high-cognitive-cost content, such as documentaries, intangible cultural heritage and in-

4.2. Reclaiming creator agency via brand equity and community building
Algorithmic Fluctuations Restrict Creator Autonomy; Therefore, Emphasis Will Shift from Traffic
Maximization to Long-Term Brand Equity Creation. First of all, creators should develop their own
style and professional niche, participate in all sorts of fields such as original editing techniques,
narrative styles and knowledge-based content creation, build a "brand", and avoid merely imitating
the template of viral videos. Second, creators can build their own private traffic pools, such as fan
communities and membership programs, to reduce their dependence on algorithmic
recommendations in the public traffic pool. Through frequent interaction and co-creation in these
private communities, "casual followers" can be turned into "core supporters", and highly engaged
social connections can help mitigate the risks of changes in algorithmic traffic. This trust-based
connection can help reduce the problem of sacrificing the depth of content for algorithm appeal, and
allow creators to return to their original intention of artistic creation.
4.3. Platform accountability and curatorial governance
The platform needs to move beyond profit maximization and develop a model of curation and
governance for culture. Platforms can adopt an "editor's pick" model to create an "aesthetic
discovery" section that is not based on algorithms, and have professional editorial teams manually
select content with artistic value and cultural substance to break the closed loop of cocoons. In
addition, the recommendation formula can be improved by increasing the weight of "social
connections" moderately, and a model used by Kuaishou in 2017 can be referenced to give more
weight to content liked or viewed by friends and followed users. Leverage the trust among strong
social networks to enhance the quality of the content. Platforms need to build an open-source
explanation mechanism for algorithms, inform creators of the limits and forbidden behaviours, and
reduce the creation of "speculative content" due to information asymmetry.
4.4. Cultivating audience reflexivity through media literacy
Prolonged exposure to algorithms may reduce people's sense of the aesthetic and thus requires
extensive media literacy education. Educational institutions and media organizations should broadly
promote media literacy education to help users, especially adolescents, learn about the limitations of
algorithms for recommendations. Such education should cover how to identify "cocoons" created by
algorithms, understand the commercial logic behind the data indicators, and develop the patience to
enjoy content with delayed gratification. By raising the public's "algorithmic literacy", users can
become active "content curators" instead of passive "content consumers", learn to obtain all sorts of
information consciously, and deliberately avoid uniform recommendations. Therefore, on the
demand side, algorithms and creators will be motivated to produce high-quality, in-depth content,
and a virtuous cycle of the cultural ecosystem will be established.

5. Conclusion
5.1. Key findings
This paper presents two forms of alteration by short-video algorithms to content creation and public
aesthetic concepts. The original algorithmic division of access to the matching function had certain
advantages, but these were not permanent. As a result, the logic became rigid and, to some extent,
reduced the freedom of expression for artists. Through the "traffic pool competition" mechanism, the
algorithmic reward of sensory stimulation may lack richness due to excessive emphasis on this
aspect. At the same time, "cocoons" restrict the public's horizons, and "aesthetic stratification"
creates separate "symbolic universes" for different social groups, leading to cultural fragmentation.
Data anxiety is also reducing the freedom of creation, and creators are becoming "data workers". In
light of the above problems, more algorithm parameters are needed, a creator brand system, a sense
of social responsibility, and improved public media literacy.
5.2. Research significance
Theoretically, this study develops a closed-loop framework of "algorithmic parameters–creator
strategies–content ecosystem–audience aesthetics" to address the shortcomings of previous research
in isolating the supply and demand sides, and offers a new perspective on the logic of cultural
production in the digital age. Research has shown that it can promote the development of a fair-play
environment for platforms and improve public trust, help creators build private-traffic brand moats
to reduce the risk of algorithmic traffic fluctuation, shine a light on the "algorithmic black box", and
provide a basis for regulatory policy formulation and media literacy education. Thus, it will be
possible to promote the development of a "value-oriented, culture-respecting, and user-friendly"
system for the industry.
5.3. Limitations and future study
This study is restricted by the use of secondary data and the absence of primary evidence from
questionnaires and interviews. Only the leading platforms of Douyin and Kuaishou have been
studied to date, and their generalization and comparison have not been further examined. Future
research can quantify the differences in aesthetic perception among different age groups, conduct
cross-cultural comparative studies, and further explore the dissemination mechanisms of AI-
generated content (AIGC) under algorithmic control and its changing impact on aesthetic
preferences.
