The “River Publishers Series in Management Sciences and Engineering” aims at publishing high qual -
ity books on management sciences and engineering. The Series provides discussion and facilitates
exchange of information on principles, strategies, models, techniques, methodologies and applications
of management sciences and engineering in the field of industry, commerce, government and services.
The Series aims to communicate the latest developments and thinking on the management and engi-
neering subject world-wide. Thus, the main aim of this book series is to serve as a useful reference and
to provide a channel of communication to disseminate knowledge between academics, researchers,
managers and engineers and other professionals.
The Series seeks to link management sciences and engineering disciplines to helping organiza-
tions in private and public sectors addressing technological, business and societal challenges in a con -
text of complexity, uncertainty and change. The Series stimulates the development of new approaches
to management and engineering in a human, organizational and societal context, from a socio-tech -
nical systems perspective, and in aspects as design, innovation, planning, coordination, communica-
tion, engagement and decision-making. Examples of societal challenges requiring new approaches to
management and engineering include the sustainable, secure and responsible development of energy,
transport, water, infrastructural and informational resources, infrastructures and services. The Series is
highlighting cultural and geographical diversity in studies oriented to transformation in organizations
and work environments. It emphasizes the role of human resource management, the role of organi -
zational decision-making and collaboration, empowerment of stakeholders, and the management of
innovation and engineering activities in a context of organizational change. This way it reflects the
diversity of human, organizational and societal conditions.
Books published in the series include research monographs, edited volumes, handbooks and text
books. The books provide professionals, researchers, educators, and advanced students in the field
with an invaluable insight into the latest research and developments.

It begins, as it often does, with a question. Not the kind that seeks quick

What is the university for?
Not what it does. Not how it is ranked. Not how many students it grad-
uates or patents it files. But why does it exist – why we, as societies, continue
to build them, fight over them, walk through their corridors, and pin so many
of our hopes, fears, and futures upon them.
This book is, in many ways, an attempt to dwell on that question.
Not to answer it definitively – because no single answer could hold – but to
trace its contours. To listen for its echoes across time, geography, and ide -
ology. To explore how our understanding of higher education systems and
university management has evolved, fractured, and recomposed itself in
response to shifting landscapes of meaning, power, and necessity.
It is not a neutral project. Nor is it merely a scholarly one. The writ -

 traveler – I offer not just an overview of its chapters, but an invitation to
pause. To consider what it means to be part of a world where learning, in all
its messy, contested glory, still matters.
Universities have long been cast as the sanctuaries of civilization.
Libraries of thought. Temples of reason. But behind the romantic myths lies
a truth that is harder, sharper, more honest: they are also sites of struggle. Of
bureaucracy and brilliance. Of access and exclusion. Of ambition and inertia.
They are both noble and flawed. Both resilient and brittle. And it is precisely
this contradiction that gives them their strange, stubborn vitality.
The chapters in this book emerged from a growing discomfort with how
often the discourse surrounding higher education remains stuck – either lost
in nostalgia for a golden age that never quite was, or dazzled by futuristic
promises that rarely materialize. Between these two poles, we lose something
essential: a reckoning with the present.

Abstract
Higher education has never existed in a vacuum. It has always reflected the
broader social, economic, and political forces of its time. This chapter offers
a comprehensive yet nuanced exploration of how higher education systems
have evolved from ancient centers of learning to complex institutions embed-
ded in today’s global knowledge society. Tracing key historical moments –
from the scholasticism of medieval Europe to the entrepreneurial spirit of the
contemporary university – the chapter lays bare the shifting purposes and
expectations of higher education across eras. More than a chronological nar-
rative, the chapter investigates how universities have continuously reinvented
themselves in response to external pressures and internal debates. It explores
how the rise of mass education, neoliberal reforms, digital disruption, and
global competition have redefined what it means to be a university in the
21st century. At the same time, it does not shy away from the tensions –
between tradition and innovation, access and elitism, autonomy and account-
ability – that continue to shape the sector. This chapter seeks not only to
inform but also to provoke reflection. What should a university be for in our
time? How should systems of higher education adapt to serve both society
and the individual in a rapidly changing world? These are not questions with
simple answers, but they are the questions that this chapter invites readers to
consider.
Introduction
What is the purpose of a university? This deceptively simple question has ani-
mated philosophers, policymakers, educators, and students for centuries – and
yet, it remains unresolved ( DeVitis, 2023). Higher education systems, often
seen as monoliths, are in fact fluid entities, constantly shaped and reshaped

The Roots of Higher Learning: From Antiquity
to the Medieval University
There is a certain romance in tracing the origins of human inquiry. Not just
because of what it reveals about knowledge, but because it reveals some -
thing far more intimate – our restless yearning to understand, to connect,
to transcend the confines of immediate life. Before we built institutions, we
gathered under trees, in stone courtyards, beside rivers. We asked questions.
We argued. We speculated. And, in doing so, we began something that would
outlive us all: higher learning. It is tempting to think of higher education as
a modern invention – sleek buildings, digital libraries, layered bureaucra -
cies. But the impulse behind it? Ancient. Raw. Spiritual, even. The very idea
that learning could be pursued for its own sake – not to till the soil or cure
disease or calculate taxes, but to grasp the nature of truth, to shape the soul –
that is not a recent invention. That has been with us since the first flickers of
civilization.
In ancient Egypt, long before the term “university” existed, centers of
specialized learning emerged within temple complexes (Zakaria, 2024). These
were not open schools but carefully guarded hubs of knowledge, reserved for
scribes, priests, and elites. Hieroglyphics were not taught widely; knowledge
was power, and power was hoarded. Yet even there, you see early signs of a
curriculum – arithmetic, astronomy, healing practices, religious texts. The
desire to encode the world, to make sense of the celestial rhythms and social
order, was already embedded in these proto-institutions. Then there was
Mesopotamia. In the clay-strewn cradles of Sumer and Babylon, students
hunched over tablets, memorizing cuneiform under the stern gaze of their
teachers. Mathematics, law, trade calculations – these were the subjects of the
day. But learning was not just about utility; it was about order. In a chaotic
world of floods and famine and fickle gods, education was a way to impose
structure, to create coherence.
Fast forward to ancient India, where Takshashila and Nalanda emerged
not as isolated monasteries but as thriving intellectual crossroads ( Singh,
2022). Here, philosophical schools did not just coexist – they debated, they
clashed, they enriched each other. Buddhist monks studied alongside laypeo-
ple; texts on logic sat beside treatises on medicine and grammar. Knowledge

was not compartmentalized. It was fluid, layered, deeply intertwined with
the metaphysical and the ethical. Nalanda, especially, deserves attention.
Imagine, for a moment, a library so vast and precious that invading armies
specifically targeted it for destruction. The burning of Nalanda was not just a
military act – it was a cultural rupture, a symbolic annihilation of intellectual
legacy. In ancient China, Confucian academies laid the groundwork for an
educational tradition that emphasized harmony, moral cultivation, and socie-
tal duty (Yang, 2022). The imperial examination system, while rigid by mod-
ern standards, was revolutionary in its idea: that merit, not birth alone, should
determine access to power. Of course, reality was more complex – access to
the preparation needed for such exams remained unequally distributed. But
the ideal, the aspiration, matters. It marked a shift: education as a ladder, not
just a fortress.
Then there is the Greek world – often romanticized, sometimes deserv-
edly so. Athens, in its golden age, became the intellectual heartbeat of the
Mediterranean (Spawforth, 2023). Socrates, with his relentless questioning,
wandered the agora not to deliver lectures but to dismantle assumptions. He
did not offer knowledge; he demanded awareness. Plato took this further,
imagining the Academy – a place where the dialectic, the search for abstract
forms, became the highest good (Nicolae, 2022). Aristotle, pragmatic and
systematic, turned knowledge into a taxonomy, a structure, a discipline. His
Lyceum was not just a place of ideas; it was a proto-laboratory, a space where
observation and logic began to reshape the world. Greek thought did not remain
in Greece. It migrated, merged, and metamorphosed. As Hellenistic influence
spread through conquest and commerce, centers like Alexandria emerged.
The great Library of Alexandria – part myth, part  memory –  symbolized a
dream of universal knowledge. Scrolls from across the known world, gath -
ered in one place. It is said that scholars who docked in Alexandria had their
books copied – one copy for them, one for the library. Whether apocryphal or
not, the image resonates: knowledge not as possession, but as shared legacy.
Yet the world is not linear, and progress is rarely smooth. Empires rise
and fall, and with them, so do their intellectual infrastructures. As the Western
Roman Empire crumbled, formal learning in Europe retreated into monas-
tic silence (Reeves, 2021). But even in that silence, seeds were kept alive.
Monasteries became quiet custodians – copying manuscripts, preserving
fragments of classical thought, interpreting them through theological lenses.
It is here, in the shadows of fading empires and rising faiths, that the idea of
the medieval university begins to stir. But first, we must look eastward again.
In the Islamic world, particularly between the 8th and 13th centuries,
a flourishing of scholarship occurred that reshaped the intellectual landscape

Meanwhile, in the Latin West, something was slowly reawakening. The
12th century witnessed what some call a “renaissance” – a rediscovery of lost
classical texts, many reintroduced through Arabic translations. This intellec-
tual revival coincided with socio-political transformations: the rise of urban
centers, the increasing power of the Church, and a growing need for trained
clerics and administrators. And thus, the first medieval universities began to
emerge – not as grand designs but as organic responses to practical needs.
Bologna, Paris, Oxford – names that would come to define the European
educational canon. But these were not modern institutions. They were, at
first, loose guilds. Communities of teachers and students are bound by mutual
agreement. A university, in this early form, was less a place and more a legal
concept – a collective with rights, protections, and internal governance
(Almada & Petit, 2025). Paris leaned heavily into theology and philosophy;
it was the seat of scholasticism, where faith and reason wrestled under the
vaulted ceilings of the cathedral schools. Bologna, by contrast, became the
heart of legal studies, drawing students from across Europe to study Roman
law. Oxford, somewhat in between, began as an echo of Paris but soon devel-
oped its own peculiar rhythm.
What distinguished these medieval universities was not just their cur -
riculum – it was the fact that they could grant degrees, recognized across
Christendom. The degree was not just a credential; it was a license, a pub -
lic declaration of mastery. And with it came status, mobility, and authority.
Yet these institutions were not havens of egalitarian enlightenment. Access
remained fiercely limited – by gender, by class, by geography. Lectures were
often rote; dissent could be dangerous. Original research, in the modern
sense, was rare. But even so, something radical was happening. A culture of

inquiry, of structured disputation, of textual engagement, began to solidify.
The medieval university made knowledge portable. It created networks. It
forged traditions. Still, it is important not to romanticize too much. These
early universities were enmeshed in ecclesiastical power and political patron-
age. They reproduced hierarchies even as they challenged others. But within
those contradictions lay their dynamism.
And so, as we look back on these roots of higher learning, what emerges
is not a tidy lineage but a mosaic. A tapestry woven from Babylonian scribes
and Confucian classics, from Alexandrian scrolls and monastic candlelight,
from Muslim polymaths and scholastic theologians ( Miller, 2022). There is
no single origin, no pure genesis. The university, like all enduring human
inventions, is an inheritance cobbled together across cultures, epochs, and
contradictions. What unites them all – what endures across time and space –
is the belief that learning matters. That there is something sacred, or at least
necessary, in the act of seeking understanding. That ideas can shape lives, not
just policies. That inquiry, however flawed, is worth preserving. And maybe,
in a world increasingly dominated by speed, commodification, and crisis,
remembering these roots is not just academic nostalgia. Maybe it is a quiet
act of resistance – a way of anchoring ourselves to the deeper currents of
human striving. Maybe, just maybe, it reminds us why we started learning in
the first place.
The Rise of the Modern University: Enlightenment,
Industrialization, and Reform
Universities, as we knew them in the medieval world, were cloistered, slow,
and largely insular – places of careful theological reasoning, disputation over
classical texts, and a curriculum rooted in tradition ( Proctor, 2021). But tra-
dition has its limits. It crumbles under the weight of too much change or not
enough oxygen. By the dawn of the Enlightenment, something was shifting –
something deep. Not just in science, or politics, or art, but in the very idea of
what knowledge is, and who it is for. The rise of the modern university was
not a single moment. It was a long, uneven awakening – sometimes sudden,
sometimes halting, always contested. It emerged from a collision: between
the rational spirit of the enlightenment and the grinding gears of industrial -
ization, between the promise of liberty and the hunger for order. Universities
were no longer to be the dusty homes of clerical debate – they were being
recast, almost forcibly, as engines of modernity. But let us slow down. Before
we arrive at laboratories and lecture halls lit by gaslight, we have to pass
through the enlightenment – a period messy with contradiction. For all its

emphasis on reason, this era was anything but sterile. It burned with ques -
tions: What is the nature of truth? Can knowledge be divorced from supersti-
tion? Should education serve the crown, the church, or the people?
In 18th-century Europe, thinkers began to rebel – quietly at first, then
loudly. Universities, still tethered to church doctrine and aristocratic privi -
lege, found themselves out of step with the new philosophies (Sacchini, 2022).
Immanuel Kant’s 1798 treatise, The Conflict of the Faculties, posed a bold
proposition: that the university must include a faculty of free thinkers – philos-
ophers – who answer not to power, but to reason itself (Tanzella-Nitti, 2024). It
sounds abstract, maybe even quaint, until you realize what is being said. That
the university, if it is to matter, must be autonomous. It must stand apart. This
spirit took root most clearly in Germany. Prussia, emerging from the embers of
feudal fragmentation, saw in education a new kind of power. But the Prussian
state was not merely interested in discipline or obedience. Something more
interesting was happening. Wilhelm von Humboldt, a linguist and statesman,
imagined a university that fused teaching and research, theory and practice.
He envisioned scholars not just transmitting knowledge but producing it. The
University of Berlin, founded in 1810, became the template for what we now
call the modern research university (Amaral & Magalhães, 2023).
Humboldt’s model was radical in its simplicity. Let the scholar be free.
Let knowledge grow not from dictates, but from inquiry. Unite students and
professors in a shared search, not a static hierarchy. No standardized cur -
riculum. No final authority on truth. Just a community of minds, groping
toward insight. It was not a perfect system – it privileged elite, mostly male
scholars, and left much of society out – but it set something in motion. A new
idea of what a university could be. As this German model began to spread, it
collided – sometimes elegantly, sometimes chaotically – with the demands of
the Industrial Age. The 19th century did not just bring factories and railroads;
it brought a total reshaping of life. Time became clocked. Work became spe-
cialized. Knowledge, too, had to be refitted for this new world.
The Industrial Revolution did not merely need workers. It needed engi-
neers, chemists, statisticians – minds trained not in scripture, but in appli-
cation (Bühler et al, 2022). And so, across Europe and the United States,
universities began to bifurcate. On one side: the old, humanistic core.
Philosophy, history, literature, the slow study of what it means to be human.
On the other: the rising disciplines of science, medicine, and technology.
Laboratories popped up beside libraries. Practical knowledge gained pres -
tige. The very hierarchy of disciplines shifted, and with it, the architecture
of learning. The Morrill Land-Grant Acts in the United States offer a sharp
illustration. Passed in 1862, during the bloodied midst of the Civil War, this

a tool of national development. The people’s university. And yet, even this
democratization was layered with complexity. These land-grant institutions
were inclusive in mission but often exclusive in practice – segregated by race,
sometimes by gender, and shaped by the needs of industry more than the
ideals of liberation. Still, they expanded the university’s reach. They changed
its tone. And they laid the foundation for something deeper: the belief that a
nation’s future could – and should – be built through education.

Postwar Expansion and the Massification
of Higher Education
When the dust settled after World War II, the world was not the same. Cities
had been flattened, economies shattered, populations displaced. But in the
wreckage, something curious stirred – a strange and defiant optimism. There
was this almost audacious belief that, from catastrophe, renewal was not only
possible, but it was necessary. That society, if it was to have any future at all,
needed to rebuild not just its infrastructure or its borders, but its very sense of
self. And at the heart of that vision stood education. Higher education, once
the guarded enclave of elites, was about to explode – not with noise, but with
numbers (Musto, 2022). A quiet revolution, really, masked in paperwork and
policy, but one that would upend centuries of exclusivity. The gates of the
university, long bolted shut to most, began to creak open. And behind them
waited millions. This was no accident. Governments knew what they were
doing – at least, they thought they did. The postwar consensus, particularly
in Western democracies, rested on a few unspoken promises: that the horrors
of fascism would not return, that prosperity could be broadly shared, and
that education – especially higher education – would be the engine of both
democracy and development. These were not just rhetorical flourishes. They
became institutional mandates.
In the United States, the GI Bill – officially the Servicemen’s
Readjustment Act of 1944 – was a game changer (Lanneau, 2022). It offered
returning veterans access to college education on a scale previously unimag-
inable. Universities swelled. Lecture halls overflowed. Professors who had
taught a few dozen students before the war now faced hundreds. The bill
was, in many ways, the first truly mass higher education policy in modern
history. And while it had its exclusions – Black veterans in the South were
often denied access – it redefined what higher education could be: not just a
privilege, but a right of service, a currency of citizenship. Across the Atlantic,
Britain was undergoing its own reckoning. The Beveridge Report, and later
the Robbins Report of 1963, put education at the center of the welfare state.
Robbins made it explicit: higher education should be available to all “who
were qualified for it by ability and attainment.” The logic was simple, even
beautiful (Race, 2021). Intelligence is not the property of a class. Talent is not
inherited through wealth. And so the university must open its doors – wider,

broader, deeper. But massification was not just a Western affair. In the newly
independent nations of Africa, Asia, and Latin America, higher education
took on an almost sacred role. It was not just about learning – it was about
nation-building. Universities were expected to produce engineers, teach-
ers, civil servants – the human scaffolding of postcolonial sovereignty.
The University of Ghana, the University of Delhi, Makerere University in
Uganda, the University of São Paulo – these were not mere institutions. They
were symbols. Proof that independence was not just political, but intellectual.
Still, it is worth asking: what do we mean by massification? Numbers,
yes – but also something more unsettling. A shift in identity. A change in tex-
ture. Universities, once intimate and introspective, have now become sprawl-
ing systems. Enrollment doubled, tripled, then doubled again. The student
body diversified – not just in background, but in intent. Not everyone came
for the love of learning. Many came for a job. And that was both new and
necessary. The expansion required more than just buildings and blackboards.
It demanded new ways of thinking. The old university model – rooted in face-
to-face mentorship, leisurely contemplation, and a slow digestion of canon -
ical texts – simply could not scale ( Cathelain, 2024). New pedagogies were
needed. So were new administrative logics. Modular courses. Standardized
assessments. Degree audits. The bureaucracy of education ballooned.
And with it came a strange irony. As access expanded, so did alienation.
Massification brought millions to campus – but it also made those campuses
feel impersonal. You could now get a degree without ever really being known.
Students became numbers; faculty became managers of metrics. The intimacy
of learning, in many places, gave way to the machinery of throughput. And
yet – this was progress ( Steinmann et al, 2023 ). However messy, however
bureaucratic, it was undeniable. More women entered university than ever
before. Working-class students sat beside the children of doctors and diplo -
mats. Racial and ethnic minorities, long excluded or marginalized, began to
carve out spaces – though not without resistance, not without struggle.
The 1960s and 70s were not just decades of enrollment – they were
decades of eruption. The university became a stage, and sometimes a bat -
tlefield (Dressler et al, 2025 ). Students demanded more than access – they
demanded transformation. In Paris, the streets of May ’68 shook with chants
against authoritarianism, capitalism, and academic paternalism. In the United
States, protests against the Vietnam War bled into critiques of the university’s
complicity with military and corporate power (Schultz, 2024). Civil rights
activists challenged the curriculum, hiring, and representation. What is the
point of education, they asked, if it simply replicates the hierarchies of the
world it claims to critique? This was the paradox of massification: it prom-
ised equality, but often reproduced inequality. Getting into university was no

a public expense. They wanted outcomes. Returns on investment. Efficiency.

of massification – access, growth, democratization – had been overtaken by
another logic: management. The age of the “enterprise university” was born.

Globalization and the Shifting Purpose of the University
There was a time – not that long ago – when the university felt like a self-con-
tained world. A closed loop. You entered through stone gates, passed under
oak canopies, and, for a few precious years, existed in a different kind of
time. Ideas mattered there. Questions had weight. The pace slowed down just
enough for reflection to slip in. The boundaries were clear, almost sacred. And
yet, even then, even in that illusion of separateness, the world was pressing
in. Now, those boundaries are porous – if they even exist at all. Globalization
did not just arrive at the gates of the university; it swept through every corri-
dor, every syllabus, every hiring decision, every funding model (Crea, 2023).
It redefined not only how universities operate, but why they exist. And for all
the celebration of connectivity and cosmopolitanism, the truth is: the transi -
tion has been messy. Uneven. Complicated. Sometimes thrilling. Often dis -
orienting. Globalization – the word itself is slippery. Overused, underdefined.
But in the context of higher education, it means a few distinct things. It is the

Globalization and the Shifting Purpose of the University 15
circulation of students, faculty, and ideas across borders at unprecedented
speed and scale. It is the internationalization of research agendas, the rise
of transnational education hubs, and the proliferation of English as the aca-
demic lingua franca. It is partnerships, rankings, cross-border campuses, and
satellite institutions in Dubai, Singapore, or Kigali. It is the dream of global
citizenship… and the bureaucracy of visa denials.
Let us not pretend this started recently. The movement of scholars has
always been part of the academic story. Medieval students traveled between
Paris, Bologna, and Oxford. Muslim scholars crossed empires to debate in
Baghdad or Cordoba. Nalanda drew pilgrims from all over Asia. But what is
different now is velocity. And volume. And intent. Universities are no longer
just in the world – they are of it, entangled in its supply chains, its poli -
tics, its markets (Malik, 2021). They compete on global stages. They recruit
internationally, publish internationally, and fundraise internationally. Their
reputations are no longer local ornaments; they are global currencies. And
this, for better or worse, has shifted their purpose. There was a time when a
university’s mission was largely provincial: serve the nation, train the elite,
advance the local. Now? Mission statements are riddled with the language of
impact, innovation, and global reach. The metrics of success have changed.
No longer is it enough to educate well or produce meaningful scholarship.
Now one must rank. Must scale. Must disrupt. The pressure is palpable.
Global rankings – those seductive, numerical hierarchies – have played
a powerful role in this transformation (Kayyali, 2023). Suddenly, institu-
tions are chasing visibility. Chasing citations. Chasing metrics that flatten the
beautiful complexity of academic life into a handful of digestible indicators.
Faculty are pushed to publish in “international” journals – read: English-
language, peer-reviewed, high-impact – often at the cost of local relevance.
Research agendas become skewed toward what is fundable, fashionable,
and quantifiable. What gets lost? The slow work. The contextual work. The
kind of inquiry that does not shout. And yet, it is not all cynical. There is a
real openness here too – a kind of intellectual permeability that is deeply
inspiring. A lecture delivered in Cape Town can be discussed in Toronto the
next morning. A student in Jakarta can take an online class from Yale. A

The Knowledge Economy and Market-oriented
Higher Education
Stopped being a sanctuary of ideas and quietly became a supplier of labor.
It did not happen overnight. There was no grand proclamation, no definitive
turning point. It crept in – through budget spreadsheets, job market reports,
policy white papers, accreditation guidelines, and slogans about “skills for
the future.” By the time most people noticed, the transformation had already
taken root. The university was no longer just a place to think – it was a place
to produce. Welcome to the knowledge economy.
It is a phrase so widely circulated now that it feels like wallpaper –
present, unexamined. But when you pause for a moment and consider what
it implies, the dissonance hits hard. Knowledge – once cultivated slowly,
painfully, intimately – is now currency. A commodity to be packaged, sold,
and exchanged. The university, once a custodian of intellectual and cultural
heritage, now functions increasingly as a cog in the machine of economic
growth. And the tension – between learning as exploration and learning as
transaction – has never been more palpable.
Let us start with the basics. The term “knowledge economy” emerged
in the latter half of the 20th century, as economies in the Global North began
shifting away from manufacturing toward services, technology, and informa-
tion (Unger, 2022). In this new paradigm, raw materials were no longer coal
or oil, but ideas, data, and intellectual property. Economic value, we were
told, would come not from physical labor but from human capital – from
what people know. And who better to train these knowledge workers than
the university? Suddenly, policymakers, economists, and education ministers
were all in agreement: higher education was no longer just a public good; it

The Knowledge Economy and Market-oriented Higher Education 19
was a strategic investment. Nations competed not on natural resources, but
on innovation. Graduates became the lifeblood of GDP. Research became the
front line of global competitiveness ( Hicham & Hamza, 2024). Universities
found themselves drafted into an economic war they did not entirely choose –
but could not afford to refuse. And so, the market entered the campus. Not
through the front door, but through the wiring in the walls. Business met -
aphors flooded academic discourse. Students were now “clients,” degrees
were “products,” and universities became “brands.” Course offerings were
shaped not by curiosity or civic need, but by employability stats and ROI
metrics. Humanities programs shrank; STEM fields boomed. Philosophy was
asked to justify itself in job placement reports (Zack, 2025). History was told
to get lean, get relevant, or get lost.
Of course, the logic was not entirely irrational. In a world of precar -
ious work, rising tuition, and widening inequality, students wanted – and
deserved – education that could open doors. But something subtle and deeply
human was lost in this shift. Learning became less about the formation of the
mind and more about the optimization of the résumé. The messy, beautiful
ambiguity of education – the idea that it might change you, rather than sim-
ply prepare you for something – was pushed aside. And here is the kicker:
the university internalized this logic. It did not just respond to the market. It
began to think like the market.
Faculty were increasingly evaluated not on the depth of their teaching or
the originality of their thought, but on quantifiable outputs ( Constantinou &
Wijnen-Meijer, 2022). How many articles? In which journals? Cited how
often? Teaching became performance, research became deliverables, and
innovation – once the slow, stubborn cousin of insight – was reduced to
buzzwords in grant applications. Even the physical architecture changed.
University buildings started looking like corporate headquarters. Glass. Steel.
Sleek interiors with open-plan workspaces. Entrepreneurship centers popped
up where philosophy departments used to be. Incubators replaced archives.
The library, once a sanctuary of silence, became a café with Wi-Fi. But the
story is not so simple, not so clean. Because despite all this, the university
resisted. Not loudly, not in every corner, but in flickers – in the classroom
where a professor dares to ask students what matters to them, not just what
they need to pass; in the seminar where theory is wrestled with, not for util-
ity, but for meaning; in the office hours that stretch longer than they should
because someone is struggling – not with a formula, but with life. These
quiet acts are not inefficiencies. They are the soul of the university, trying to
breathe in a room filled with spreadsheets. Still, the pressure to conform is
immense.

world. And in doing so, it participated in a cruel fiction – because the econ-

does this leave us?
We are living through a moment where the purpose of the university
feels deeply unsettled. The language of the knowledge economy has infil -
trated every corner – but it has not satisfied the longing at the heart of edu -
cation. Because despite all the job fairs and LinkedIn workshops, something
more elemental remains. The desire to understand. To question. To belong to
a community of inquiry. What is tragic is not that the university has changed.
Change is inevitable. Necessary, even. What is tragic is how little space is left
for dissent within that change. For ambiguity. For saying: I do not know what
this degree will get me, but I know what it gave me. And what it gives – at its
best – is not just a skillset, but a worldview. A way of noticing the world, of
attending to it with care, with nuance, with humility. It offers tools not just for
employment, but for life. This is not a nostalgic plea to return to some ivory
tower past. That past was never truly golden. It was exclusionary, patriarchal,
and often out of touch. But in the rush to modernize, to monetize, we risk
swinging the pendulum too far – toward a university that produces function
but not wisdom, credentials but not consciousness.

of Traditional Models
It used to be that a classroom had walls – chalkboards, desks, the scent of dust
and paper, and time ticking by in measured silences ( Done, 2022). Learning
was bounded. Spatial. Embodied. There was a ritual to it. The walk across
campus, the murmur before a lecture, the gentle clatter of pens in notebooks.
The university, for all its flaws, was a place. A rhythm. A container for ideas
and encounters. And then came the digital wave – not a gentle ripple, but a
tectonic shift. At first, it arrived as convenience: email instead of office hours,
PowerPoint instead of overhead transparencies. Nothing radical. Just tweaks.
Tools. Augmentations. But then, somewhere between the broadband revolu-
tion and the rise of smartphones, the university began to dematerialize. Now,
education floats in the cloud. Lectures are streamed, discussions happen in
threads, assignments are auto-graded, proctoring is done by surveillance soft-
ware, and a degree can be earned without ever stepping onto campus. What
used to be geography is now bandwidth. What used to be presence is now
connectivity. And the traditional university – rooted in land, in buildings, in
centuries of physical ritual – finds itself flickering between screens. This is
not just a change in format. It is a change in epistemology. A change in the
way we think about knowledge, about relationships, about what it means to
teach and to learn.
Digitalization is not merely about going online – it is about rewiring
the logic of the university itself. Take MOOCs, for instance (Bygstad et al,
2022). When Massive Open Online Courses burst onto the scene in the early

2010s, there was a flurry of breathless excitement. This was the future, we
were told. Elite universities would be accessible to all. Learning would be
democratized. Education would be scalable like never before. And some of
that was true. Suddenly, someone in Nairobi or Dhaka or a rural corner of
Alabama could watch a Harvard lecture on constitutional law. Could engage
in a forum with peers from six continents. Could access content previously
hidden behind privilege. But scale has its price.
The MOOC model, in its early form, often reduced learning to content
delivery. Watch a video. Take a quiz. Get a badge. Repeat. It was educa -
tion as consumption, not dialogue. The messy beauty of in-person learning –
the awkward question, the digression, the serendipitous insight – was lost
in translation. And completion rates? Abysmal. The promise of revolution
began to wobble. Still, the seeds were planted. And the pandemic would
later water them with force. When COVID-19 hit, universities had no choice
(Kayyali, 2020). Overnight, centuries of tradition were shoved into Zoom
boxes. Professors scrambled. Students scattered. Wi-Fi replaced lecture halls.
Engagement dropped. Exhaustion soared. But something else happened too.
The rigidity of “how things have always been done” cracked. And in that
crack, something like possibility emerged. What if learning could be asyn-
chronous? What if lectures did not have to be live? What if students could
pause, rewind, and review at their own pace? What if assessment could be
more personalized? What if access could be widened – not by lowering stan-
dards, but by lowering barriers?
Digital tools, used well, offered some of these answers. But they also
raised deeper questions. What happens to intellectual intimacy in a digital
world? Can you mentor someone over Slack? Can you spark lifelong curiosity
through a prerecorded module? What do we lose when we lose eye contact,
hallway conversations, the ineffable energy of a seminar where everyone’s
a little too passionate? And yet – it would be naïve to pretend digitalization
is the enemy. It has made things possible that were once unimaginable. It
has allowed students who work full-time, who are parents, who live in con -
flict zones, who are neurodiverse, to engage with education on their terms.
It has expanded the reach of learning. It has opened doors. But every door
opens onto something. And what it opens onto, increasingly, is the terrain of
disruption.
Innovation has become the mantra of our time. The buzzword of grant
applications, university mission statements, TED Talks, and startup pitch
decks (Norell, 2024). We are told we must disrupt or be disrupted. Higher
education is ripe for transformation. The current model is obsolete, ineffi -
cient, and exclusionary. And there is truth to that. But let us interrogate the

rhetoric for a moment. What does it mean to “disrupt” education? Who gets
to define the disruption? Who benefits? Who is left behind?
EdTech firms have rushed to answer those questions – with platforms,
with AI tutors, with predictive analytics, with learning management systems
that promise to personalize instruction and optimize outcomes ( Adeoye &
Otemuyiwa, 2024). Investors pour in. Entrepreneurs declare that they will
fix higher education the way Uber fixed taxis or Airbnb fixed hotels. But
education is not a commodity. Learning is not a ride-share. A classroom is
not an app. Something irreplaceable happens when people gather in pursuit
of understanding. Something that resists quantification, automation, and effi-
ciency. The best education is not seamless – it is disruptive in its own way. It
unsettles. It provokes. It lingers. Innovation, then, should not be about replac-
ing the human with the algorithm. It should be about deepening the human.
About designing tools that enhance – not erase – dialogue. About using tech-
nology to support reflection, not just accelerate progression. And there are
examples. Thoughtful uses of digital portfolios allow students to track their
intellectual growth over time. Platforms that support peer feedback across
disciplines and geographies. Virtual labs that simulate complex scientific
processes for students who do not have access to state-of-the-art equipment
(Kayyali, 2025 a). Language exchange apps that connect learners across the
globe. These are not just gadgets. They are pedagogical extensions. When
done well, they do not flatten education – they expand it. But the danger lies
in mistaking the tool for the teaching.

fail. They can score essays with unsettling accuracy (Ramesh & Sanampudi,

experience. Only humans can do that. And only when they are given time,
freedom, and care. Which brings us back to the university. In this new digital
landscape, what does it mean to be a university? Is it a content provider? A
certification platform? A data processor? Or is it still, somehow, a space for
transformation?
Because innovation, real innovation, is not just about what is possible.
It is about what is meaningful. Yes, we can digitize libraries. But how do
we preserve the serendipity of stumbling on a book you did not know you
needed? Yes, we can automate grading. But how do we ensure that feed -
back still feels like a conversation, not a verdict? Yes, we can record lectures.
But how do we create moments of collective presence, even across screens?
These are not nostalgic questions. They are human ones. The digital revolu -
tion has changed higher education – profoundly, irreversibly. But whether it

that are more than credentials factories? Can we center joy, curiosity, and jus-
tice? Can we prepare students not just for jobs, but for life – for lives that are
complicated, relational, uncertain? And can we do this across borders, with -
out replicating colonial hierarchies? It is not a blueprint. It is a question. And
maybe that is the point. The future of higher education is not fixed. It is emer-
gent. It is plural (Whaples, 2022). It is being written – right now – in policy
rooms and classrooms, in protests and prototypes, in quiet conversations and
loud rejections. No one owns it. No one controls it. But we all shape it. So here
is the invitation: Let us shape it with care. With imagination. With the knowl-
edge that the university – however battered, however bureaucratic – is still one
of the few places left where we can ask what it means to be human, and how
we might live together in a world that is burning and blooming all at once.
Conclusion
To reflect on the historical arc of higher education is to see more than the rise
and fall of institutions or the evolution of pedagogical models. It is to engage
in a deeper meditation on how societies understand knowledge, power,

Conclusion 29
identity, and progress. From the cloisters of early monastic schools to today’s
sprawling, digitized campuses, higher education has served many masters  –
church, state, market, and increasingly, global networks. Yet despite these
shifting allegiances, it has remained a vital force in shaping what we know,
how we think, and who gets to speak. This endurance, however, should not be
mistaken for immunity. Today’s universities stand at a crossroads, confronted
by crises of legitimacy, relevance, and sustainability. In an era characterized
by rapid technological advancement, ecological breakdown, economic pre -
carity, and political polarization, the role of the university is once again under
scrutiny. Is it a sanctuary for critical thought or a training ground for indus -
try? Should it reproduce the status quo or challenge it? Can it be both?
One of the great tensions in contemporary higher education is the bal -
ance between continuity and change. On one hand, the legacy of the past – its
institutional traditions, disciplinary hierarchies, and governance structures –
offers stability and identity. On the other hand, clinging too tightly to those
traditions risks alienating a new generation of learners who experience the
world differently and demand more from their education. The challenge, then,
is not simply to preserve or discard the past, but to critically engage with it,
drawing forward what remains valuable while boldly discarding what no lon-
ger serves. The global context further complicates this balancing act. Higher
education is increasingly transnational, and yet deeply local. Institutions are
expected to compete in international rankings while also serving regional
needs. They must be agile enough to adapt to technological shifts but rooted
enough to sustain communities of scholarship and practice. They are asked
to be entrepreneurial, yet inclusive; efficient, yet humane. These are not just
technical challenges – they are moral ones.
Indeed, perhaps the most profound insight that emerges from a histori-
cal and contemporary analysis of higher education systems is this: the future
of the university is not preordained. It will be the product of choices – made
by educators, students, governments, and society at large. These choices
will determine whether higher education remains a public good or becomes
an exclusive commodity; whether it fosters democratic engagement or
entrenches social divides; whether it remains tethered to narrow performance
metrics or reclaims its broader ethical mission. If we are to reimagine higher
education for a volatile, interconnected world, we must start by reclaiming
its purpose. Education is not just about skills or credentials – it is about culti-
vating critical consciousness, empathy, and the capacity to live meaningfully
with others in complex societies. These are not luxuries; they are necessities.
This chapter has traced the journey of higher education through time – not
to offer a definitive map, but to spark inquiry. The systems we inherit are not
fixed; they are open to reinvention. And that reinvention must be grounded
