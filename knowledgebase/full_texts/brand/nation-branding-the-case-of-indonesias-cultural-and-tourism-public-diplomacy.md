# Nation Branding: The Case of Indonesia’s Cultural and Tourism Public Diplomacy

Nation Branding: The Case of Indonesia's Cultural and Tourism Public
Diplomacy
Riska Luneto1, Khuzaimah2
rizka.luneto@uin-alauddin.ac.id

Abstract
This paper examines the implementation of Indonesia's tourism nation branding through the
“Wonderful Indonesia” campaign as an instrument of state diplomacy. As a soft -diplomacy
initiative, Wonderful Indonesia represents the diversity of Indonesia's culture, tourism, history,
and religious life. Using a tourism image as a tool of public diplomacy is not a new
phenomenon in diplomatic studies: where traditional diplomacy once relied primarily on
formal negotiation, modern diplomacy increasingly channels a state's cultural and social
resources toward cooperative advantage. The analysis finds that a favorable national image
can shape foreign perceptions of a country, yet converting that image into measurable tourism
growth remains difficult in practice. Tourism security, in particular, continues to constrain
how far a positive image translates into higher visitor numbers.
Keywords: Diplomacy, Tourism, Nation Branding, Cultural Diplomacy, Public Diplomacy

INTRODUCTION
Diplomacy sits at the center of interaction among nations. It captures several
characteristics of engagement between states and non-state actors through the use of soft power
– public diplomacy, information media, public relations, nation branding, self -presentation,
and foreign policy – directed at managing both short- and long-term national concerns (Gilboa,
2008). As diplomacy h as evolved, its modern form has come to treat culture and tourism as
instruments for building state strength and expanding economic influence, Indonesia included
(Sujai, 2016).  Cultural resources are especially significant for Indonesia, a country whose
territory spans a wide expanse and whose cultural and touristic diversity is distributed across
what the national geospatial agency now counts as 17,380 islands (Badan Informasi Geospasial
[BIG], 2024).

The World Economic Forum's Travel and Tourism Development Index identifies
infrastructure investment and well -packaged tourism branding as key drivers of a country's
tourism growth; Indonesia is considered competitively advantaged in the diversity and richness
of its local culture. At the same time, Indonesia faces persistent constraints in how it uses that
culture and its tourism assets. Chief among these is tourism security, unsettled since the wave
of violent incidents in the early 2000s and still a live concern today, alongside the
environmental pressure that tourism-related waste places on destination areas.
Beyond security, uneven infrastructure development is a continuing challenge. While
Indonesia has several well-equipped tourism hubs, many other areas of high tourism potential
– particularly in eastern Indonesia – remain difficult to reach, even though ease of access and
safety are basic requirements for prospective visitors.
Addressing this gap requires coordinated effort from both government and private
actors to restore the country's tourism  image, an effort that also sits within the wider
architecture of global tourism governance built around the World Tourism Organization
(UNWTO). Established in 1975 as a specialized United Nations agency – its statutes having
been adopted at the IUOTO's extraordinary assembly in Mexico City in 1970 – UNWTO exists
to advance tourism's contribution to economic development and employment, to support peace
and mutual understanding between nations, and to promote basic freedoms without regard to
sex, religion, l anguage, or race (Kementerian Luar Negeri Republik Indonesia, 2012; UN
Tourism, n.d.).
Treating culture and tourism as instruments of state power follows the trajectory Joseph
Nye traced after the end of the Cold War: as military strength alone ceased to d efine national
power, states increasingly drew on cultural resources and related soft-power approaches (Nye,
2004). Cultural instruments of diplomacy – cultural exhibitions, student exchange, and the
circulation of cultural products through television and the internet – have become
correspondingly more prominent (Mustaqim, 2018).
Tourism and culture play an important role in building both domestic and international
attention around a country's natural and cultural diversity, and are used as diplomatic
instruments through which Indonesia introduces its culture to construct its image abroad
(Simanjuntak, 2018). Tourism branding accordingly carries meaning, purpose, and function for
Indonesia's tourism sector.

This study examines how tourism branding has been implemented as part of Indonesia's
public diplomacy. In diplomatic theory, hard diplomacy and soft diplomacy are treated as the
principal variables of the concept, with branding representing one form of soft diplomacy.
Building on existing definitions of branding, this study examines the function, correlation, and
implementation of branding within Indonesia's public diplomacy.
Since 2011, Indonesia's Ministry of Tourism and Creative Economy has used the nation
brand “Wonderful Indonesia” t o project the country's image (Prawibowo & Purnamasari,
2018). The slogan was created to support tourism promotion and to construct a positive image
of Indonesia through a series of accompanying programs, intended to reintroduce the meaning
and philosophy behind Indonesia's identity as the country worked to address existing barriers
to tourism growth. Promoting Wonderful Indonesia across multiple media channels has been
treated as a key mechanism for restoring the country's tourism image amid ongoing securi ty
concerns (Idriasih, 2016).
ANALYSIS FRAMEWORK
Diplomacy: Public and Cultural Dimensions
Diplomacy occupies a central place in international political interaction, serving as a
means through which states pursue their national interests. It is not limited  to formal
negotiation; rather, it covers the full range of foreign -relations efforts aimed at achieving a
state's interests, and functions as a method for conveying messages and interests across
political, economic, trade, social, cultural, and defense do mains within the framework of
international relations, in pursuit of mutual understanding between two or more states (Idriasih,
2016).
As a form of power, diplomacy belongs to the category of soft power available to
international political actors. In Soft Power: The Means to Success in World Politics, Joseph
Nye (2004) defines soft power as the capacity to shape the choices available to others — the
ability to attract another party into willingly doing what one wants. Nye later added the concept
of smart power, describing a deliberate combination of hard and soft power that a state draws
on to achieve its goals (Gilboa, 2008). Korb and Boorstin (2005) take a different view, arguing
that hard and soft power are better understood as “integrated power,” since t he two operate as
complementary patterns rather than as mutually exclusive alternatives; integrated power, in

their formulation, involves developing new strategies that combine traditional approaches to
security, diplomacy, and foreign assistance.
Eytan Gilboa further characterizes the soft-power interactions that take place between
states and non -state actors as encompassing two -way communication, strategic public
diplomacy, media framing, information management, public relations, nation branding, self -
presentation, e-image, and foreign policy — all directed at managing problems across both
short- and long-term horizons (Gilboa, 2008).

The table above distinguishes hard and soft power along three dimensions: behavior,
resources, and government politics. Public diplomacy is classified as a government policy that
falls under soft power. Gilboa further distinguishes public diplomacy by time horizon. In the
short term, diplomacy focuses on seizing opportunities and minimizing errors, primarily
through the use of media as an instrument for shaping public opinion. I n the medium term,
public diplomacy emphasizes careful planning and policy implementation, pursued through
communication and cooperation between government and non -government actors to build
international public opinion. In the long term, public diplomacy takes the form of cooperation
among government, business, and non -governmental organizations to build public trust, with
cultural diplomacy and nation branding serving as its principal instruments (Gilboa, 2008).
Gilboa's categorization situates culture and nation branding as instruments of long-term
public diplomacy, one whose influence is meant to be sustained rather than confined to a single
period. Keith Dinnie (2008) adds that national identity plays a role in nation branding: a
country's national identity helps build and promote the shared goals of public diplomacy, while
culture in turn forms a core part of that identity and of national branding – underscoring the
connection between public diplomacy and nation branding.

Within this context, the touris m industry draws attention through a country's natural
wealth and cultural arts, placing it within the category of a state's cultural diplomacy. Cultural
diplomacy can be understood as a state's effort to advance its national interest through the
cultural dimension, whether at the micro level (education, science, sport, and the arts) or at the
macro level, in line with a country's distinguishing characteristics – including propaganda –
which, in conventional understanding, are not considered political, econ omic, or military
instruments (Wiratma, 2017).
Milton Cummings Jr. defines cultural diplomacy as the exchange of ideas, information,
art, and other aspects of culture between nations and their societies. Warsito and Kartikasari
(2007) similarly describe it as a state's effort to realize its national interest through the cultural
dimension, encompassing the use of ideology, technology, politics, economics, the military,
and the arts within the arena of international society.
Through the use of cultural eleme nts, government – as one actor in diplomacy – can
employ cultural instruments to pursue its national interest. Differences in social background do
not pose a serious obstacle to this approach, since culture communicates readily and is easily
understood; culture can also draw nations closer together. These positive qualities of culture
help open the path toward the objectives of cultural diplomacy, pursued to understand, inform,
and influence other nations, thereby increasing appreciation, building mutual un derstanding,
and improving a nation's image. Cultural diplomacy is more than performance: as a form of
soft power, it is the conduct of politics through cultural means, without recourse to coercion
(Nurlelawati, 2019). Cultural diplomacy is carried out thr ough communication intended to
disseminate information or shape public opinion, and it must be pursued through the public in
the service of national interest.
Nation Branding
Nation branding is not a new subject, but it has drawn increasing international attention
in recent years (Gilboa, 2008). Nation branding matters because it explains a country's image
to other actors; in another sense, a country's branding signals a paradigm shift away from power
and geopolitics and toward image and influence (Van Ham , 2002). A range of actors have
accordingly taken an interest in shaping such branding, aiming to construct a particular face or
image for foreign audiences (Gilboa, 2008).

Simon Anholt's Nation Brand Index combines six sectors: tourism, exports, culture and
heritage, investment and immigration, people, and governance. Public diplomacy and nation
branding are distinct concepts that nonetheless share similar functions and goals – both aim to
elevate a public image within a given arena and to manage governmen t policy on diplomacy,
image, and communication. Nation branding can be defined as a country's competitive and
appealing strategy, reinforced through the state's actions and communication across its various
interactions (Szondi, 2008).
As a marketing strat egy applied to socio-economic activity, nation branding has been
recognized since 1996, when Anholt proposed treating a country's economic, business, and
political relationships as a brand – a brand that may refer directly to a product's identity
(packaging, logo, communication), to the culture or actors behind that brand, or to the public
reputation of what a company or state produces (Szondi, 2008).
Nation branding is also understood as the process of shaping the image of a product or
country and the prom otional efforts that support it: it occurs when government and private
enterprise use their combined influence to change the image of what they produce (Szondi,
2008). As more countries have come to appreciate the pull of a strong global image on
international audiences, the practice of nation branding has spread quickly and been adopted
by an increasing range of actors, to their benefit (Fan, 2010).
A country's branding is ultimately oriented toward positioning itself within a global
system capable of affe cting its relative strengths and weaknesses; nation branding, in other
words, is chosen and shaped as a reflection of a country's place in the global order. It is not
limited to how a logo is designed or a tagline advertised – other values factor into, and  are
shaped by, the process. As a state's effort to position itself for opportunity in global
competition, nation branding can ultimately serve as one avenue toward economic, political,
and social gain.
While national branding is properly the government's responsibility, it is not carried out
by government alone. Building and running such branding requires the participation of multiple
actors. Government, as policyholder, retains the authority to determine and shape the nation's
image in the eyes of others, while society, culture, history, and the social order function as the
essential variables from which that image is built and sustained.

Anholt argues that national branding is meant to promote a positive national image for
the state and its citizens, to build a national brand identity, to attract tourists, to expand product
exports, and to increase foreign direct investment. He cautions, however, that a country seeking
to strengthen its international image should concentrate more on product development and
marketing than on branding efforts confined to name, symbol, and design as communicated
markers of national identity. Consistent coordination of ideas relevant to product development
and policy formulation is what gradually, and organically, improves a co untry's reputation
(Rahayu & Arianti, 2014).
Anholt views international relations through a marketing lens, in which social
interaction among societies functions as a form of exchange governed by market principles of
supply and demand: sellers offering pro ducts with a genuine advantage attract more demand,
while products lacking added value do not. This logic suggests that states will seek to create
national products with genuine, competitive added value for the international public. Beyond
the aims noted above, nation branding can also ideally contribute to nation-building, insofar as
it strengthens a nation's collective confidence (Nurlelawati, 2019).
Anholt develops the concept of nation branding in Brand New Justice: The Upside of
Global Branding (2005) as the synthesis of representation, preference, and perception that an
individual holds when thinking of a particular country.
Figure 1. Nation Brand Hexagon (Simon Anholt)

The Nation Brand Hexagon comprises six dimensions: (1) brand exports – products
carrying a given country's mark of origin; (2) governance – perceptions of a country's political
dynamics; (3) investment and immigration – perceptions of how attractive a country is to
newcomers and investors; (4) culture and heritage; (5) people – a count ry's image as held
abroad through its population; and (6) tourism – a country's appeal to foreign visitors (Yamin
& Kristiawan, n.d.).

A city, region, or country's image and the positive perception it holds in the public eye
are built through branding – particularly within the tourism industry, amid intensifying global
competition. On this basis, nation branding is expected to expand a country's trade, tourism,
and investment. It is this logic that has led Indonesia's Ministry of Tourism to use the
“Wonderful Indonesia” slogan to draw international attention toward visiting the country.
RESEARCH METHOD
This study adopts a qualitative, descriptive-analytical approach based on documentary
research. Data were drawn from secondary sources: academic literature on  diplomacy, soft
power, and nation branding theory; Indonesian government policy documents, including
Ministry of Tourism and Creative Economy publications and the ministerial decree governing
the Wonderful Indonesia logo; and institutional and news reports documenting the campaign's
implementation. The analysis proceeds in three stages. First, it maps the theoretical framework
of public diplomacy, soft power, and nation branding that underpins the study (Section 2).
Second, it traces the policy history and  institutional design of the Wonderful Indonesia
campaign as a case of long -term public diplomacy. Third, it evaluates the campaign's
implementation across cultural, social, and marketing dimensions against that framework
(Section 4).
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Tourism potential is an important asset for any country: the industry makes a substantial
contribution to national income, employment, and foreign-exchange earnings, and Indonesia's
cultural and regional diversity gives its tourism industry a comparative  advantage
(Simanjuntak, 2020). Wonderful Indonesia functions as the country's national tourism brand, a
soft-diplomacy policy for introducing Indonesian culture and tourism. As the country's image,
Wonderful Indonesia is not run unilaterally by government  as policymaker; it depends on
cooperation among stakeholders in introducing Indonesia's tourism branding and culture, and
the rapid growth of social media has itself become a significant influence on visitation interest.
Nation branding, in this context, rests on three important aspects: history, culture, and
social interaction (Simanjuntak, 2020). Indonesia's historical influence can be traced across
several periods and carries weight beyond the region alone — Bandung, for instance, as the
city that hoste d the 1955 Asian -African Conference (Arsip Nasional Republik Indonesia,
2012). Public diplomacy channels tourism as a soft -power instrument of the state; tourism is

an influential industry that many countries treat as a leading sector, and the presence of tourism
nation branding has become a national focal point for using that sector to strengthen the
economy.
Indonesia's tourism logo appears under two related names – Pesona Indonesia and
Wonderful Indonesia – carrying similar meaning and the same underlyin g image. Both use a
Garuda emblem with its own philosophical grounding: the Garuda, Indonesia's national
symbol, carries a message of peace consistent with the country's constitutional foundations.
The logo's shape and color scheme are generally read as co mmunicating Indonesia's diversity
and unity across its many cultures.
The Ministry of Tourism's official guidance describes five thematic pillars of “pesona”
(charm): natural wonders, sensory wonders (taste), cultural wonders, modern wonders, and
adventurous wonders (Setiawan, 2017). In pursuing tourism and creative -economy
development, the Ministry of Tourism and Creative Economy has organized its potential -
development policy around three areas (Kementerian Pariwisata dan Ekonomi Kreatif, 2021):
1. Tourism destination development potential. Destinations are the primary target of the
tourism industry; growing visitor interest can be supported by improving tourism facilities
alongside empowering local communities in tourism areas. Collaboration between
infrastructure improvement and community empowerment is a key element in strengthening
sustainable tourism, encompassing regional and destination infrastructure development,
opportunities for foreign investment in the tourism industry, and community empowerment in
managing and developing tourism potential.
2. Tourism marketing development potential. Marketing is a further focus of tourism
development. Branding or image gives a product its distinguishing character and adds value –
a logic that extends directly to tourism marketing. Potential in this area includes the image and
branding created through national logos and slogans, the rapid growth of communication and
information media, broad partnership across marketing actors, and a growing and increasingly
focused range of promotional media for Indonesia's tourism appeal.
3. Tourism institutional development potential. Tourism development depends on
effective supporting institutions. Ministry strategy in this area includes synergy among
government, business actors, and society from the local to the national level; quality assurance
of human resources within the tourism sector; recognition of tourism as an inherently

multisectoral activity; and enabling regulation that also draws on Indonesia's demographic
advantages.
Analysis of Indonesia's Tourism Nation Branding
Image and brand formation are central to product marketing, including the marketing
of culture and tourism (Makhasi & Sari, n.d.). “Wonderful Indonesia” is the term used to
introduce Indonesian tourism to the international market, while the domestic market is
addressed through “Pesona Indonesia.” This branding, in place since 2011, replaced the earlier
“Visit Indonesia” slogan.
Introducing and promoting Indonesia abroad relies heavily on the Ministry of Tourism
and Creative Economy's use of electronic media – tourism videos, online advertising, print
media, and direct cultural exhibitions in public spaces (Prawibowo & Purnamasari, 2018). This
branding is meant to broaden recognition of Indonesia's tourism potential beyond Bali,
extending it across the country's provinces and their distinct local character.

The logo's five colors each carry a distinct meaning: green fo r harmony and natural
beauty, purple for faith and unity, orange for innovation and openness, blue for peace, and
magenta for the balance of reason and conduct (Prawibowo & Purnamasari, 2018). The Garuda
emblem is read as a symbol of communal life, signify ing peaceful coexistence with others in
the wider world; the bird, Indonesia's most populous fauna species and its national symbol, has
outstretched wings that signify openness, aspiration, the will to cross boundaries, universality,
and broad recognition (Keputusan Menteri Pariwisata dan Ekonomi Kreatif tentang Logo
Wonderful Indonesia).
Campaigning Wonderful Indonesia as national branding is meant to spread awareness
of Indonesia's tourism appeal worldwide, deepen international recognition, and build a positive
global image; as Indonesia's international image improves, the likelihood of an increase in

foreign visitor numbers is expected to grow (Idriasih, 2016). Wonderful Indonesia, as a nation
brand, foregrounds Indonesia's natural beauty, the hospitality  of its people, and its cultural
diversity through five components:
1. Wonderful Nature refers to Indonesia's natural beauty – its many islands, scenic
landscapes, and coastal attractions.
2. Wonderful Culture covers traditional music, dance, food, and clo thing; well-known
examples of Indonesian culture include batik and wayang (shadow puppetry).
3. Wonderful Food reflects Indonesia's abundance of spices and distinctive flavors, with
each region maintaining its own traditional cuisine.
4. Wonderful People refers to the hospitality of Indonesian society.
5. Wonderful Value of Money conveys that travel to Indonesia need not be expensive
– a point of continuing importance for the sector, since the independence and sustainability of
tourism drive growth in foreign-exchange earnings and employment.
Through this branding, Indonesia foregrounds several dimensions of the campaign,
with “Wonderful Indonesia” as a public policy for the tourism industry supporting the
following aspects:
a. Cultural Aspect: Branding as a Vehicle for Introducing Local Culture
In 2016, the Indonesian government cooperated with the city of Paris to introduce
Indonesian tourism and culture, using Paris city -tour buses carrying imagery of Indonesian
destinations. This promotion functioned as a n invitation to the international community,
showcasing Indonesia's natural beauty and cultural diversity, and is consistent with the public-
diplomacy concept of disseminating information about an opportunity or policy to foreign
audiences in pursuit of the policymaker's interests.
b. Social Aspect
Social life is one of the aspects that supports the formation of a nation's image. The face
a country presents is shaped by the social behavior and everyday life of its people, which in
turn affects how other parties assess that social image. Building a country's self-image requires
coordination and integration across multiple aspects that together produce a picture of the
nation – its social, economic, and political life among them. A well -coordinated national

branding effort accordingly produces a more po sitive influence. As an industry traded
internationally and growing rapidly, tourism ranks among the leading categories of the global
economy.
For Indonesia, tourism is an important sector supporting the national economy, capable
of sustaining employment a nd opening further opportunities for national development.
Branding communicated through marketing and promotion is intended to shape a nation's
image in ways that influence visitor numbers. In Anholt's categorization, nation branding rests
on several supporting variables, including tourism, people, and culture and heritage (Utami &
Gaffar, 2014).
Kagon Rage, as discussed in Mustari et al. (2021), identifies several variables central
to branding strategy. Brand positioning — positioning the image or brand — is fundamental to
determining an actor's subsequent direction and policy; reflecting on a product's existing brand
becomes part of how an actor evaluates its next steps, generating feedback that reveals public
reception. Brand positioning is accordingly treated as a key stage in how an actor assesses and
directs its product branding. Philip Kotler describes positioning as the effort to design a product
and its marketing so as to create a specific impression that remains in the consumer's memory.
A further variable is brand personality, understood as a strategy for building a brand's
external appeal from the consumer's point of view — shaping public opinion of a product based
on the image or personality that has been constructed, often pursued through social media as a
channel for building a positive public image. Brand personality aims to create attraction by
shaping characteristics grounded in consumer experience and public opinion of the brand
(Mustari et al., 2021).
Under the Ministry of Tourism's Brand Guidelines, the meaning of “Wonderful” or
“Pesona” is described as Indonesia's promise of tourism to the world: a promise that Indonesia
is rich in wonders, both human and natural, and that it offers new, enjoyable, and captivating
experiences. The accompanying use of “Indonesia” in the logo is intended to communicate the
country's position more firmly amid international tourism competition (Kementerian
Pariwisata dan Ekonomi Kreatif, Brand Guidelines).
Indonesia's tourism promotion follows the stages of branding, advertising, and selling.
Tourism branding built to carry meaning across Indonesia's diverse cultures is subsequently
promoted through advertising, supported by cooperation between countries and private parties,

before being offered to the wider public. Social-media influencers, Miss Indonesia titleholders,
tourism ambassadors, and related actors play an important role in promoting the country's
tourism.
The use of communication, information, and technology media is a strategy with
substantial influence on introducing Indonesian tourism, and government, business actors, and
society are all expected to make active use of these media to introduce Indonesia's tourism and
culture globally (Prawibowo & Purnamasari, 2018). While conventional media such as leaflets
remain in use in some Indonesian provinces, the adoption of online technology continues to
grow and has a significant effect on the reach of tourism marketing (Alias, Yahya, & Rusmini,
2020).
Among the Ministry of Tourism and Creative Economy's pr omotional programs is the
digital visualization of Indonesia's diversity, featuring content on natural potential, arts tourism,
culture, religion, and regional cuisine from across the country. Wonderful Indonesia's
visualization, promoted through a range of online media, has drawn considerable attention from
both domestic and international audiences, with individual Indonesians frequently contributing
to the Wonderful Indonesia narrative through their own material (Afrianto & Santiko, n.d.).
Recent tourism data suggest that this branding has continued alongside a broader post-
pandemic recovery in the sector. Indonesia recorded approximately 13.9 million foreign visitor
arrivals in 2024, an increase of about 19.05 percent over 2023, generating an estimated US D
16.71 billion in foreign -exchange earnings, alongside 1.02 billion domestic trips recorded in
the same year (Kementerian Pariwisata dan Ekonomi Kreatif, as reported in ASEAN Magazine,
2026). Indonesia's position on the World Economic Forum's Travel and Tourism Development
Index likewise improved, from 40th of 140 countries in 2019 to 22nd of 119 countries in 2024
— an improvement that coincides with, without being fully explained by, the continued use of
the Wonderful Indonesia campaign (World Economic Forum, 2024).
CONCLUSION
Since 2011, the Indonesian government has promoted a new tourism image known as
Wonderful Indonesia, replacing the earlier Visit Indonesia campaign. This change in name
signaled a shift in focus and purpose: beyond attracting visitors, it sought deeper international
recognition of the diverse culture, tourism, and traditions found across Indonesia.

Wonderful Indonesia carries meaning that extends beyond the tourism sector alone,
encompassing customs, ethnicity, language, culture, and other forms of diversity across the
country. Understood this way, the branding is read as a representation of that broader diversity.
Indonesia's tourism promotion strategy spans multiple media, both online and offline.
The selection of tourism ambassadors  from each Indonesian province is also part of the local
cultural promotion carried out by respective regional governments.
Branding functions as both a policy and a supporting strategy in tourism promotion:
tourism branding forms part of a national identity consumed by both domestic and international
audiences. Wonderful Indonesia is a tourism identity visualized through its slogan and logo,
representing Indonesia's cultural diversity, customs, languages, religions, beliefs, and other
distinguishing features embodied in that logo.
At the same time, the analysis indicates that a positive national image does not
automatically translate into proportional tourism growth. Tourism security remains a
significant constraint that the government must continue to address if the branding's economic
potential is to be more fully realized.
