Abstract
The construct of brand purpose has received increasing attention from scholars and managers alike, yet
the literature is scattered and inconsistent regarding its dimensions and lacks operationalization for
measurement purposes. To this end, this research project aims to develop a preliminary
multidimensional framework of brand purpose and obtain expert-informed conceptual reﬁnement. To
develop dimensions for brand purpose, a multi-phase qualitative approach was conducted. First, prior
literature on brand purpose and related concepts, such as stakeholder theory, brand equity, brand
authenticity, brand sustainability, and related frameworks/models, was reviewed to derive initial
dimensions of brand purpose. The provisional dimensions were then subjected to expert consultation. A
15-member panel of academics and practitioners provided written input to reﬁne the provisional
dimensions, while a separate 11-member expert panel responded to dimension-speciﬁc prompts used by
the authors to formulate preliminary items. Expert feedback was coded via grounded-theory-informed
coding processes and expert veriﬁcation loops. Results of this qualitative analysis provide preliminary
expert-informed support for eleven dimensions of brand purpose: Beyond-Proﬁt Orientation, Enduring
Commitment, Integrated Values, Genuine Action and Authenticity, Stakeholder Nurturing, Consumer
Empowerment, Transformation of Practices/Social Impact, Sustainability Stewardship, Dialogue and
Involvement, Brand-Purpose Equity Roles, and Emotional and Inspirational Value. Moreover, the results
indicate that brand purpose is both manifested internally (e.g. reﬂected in organizational strategy,
culture, day-to-day operations, decision-making) and externally (experienced by stakeholders via trust,
dialogue, fairness, emotional attachment, societal value). This framework advances the initial scale
development of brand purpose by bringing together previously scattered literature to provide initial
dimensions of the construct for greater construct clarity, as well as initial items to build on for future
scale development. Additionally, this framework may be used by practitioners to gauge how legitimate,
deep-seated, and well-aligned their brand purpose activities are on both organizational and stakeholder
levels. Accordingly, the results are interpreted as expert-informed conceptual reﬁnement of a literature-
derived framework rather than independent empirical or psychometric validation of a measurement
scale.
1. Introduction
Purpose-driven marketing and branding have been embraced by both scholars and practitioners alike in
recent years. In contrast to past decades, current-day organizations are expected to “do good” and tackle
prevailing social, environmental, and ethical issues besides providing goods and services (Loureiro et al.,
2023). This shift in consumerist culture and market trends has forced companies to adopt values-based
leadership, acting as vehicles of change by investing in long-term sustainable impact on society (Rüger
et al., 2025). France et al. (2024) noted that brand purpose has become a core brand attribute
overarching taglines and affecting businesses’ strategic choices and stakeholder relationships.
Furthermore, both scholarly conversations and industry activities around social-focused movements
such as Brand Activism, Conscientious Branding, and Sustainable Consumption reinforce the need for
Page 2/29

organizations to think beyond proﬁts and deﬁne their reason for being (Walter et al., 2024). Thus,
scholars have sought to develop a clearer understanding of what brand purpose entails and how it
affects stakeholders’ perceptions of brands, their overall equity, and long-term performance.
Nonetheless, there is a major gap in the literature, as no uniﬁed, validated scale has been established to
quantify the phenomenon. Previous attempts have either been conceptual or case-driven and failed to
provide suﬃcient operationalization to test empirically.
France et al. (2024) deﬁned the BEING framework for purpose-driven companies consisting of ﬁve
dimensions including ‘beyond proﬁt’ and ‘enduring commitment’. Although widely recognised in business
practice, Zürn et al. (2023) also developed a Perceived Purpose Score containing three major
components: customer beneﬁt, social value, and proﬁt. However, these are by no means exhaustive, and
the existing literature still lacks an understandable construct structure from which potential items can be
drawn and further tested.
Therefore, this study’s main objectives are threefold. First, it seeks to provide better depth and
understanding of “brand purpose” from a theoretical standpoint. Next, the research aims to identify and
reﬁne major factors or dimensions through which organizations can approach brand purpose. Finally,
this study attempts to provide and propose items that could act as a preliminary guideline/reference for
scholars and practitioners to develop their scales to measure brand purpose within varying industries
and organizational settings.
The purpose and relevance of this study are derived from its potential academic and managerial
implications. On one hand, by providing a foundation to develop constructs that measure brand purpose,
this study offers scholarly insights into a concept that has been inﬂuencing consumers’ behavior and
companies’ missions. On the other hand, the model presented in this study can serve as a tool for
managers and decision-makers to evaluate the depth and authenticity of their brand purpose efforts.
2. Literature Review
2.1. Brand Purpose as a Strategic Branding Construct
Brand purpose has been deﬁned quite extensively in the literature. Many deﬁne it as “a brand’s reason to
exist beyond proﬁt” and describe a brand’s intention to create value for their customers, society, and
environment (Brosch, 2023; Ibn-Mohammed et al., 2026, p. 3). France et al. (2024) state that brand
purpose is “a multi-dimensional construct that is lived within strategic intent, embedded in organizational
values and purpose, and actualized through stakeholder partnerships.” Unlike simplistic deﬁnitions of
brand purpose as mere marketing rhetoric or corporate social responsibility efforts, Calder (2020)
argues that purpose enables brands to resonate with consumers on a cultural and emotional level by
connecting corporate existence to the betterment of society at large. Lastly, Zürn, Buder, and Unfried
(2023) developed their own deﬁnition of brand purpose as the consumers’ perceived trade-off between
customer beneﬁt, ﬁnancial performance, and socio-ethical goodwill. Because of this, brand purpose
Page 3/29

should be articulated and demonstrated across an organization’s business model, stakeholders, and
practices, as well as long-term vision. It has become a key ingredient in developing brand equity, trust,
and competitive advantage (Walter et al., 2024). Earlier research around branding mostly focused on
whether brands had social responsibilities (Porter and Kramer, 2011). Today, researchers acknowledge
brand purpose as a key strategic driver that impacts brand equity, trust, and long-term stakeholder
outcomes (France et al., 2024; Feri, Ind, & Tjandra, 2024). Brand purpose is now considered a method to
differentiate; it is also becoming a social and moral indicator of the brand’s willingness to help.
Other research has attempted to break down brand purpose further into dimensions that comprise the
overall idea. France et al.’s (2024) BEING framework is considered one of the most comprehensive
academic efforts. The authors identify ﬁve dimensions of brand purpose: Beyond, Endure, Integrated,
Nurture, and Genuine. As depicted in the model, brand purpose is portrayed as multidimensional and
prompts readers to look beyond purpose statements and evaluate how purpose is infused throughout an
organization’ purpose. Zürn, Buder, and Unfried (2023) took an empirically driven approach and based
their model off of how individuals perceive a brand’ motive. The authors identiﬁed Perceived Purpose
Score which consists of a consumer’s perception of the brand’ purpose across three dimensions:
creating ﬁnancial performance, creating customer beneﬁt, and creating social contribution. The research
discovered that individuals do not simply accept brand purpose at what it appears to be. Instead,
consumers draw on prior experiences with the brand and current brand cues to form judgments about
brand purpose.
Researchers have begun to look at the relationship between brand purpose and outcomes similar to
brand equity. These authors suggest that brands with purpose cultivate higher levels of brand trust,
loyalty, and competitive differentiation (Calder, 2020; Mirzaei, Webster, & Siuki, 2021). Mirzaei et al.’s
(2021) study on nonproﬁt organizations is unique as it uncovers that there are four types of brand
purpose: empower, support, ﬁght, and change. The study goes on to suggest that these purposes inform
stakeholders about what the organization cares about at its core, inﬂuencing brand authenticity
perceptions. Walter et al. (2024) uncovered another dimension that researchers should consider when
thinking about brand purpose: “woke-washing.” This article sheds light on how brands can use social
causes to proﬁt off of movements they may not actually support. Results show that it mattered if these
brands were perceived as authentic, and stakeholders’ perceptions of “woke” brands inﬂuenced if these
brands could avoid negative backlash. Therefore, brand purpose can be thought of as not only a
branding tactic but also as a brand liability if consumers do not believe what the brand is saying.This
research is therefore guided by the need to systematically deﬁne and measure brand purpose using a
multidimensional approach rooted in the existing literature. It begins by addressing the ﬁrst research
question: RQ1: What is the conceptual nature of brand purpose as discussed in contemporary literature?
The study then explores the second research question, RQ2: What dimensions or factors constitute
brand purpose, as derived from literature and reﬁned through expert input? Finally, it addresses the ﬁnal
research question, RQ3: What items can be proposed to evaluate the explored factors in the study?
2.2. Theoretical Foundations of Brand Purpose
Page 4/29

Articles on brand purpose often draw on theories from various disciplines. Among these theories,
stakeholder theory is the most frequently used theory to explain brand purpose. According to the brand
purpose literature, brands should provide value to all stakeholders rather than focusing solely on
shareholder returns. Thus, stakeholder theory helps inform multiple studies' explanations of stakeholder
nurturing as a dimension of purpose (France et al., 2024). The next most frequently used theory in the
brand purpose literature is institutional theory, which states that brands change as institutions change in
society (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). From this theory, as purpose becomes more institutionalized, there
is potential for mimetic isomorphism, and companies’ purpose becomes purely about being seen as
legitimate (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). This could be done knowingly through falsiﬁcation, but it could
also be present in companies that copy purpose-driven actions to beneﬁt strategically or proﬁtably,
despite different intentions and goals.
Studies examining brand equity also contribute to the theory on how purpose affects consumer-based
brand evaluations. Calder (2020) found that brand purpose is crucial for emotional attachment, identity
alignment and meaningful differentiation of brand equity. Gonçalves Filho et al. (2025) utilized grounded
theory to discover brand-purpose equity drivers from the perspective of practitioners. Their study helps
conﬁrm the practical applications of purpose as a driver of value. Transformational leadership theory is
also evident in works such as Feri et al. (2024), which describe brands as able to inﬂuence society and
change behaviors, narratives, and systems.
2.3. Existing Frameworks and Measurement Approaches
Although brand purpose has recently been garnering signiﬁcant academic and managerial interest, the
construct is still nascent in terms of measurement. Research has introduced several measures of brand
purpose, but scholars generally lack consensus on what dimensions are of primary importance. While
many researchers have offered unique and valuable frameworks (e. g., France et al., 2024; Feri et al.,
2024; Calder, 2020), there is yet to be an agreed upon method for how brand purpose should be
operationalized and measured consistently. Research has mainly focused on qualitative analysis, case-
based reasoning, or semantic analysis of brands’ purpose statements (Rudeloff and Michalski, 2024;
Hajdas and Kłeczek, 2021). Though frameworks like BEING (France et al., 2024) and the Perceived
Purpose Score (Zürn et al., 2023) provide helpful constructs, they fail to offer a psychometrically valid
multi-item scale. Gonçalves Filho et al. (2025) offered one of the ﬁrst theoretical models grounded in
brand-purpose equity but does not validate their measurement scale. To date, there has been no agreed
upon method for how scholars should measure brand purpose that can be applied across industries,
demographics, and organization types.
There is a critical need for this within the academic literature as the lack of standardization hinders
academic generalizability and managerial application. Benchmarking. Valid scales are needed that
assess multiple dimensions of brand purpose. Not only should these dimensions speak to how a
company internalizes its purpose and its stakeholders’ perceptions of a brand’s purpose (Zürn et al.,
2023), but they should also assess emerging dimensions of purpose such as brand authenticity (Hajdas
and Kłeczek, 2021), brand empowerment (France et al., 2024), and sustainability (Delagnes et al., 2017).
Page 5/29

Additionally, having a validated measure would allow for brand comparisons across different industries
and contexts. To the authors’ knowledge, this research will be the ﬁrst to make an attempt to synthesize
the current literature on brand purpose in order to create something new. With this research, we hope to
answer France et al.’s (2024) and Walter et al.’s (2024) calls to action to think beyond traditional
frameworks and measure brand purpose in a way that differentiates purpose that is embedded in
company culture versus purpose that is superﬁcially displayed for the sake of marketing.
Building on the measurement gap identiﬁed above, this study argues for a multidimensional framework
that captures both the internal expression of a brand's purpose and external stakeholder perceptions.
Such a framework should include dimensions such as authenticity, employee and consumer
empowerment, sustainability initiatives, stakeholder engagement, and brand equity roles while remaining
applicable across industries and brand contexts.
3. Research Methodology
This research employs a multi-phase, theory-driven methodology to formulate dimensions for assessing
brand purpose. The methodology proceeds through sequential yet interrelated phases: literature-based
dimension identiﬁcation, expert consultation and reﬁnement, and exploratory item generation. The
methodology has been informed by grounded theory (Corbin and Strauss, 2008), which was integrated
during the qualitative phase to promote inductive analysis of expert insights, thereby enhancing the
construct framework. An effort was made to combine inductive and deductive strategies for dimension
justiﬁcation and proposed item creation. The deductive component was reﬂected in the use of prior
literature to identify initial domains, while the inductive component was reﬂected in the interpretation of
expert responses.
3.1. Literature-based derivation of the preliminary
dimensions
To make the derivation of the initial domains explicit, the literature stage was undertaken as a concept-
focused synthesis. First, studies and frameworks that directly deﬁned or conceptualised brand purpose
were examined. Second, the review was extended to closely related streams—stakeholder orientation,
brand authenticity and purpose-washing, consumer empowerment, sustainability and SDG-oriented
branding, dialogic stakeholder engagement, social transformation, brand equity, and emotional
attachment—to capture aspects not represented fully in the focal frameworks. Third, recurrent concepts,
labels, and deﬁnitions across these streams were compared, and conceptually similar terms were
consolidated under a common domain label. Finally, the domains were checked for conceptual
distinctiveness so that a preliminary dimension represented a theoretically distinguishable aspect of
brand purpose rather than a duplicate label. A domain was retained when it had direct support in the
brand-purpose literature or convergent support across related literature streams.
Page 6/29

This synthesis produced the eleven preliminary dimensions used in the expert phase. The BEING
framework primarily informed Beyond-Proﬁt Orientation, Enduring Commitment, Integrated Values,
Genuine Action and Authenticity, and Stakeholder Nurturing (France et al., 2024). Work on perceived
purpose and stakeholder value further clariﬁed the beyond-proﬁt and stakeholder-balance domains
(Freeman, 1984; Zürn et al., 2023). Research on consumer empowerment and social transformation
informed Consumer Empowerment and Transformation of Practices/Social Impact (Hajdas & Kłeczek,
2021; Mirzaei et al., 2021). Sustainability- and SDG-oriented branding research informed Sustainability
Stewardship (Feri et al., 2024), while studies of dialogic communication and co-creation informed
Dialogue and Involvement (Rudeloff & Michalski, 2024). Brand-purpose equity research informed Brand-
Purpose Equity Roles (Calder, 2020; Gonçalves Filho et al., 2025), and research on purpose engagement,
emotional attachment, and stakeholder trust informed Emotional and Inspirational Value (Gulati &
Wohlgezogen, 2023; van Ingen et al., 2021). These eleven dimensions were treated as provisional,
literature-derived domains for subsequent expert examination rather than as a ﬁxed ﬁnal framework.
3.2. Expert panel and consultation rounds
An extensive review of the academic literature on brand purpose identiﬁed 11 preliminary dimensions
that capture its multifaceted nature. These preliminary dimensions were then examined through expert
panel consultation. The expert panel consisted of 15 experts with relevant marketing and management
expertise, including senior academic researchers, managers working in sales and marketing, corporate
social responsibility (CSR) specialists, and business consultants. The experts provided written
responses to open-ended prompts concerning the concept of brand purpose within a speciﬁed
timeframe. The expert consultation was used to examine whether the dimensions identiﬁed through the
literature were conceptually relevant, understandable, and suﬃciently representative of brand purpose.
The panel was constituted to obtain perspectives from both academic and practitioner settings.
Selection was based on the relevance of the experts’ academic or professional roles to marketing, sales
and marketing management, corporate social responsibility, or business consulting. This selection
constituted a purposive, criterion-based expert-sampling approach. In the ﬁrst consultation, the 15
experts independently considered the meaning, essential features, implementation, and organisational
and stakeholder manifestations of brand purpose. The provisional dimension names, deﬁnitions, and
keywords were not shown during Round 1; they were introduced only in Round 2. Their input was not
restricted to accepting the literature-derived domains. Experts could conﬁrm a domain or raise a doubt,
objection, or suggestion concerning its name, deﬁnition, conceptual boundary, overlap, or completeness.
Such feedback could therefore lead to reﬁnement, reclassiﬁcation, merger, or the proposal of an
additional domain, and the preliminary framework was not treated as ﬁxed. In the second consultation,
the same experts were provided with the provisional domain names, deﬁnitions, and literature-derived
keywords. Individual comments were circulated to the panel iteratively until a consensual interpretation
was reached. Round 1 responses were collected in written form. The excerpts presented in Table 1 were
lightly edited for grammatical readability without altering their substantive meaning. The study

comprised two formal consultation rounds; the iterative circulation of comments constituted follow-up

3.3. Coding and consistency of interpretation
Expert responses were analysed using a hybrid deductive–inductive, grounded-theory-informed coding
procedure. Open coding captured concepts expressed by the experts, while axial and selective coding
examined how those concepts related to, challenged, or reﬁned the provisional literature-derived
domains. The same experts were again contacted to verify the domains. This time, the names of the
domains, their respective deﬁnitions, and the keywords associated with each domain, taken from the
literature, were provided to the experts for veriﬁcation. If any doubt, objection, or suggestion was put
forward by experts at the individual level, it was again shared with all experts iteratively. After their
consensual veriﬁcation, the domains were considered as dimensions. This iterative process helped
reﬁne the dimension structure and ensured that the ﬁnal dimensions were supported by both prior
literature and expert judgement.
The analysis followed a grounded-theory-informed coding sequence. During open coding, meaning-
bearing expressions in the expert statements were assigned descriptive labels that stayed close to the
participants’ wording. During axial coding, related open codes were compared and grouped in relation to
the provisional literature-derived domains; because a single statement could express more than one
idea, multiple axial codes were permitted. During selective coding, the resulting categories were
reviewed against the overall concept of brand purpose to determine the domain to which each code
contributed and to identify conceptual overlap requiring reﬁnement. Coding consistency was pursued
through a documented coding table, repeated comparison of expert statements with their open and axial
codes, and veriﬁcation of the coded interpretations by the expert panel. Disputed classiﬁcations were
reconsidered and recirculated until consensus was obtained. The initial coding was undertaken by the
ﬁrst researcher and independently reviewed by the second researcher. Differences in code assignment
and domain mapping were resolved through discussion. The study therefore relied on independent
review, discussion, and iterative interpretive veriﬁcation rather than a statistical intercoder-reliability
coeﬃcient. After the eleven dimensions were ﬁnalised, the four broader conceptual groups shown in
Fig. 1 were reviewed by the 15-member expert panel.
3.4. Item-generation stage and participant numbers
Eleven questions were developed for the item-generation stage. Each question was linked to one of the
dimensions and administered to 11 experts. Their written responses were collected, and the authors
developed candidate items from those responses. Therefore, item generation was done in an exploratory
way to create an initial pool of items, not an empirically validated measure. These items can be used as
a starting point for empirical validation through processes such as exploratory factor analysis,
conﬁrmatory factor analysis, reliability analysis, and validity analysis.
The two reported expert numbers refer to different stages and separate panels. Fifteen experts
participated in the dimension-development and veriﬁcation rounds, whereas a separate panel of 11
Page 8/29

experts participated in the subsequent item-generation exercise. The 11 item-generation experts were
not drawn from the original 15-member panel. At this stage, the dimensional structure had been
ﬁnalised, and the purpose was to translate each dimension into a preliminary set of candidate items.
3.5. Consultation protocol
The consultation protocol comprised three components. Round 1 used open prompts concerning (a) the
experts’ understanding of brand purpose, (b) the features required to capture its essence, (c) how
organisations implement brand purpose, and (d) how it is reﬂected in organisational and stakeholder-
related practices. Round 2 asked experts to examine each provisional domain name, deﬁnition, and
associated keyword and to provide conﬁrmation, doubt, objection, or a suggested revision, including
possible reclassiﬁcation, merger, or addition. The item-generation component used one open-ended
question for each of the eleven ﬁnal dimensions; these eleven questions are reported in Section 5.
4. Identiﬁcation of Brand Purpose Dimensions
Informed by previous literature, we identiﬁed 11 initial dimensions of brand purpose: Beyond-Proﬁt
Orientation, Enduring Commitment, Integrated Values, Genuine Action and Authenticity, Stakeholder
Nurturing, Consumer Empowerment, Transformation of Practices/Social Impact, Sustainability
Stewardship, Dialogue and Involvement, Brand-Purpose Equity Roles, and Emotional and Inspirational
Value. Together, these initial dimensions represented psychological, emotional, social, and ethical
considerations related to brand purpose. We used these dimensions to guide expert consultation,
reﬁnement, and item generation.
4.1. Beyond-Proﬁt Orientation
Central to brand purpose is its beyond-proﬁt orientation. Many scholars note that brands must seek to do
something beyond creating value for shareholders (Bocean and Vărzaru, 2025). The “Beyond proﬁt”
dimension in France et al.’s (2024) BEING framework encapsulates purpose statements that explicitly
seek to beneﬁt society—not slogans designed to increase proﬁt. Likewise, Calder (2020) states that
purpose must indicate how a brand will positively contribute to humanity or the ecosystem to be deemed
legitimate. In so doing, purpose becomes framed as something transcendent to traditional marketing
methods. This does not imply that proﬁt is irrelevant; rather, ﬁnancial viability enables a brand to sustain
and extend its positive impact. Purpose needs proﬁt to further empower and support its positive impact
(Whitler & Pohl, 2021; Zürn et al., 2023). However, when considering why a brand exists, there needs to
be something more than proﬁts. Therefore, brand purpose provides a reason for a brand’s existence. We
deﬁne a brand’s beyond-proﬁt orientation as ‘a brand’s core reason for existence, that is, not conﬁned to
making money but instead devoted to creating a beneﬁcial and signiﬁcant impact on society or the
environment.’
4.2. Enduring Commitment
Page 9/29

True brand purpose is not a short-lived campaign but a sustained strategic commitment. France et al.
(2024) describe enduring commitment as evidence of a long-horizon vision of brand purpose. Walter et
al. (2024) emphasize that enduring brand efforts build stakeholder trust, especially when tied to long-
term programs and embedded in decision-making processes. Brand-purpose objectives pursued through
strategies such as CSR require long-term commitment (Pandey & Joseph, 2025). Hence, ‘enduring
commitment’ is deﬁned as a ‘brand’s stable and continuous dedication to its purpose over time’.
4.3. Integrated Values
A brand’s purpose achieves authenticity when it permeates internal systems and is reﬂected in strategy,
operations and culture. France et al. (2024) describe integrated purpose as one that inﬂuences every
aspect of an organization, from supply chains to incentive structures and employee onboarding. This
echoes Feri et al.’s (2024) ﬁndings that purpose-aligned organizations uphold social values. According to
France et al. (2024), integrated values means “…brand purpose should act as a guiding principle that
connects and aligns the core values of the brand within the ﬁrm…”. Integrated values are essential for
maintaining consistency in communication with stakeholders and for facilitating purposeful dialogue
with them (Duncan, 2015). Therefore, in this study, the deﬁnition of Integrated Values is ‘the extent to
which brand purpose is embedded throughout an organization’s operations, strategy, internal culture and
brand identity.’
4.4. Genuine Action and Authenticity
Authenticity is a cornerstone of brand purpose credibility (Sebastián-Morillas et al., 2023; Abratt et al.,
2025). Walter et al. (2024) demonstrate that stakeholders evaluate purpose based on observable and
consistent brand actions rather than aspirational claims. Feri et al. (2024) note that authenticity
increases when purpose-driven actions are validated by third parties or supported by transparent
reporting. France et al. (2024) further emphasize “genuine action” as critical to stakeholder trust.
Accordingly, to establish brand authenticity, brand actions must be guided by brand purpose (Whitler &
Pohl, 2021). In this study, Genuine Action and Authenticity is deﬁned as ‘the alignment between a brand’s
stated purpose and its actual behaviours, characterized by transparency, consistency, and evidence-
based initiatives.’
4.5. Stakeholder Nurturing
Purposeful brands recognize and address the interests of multiple stakeholders — including employees,
customers, communities, and investors. Many organisations have begun to adopt this approach (Whitler
& Pohl, 2021). France et al. (2024) underscore “nurturing stakeholders” as a key element of meaningful
purpose, while Feri et al. (2024) highlight multi-stakeholder engagement as central to achieving SDG-
aligned outcomes. This reﬂects Freeman’s (1984) stakeholder theory, which proposes that organizational
success is contingent upon the well-being of all stakeholder groups. Effective stakeholder management
can yield desirable outcomes (Loi, 2016), and active stakeholder participation can lead to supportive
behaviour towards the brand (Källström & Ripoll González, 2025). Hence, ‘Stakeholder Nurturing’ refers
Page 10/29

to ‘a brand’s intentional effort to balance value creation across all key stakeholders, fostering mutual
beneﬁt and trust.’
4.6. Consumer Empowerment
Empowering consumers to act in alignment with their values is an emerging indicator of brand purpose.
For example, younger consumers expect organisations to take a stand on social issues, and they
evaluate the alignment between their beliefs and those expressed by organisations (Williams et al., 2022;
O’Sullivan et al., 2026). Rudeloff and Michalski (2024) found that purpose-led brands on social media
enable consumers to participate meaningfully in brand initiatives and societal issues. Mirzaei et al.
(2021) also noted that non-proﬁt brands use empowerment as a lever for deepening relational ties.
Empowerment manifests through tools, calls to action and participatory mechanisms. For example,
allowing customers to propose ideas or choose among product options (Bachouche and Sabri-Zaaraoui,
2017; Hur and Ha, 2020). Hence, in the present study, ‘Consumer Empowerment’ is deﬁned as ‘a brand’s
facilitation of consumer agency, enabling individuals to act on their values and participate in meaningful
societal engagement through the brand.’
4.7. Transformation of Practices / Social Impact
A key indicator of authentic brand purpose is its capacity to catalyze lasting transformation. AlQahtani
(2025) argues that SDG-aligned brands must demonstrate changes in behaviour, systems, or norms, not
just intentions. Kapitan et al. (2022) suggest that brands can act as institutional forces capable of
producing collective beneﬁt when purpose is linked to strategic transformation. The difference between
authenticity and transformation of practices is that authenticity concerns the consistency of behaviour,
whereas transformation concerns the outcomes of that behaviour. Hence, in the study, transformation of
practices is deﬁned as ‘the ability of a brand’s purpose to produce sustained and measurable changes in
organizational behaviour and contribute to meaningful changes in societal norms or community-level
outcomes.’
4.8. Sustainability Stewardship
Environmental stewardship is often tied to a credible brand purpose (Zhang et al., 2025). France et al.
(2024) note that purpose-driven brands often align with environmental goals such as carbon reduction,
circularity, or lifecycle transparency. Feri et al. (2024) emphasize SDG integration as both a strategic and
moral imperative for modern brands. These practices help deﬁne the environmental legitimacy of a
brand’s purpose. A well-deﬁned brand purpose aligned with sustainability enhances trust among
customers, resulting in loyalty (Papasureeechote and Wonglorsaichon, 2025; Armutcu, 2025). Hence,
Sustainability Stewardship is deﬁned as ‘the incorporation of environmental responsibility and alignment
with sustainability goals as integral elements of a brand’s purpose.’
4.9. Dialogue and Involvement
When organizations follow brand purpose, their communication is highly context-speciﬁc (Rudeloff &
Michalski, 2024). With the growth of social media, engaging in meaningful two-way communication is
Page 11/29

essential for co-creating brand purpose with stakeholders and building strong relationships (Rudeloff
and Michalski, 2024; Herrada-Lores et al., 2025). Hardianto (2023) found that social platforms offer new
ways for consumers to shape and co-own purpose. Involving customers through dialogue, e.g., via social
platforms, leads to higher satisfaction and stronger relationships with the brand (Colliander et al., 2015).
Dialogue must be continuous for organisations to maintain customer engagement and satisfaction
(Merdiaty & Aldrin, 2022). Dialogue and involvement are deﬁned as ‘the continuous and participatory
interaction between a brand and its stakeholders, through which purpose is co-developed, reﬁned, and
enacted.’
4.10. Brand-Purpose Equity Roles
Brand purpose contributes strategically to a brand’s market position and meaning-making. Research
suggests that purpose serves as a differentiator, source of emotional equity, and long-term loyalty driver
(Calder, 2020; Bianchi et al., 2020). Practitioner studies, for example, Gonçalves Filho et al. (2025), show
that brand managers view purpose as central to consumer identity and category leadership. In the
present study, brand-purpose equity roles are deﬁned as ‘the functional and emotional contributions that
purpose makes to brand value—including differentiation, identity creation and stakeholder inspiration.’
4.11. Emotional and Inspirational Value
Finally, purpose must resonate emotionally to foster brand attachment. Studies (Calder, 2020; Chebab
and Boukerch, 2025; Cestare and Ray, 2019) emphasize that emotional meaning, such as hope,
belonging and inspiration, drives loyalty and advocacy. Walter et al. (2024) and van Ingen et al. (2021)
show that emotionally rich purpose communication enhances credibility and engagement. When
purpose is enacted sincerely, it fosters positive stakeholder emotions (France et al., 2024; Gulati and
Wohlgezogen, 2023). Positive emotional value only emerges when purpose is trusted and aligned with
action (Vredenburg et al., 2020). Emotional and inspirational value in the study is deﬁned as ‘the
emotional connection and sense of meaning that a brand’s purpose generates in stakeholders, often
reﬂected in feelings of hope, pride or shared identity and strengthened identiﬁcation when purpose is
communicated and enacted authentically.’
5. Findings
The literature review and expert consultation process supported the retention and reﬁnement of eleven
dimensions of brand purpose: Beyond-Proﬁt Orientation, Enduring Commitment, Integrated Values,
Genuine Action and Authenticity, Stakeholder Nurturing, Consumer Empowerment, Transformation of
Practices/Social Impact, Sustainability Stewardship, Dialogue and Involvement, Brand-Purpose Equity
Roles, and Emotional and Inspirational Value.
Expert responses showed that the literature-derived dimensions were also reﬂected in practitioner and
academic interpretations of brand purpose, including authenticity, long-term commitment, stakeholder
fairness, sustainability, dialogue, and emotional value.

The literature-derived and expert-reﬁned structure indicates that brand purpose is a multidimensional
construct shaped by beyond-proﬁt orientation, internal organizational embedding, stakeholder
experience, and impact-oriented brand outcomes. Figure 1 presents the eleven dimensions organized
into four broader conceptual groups. The four broader conceptual groups shown in Fig. 1 were reviewed
by the expert panel after the eleven dimensions had been ﬁnalised.
In the subsequent item-generation stage, the eleven ﬁnalised dimensions and their deﬁnitions were
provided to the separate panel of 11 experts. Each question focused on one dimension:
1. How could we show that brand purpose is not conﬁned to proﬁt but is beyond that?
2. What do you think could be the indicators of enduring or long-term commitment to brand purpose?
3. In what ways should a brand set its purpose across departments, policies, and employee behaviour?
4. How do experts distinguish between authentic and performative purpose communication? How
could customers ﬁnd out whether the efforts of an organization are genuine or not?
5. How can purpose balance value creation and beneﬁts among customers, employees, communities,
and investors? What examples can one give to show this fairness and solidarity?
6. How is consumer empowerment related to brand purpose?
7. Which indicators or statements could show real impact of genuine efforts on society and
stakeholders by an organization?
8. How can we know that brand purpose is supporting sustainable development goals?
9. How can reciprocal engagement with stakeholders be demonstrated?
10. How can we capture the equity-building role of brand purpose?
11. How does brand purpose arouse emotional connection or inspiration among stakeholders?
The responses were analysed to identify dimension-speciﬁc keywords, and preliminary items were
developed from those keywords and expert inputs. These items require future empirical validation before
they can be used as a standardized measurement scale. The authors extracted dimension-relevant
expressions from the written responses, converted them into concise declarative statements, and
removed substantively repetitive wording. This process produced a preliminary pool of 43 items. The
ﬁnal item wording was formulated by the authors and was not returned to the experts for a separate
wording review.

Some keywords for the dimensions Beyond-Proﬁt Orientation and Sustainability Stewardship were found
to be similar. This is because when organizations go beyond proﬁt, they may beneﬁt society and the
environment. These domains may overlap with sustainability. However, this does not mean that all
efforts beyond proﬁt will come under SDGs. For example, efforts focused on legal or ethical
responsibilities may not come under SDGs (He & Lai, 2012). In the present study, Beyond-Proﬁt
Orientation means all activities or efforts beyond proﬁt. It does not necessarily have to be part of SDGs.
On the other hand, Sustainability Stewardship means that the organization focuses on SDGs. This
distinction helps maintain conceptual clarity between a broad beyond-proﬁt orientation and a speciﬁc
sustainability-oriented commitment.
6. Discussion
Page 20/29

Purpose should not be approached as just another “language” tool. There have been several
conceptualizations of purpose from an ethical, stakeholder, consumer-perception, and brand-equity
standpoint (France et al., 2024; Feri et al., 2024; Zürn et al., 2023). In this paper, purpose fragmented
insights were woven into a single framework for how brands conceive, communicate, experience, and
translate purpose into brand and societal value.
As such, this paper’s contribution should be seen as a literature-informed and expert validated
conceptual framework. Fully inductive grounded theory was not the study goal because experts reviewed
a priori domains grounded in the literature. Experts challenged concepts, reﬁned deﬁnitions, and
provided initial content validation, but the dimensionality and suggested items are still open to further
empirical testing.
Expert consultation and literature review established beyond-proﬁt orientation as a distinct dimension of
brand purpose. Yet, it is not the same as sustainability stewardship. Beyond proﬁt includes
responsibilities a brand has to ethical, legal, social, and community principles which can be
communicated independently from or through the lens of SDGs. Sustainability stewardship implies a
purpose centered on environmental impact or impacting certain SDGs (He & Lai, 2012; Feri et al., 2024;
Zürn et al., 2023).
Duration and embededdness were identiﬁed as necessary conditions for stakeholders to view purpose
as legitimate. When a company practices what they communicate and has been doing so over a long
period, stakeholders can assume the organization values are applied to decision-making and likely
embedded throughout strategy, culture, governance, and daily operations (France et al., 2024; Feri et al.,
2024; Freeman, 1984; Loi, 2016).
Experts identiﬁed several items related to action and proof. Stakeholders care about action – they
observe what a brand says and whether it backs it up over time, demonstrates evidence, and has
transparency in its efforts. This includes whether brands stand by their values when it becomes diﬃcult
or costly. This also aligns with insights from institutional theory as if society expects brands to have
purpose, then there will be businesses engaging in decoupled behaviors using the language of purpose
to appear socially legitimate without undergoing true organizational change (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983).
Several dimensions included items that relate to stakeholders, whether they are customers or other
stakeholder groups. Nurturing stakeholder wellbeing, empowering consumers, and encouraging dialogue
and engagement show that stakeholders help co-create and shape brand purpose. Brands can earn
greater legitimacy in their purpose by considering stakeholder needs, facilitating opportunities to
participate, and allowing consumers to demonstrate their values by interacting with brands that support
their own (Freeman, 1984; Mirzaei et al., 2021; Rudeloff & Michalski, 2024).
Lastly, brand purpose should have tangible effects on the organization and society. Stakeholders can
experience brand purpose which can translate to organizational metrics, such as trust, brand preference,
loyalty, and advocacy and social ones such as employee pride, emotional connection, and meaningful
Page 21/29

identiﬁcation. Brand purpose is multidimensional because it is conceptualized as a moral and business
strategy, relational, and should ultimately drive brand and social values.
7. Implications of the study
The present study offers academic value by clarifying brand purpose as a multidimensional construct
and organizing a fragmented body of literature into eleven coherent dimensions. This contributes to
construct clarity and provides a foundation for future scale development.
From an industry perspective, organizations can use the eleven dimensions as a diagnostic framework
to evaluate whether their brand purpose is deeply embedded, authentic, stakeholder-oriented, and
capable of producing meaningful outcomes.
Brand purpose should be treated as an organization-wide and stakeholder-inclusive practice rather than
the responsibility of the marketing division alone. It must be reﬂected across departments, policies,
employee behaviour, stakeholder relationships, and communication practices.
When consistently embedded and authentically enacted, brand purpose can strengthen brand equity by
enhancing trust, loyalty, differentiation, advocacy, and emotional attachment.
8. Limitations and Future Research
One limitation of the study is that it does not empirically validate the explored dimensions. The reason is
that the explored dimensions are general and not focused on any speciﬁc brand, industry, or sector. The
relative importance of each dimension may vary across industries because of differences in strategic
focus. For example, health care could be more aligned with health-related Sustainable Development
Goals under category three (SDG 3) (Hung-Baesecke et al., 2025), FMCG could focus more on brand
distinctiveness and high-purpose communication (Henning and Nussbaum, 2026), and consumer goods
could use sustainability for differentiation (Lu and Ben Abdelaziz, 2026; Gupta et al., 2013). Future
research should therefore examine the relevance and relative importance of the identiﬁed dimensions
across industries.
Another limitation of the study is that the items for the dimensions were proposed through qualitative
analysis and require validation before use as an established scale. Future studies may therefore test the
proposed items using quantitative scale development procedures, such as exploratory and conﬁrmatory
factor analyses, reliability analyses, convergent and discriminant validity analyses, and measurement
invariance across sectors or consumer groups.
A further limitation is that presenting literature-derived domains during the veriﬁcation round may have
encouraged conﬁrmatory judgments. Future research should therefore examine the framework through
unprompted interviews or independent expert and stakeholder samples before proceeding to
quantitative scale validation.
Page 22/29

Another future research direction could be to clarify the distinction between the beyond-proﬁt concepts
of brand purpose and sustainability stewardship, and to examine the criteria on which the two constructs
differ. Such work would help clarify whether sustainability stewardship should be treated as one speciﬁc
domain of brand purpose or as an overlapping but distinct construct in certain industry contexts.
9. Conclusion
The present study contributes to the emerging literature on brand purpose by addressing the lack of
clarity around its conceptual boundaries and measurable dimensions. By synthesizing prior literature
and expert inputs, the study structures brand purpose as a multidimensional construct and proposes
eleven dimensions that can guide future scale development.
A key insight is that brand purpose is both internally embedded and externally experienced. It is reﬂected
in organizational strategy, culture, operations, policy alignment, and decision-making, while also being
judged by stakeholders through fairness, consistency, emotional connection, dialogue, trust, and
perceived societal value.
Overall, the ﬁndings suggest that authentic purpose can support trust, preference, advocacy, resilience,
identity ﬁt, pride, and meaningful participation when stakeholders perceive that the brand’s values are
enacted consistently and responsibly. The proposed framework therefore provides a theoretical basis for
future empirical validation of brand purpose as a measurable construct.
