Abstract
In the present situation, organizations started to continuously invest in employee recruitment and selection,
career development, compensation and benefits, succession, and retention. But these practices do not always
create a positive employee experience in the organization. Employees nowadays judge whether talent decisions
are clear, fair, useful, and consistently applied in the organization. These shared judgments create a positive
talent climate. The idea became more visible after King (2017) formally introduced the construct in literature.
However, researchers still lack a widely accepted measure for general organizational use. This conceptual paper
addresses that need. It traces talent management from the “war for talent” debate to current concerns about
inclusion, wellbeing, skills, and artificial intelligence. It then defines talent climate and separates it from talent
management practices, organizational culture, engagement, and employer branding.
The paper proposes twelve dimensions for measuring talent climate. They cover strategy, attraction, fairness,
performance, learning, mentoring, careers, empowerment, rewards, wellbeing, belonging, and responsible talent
technology. A 36-item draft instrument is presented for later empirical validation. The paper also explains
scoring, aggregation, reliability, validity, and measurement invariance. Finally, it offers reasoned outlooks for
2030, 2040, and 2047. The 2047 outlook connects organizational talent climate with India’s centenary
development goals. The paper argues that future talent advantage will depend on trusted systems that help
many people grow. It will not depend only on selecting a few high performers.

1. Introduction
Corporate strategy is a central component of strategic
management because it defines the firm's overall
scope,
Talent became as a buzz word and central concern
for any organization across sectors. The skilled
employees influence innovation, service, resilience,
and long-term growth for the organization. This
concern increased after McKinsey described a global
“war for talent” during the late 1990s (Chambers et
al., 1998). In the early talent management practices,
attracting and retaining high performing employees
within the organization was the main focus. Later
literature added pivotal positions, talent pools,
succession, leadership development, and global
mobility (Collings & Mellahi, 2009; Tarique & Schuler,
2010). This progress created a broad field, but it also
created confusion. Researchers disagreed that what
will be considered as talent, and what are all the
practices explain and related to talent management
(Lewis & Heckman, 2006; Dries, 2013).
Organizations often report talent practices through
policy documents. Employees may experience and
understand those policies differently. A development
policy may exist, but access may remain unequal
within the organization. A succession plan may exist,
but its decisions may appear hidden with the decision
makers and job management. This difference
separates talent management from talent climate.
Talent management concerns towards intended
systems and managerial actions. Talent climate
concerns the meanings that employees attach to
those systems (King, 2017; Schneider et al., 2013).
King (2017) formally introduced a strategic climate
for talent. Researchers argued that employees read
talent practices as signals about organizational
priorities. Clear and consistent signals can create a
strong talent climate. The concept gained more
importance because talent systems increasingly
shape employee identity, fairness, and career
opportunity. Employee reactions depend mainly on
interpretations, not only on policy design developed
(Gelens et al., 2013; De Boeck et al., 2018).
Recent changes have made this issue more crucial.
The new normal hybrid work, skill disruption,
demographic change, and artificial intelligence now
affect every stage of talent among employees.
Employers now expect major skill changes before
2030 (World Economic Forum, 2025). These shifts
require a broader measure. A useful scale should
examine attraction, development, contribution, and
continuity. It should also capture fairness, wellbeing,
employee voice, and responsible technology.
This paper addresses three questions.

2. Conceptual Review Approach
This paper uses an integrative conceptual review.
This method connects with established ideas and
builds a new framework from existing literature. It is
suitable when a field contains several related
research streams (Snyder, 2019). The review
examined foundational in the existing literature and
recent work on talent management, organizational
climate, HR system strength, justice, support, and
scale development. It also examined future-work
reports from recognized international and Indian
institutions.
The researcher gave priority to highly cited journal
articles, recent reviews, and original conceptual
papers. The attached scale studies explained the
measurement structure (Knies et al., 2020;
Yogalakshmi & Supriya, 2020). The review was not
designed as a systematic literature review. It did not
estimate publication frequencies or effect sizes. Its
purpose was to concept building, dimension selection,
and measurement guidance. In this paper, the
researcher done analysis with four stages. First, the
history of talent management was arranged by period.
Second, repeated concepts were grouped by meaning.
Third, overlapping dimensions were combined.
Finally, future dimensions were checked against
workforce trends.
3. From Personnel Planning to Talent
Management
3.1 Foundations before the “war for talent”
Talent management did not begin with one article. Its
roots lie in manpower planning, succession, career
management, leadership development, and strategic
human resource management. These fields already
addressed workforce quality and continuity. The
resource-based view gave people a stronger strategic
position. It argued that valuable and difficult to copy
resources can support lasting advantage (Barney,
1991). Human knowledge and capability could meet
these conditions. Strategic HRM then examined
bundles of practices, rather than isolated activities.
Recruitment, development, performance, and
rewards were viewed as connected systems (Huselid,
1995; Wright et al., 2001). These foundations
prepared the field for talent management. However,
the word “talent” gave greater attention to scarcity,
potential, critical roles, and competitive advantage.
3.2 The competitive era: 1997 to 2005
The phrase “war for talent” became influential after
the McKinsey study in 1997. The published article
argued that managerial talent would become scarce
and strategically important (Chambers et al., 1998).
Michaels et al. (2001) expanded this argument. They
encouraged leaders to build a talent mindset, strong
employee value propositions, and differentiated
development. Talent became a senior management
responsibility. This period had a clear message.
Organizations needed better people than competitors.
Recruitment, high-potential identification, leadership
pipelines, and employee retention therefore received
strong attention by the researchers. At the same time
the approach also had its own limitations. It often
treated talent as a small elite group inside the
organization. It could create internal competition,
status divisions, and narrow judgments about
potential (Pfeffer, 2001; Swailes, 2013).
3.3 The definition and strategy era: 2006 to 2012
Lewis and Heckman (2006) provided an important
critique. They found three broad meanings of talent
management. Some studies renamed normal HR
practices. Others focused on talent pools. A third
stream emphasized talent positions and flows. Their
review showed that the field lacked with a clear
theoretical base. It also showed that talent
management needed a system-level perspective. This
critique moved research beyond popular
management language.
Collings and Mellahi (2009) then offered a strategic
definition for talent management. They placed pivotal
positions at the center. Organizations should identify
critical roles, create talent pools, and build a
differentiated HR architecture. Global talent
management also developed during this period.
Research examined multinational staffing, mobility,
global leadership, and coordination across locations
(Tarique & Schuler, 2010; Scullion et al., 2010).
Cappelli (2008) questioned long-range talent
forecasts. He proposed a demand-based approach
that balanced internal development with external
hiring. This view treated talent decisions as uncertain
investments.
3.4 The employee and context era: 2013 to 2017
Research after 2013 examined the meaning of talent
more closely. Gallardo-Gallardo et al. (2013)
separated subjective and objective views. Talent
could mean certain people, or valued qualities that
people possess. Meyers et al. (2013) examined and
identified that talent may be seen as distinctive or
developable. These beliefs influence investment,
selection, and employee opportunity.
Dries (2013) brought psychological questions into
the field. Talent status can affect identity, motivation,
expectations, and career behaviour. This view placed
employee experience beside organizational strategy.
Justice research showed that exclusive talent systems
may create mixed reactions. Selected employees may
feel valued. Others may feel ignored or unfairly

3.5 The responsible and future-ready era: 2018 to
2026
Later research questioned several common
assumptions. Talent identification does not produce
uniformly positive responses. Outcomes depend on
communication, opportunity, fairness, support, and
personal meaning (De Boeck et al., 2018).
Responsible talent management expanded the field’s
moral boundary. It asked whether differentiation
respects dignity, equality, and social responsibility
(Swailes, 2020). Inclusive talent development also
received renewed support (Kaliannan et al., 2023).
Digital tools changed talent decisions. Organizations
began using analytics, digital platforms, and artificial
intelligence across hiring, learning, performance, and
retention. These tools can improve speed and
personalization. However, technology can also
increase opacity, surveillance, and bias. Employees
therefore judge whether digital talent decisions are
understandable and contestable. Trust has become
part of talent effectiveness. The pandemic also
changed employee expectations. Flexibility, wellbeing,
meaningful work, and employability became central
retention concerns. Future-proof talent management
now requires resilience and continuous skill renewal
(Van der Merwe et al., 2023). Current research
therefore views talent management as a contextual
and dynamic system. It connects organizational
capability with employee opportunity, fairness, and
sustainable work (Evans et al., 2025).

4. The Emergence of Talent Climate
4.1 From intended practice to experienced climate
Senior leaders design talent strategies for the
organziation. HR teams convert those strategies into
policies. Line managers deliver many daily talent
practices. Employees then interpret what they
actually experience. These four stages may not align.
A policy may promise career opportunity, while
managers repeatedly favour familiar employees. The
intended system then differs from the experienced
system.
HR attribution research explains this difference.
Employees ask why practices exist and whose
interests they serve. Their answers influence
attitudes and behaviour (Nishii et al., 2008).
Organizational climate research provides the next
step. Climate represents shared meanings attached to
connected workplace experiences (Schneider et al.,
2013). A specific climate forms around a valued
organizational priority. A safety climate forms when
employees see that safety is truly rewarded. A service
climate forms when service receives consistent
support. A talent climate forms when growth and
contribution receive credible support.
4.2 Definition of talent climate
The author defines talent climate as follows: Talent
climate is the shared employee perception that
organizational systems consistently help people join,
grow, contribute, and continue with fairness and
dignity. This definition contains six choices. First,
talent climate is a perception. Second, it becomes a
climate when perceptions show sufficient agreement.
Third, it concerns connected organizational systems.

4.3 What talent climate is not
Talent climate is not the same as talent management
practices. Practices are actions and systems. Climate
is the meaning employees draw from those actions.
Talent climate also differs from organizational
culture. Culture concerns deeper values and
assumptions. Climate concerns visible experiences
and shared interpretations around talent (Schneider
et al., 2013). It differs from employee engagement.
Engagement describes an employee’s energetic
connection with work. Talent climate describes the
surrounding conditions that may support
engagement (Schaufeli et al., 2006). It differs from
employer branding. Employer branding
communicates an employment promise to labour
markets. Talent climate tests whether employees
experience that promise after joining. It also differs
from a talent management scorecard. A scorecard
may track vacancies, learning hours, promotions, and
turnover. Talent climate captures whether employees
experience those activities as meaningful and fair.

5. Theoretical Foundations
5.1 Resource-based view
The resource-based view explains why talent matters
strategically. Knowledge, relationships, and
coordinated capabilities can support advantage when
competitors cannot easily copy them (Barney, 1991;
Wright et al., 2001). Talent climate adds a practical
condition. Human capability creates value when the
organization helps people use and renew it.
Possessing skilled employees alone is not enough.
5.2 HR system strength and attribution
Bowen and Ostroff (2004) argued that strong HR
systems send clear signals. Distinctiveness,
consistency, and consensus reduce uncertainty about
expected behaviour. King (2017) applied this logic to
talent. Talent practices should be visible, coherent,
agreed by decision makers, and relevant to the
context. Otherwise, employees receive mixed
messages. Attribution theory explains employee
interpretation. Employees judge why talent decisions
occur. Fair and developmental reasons can build trust.
Political or cost-saving reasons can weaken it (Nishii
et al., 2008).
5.3 Social exchange and organizational support
Social exchange theory suggests that valued
treatment creates a felt obligation to reciprocate.
Development, support, and recognition can therefore
strengthen employee commitment and contribution.
Perceived organizational support applies this logic to
employment. Employees respond positively when the
organization values their contribution and cares
about their wellbeing (Eisenberger et al., 1986;
Rhoades & Eisenberger, 2002).
5.4 Organizational justice
Talent systems distribute valuable opportunities.
Selection, promotion, rewards, and development
therefore raise justice questions. Employees judge
outcomes, procedures, explanations, and
interpersonal treatment. Fairness is especially
important when organizations identify a small talent
group. Clear criteria and respectful communication
can reduce harmful reactions in the organziation
(Gelens et al., 2013; De Boeck et al., 2018).
5.5 Job demands, resources, and psychological safety
The job demands-resources model explains
sustainable performance. Challenging work can
support growth when employees also receive
resources, autonomy, feedback, and recovery (Bakker
& Demerouti, 2007). Psychological safety supports
learning and employee voice. Employees learn faster
when they can ask questions, admit mistakes, and
offer ideas without fear (Edmondson, 1999).

5.6 Signalling and employer attractiveness
Applicants cannot fully observe and understand an
organization before joining. They use reputation,
recruitment behaviour, social responsibility, and
employee stories as signals about the organization.
These signals shape employer attractiveness. After
joining, employees compare the promises done
before joining with daily experience. A positive talent
climate therefore connects external attraction with
internal credibility.
6. A General Framework for Measuring Talent
Climate
6.1 Framework structure
The proposed framework treats talent climate as a
higher-order construct. It contains twelve related
dimensions. Each dimension captures a distinct part
of the employee’s talent experience. The dimensions
fall across four connected domains. Direction
provides clear signals. Access brings people into
opportunity. Growth enables contribution. Continuity
supports sustainable careers and retention. Talent
intelligence connects all four domains. It helps
organizations anticipate skills and personalize
support. The framework remains transparent, fair,
and human centered and designed for general
organizational use. It avoids sector specific terms.
Researchers may later test its structure across
industries, countries, occupations, and employment
forms.

6.2 Dimension 1: Strategic talent clarity and
consistency
Employees should understand what the organization
means by talent. They should also understand how
talent priorities support organizational goals.
Repeated managerial actions should match stated
priorities. This dimension reflects HR system
strength. Clear, consistent, and agreed signals help
employees form shared meanings (Bowen & Ostroff,
2004; King, 2017).
6.3 Dimension 2: Employer purpose and
attractiveness
People are attracted by more than salary. They also
consider purpose, reputation, meaningful work, job
quality, and social contribution. Existing employees
influence these signals through their stories. This
dimension links attraction with lived credibility. A
strong climate exists when the employment promise
matches daily experience (Beechler & Woodward,
2009; Gallardo-Gallardo et al., 2020).
6.4 Dimension 3: Inclusive opportunity and fairness
Talent opportunity should not depend on hidden
sponsorship or personal similarity. Employees need
fair access to selection, development, projects, and
advancement. Inclusive development broadens the
talent base. Transparent criteria also reduce harmful
reactions to talent differentiation (Gelens et al., 2013;
Kaliannan et al., 2023).
6.5 Dimension 4: Performance enablement and
innovation
A talent climate should help employees perform, not
merely judge them. People need role clarity, useful
feedback, resources, technology, and permission to
improve work. Performance systems should connect

Learning should be available before skills become

6.7 Dimension 6: Mentoring and managerial support
Line managers convert formal policies into personal
experience. They allocate assignments, provide
feedback, recommend employees, and create daily
learning conditions. Mentoring employees provide
guidance, confidence, networks, and career
knowledge. Meta-analytic provided evidence by
connecting mentoring with career benefits (Allen et
al., 2004; Purcell & Hutchinson, 2007).
6.8 Dimension 7: Career mobility and leadership
continuity
Employees need visible pathways for movement.
These pathways may include promotion, lateral roles,
project assignments, international exposure, and
specialist careers. Organizations also need leadership
continuity. Succession should create prepared
choices, rather than secret replacement lists. Mobility
and succession therefore belong within one career
system (Collings & Mellahi, 2009).
6.9 Dimension 8: Voice, autonomy, and psychological
safety
Talented contribution requires room to think and act.
Employees should influence work decisions, question
weak practices, and propose improvements. Voice
remains weak when disagreement carries personal
risk. Psychological safety therefore supports learning,
innovation, and responsible challenge (Edmondson,
1999).
6.10 Dimension 9: Recognition and total rewards
Employees compare their contribution with the
rewards they receive. Pay matters, but so do
recognition, growth, flexibility, benefits, and
meaningful assignments. A positive climate does not
require identical rewards. It requires understandable
principles and credible links between contribution,
opportunity, and recognition (Gelens et al., 2013).
6.11 Dimension 10: Wellbeing and sustainable work
Talent cannot remain productive under continuous
exhaustion. Sustainable work protects physical health,
mental health, recovery, and manageable work
demands. Flexibility should support different life
stages without career penalty. This dimension places
wellbeing inside talent strategy, rather than treating
it as a separate welfare activity.
6.12 Dimension 11: Belonging, trust, and workforce
continuity
Retention begins with belonging and trust.
Employees remain when they feel respected,
connected, and able to imagine a future within the
organization. Continuity also depends on knowledge
transfer and succession. Strong retention does not
mean preventing every employee from exit. It means
sustaining capability through fair relationships and
planned transitions.
6.13 Dimension 12: Talent intelligence and
responsible technology
Talent technology can support skill mapping, internal
mobility, personalized learning, and workforce
planning. Artificial intelligence may improve speed
and pattern recognition. However, employees need
transparency, privacy, review, and human
accountability. Responsible technology therefore
becomes a climate dimension, not only an operational
tool (International Labour Organization, 2023; Evans
et al., 2025).

7. How Talent Climate Should Be Measured
7.1 Measure employee experience, not policy
presence
The primary respondent should be the employee.
Senior leaders can describe intended policy, but
employees report the experienced climate. Both
sources may be compared during validation. Items
should use an organizational referent. “In this
organization” is better than “I receive.” This wording
encourages respondents to judge shared conditions,
not only personal outcomes.
The proposed instrument uses a five-point agreement
scale. Response choices range from strongly disagree
to strongly agree. A five-point scale is simple and
familiar across workforces. The initial instrument
contains 36 items. Each dimension has three items.
Later studies may retain, revise, or remove items
after rigorous testing.
7.2 Establish content and response quality
Scale development should begin with construct
definition and item-domain mapping. Each item
should express one idea. Double questions, technical
terms, and sector-specific language should be
avoided (Hinkin, 1998; DeVellis & Thorpe, 2021). An
expert panel should assess relevance, clarity, and
coverage. The panel should include HR scholars,
psychometric experts, line managers, and employees
from varied sectors.
Cognitive interviews should follow expert review.
Employees should explain how they understand each
item. Their answers can reveal hidden ambiguity,
cultural meaning, and difficult wording. A small pilot
should test completion time, missing responses,
extreme scores, and item variation. Items with little
variation may not distinguish strong and weak
climates.
7.3 Validate the structure with separate samples
Exploratory factor analysis should examine the initial
structure. Parallel analysis should guide factor
retention. Oblique rotation is appropriate because
the dimensions should relate. Confirmatory factor
analysis should use a separate sample. It should
compare correlated factors, a higher-order model,
and plausible alternatives. Model choice should
follow theory and evidence. Ordinal estimators are
preferable for Likert items when distributions are
non-normal. Researchers should report loadings,
factor correlations, residuals, and established fit
indices.
Internal consistency should not rely only on
Cronbach’s alpha. Omega and composite reliability
should also be reported. Very high reliability may
indicate repeated items rather than strong
measurement. Convergent validity should compare
talent climate with perceived organizational support,
justice, and learning climate. Relationships should be
positive, but not so high that constructs become
identical.
Discriminant validity should compare talent climate
with engagement, employer attractiveness, and
organizational culture. Talent climate should remain
empirically distinct from these related constructs.
Criterion validity should examine attraction, internal
mobility, skill growth, innovation, commitment,
wellbeing, and intention to stay. Objective indicators
may include vacancy acceptance, promotion, and
regretted turnover. Predictive validity requires time
separation. Talent climate should be measured before
later employee and organizational outcomes. This
approach reduces claims based only on simultaneous
correlations.

7.4 Test climate as a shared construct
Talent climate begins as an individual perception. It
becomes a team or organizational climate only when
employees show sufficient agreement. Researchers
should report within-group agreement and intraclass
correlations. These tests show whether aggregation is
justified. A simple organizational average is not
enough (Bliese, 2000).
Climate level and climate strength should both be
examined. Climate level is the average score. Climate
strength reflects the amount of agreement among
employees. Two organizations may have the same
average score. One may have strong agreement, while
the other has divided experiences. The second
organization has a weaker and less stable climate.
Researchers should collect responses from enough
teams and organizations for multilevel analysis.
Employees are nested within managers, units,
locations, and organizations.
7.5 Test fairness across groups and contexts
A general scale must work similarly across groups.
Measurement invariance should be tested across
gender, age, career level, employment type, language,
sector, and country. The scale should also be tested
across inclusive and exclusive talent philosophies.
Some items may behave differently when “talent”
means everyone or a selected group. Translation
should use forward translation, independent back-
translation, and cognitive interviews. Literal
translation alone cannot preserve workplace
meaning.
7.6 Score and interpret with care
Each dimension score should equal the mean of its
valid items. Researchers should require at least two
completed items within each three-item dimension.
An overall score should be calculated only when the
higher-order structure receives support. Until then,
the twelve-dimension scores provide the safer
interpretation. No fixed dimension weights should be
imposed before validation. Equal weights are
transparent. Evidence-based weights may later be
developed for specific predictive purposes.
Organizations should compare scores across time,
units, and relevant benchmarks. Small differences
should not be treated as meaningful without
confidence intervals or practical effect sizes. Results
should guide conversation, not employee ranking.
Talent climate data should never identify or punish
individual respondents. Confidentiality supports
honest reporting.
7.7 Combine perceptions with organizational
evidence
Climate scores show how systems are experienced.
Operational measures show what happens. Both
forms of evidence are needed for responsible
diagnosis. Attraction evidence may include applicant
quality, offer acceptance, and new-hire expectations.
Development evidence may include learning access,
skill use, internal movement, and succession
readiness. Retention evidence may include wellbeing,
regretted turnover, career exits, and knowledge
transfer. Technology evidence may include audit
findings, appeal rates, and detected bias. A practice
should not be judged successful only because
participation is high. Participation can be compulsory.
Employee meaning and later outcomes remain
important.

The framework supports several testable
propositions. These propositions connect talent
system design with employee interpretation and
organizational outcomes.
Proposition 1. Clear and consistent talent signals will
increase agreement about the organization’s talent
priorities. This proposition follows HR system
strength theory. Employees form stronger shared
meanings when leaders and managers send aligned
signals (Bowen & Ostroff, 2004; King, 2017).
Proposition 2. Inclusive opportunity and procedural
fairness will strengthen employer attractiveness,
belonging, and trust. Fair access improves both actual
opportunity and the meaning attached to talent
decisions. It may also reduce negative reactions
among employees outside formal talent pools (Gelens
et al., 2013).
Proposition 3. Learning, mentoring, and career
mobility will increase skill renewal and perceived
employability. Development becomes more credible
when learning connects with work, coaching, and
movement. Training without application may
produce weak climate effects.
Proposition 4. Managerial support will strengthen the
relationship between formal talent practices and
experienced talent climate. Line managers deliver
many practices and explain their purpose. Their
behaviour can strengthen or weaken the intended
system (Purcell & Hutchinson, 2007; Knies et al.,
2020).
Proposition 5. Voice, autonomy, and psychological
safety will strengthen innovative contribution.
Employees contribute more fully when they can
question routines and test ideas. Fear can silence
valuable knowledge, even among highly skilled
people (Edmondson, 1999).
Proposition 6. Fair rewards and sustainable work will
reduce harmful turnover through support and trust.
Retention will not depend on rewards alone. It will
depend on whether employees view the full
employment relationship as valuable and sustainable
(Rhoades & Eisenberger, 2002).
Proposition 7. Responsible talent technology will
strengthen trust when employees understand
decisions and retain access to human review. Opaque
technology may weaken climate, even when it
improves speed. Transparency and accountability
should therefore shape employee reactions to talent
analytics.
Proposition 8. Talent climate will predict employee
outcomes beyond the reported presence of talent
practices. This proposition captures the paper’s
central argument. Practice presence matters, but
experienced meaning should explain additional
variation in employee behaviour.
9. Talent Climate in 2030, 2040, and 2047
Future statements should not be presented as certain
facts. They are reasoned outlooks based on current
workforce, technology, demographic, and
sustainability signals.
9.1 Talent climate in 2030: the skills and trust climate
By 2030, skill disruption will shape most talent
decisions. The World Economic Forum expects many
job skills to change before that date. AI, data,
cybersecurity, creativity, resilience, and lifelong
learning will gain importance (World Economic
Forum, 2025). Employees will judge organizations by
their reskilling promise. A positive climate will
provide learning before displacement. It will also
connect new skills with real work and career
movement.
AI will support recruitment, learning, performance,
and workforce planning. Employees will ask clear
questions. What data were used? Can a decision be
explained? Who remains accountable? Internal talent
marketplaces will become more common. Work may
be matched through skills and projects, rather than
only through fixed jobs. Access and visibility will
become important climate signals. Hybrid work will
remain uneven across occupations. Fairness will
require job-sensitive flexibility, not identical
arrangements. Organizations must avoid creating a
hidden status gap between remote and frontline
employees. The leading 2030 talent climate will
therefore combine skills, mobility, trust, and human
review. Development speed will matter, but
perceived fairness will decide employee acceptance.
9.2 Talent climate in 2040: the human-AI talent
ecosystem
By 2040, many organizations may manage work as
changing bundles of tasks and skills. Careers may
move across roles, projects, platforms, and partner
organizations. Artificial intelligence may act as a
work partner, coach, and decision aid. Talent
advantage will depend on productive human-AI
combinations, not simple labour replacement.
Employees may carry verified skill records across
organizations. Learning credentials may become
smaller, continuous, and portable. Internal career
systems will need to recognize learning from several
sources. Longer working lives may increase age
diversity. Talent systems will need repeated
reskilling, phased careers, caregiving support, and
knowledge exchange across generations.
Climate risks will also increase. Continuous data
collection may feel like surveillance. Algorithmic
recommendations may narrow career choices.
Platform work may weaken belonging and mutual
obligation. A positive 2040 climate will protect
agency. Employees should influence how technology
shapes their work. Organizations should provide
appeal, explanation, privacy, and routes for human
judgment. The 2040 climate will therefore resemble
an ecosystem. Talent will move across internal and
external boundaries. Trust, portability, and shared
standards will hold the ecosystem together.

become capable, visible, mobile, and trusted

It creates a larger and fairer base from which

recognize exceptional contribution. The desired 2047

10. An India@100 Talent Climate Agenda
The 2047 outlook needs action before the centenary
year. Organizations, educators, government, and
professional bodies should build shared workforce
foundations.
10.1 Build skill visibility
Organizations should maintain current skill
information and employee aspirations. Skill profiles
should support development, not permanent labels.
Employees should be able to review and correct their
records. National and sector skill standards can
improve movement across education and work.
Portable evidence can help smaller employers
recognize capability.
10.2 Expand opportunity across geography
Talent should not be defined through location alone.
Distributed work, regional centers, apprenticeships,
and local learning partnerships can widen access.
Regional access also supports inclusion and resilience.
It can reduce forced migration while strengthening
local economic capability.
10.3 Strengthen the education-to-work bridge
Organizations should co-create curricula, internships,
apprenticeships, and applied projects. Students need
evidence of capability, not only course completion.
Faculty and managers should also share current
knowledge. Stronger institutional links can shorten
the distance between learning and productive work.
10.4 Support women across career stages
Women’s talent participation requires safe work, fair
progression, flexible careers, care support, and return
pathways. These practices should not carry hidden
promotion penalties. The issue is both social and
strategic. India cannot build full national capability
while major talent groups face avoidable barriers.
10.5 Protect dignity in digital talent systems
Employees should know when AI influences a
decision. They should understand the purpose,
important data, and available review process.
Independent audits should examine bias, privacy,
security, and unintended harm. Efficiency cannot
replace accountability.

11. Discussion
This paper began with a simple problem. Talent
practices exist in many organizations, but employees
do not always experience a supportive talent
environment. The talent climate concept explains this
gap. Employees interpret connected practices,
leadership behaviour, and career outcomes. Their
shared interpretations influence whether talent
strategy becomes credible.
The history of talent management shows a steady
widening of focus. The field moved from scarcity and
elite selection toward context, fairness, inclusion,
wellbeing, and future skills. The proposed twelve
dimensions reflect this development. They retain
attraction, development, and retention. They also add
strategy, contribution, justice, safety, and responsible
technology.
This framework does not assume that every
employee must receive the same outcome. It requires
fair access, clear criteria, respectful treatment, and
credible development. The framework also treats
managers as climate makers. Employees usually
experience talent policy through assignment,
feedback, sponsorship, and everyday decisions.
Manager behaviour should therefore be measured
beside HR policy.
Another contribution is the distinction between
climate level and climate strength. A positive average
can hide unequal experiences. Agreement patterns
can reveal whether opportunity is shared or
concentrated. The future outlook extends the
construct beyond current practices. By 2030, skill
renewal and AI trust should become central. By 2040,
agency within talent ecosystems should matter more.
By 2047, Indian organizations may need to connect
talent climate with national capability. Regional
access, inclusion, dignity, and sustainable skills will
become strategic concerns.
12. Practical Implications
Organizations can use the framework as a diagnostic
map. The twelve dimensions scores can show where
the talent promise loses credibility. Low strategic
clarity suggests mixed leadership messages. Low
fairness suggests unequal access or unexplained
decisions. Low mobility suggests that learning does
not lead to opportunity. Leaders should examine
score gaps across groups and units. A strong overall
score can hide weak experiences among frontline
employees, women, contract workers, or regional
teams. Talent climate should be reviewed with
operational evidence. A low retention score may
reflect wellbeing, careers, rewards, leadership, or
external labour demand. One response will not solve
every cause.
Managers should receive local results and
development support. They should not receive
employee identities. Confidentiality is necessary for
honest climate assessment. Boards can review talent
climate beside business risk. Weak skill renewal, poor
succession, and low trust can limit strategy execution.
These are not only HR concerns.
13. Limitations and Research Agenda
This paper is conceptual. The twelve dimensions and
36 items require empirical testing. Their number and
wording may change after content review and factor
analysis. The framework is intentionally broad. Some
sectors may require additional modules. Healthcare
may need professional credential climate.
Universities may need academic career and research
support. Future research should test whether the
dimensions form one higher-order construct. A
network model may also be possible. Some
dimensions may influence others without one
common factor. Longitudinal research should
examine how talent climate changes after major
events. Leadership change, mergers, technology
adoption, and restructuring may alter employee
interpretations quickly.
Multilevel research should compare intended,
enacted, and experienced systems. Data should come
from executives, HR leaders, managers, and
employees. Researchers should also test whether
inclusive and exclusive systems create different
climate patterns. Fairness may depend on both
principles and personal position (Dries & Kase, 2023).
The future horizons remain scenarios. Technology,
policy, climate, and economic shocks may change
their timing. Repeated reviews should update the
framework.
14. Conclusion
Talent management began as a response to
competitive scarcity. It later developed into a
strategic system for pivotal positions, talent pools,
development, and continuity. The field then learned
an important lesson. A well-designed practice has
limited value when employees interpret it as unclear,
unfair, or inaccessible. Talent climate captures that
interpretation. It asks whether people experience a
consistent environment for joining, growing,
contributing, and continuing with dignity. The
proposed framework offers twelve dimensions and a
clear validation path. It can support research across
organizations, sectors, and countries. The future will
make talent climate more important, skills will
change faster and technology will influence more
decisions. Careers will cross more boundaries. India’s
centenary adds a larger purpose. The strongest
organizations in 2047 should not only find talent,
they should create conditions where diverse people
can become talent.
