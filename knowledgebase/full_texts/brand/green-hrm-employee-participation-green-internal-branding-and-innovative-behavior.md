Abstract
Drawing on the ability-motivation-opportunity (AMO) framework, this study investigates the indirect impact
of green human resource management (GHRM) on employees’ in-role and extra-role green innovative behavior
through green internal branding. It also assesses the conditional impact of employee participation on the rela -
tionship between GHRM and green internal branding. Using a cross-sectional approach, data were collected
from 197 employees of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in China. Partial least squares structural
equation modeling (PLS-SEM) was employed to test the proposed hypotheses. The empirical evidence supports
the mediating role of green internal branding in the relationship between GHRM and both types of green inno -
vative behavior. The study further concludes that the positive relationship between GHRM and green internal
branding intensifies with higher levels of employee participation. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is
the first study to examine the integrated roles of GHRM, employee participation, and green internal branding
in fostering employee green innovative behavior. Limitations and practical implications are elaborated at the
end of the paper.

Introduction
 With increasing climate severity, businesses face burgeoning pressure to integrate pro-environmental initiatives
into their activities, comply with environmental regulations, and minimize adverse effects on nature1,2. Environ-
mental management emphasizes fostering green behaviors such as saving water, decreasing waste, and conserv-
ing energy3. However, a key challenge in environmental management is “greening” the workforce by encouraging
employees to initiate, share, and implement viable ideas aligned with an organization’s environmental strategy4.
When employees generate and implement novel pro-environmental ideas that impact a firm’s internal processes,
products, and services, this is referred to as employee green innovative behavior5. It is well established that such
behavior directly influences a firm’s environmental performance 6. Hence, effectively fostering employee green
innovative behavior has emerged as a critical challenge for firms conducting environmental management.

Given inconsistencies in how green innovative behavior has been evaluated in prior studies 7–11. Piwowar-
Sulej12 categorized it into in-role (mandatory) and extra-role (voluntary) green innovative behavior, specifically
calling for research on both types. Responding to this call and addressing the identified research gap, this study
examines these two distinct categories.
Prior research has identified both individual and organizational factors as playing substantial roles in managing
employee green innovative behavior13. Prominent factors include individual attributes (e.g., green passion), team
leadership (e.g., green transformational leadership), and organizational management, particularly green human
resource management (GHRM)14–16. GHRM is defined as “the systematic alignment of typical human resource
management practices, such as recruitment, training, and motivation, with the organization’s sustainability
goals”17, and it substantially influences a firm’s environmental performance 18. GHRM has emerged as a crucial
factor in environmental management13,19. Extant studies have explored the effectiveness and outcomes of GHRM
at individual and organizational levels in developing countries 2,13. For example, GHRM positively influences
firms’ environmental performance20, green innovation in Palestine 21, and employees’ green skill competencies
in Pakistan22. Furthermore, GHRM facilitates a green atmosphere in which employees contribute to a healthier
and safer environment. Despite this, there is limited research on the potential mechanisms and conditional fac -
tors driving its impact on employee green innovative behavior 23. Accordingly, this study addresses this gap by
examining a potential mechanism linking GHRM to employees’ in-role and extra-role green innovative behavior.
The ability-motivation-opportunity (AMO) theory classifies GHRM practices into three dimensions: green
ability (e.g., training and development), green motivation (e.g., compensation and rewards), and green opportu -
nity (e.g., empowerment and green ways of working)24–26. The regular application of AMO theory in the GHRM
domain emphasizes that these three dimensions, when integrated, improve employee performance 27. However,
these transactional attributes alone may be insufficient to foster the risk-inherent and discretionary nature of green
innovative behaviors28. Therefore, green internal branding emerges as a potential mechanism that transforms
these inputs. It elevates ability into empowered expertise by linking skills to a meaningful mission, converts
extrinsic motivation into internalized commitment by aligning personal values with the organization’s green
identity, and reframes opportunity into a legitimized platform where innovation is expected as a brand-building
act29,30. This sensemaking process ensures that foundational AMO elements are not merely present but are syn -
ergistically integrated and amplified within a coherent, value-driven narrative. This explains how formal HR
practices translate into both expected (in-role) and voluntary, proactive (extra-role) green innovative behaviors
essential for sustained environmental performance. Therefore, based on AMO theory, we posit an indirect effect
of GHRM on both types of green innovative behavior through green internal branding.
Green internal branding refers to the combination of effective communication, directed behavior, and expressed
symbolism used to disseminate a business’s eco-friendly approach and outlook among its internal members31. It
effectively facilitates employee coherence with the firm’s environmental goals and reinforces eco-friendly behav-
ior in daily activities32. Furthermore, it ensures employees understand the eco-friendly aspect of organizational
brand promises and enables the alignment of organizational and brand values with individual values33. However,
fostering green internal branding requires efforts beyond GHRM practices 34. Investments in GHRM practices
(e.g., green training, performance appraisals, compensation) signal an organization’s commitment to sustainabil-
ity35. In response, employees may feel obligated to reciprocate with positive attitudes and behaviors, including
participation in pro-environmental activities. Participation can act as a catalyst for positive social exchange 36.
As core elements of the internal environment, employee participation increases their knowledge, awareness,
attitudes, and skills related to a firm’s pro-environmental activities 37. Employee participation is defined as the
extent to which employees are involved in decision-making processes, problem-solving, and the overall man -
agement of the business38. Employees participation, as conceptualized in this study, refers to the actual involve-
ment of employees in decision-making processes, problem-solving, and organizational management2. In contrast,
the “Opportunity” dimension in AMO theory refers to the organizational arrangements that enable employees
to deploy their abilities and motivation—such as autonomy, decision-making authority, knowledge-sharing

RQ1: Does green internal branding mediate the relationship between GHRM and (a) in-role and (b) extra-role

This study offers several contributions. First, it addresses a research gap by responding to calls to explore
mechanisms linking GHRM and innovative behaviors 2,12,23. Second, it enriches innovation management liter -
ature by offering empirical evidence on the integrated relationships among GHRM, green internal branding,
employee participation, and green innovative behavior. Third, it contributes to AMO theory and social exchange
theory by empirically investigating the mediating role of green internal branding and the moderating role of
employee participation, respectively. Fourth, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, it is the first study to assess
the conditional role of employee participation in the relationship between GHRM and both types of green innova-
tive behavior. Finally, it responds to the call by Piwowar-Sulej42 by offering empirical findings from a developing
country—China.
Theoretical background and hypotheses development
Theoretical foundations
Empirical research has consistently identified the ability-motivation-opportunity (AMO) framework as the
predominant theoretical lens for analyzing the impact of HRM practices on organizational performance 43,44.
According to AMO theory, high-performance work practices comprise three distinct but interrelated HR prac -
tice categories: ability, motivation, and opportunity 43. Ability-enhancing practices include recruitment, selec -
tion, training, and development programs. Motivation-enhancing practices encompass performance appraisal and
financial and non-financial incentives. Opportunity dimension in the AMO framework concerns the conditions
created by management to improve employees performance27. Opportunity-enhancing practices involve employee
involvement, knowledge sharing, and autonomy-enhancing practices39. The theory posits that employee behavior
will align with organizational goals if organizations provide relevant abilities, motivation, and opportunities43,45.
Accordingly, organizations should design effective recruitment and training programs to ensure employees pos -
sess necessary skills, implement appropriate incentive structures to maintain motivation, and provide opportuni-
ties for employee participation in meaningful activities39.
Past studies have concluded that GHRM directly influences in-role and extra-role green innovative behav -
ior2,12. However, GHRM practices such as training, development, and empowerment alone may be insufficient to
foster the discretionary and risk-inherent nature of green innovative behaviors28. Hence, green internal branding
emerges as a potential mechanism that transforms these raw inputs. It elevates ability into empowered expertise,
converts extrinsic motivation into internalized commitment, and reframes opportunity into a legitimized plat -
form for innovation29,30. This sensemaking process ensures that foundational AMO elements are synergistically

While the AMO theory core focus is on the provision of resources and works as a robust ground to understand
the potential mechanism of green internal branding. It is silent how these resources are reciprocated, in other
words, is less specific about quality of relational context. Accordingly, the authors integrate social exchange
theory (SET) to set a clear theoretical boundary: AMO theory explains the mechanism, whereas SET explains
the boundary condition. The proposition that employee participation conditionally influences the GHRM–green
internal branding relationship is grounded in social exchange theory. The effectiveness of GHRM signals is
contingent upon employee reciprocation; such signals translate into strong green internal branding only when
they initiate a socio-emotional exchange, fostering the trust, identification, and mutual obligation necessary for
employees to embrace and enact the organization’s environmental brand identity33. According to SET, norms of
reciprocity govern organizational relationships, where employees respond to favorable organizational actions
with positive attitudes and behaviors41,46. The strength of reciprocity also depends on employee involvement in
organizational processes47. Organizational investment in the form of GHRM does not automatically yield favor-
able outcomes unless employees actively participate in the exchange process. Under GHRM, organizations invest
in employees’ abilities and motivation and offer opportunities to demonstrate skills 43. In return, employees feel
morally obligated to reciprocate with behavior aligned with organizational goals. In the context of sustainable
development, GHRM represents a salient organizational investment signaling commitment to environmental val-
ues. Therefore, we employ social exchange theory to examine the conditional role of employee participation. In
current study, AMO theory accounts for the reosurce-based pathway from GHRM to innovative behavior, while
SET accounts for the moderating role of employee participation, which demonstrates the genuineness of the
organizational investment, thereby intensifying the exchange relationship.
Hypotheses development
Green HRM, green internal branding and green innovative behavior
Green innovative behavior has emerged as a critical source of competitive advantage in dynamic markets 48.
Defined as employee behavior directed at the creation, promotion, and realization of new, environmentally
friendly ideas49, Green innovative behavior is substantially enhanced by green HRM. Green HRM practices—
such as green training, coaching, and performance management—build employee green knowledge and capabili-
ties, leading to favorable green behavioral outcomes50. Furthermore, social exchange theory suggests employees
reciprocate organizational investments in green HRM by exhibiting greater green innovative behavior 51. How-
ever, to drive this behavior consistently, green HRM practices require a coherent and internalized narrative that
transforms them from isolated initiatives into a shared purpose2,12.
Green internal branding serves as this critical meaning-making and sensemaking bridge. It translates green
HRM systems into a unified organizational identity, motivating employees to extend their efforts beyond basic
compliance (in-role behavior) to include proactive, discretionary initiatives (extra-role behavior). Conceptual -
ized as a key motivational mechanism, green internal branding fosters employee green innovative behavior 29.
Grounded in the ability-motivation-opportunity (AMO) framework, this paper posits green internal branding as a
pivotal mediator that explains how green HRM influences both in-role and extra-role green innovative behavior.
The AMO framework posits that performance is a function of an employee’s Ability, Motivation, and Opportu -
nity43. GHRM practices function as organizational resources that provide these three components to facilitate
goal achievement and positive work behaviors 52. Green internal branding is proposed as the mediating mecha -
nism that activates and directs these resources toward sustained green innovation.
Green HRM builds a green talent pool with high potential for eco-innovation 53. Green internal branding
mediates this relationship by providing meaning and direction to this potential. It connects individual green

competence—developed through training and recruitment—to the organization’s core environmental mission.
This fosters a sense of competence and ownership, fueling the confidence to apply green skills innovatively
within and beyond one’s formal job description 54. While green HRM’s extrinsic rewards (e.g., green perfor -
mance pay) can motivate compliant, in-role behavior, they are often insufficient for sustaining the proactive and
risk-taking nature of extra-role innovation. Green internal branding mediates by cultivating intrinsic motivation.
It embeds environmental values into the organizational culture and employee identity 55. When employees inter-
nalize the green brand as part of their self-concept (“I am a member of a genuinely sustainable organization”),
they are motivated to innovate not merely for a reward but to uphold and advance that identity 56. Green internal
branding thus transforms motivation from “I have to” to “I want to.” Green HRM creates structures and forums
for green action (e.g., green teams, suggestion schemes). Green internal branding ensures these opportunities
are perceived as legitimate, prioritized, and safe. By consistently communicating that green innovation is a core
strategic priority, green internal branding signals unwavering managerial support and reduces the perceived risk
associated with proposing novel ideas 57. It creates a “green psychological safety net,” encouraging employees
to fully utilize provided opportunities to engage in both in-role process improvements and extra-role creative
initiatives.
In summary, green HRM provides the foundational AMO resources for green innovation. Green internal brand-
ing acts as the essential mediator that interprets these resources, aligning them with a powerful shared identity. It
transforms ability into confident application, supplements extrinsic motivation with intrinsic drive, and converts
structural opportunity into psychological safety. Consequently, green internal branding is the critical mechanism
through which green HRM most effectively fosters comprehensive green innovative work behavior, encompass-
ing both mandated in-role actions and voluntary extra-role contributions. Hence, the following hypotheses are
proposed.
H1a: Green HRM indirectly significantly influences the in-role green innovative behavior through green inter-
nal branding.
H1b: Green HRM indirectly significantly influences extra-role green innovative behavior through green inter-
nal branding.
The moderating role of employee participation
In current study, the authors proposed the moderating impact of employee participation distinctly based on social
exchange theory, demarcating it from the AMO-based mediation pathway. While AMO explains how GHRM
builds a reservoir of green capability (ability), desire (motivation), and space to act (opportunity), SET explains
the reciprocal intensity of the exchange. In other words, AMO theory explains the necessary conditions for green
behavior, Social Exchange Theory explains the relational quality that determines the strength of the employee’s
response to those conditions. Accordingly, employee participation acts as a signal verifier; it influences whether
employees perceive GHRM as a genuine, trust-based investment (activating strong reciprocity) or a coercive,
top-down mandate (weakening reciprocity).
The social exchange theory posits that relationships develop through interdependent interactions wherein trust,
loyalty, and mutual obligations emerge over time58. Within this framework, GHRM practices represent the orga-
nization’s initial investment in employees—such as through green training and green performance rewards—sig-
naling a commitment to environmental sustainability59,60. Green internal branding, in turn, constitutes the desired
reciprocation in the form of shared beliefs and organizational identification29. Employee participation is proposed
to moderate this exchange by influencing how sincerely and meaningfully employees perceive the organization’s
green investments, thereby either strengthening or weakening the development of a collective green identity.
Employee participation is conceptualized across four ascending levels: informative, consultative, coopera -
tive participation, and employee empowerment, the latter representing the highest degree of involvement 12,40.
As participation increases from mere information-sharing toward empowerment, the impact of GHRM on green
internal branding is expected to intensify. This is because higher levels of participation enrich the social and

psychological resources exchanged, thereby enhancing the credibility, internalization, and motivational force of

Specifically, employee participation functions as a signal verifier. When participation is high—manifested
as consultative dialogue, cooperative decision-making, or delegated authority—GHRM investments (e.g., time,
resources, shared control) are perceived as credible evidence of the organization’s authentic commitment to its
environmental values, which reduces employee skepticism61,62. Conversely, when participation is limited to pas-
sive information-sharing, GHRM practices may be interpreted as top-down directives rather than genuine part -
nership invitations, potentially violating norms of reciprocity and attenuating the exchange relationship.
Furthermore, participation creates psychological pathways for internalization. Cooperative participation and
empowerment foster a sense of ownership among employees 63. When employees have a voice in shaping green
goals and actions, the green brand transforms from an externally imposed label into a personal and collective
project, facilitating deeper internalization 64. In this sense, the “house of participation” operates not merely as
a static typology, but as a dynamic moderating amplifier: each successive level amplifies the positive effect of
GHRM on green internal branding by enhancing the perceived trustworthiness, relevance, and co-created nature
of the green organizational identity65.
In summary, while GHRM extends the “green offer” to employees, the level of employee participation deter -
mines whether this offer is perceived as a genuine invitation to a meaningful partnership—leading to strong,
internalized green branding—or merely as a set of compliance-focused instructions, resulting in weaker brand
internalization. Thus, employee participation is posited as a pivotal boundary condition that enhances or con -
strains the social exchange dynamics through which GHRM fosters a shared green identity. Hence, following
hypothesis is proposed.
H2: With increasing level of employee participation i.e., moving from information to consultative, coopera -
tive, and structural empowerment, there is stronger impact of GHRM on the green internal branding (see Fig. 1).

chological separation and scale variety were employed as a procedural remedies to reduce common method
bias. The authors employed psychologcial separation by structuring distinct sections with clear headings in sur -
vey form to establish psychological separation between predictors and outcome variable. Under scale variety

(methodological heterogeneity) approach, employee participation was assessed as a categorical variable along
with other constructs measured at five-point likert scales. In result, an online questionnaire was developed using
Google Forms, comprising five sections: demographic information, GHRM, green internal branding, employee
participation, and green innovative behavior. The original English questionnaire was translated into Chinese
using a translation-back-translation process66 to ensure accuracy. Two language professors performed the double

Google Forms was selected for its ease of use, cost-effectiveness, and data consolidation features. As Google

platforms, the survey was also distributed via the locally accessible platform Wenjuanxing (commonly used
on WeChat) to ensure a representative sample within China. Consequently, the survey link was disseminated
through professional networks, including WeChat and LinkedIn (the latter also accessed via VPN in China),
to 193 employees working across various departments in manufacturing small and medium-sized enterprises
(SMEs). Participation was entirely voluntary.
To ensure a sufficient sample size for reliable and valid empirical findings, a G*Power analysis 67 was con -
ducted. With two predictors, an effect size of 0.15, and a significance power of 0.80, the analysis indicated a mini-
mum required sample size of 68. Although there are approximately 4.42 million manufacturing SMEs in China
(Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, 2023)—defined as small (20–300 employees) and medium
(300–1,000 employees) firms—no official sampling frame exists for this population. Therefore, a convenience
sampling approach was adopted.
Considering the average response rate of 35.70% (SD = ± 18.70) in social science research68 and the minimum
sample size requirement, the survey link was initially sent to 400 SME employees. To mitigate non-response
bias, the study objectives were clearly explained, anonymity was assured, and participants were informed that
their data would be used solely for research purposes and not shared with any third party. A total of 197 complete
responses from 47 distinct SMEs were received, exceeding the required sample size.
Male participants ( n = 132, 67.01%) outnumbered female participants ( n = 65, 32.99%). The majority held
a bachelor’s degree ( n = 94, 47.72%), followed by a master’s degree ( n = 67, 34.01%) and a diploma ( n = 35,
17.77%). Most participants ( n = 93, 47.21%) had 5–10 years of work experience, followed by those with less
than five years ( n = 65, 32.99%); only 39 participants had more than 10 years of experience. The majority of
respondents (n = 128, 64.97%) were employed in small firms, with 69 respondents from medium-sized firms.
Geographically, most participants’ firms were located in Guangzhou (n = 67 respondents from 16 firms), followed
by Shenzhen ( n = 49 respondents from 11 firms), Shanghai ( n = 38 respondents from 9 firms), Beijing ( n = 24
respondents from 6 firms), and Chengdu (n = 19 respondents from 5 firms).
Measures
In this study, participants rated all continuous variables—GHRM, green internal branding, and in-role and extra-
role green innovative behavior—using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly
agree).
Employees’ GHRM was measured using a five-item scale developed by Dumont69. The items cover job descrip-
tions, training, performance management, compensation, and promotion-related HRM practices. This scale has
also been used by Piwowar-Sulej 12 in medium- and large-sized manufacturing companies in Poland, where it
demonstrated high reliability (α = 0.784). To assess green internal branding, the authors adopted four items from
Srivastava70 and Punjaisri 71. In the Indian higher education context, Srivastava 32 reported high reliability (α =
0.88) for this scale.
Following Wellens’72 hierarchical “House of Participation” model, employee participation was conceptualized
as comprising four distinct, qualitatively different levels: informative, consultative, cooperative, and structural
empowerment. Rather than points on a continuum, these levels are viewed as mutually exclusive categories. An
employee’s prevailing mode of participation in green initiatives typically aligns with one dominant level within

an organizational context. Therefore, this study treated employee participation as a nominal (categorical) variable.
Participants were asked to select the one category that best described their prevailing experience of participation
in green matters. This single-item categorical approach is considered psychometrically appropriate for assessing

Finally, based on extant literature suggesting potential associations between participants’ gender and age, as
well as firm size, and employees’ innovative behavior 2,73, the authors treated these variables as controls in the
analysis.
Analytical approach
The presence of both a moderator (employee participation) and a mediator (green internal branding) renders the
research framework inherently complex. This study further aims to investigate the differential effects on in-role
and extra-role green innovative behavior resulting from GHRM, green internal branding, and employee participa-
tion. Consequently, the research adopts an explanatory approach.
To examine the proposed hypotheses, the authors employed partial least squares structural equation modeling
(PLS-SEM). Sarstedt74 recommend the use of PLS-SEM for research that is explanatory and prediction-oriented.
Furthermore, compared to covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM), PLS-SEM does not require
strict data normality assumptions and is suitable for smaller sample sizes 75. For these reasons, the PLS-SEM
approach was adopted in the current study.
In PLS-SEM, there are two primary options i.e., multigroup analysis and dummy variable moderation to assess
a categorical moderator with four levels. In this study, where sample size is197, we adopted the dummy variable
approach to examine the moderating role of employees participation due to statistical power and theoretical align-
ment with our proposed hypothesis. PLS-SEM MGA typically requires sample size > 100 per group for reliable
results75. In contrast, the dummy variable approach utilizes the full sample for parameter estimation, maximizing
the statistical power to unravel the conditional effect76. As mentioned in above section, the hypothesis H2 exhib-
its a directional, ordinal pattern across participation levels. The dummy variable aproach with interaction terms
offers opportunity to examine whether the GHRM and green internal branding relationship intensifies progres -
sively as participation increases from the informative (reference category) through consultative, cooperative, and
structural empowerment.

Model fit
The model fit indices were examined to assess the overall specification of the proposed model. The standardized
root mean square residual (SRMR) value was 0.078, which is below the recommended threshold of 0.08. The
normed fit index (NFI) was 0.702, which, while below the ideal 0.90, is considered acceptable in conjunction
with the SRMR value for demonstrating a good model fit in this context78,79.
Descriptive analysis
The authors employed Sekaran80 criterion to interpret the mean values of the continuous variables. The descrip-
tive analysis indicated a moderate level of GHRM practices ( M = 3.024) within the sampled Chinese manufac -
turing SMEs. Employees also reported a moderate level of perceived green internal branding ( M = 3.893). In
contrast, employees reported high levels of both in-role (M = 4.113) and extra-role green innovative behavior (M

employees’ perception of green HRM 81,82. Furthermore, Piwowar-Sulej 2 has also employed four-item GHRM
scale excluding goal setting item. Accordingly, this item deletion is theoretically justified. The remaining items
exhibited loadings ranging from 0.636 to 0.891 (see Table 1). As all loadings exceeded the 0.60 cutoff 83, the

Discussion
The current study sought to answer a prominent question in the innovation management field: how can innovative
behavior be promoted in developing countries, and what conditions favor its development? Grounded in AMO
framework and social exchange theory, the authors proposed and tested a research model. All three hypoth -
eses were supported. First, GHRM significantly and indirectly influences the in-role green innovative behavior
through green internal branding (H1a). Second, green internal branding significantly mediates the relationship
between GHRM and extra-role green innovative behavior (H1b). Finally, it was demonstrated that employee
participation strengthens the relationship between GHRM and green internal branding (H2). These empirical
findings are elaborated below.
AMO framework was used to explain the relationship between GHRM and both in-role and extra-role green
innovative behavior via green internal branding. The empirical evidence confirmed hypotheses H1a and H1b.
Extant studies offer results consistent with these findings. For example, Shafaei and Nejati 23, researching full-
time employees in Australia, concluded that GHRM has a significant indirect impact on employee innovative
behavior through job satisfaction. In Vietnam’s hotel industry, Pham and Le89 found that green training and per-
formance management drive mindfulness, which indirectly influences innovative behavior through explicit and
implicit knowledge sharing. Tamasevicius90 provided empirical support for the mediating role of psychological
safety and empowerment in the relationship between inclusive leadership and innovative behavior. Addition -
ally, research in the Chinese context demonstrated that GHRM significantly affects employees’ green innovative
behavior through environmental enthusiasm91. Furthermore, Ye92, in a three-wave study in China, supported the
mediating impact of perceived insider status and external prestige on the relationship between GHRM and the in-
role and extra-role green behavior of new employees. In the high-tech industry, employees’ green organizational
commitment also mediates the GHRM–green innovative behavior relationship 13. In contrast, Ahmad93, using a
subordinate-supervisor dyad approach, concluded that pro-environmental behavior plays a partially mediating
role in the relationship between GHRM and employees’ green creativity.
Second, this study investigated employee participation as a boundary condition affecting the relationship
between GHRM and green internal branding (H2), based on the social exchange theory. The findings demonstrate
a positive interaction effect, whereby employee participation enhances the impact of GHRM on green internal
branding. This suggests that employee participation is a positive contextual factor that should be considered when
designing activities and programs to support internal branding. From the social exchange theory, employee partic-
ipation, in interaction with GHRM, provides employees with additional resources in the form of increased knowl-
edge and creativity, leading to more effective green internal branding. However, no other research on GHRM and
green internal branding has assessed employee participation as a conditional factor, limiting direct comparative
analysis. In support of the present findings, Veerasamy 94 concluded that employee participation moderates the
relationship such that green recruitment strategies, green institutional initiatives, and green performance manage-
ment and appraisal more positively impact employee green behavior in higher education institutions. Similarly,
a study within ISO 14,001 EMS-certified firms claimed a conditional impact of green psychological climate on

Theoretical implications
The present study offers several theoretical contributions to the literature on GHRM, branding, and innova -
tive behavior. First, it addresses research questions raised by Srivastava 32 and Piwowar-Sulej 2 concerning the
potential mechanisms linking GHRM and innovative behavior. It also responds to the call by Pham97 to examine
potential outcomes of GHRM. While previous studies have often focused on direct relationships or mediators like
job satisfaction23, environmental enthusiasm 91, green organizational commitment 13, knowledge sharing 89, and
external prestige and insider status 92, this study extends the research by introducing the perspective of internal
branding—a previously missing link between GHRM and employee behavior32.
Second, this work responds to recommendations by Piwowar-Sulej 12 and Srivastava32 to enhance the under -
standing of employees’ innovative behavior from the perspective of a firm’s internal branding, specifically green
internal branding. Extant studies provide strong evidence that firms can drive employee innovative behavior
through internal branding98 and GHRM2,12. However, this study is the first to investigate the role of green internal
branding as an intervening variable in the relationship between GHRM and innovative behavior. Furthermore, it
contributes to AMO framework by demonstrating the mediating effect of green internal branding on the relation-
ship between GHRM and both in-role and extra-role green innovative behavior. The findings show that GHRM is
substantially related to green internal branding, which in turn fosters employee green innovative behavior.
Third, this study offered a nuanced contribution by employing a dual-theory framework with clear boundar -
ies. It concluded that AMO theory is enough to explain the resource-based mediation pathway (GHRM → green
internal branding → innovative behavior), while social exchange theory confirms that employee participation
intensifies the initial pathway. This demarcation responds to the call for more precise theoretical integration in
HRM studies2,27 exhibiting that AMO explains ‘what’ is available, while SET elaborates ‘how’ the exchange
relationship is valued. Previous studies have claimed that employee participation influences brand knowledge
and emotional connection with branding99. As no other study has examined this conditional role, this work fills a
research gap and contributes to the literature on individual factors that strengthen the impact of GHRM on green
branding. The empirical evidence confirms the positive interaction between employee participation and GHRM,
resulting in improved green internal branding. Finally, by employing AMO framework and SET theory and
empirically supporting the hypotheses in the Chinese context, this study enriches the literature related to these
theoretical frameworks.
Practical implications
This study offers valuable implications for practitioners and policymakers. First, manufacturing businesses face
pressure from numerous stakeholders to adopt modern eco-friendly methods and processes to address increas -
ing environmental pollution and climate change 100. Employee participation in discovering and exploiting eco-
friendly initiatives, approaches, and solutions is highly encouraged to lower emissions, manage waste effectively,
and conserve nature 2,68. The present study revealed a high level of employee participation in both in-role and
extra-role green behaviors. Accordingly, there is a need to monitor these eco-friendly behaviors to ensure their
sustainability. Practitioners and policymakers can design policies to keep such employees motivated and ensure
their continuous involvement in environmental management activities.
Second, the study concludes that GHRM indirectly fosters in-role and extra-role green innovative behaviors
through green internal branding. Therefore, HR managers should recognize that ad-hoc green campaigns alone
are insufficient; GHRM practices are essential foundations for driving a green identity within businesses. Practi-
tioners should understand that GHRM practices alone are not enough; they must be consistently communicated

internally to establish a green identity. For this purpose, managers should receive training to communicate green

work closely with the communications department to embed green identity into the organizational fabric. The

Third, the substantial importance of individuals in contemporary organizational management cannot be
denied102. In businesses, employees are expected to demonstrate professionalism, take responsibility for initiative
and innovation, and exercise the authority vested in their positions. This study suggests that businesses encour -

consultative and cooperative participation. Managers should also enhance employees’ informative participation
by regularly sharing data related to the firm’s environmental footprint and progress toward goals. Incentive poli-
cies such as certification, tax breaks, and public recognition should be designed to foster employee participation.
Policymakers and management should introduce reporting guidelines that mandate the disclosure of employee
participatory practices in GHRM. As frontline employees often identify inefficiencies and opportunities for green
innovations that management may overlook, firms should establish a “green idea system” with rewards or awards
for implemented suggestions, which would subsequently increase participation.
Limitations and future research directions
Despite its valuable theoretical and practical implications, this study is not free from limitations, which open
avenues for future research. First, this study adopted cross-sectional design. However, cross-sectional approach
of mediation can offer biased estimates of longitudinal estimates 103. Future studies are encouraged to adopt
longitudinal designs to establish causal relationships religiously. Second, this study collected data from a single
source and conducted statistical test to diagnose common method bias issue. However, this tests have their own
known limitations and offer conservative assessments 104. Advanced approaches, such as the marker variable
technique or multi-trait multi-method (MTMM) designs, were not feasible given our study constraints. Therefore,
future research is recommended to collect data from multiple sources i.e., supervisors could be asked to mea -
sure employees innovative behavior, while employees report on their perceptions of GHRM practices and green
internal branding. Third, the authors proposed the research framework based on strong theoretical foundations
and included relevant control variables, potential endogeneity still remains a concern. Omitted variables such as
individual personality traits and leadership style may result into endogeneity105,106. On the other hand, PLS-SEM
analysis does not inherently cope with endogeneity107. Accordingly, future studies are suggested to employ instru-
mental variable (IV) techniques or two-stage least squares (2SLS) regression to deal with endogeneity issue 105.
Fourth, employee participations was measured as a single-item categorical variable, classified into four distinct
categories (informative, consultative, cooperative, and structural empowerment). As single-item measures may
not fully assess the multifaced nature of employee involvement and do not offer traditional reliability assessment,
the future studies are recommended to develop a multi-item scales for each category or employ continuous scale
of participation. Fifth, this study focused only on small and medium-sized firms. Employees in smaller firms may
perceive greater involvement in decision-making due to flatter hierarchical structures compared to large firms108.
Future research could focus on large firms for comparison.
Sixth, Chinese culture may have influenced the findings, as national culture substantially drives individual
attitudes and behaviors. To extend the generalizability of the findings, future studies are encouraged to conduct
comparative studies across diverse cultures. Seventh, while this study supported green internal branding as a
mediating mechanism, future research could explore other potential mediators, such as job satisfaction, in the
relationship between green HRM and green innovative behavior23. Finally, this study investigated the conditional
