ABSTRACT
Measuring brand equity is of vital importance to marketing practitioners and scholars. Academics and practi-
tioners have developed a range of tools and metrics for measuring brand equity, but in the fast-paced and
transformational digital era, it may be that current metrics are not sufficient. The authors develop a conceptual
understanding of the brand equity paradigm using practitioner and scholarly views. A practitioner-focused
analysis is given on how companies can best understand and measure brand performance in a digital environ-
ment and take actionable insights, using the share of search, digital brand awareness, and digital brand sentiment
constructs. The authors argue that digital brand equity metrics cannot be based only on social media and current
digital metrics indicators but also must include a human side of the brand and the technology-consumer nuances.
The study proposes a research agenda and highlights important research and policy questions in developing
digital brand equity.
1. The changing brand environment
Consumers increasingly interact with brands in an online setting. The
average consumer spends over seven hours daily interacting with digital
media (including multitasking) and consumes 40 % less traditional
media than a decade ago (Lebow, 2022). This explosion in digital media
has happened across channels, with social media usage growing to 5.07
billion people or over 60 % of the worldwide population (Chaffery,
2024) and is paired with increasing digital media consumption. For
example, the YouTube platform has 2.5 billion active users per month,
with quarterly advertising revenue of $8 billion dollars (Dean, 2024),
and streaming services have recently overtaken cable as the leading
channel for TV consumption (Nielsen, 2022).
But, how has the increased digital interaction outlined above
changed the relationship between the consumer and the brand in the
digital environment? Digital transformation has enabled cheaper and
more powerful miniaturization of computing technologies, equipment,
and new social patterns (Saarikko et al., 2020), consequently leading to
new business practices and innovations. Branding practices and
everyday social reality show that several social and business factors
drive these changes. First, there are fewer physical boundaries to
communicate, place orders, or take follow-up marketing actions. In a
modern consumer omnichannel environment (Bijmolt et al., 2021;
Mirzabeiki & Saghiri, 2020), consumers expect seamless integration
between online and offline brand presence. Consumers feel a sense of
empowerment when they interact with brands at will, and this leads to
improved engagement and purchase intent (Mishra et al., 2022). Sec-
ond, brand power has shifted from the firm to the consumer. The
consumer-to-consumer (C2C) or consumer-to-brand (C2B) relationships
are more relevant than the firm-to-consumer (F2C) relationship. In
addition, employee brand engagement on social media is a relatively
new phenomenon. It makes the relationship between employees and
customers more important, and it is hard for a firm to control in the
digital environment. Third, services can be delivered instantaneously;
tangible goods are easily available due to international trade and
transport deregulation. Fourth, from a consumer behavior perspective,
digital transformation has changed how consumers interact with brands.
For example, in a digital environment, consumers often abandon a
shopping cart, a situation that rarely occurs in an offline setting. Simi-
larly, expectations of students in university classes have changed post-
pandemic, with even students in face-to-face courses requiring a high
level of technological integration.
In this new “digitally transformed” environment, it is paramount that
marketing managers can understand which metrics to deploy to help

often used in academic work. The term digital describes tools, processes,
and widespread use of computerized technologies to communicate,

daily life. Synonyms for “digital” can be the terms electronic, internet,
online, e-commerce, i-commerce, and virtual. These terms are used
interchangeably in common communication, scholarly work, mass
media, social networks, etc.

is followed by a summary of current commercial brand equity indices

how digital branding measures are used in the industry. Drawing on
previous research on brand equity and insights from practice, we

2. Brand equity: A primer
In its simplest form, brand equity can be considered a measure of the
“added value” that a brand gives to a product or service (Farquhar,
1989). For example, consumers will pay more for a branded consumer
product, such as a shampoo or first aid medicine, than a generic private-
label equivalent, even though the generic product has been formulated
with almost identical ingredients. Brand equity can be positive or
negative; for example, if a hotel brand has a large number of poor-
quality hotels, then the subsequent substandard brand image could
reduce the amount that a hotel of that brand could charge for rooms.
2.1. Brand equity from an academic standpoint
The classic works on brand equity define brand equity using multi-
modal measures. Keller (1993) defined consumer-based brand equity as
“the differential effect of brand knowledge on customer response to the
marketing of the brand”. Here, the definition of brand knowledge in-
cludes both brand awareness, consisting of brand recognition and recall,
and brand image, consisting of brand associations, which can include
attributes, benefits, and attitudes. Consider the example of a premium
brand of connected exercise watch, such as the “Tag Heuer Connected”
watch. It will have product attributes, such as accuracy, exercise fea-
tures, and robustness; non-product attributes, such as price, appearance,
and usage scenarios; benefits, such as making workouts easier and
improving self-image and self-efficacy; and a range of consumer brand
associations, such as luxury, quality, style, and prestige.
Aaker (1996) produced a measure of brand equity that overlaps some
of the concepts described by Keller and includes measures for loyalty,
perceived quality/leadership, brand association/differentiation, brand
awareness, and market behavior. A key to the Aaker framework is that
implementation instructions were given along with the framework,
including examples of potential survey questions to measure the
different measures and sub-measures. For instance, for the loyalty
measure and the associated sub-measure of “price premium”, there is a
question asking how much less or more a consumer would pay for a
brand over a rival brand. While the overall brand-equity construct is
complex, with five measures, eleven sub-measures, and 31 questions
(measured on different scales), core subsets of constructs have been
psychometrically validated on consumers across cultures (Buil et al.,
2008; Yoo & Donthu, 2001) and have been used for predicting
willingness-to-pay for brands (Netemeyer et al., 2004). There has been a
multitude of other papers that focus on scale development for consumer-
based brand equity, often adding specific constructs for different sce-
narios, for example, emotional loyalty (Allaway et al., 2011), brand
personality (Pappu et al., 2005), brand reputation and connection
(Veloutsou et al., 2020), and social influence (Alvarado-Karste &
Guzm´an, 2020).
2.2. Conceptualizations of brand equity
The definitions of brand equity in scholarly work have a consumer or
organizational focus (e.g., Keller, 1993; Aaker, 1996), or a marketing
and financial approach (Davcik et al., 2015). There are other concep-
tualizations of brand equity, including sales-based brand equity (SBBE),
finance-based brand equity (FBBE), and employee-based brand equity
(EBBE). Sales-based brand equity (SBBE) is described in Keller (1998)
and can be considered a measure of sales or market share due to the
brand. It can be operationalized as the brand intercept on a market share

A fourth type of brand equity is employee-based brand equity
(EBBE), which is based on the premise that a brand with more
committed employees is more likely to be successful (e.g., Piehler et al.,
2016) and is defined as the effect that brand knowledge has on an em-
ployee’s evaluation on their work environment (King & Grace, 2009).
EBBE covers a range of employee metrics, such as brand commitment,
brand citizenship behavior, employee satisfaction, employee intention
to stay, positive employee word of mouth (WOM), attitude toward the
job, and employee involvement and is positively related to firm per-
formance (Poulis & Wisker, 2016). Research by McKinsey (Dewar, 2018)
on over 1000 organizations found that companies with top-quartile
employee cultures had stock market returns of 200 % more than com-
panies in the bottom quartile. From a practical perspective, EBBE may be
particularly important in high technology and research-focused com-
panies, where human capital drives new products and growth.

and SBBE, though SBBE lacks a few of the consumer perception and

macro, finance-focused metric than CBBE, though, in FBBE, brand eq-
uity can be further decomposed into demand-enhancing performance
outcomes and reduction of marketing cost components for different
factors in the marketing mix (Simon & Sullivan, 1993; Sharma et al.,
2016).

3.2. A thematic content analysis of practitioner brand equity
As noted in the previous section, the relationship between re-
searchers and practitioners working in brand equity is symbiotic, with
major marketing research firms incorporating academic frameworks and
measurement methods and academic researchers utilizing commercially
available brand indices and metrics to further advance academic
research. However, there is still scope for further understanding of how
brand equity is measured and conceptualized by practitioners across a
broad range of companies and industries. To do this, a thematic content
analysis was performed on Google News articles mentioning concepts of
brand equity and value. Content analysis is typically used to order,
categorize, and gain insight from unstructured data (e.g., Harwood &
Gary, 2003), and is increasingly becoming automated using data ana-
lytic techniques (e.g., Hopkins et al., 2010). We utilize text analysis and
cluster analysis to help understand the different areas in which practi-
tioners encounter and manage brand equity issues.
3.2.1. Methodology
Though, in academic research, the terms “brand value” and “brand
equity” have subtly different meanings, the initial analysis found little
differentiation between mentions of “brand equity” and “brand value” in
practitioner articles. Thus, articles including the phrases “brand equity”
and “brand value” were selected using a Google News API search. Given
potential business model changes and accelerated digital transformation
due to the Covid-19 pandemic (Soto-Acosta, 2024), it may be that the
pre-2020 industry conceptualization of brand equity is already out of
date. Thus, a starting date of 2020 was chosen to balance immediacy and
depth of content, and the search was run for the period of 2020 to 2023.
After the automated search, the data were cleaned and articles with no
substantive link to brand equity were removed (e.g., the mention of
brand equity was on a link to another article). The resulting cleaned
dataset contained 1691 news articles.
Cluster analysis was utilized to split the news articles into subgroups
of homogeneous topics or themes. The articles were clustered using the
article text. Many articles contained a small section discussing brand
equity in the context of a “non-brand equity” article. Thus, a window
approach was used, and text was selected from windows of 10 words on
each side of a mention of “brand”. Punctuation and low information stop
words were removed, and the text data were converted into text ×
feature data, with both words and bigrams (combinations of two words)
taken as features. Including bigrams in addition to words allows for
more complex context and phrases (e.g., Tan et al., 2002), such as
“brand value” and “social media” and can include improved clustering
performance (Koster & Seutter, 2003). The count data for each feature
were multiplied by the feature’s inverse document frequency (idf) to
give a tf-idf formulation, which gives a measure of how common a term
is relative to the overall document corpus and can be thought of as a
measure of topic relevancy (e.g., Stephen, 2004). Several methods of
grouping the data were evaluated, including model-based partitioning
clustering, hierarchical clustering with cutoffs,3 and Latent Dirichlet
Analysis (LDA). Based on initial results, k-medoids clustering was
applied (Harikumar & Surya, 2015). This method is known to be resil-
ient to outliers. Stability analysis using holdout validation and the
adjusted Rand index (Hubert & Arabie, 1985) was used to select an
eight-topic solution. This solution displayed strong coherence of topics
and good face validity.

3.2.2. Content analysis themes
A thematic content analysis was performed by examining the cluster
summaries and the articles in the clusters. The content analysis can be
classed as semi-automated, as the initial analysis was algorithmic, but
each cluster was examined for themes. A thematic content analysis can
include both inductive and deductive elements (Kuckartz & R¨adiker,
2023, pp. 76-77). In this case, inductive content analysis was used to
examine patterns and insights from the data, but there was some
deductive comparison with theory and domain knowledge from the
academic brand equity work described in Section 2. A summary of the
results is given below.
The results show some co-occurrence with academic topic clusters,
but also clusters focused on practitioner interests. In terms of pure brand
equity clusters, there is a cluster (C5) focused on CBBE. It shows high
concordance with topics from the CBBE papers given in Table 1, with
articles discussing brand awareness, loyalty, perceptions, and experi-
ences, but with a focus on building up these aspects. There is a cluster on
brand value (C2), focusing on financial brand value and brand strength
metrics. This indicates a focus on brand “value” and is related to the
FBBE work focused on the valuation and measurement of financial
brand value. A cluster (C8) also aligns with the brand value regarding
the commercial brand metrics described in Table 2, which again shows
concordance with the FBBE metrics that utilize these measures.
There are several branding-focused clusters. There is a cluster (C4)
focusing on personal branding topics, particularly with respect to
entrepreneurship and lifestyle branding, and a cluster (C1) dealing with
brand identity, which overlaps with the CBBE work that addresses brand
identity (e.g., Alvarado-Karste & Guzm´an, 2020), but with stories
focusing on practical aspects of brand design, such as logos, and a cluster
(C3) focusing on media and digital advertising branding, with articles
discussing brand metrics (e.g., top/hottest videos) in the digital sphere.
The remaining three clusters focus on topics where brand equity and
value are important but are not the overall focus of the articles. There
are several clusters on specific product areas where branding is impor-
tant, including luxury brands (C7), and a “leisure” branding cluster (C6),
with a focus on non-durable “hedonic” categories such as food and
beverages, sport, and travel. Finally, a marketing strategy cluster (C9)
focuses on specific campaigns for major brands, including Burger King,
Kraft, Heinz, Diego, and Coca-Cola.
Overall, there is a strong concordance between academic and prac-
titioner views of brand equity. Practitioners show interest in CCBE as-
pects of brand equity, such as brand identity, awareness, loyalty,
perceptions, and experiences. There is also a strong focus among prac-
titioners on valuing brands. This relates strongly to global brand indices
such as Interbrand and academic work on FBBE. However, when dis-
cussing brand equity, practitioners are often focused on broader ideas of
branding and practical considerations, such as specific brand campaigns
and logo/branding design. Branding is particularly important in “he-
donic” categories, such as luxury brands, where brand design and aes-
thetics are important (e.g., Berthon et al., 2009). In such categories,
often underserved by academic research, there is scope for further work
in understanding how experiential and aesthetic qualities of brands
affect brand equity. From a practice standpoint, careful use of existing
brand metrics, such as those given in Table 2, combined with consumer
feedback via focus groups, surveys, and the digital marketing metrics
described subsequently in this article, should help practitioners under-
stand how to best improve brand equity.
4. Branding in digital marketing practice
Sections 2 and 3 outlined a “traditionalist” branding perspective,
looking at brand equity from both academic and practitioner angles. The
analysis in Section 3 showed a high concordance between the practi-
tioner and academic views of brand equity but with a strong focus on
CBBE and digital brand metrics (C3) in the practice realm. This analysis
focuses on the increasing importance of digital branding and digitization
and how this affects brand equity. Accordingly, in this section, we look
into the digital brand environment in more depth and look at how
practitioners perceive brands and branding in a digital environment.
Digital marketing is a fast-growing industry, with a global market
size of over $360 million in 2023, which is expected to rise at around 13
% a year from 2024 to 2032 (EMR, 2024). In addition, most traditional
marketing functions, such as sales, branding, and advertising, have gone
through some degree of digital transformation. Marketing practitioners
use various metrics to assess a brand’s digital brand value and optimize
digital marketing strategies. Practitioners must understand diverse
metrics that may impact brand reputation, consumer preferences, and
sales.

Brand sentiment shows consumers’ overall attitude, experience, and
perception toward a brand. The tone of brand sentiment can be positive,
negative, or neutral, and it can be affected by various factors, such as the
consumer’s experience with the brand, advertising campaign, word of
mouth, public relations, new product development, etc. Consumers may
consider a brand trustworthy, appealing, and reliable if brand sentiment
is positive. On the contrary, if brand sentiment is negative, consumers
may perceive the brand as untrustworthy, risky, and unappealing. The
complexity of brand sentiment analysis is in its descriptive nature and
the potential fallacy of attributing quantitative explanations to quali-
tative data. However, by frequently tracking digital brand sentiment,
marketers can obtain a more immediate metric compared to traditional
measures, allowing them to observe how specific events or campaigns
impact brand attitude. In addition, with AI being increasingly used to
create and manage brands (Gonzalez, 2024), it is hard to foresee today
how consumers will perceive the relative value and sentiment between
brands if brand x is dominantly generated and managed by AI tools in
comparison to brand y which is not AI reliant.

5. Digital brand equity – Towards a new measurement-based
approach
The brand equity metrics described in Section 2 are primarily
derived from consumer surveys (CBBE and conjoint-based SBBE), sales
and market share information (SBBE), and aggregate-level financial
information (FBBE). Running surveys can be expensive and unless sur-
veys are run daily, it may be difficult to see rapidly, emerging trends,
such as a “brand meltdown” due to service failure or bad publicity.
Similarly, market share and financial information provide useful mea-
sures of brand positioning, but these measures are often “lagged” and
may not provide up-to-date information. The concepts of digital brand
measurement described in Section 4 could be used to give accurate,
easily updateable measures of digital brand equity. The focus of this
section is on the development of the concept and associated measures of
digital brand equity.
5.1. Utilizing digital brand information
Could freely available digital brand information be utilized to help
give easily gathered, cheap, and up-to-date measures of brand equity? In
addition to the search-based metrics described in Section 4, social media
monitoring tools, such as Hootsuite and Salesforce Social Studio, have
long been used to monitor brand mentions across social platforms and
for reputation management and customer service, as well as for moni-
toring brand trends and performance. Social media, open online forums,
and unstructured digital engagement created the phenomenon of user-
generated content (UGC), where consumers discuss brands and give
brand feedback. UGC has shifted the brand relationship, recognition,
and power from firm-to-consumer (F2C) to user-to-user (U2U) brand
engagement, which is not necessarily a consumer-to-consumer (C2C)
brand relationship and engagement (Davcik et al., 2022).
The analysis of electronic word of mouth (eWOM) and UGC effects
on brand performance and a range of marketing activities has been a
mainstay of academic marketing research over the last 20 years (Donthu
et al., 2021). Scholarly work has analyzed a range of different platforms,
including online review (e.g., Ho-Dac et al., 2013), social media (e.g.,
Davcik et al., 2022), online community (e.g., Thompson & Sihna, 2008),
and blogging/microblogging (e.g., Culotta & Cutler, 2016) platforms.
These platforms could provide a rich source of brand value information.
In fact, Keller (2016), in a reflective review of progress in CBBE research,
noted that brands and branding have changed in the digital world and
stated the need for rigorous research to help understand brand value in
this new digital environment.
5.2. Digital brand equity measurement
Academic researchers have begun to look at online data as a poten-
tial source of information for tracking brands and monitoring brand
equity components such as consumer brand sentiment and brand word
of mouth, including Google Trends share of search-based measures
(France et al., 2021) and measures of Twitter sentiment polarity for
brand reputation and value (Rust et al., 2021). In fact, advances in
textual analysis, including methods for extracting sentiment emotions
(e.g., Hartmann et al., 2023) and topic information from the text (e.g.,
Tirunillai & Tellis, 2014), have widely been applied in marketing
research and could provide an insightful and cost-effective way of
monitoring brand equity.
As noted in Sections 2 to 4, there is a strong concordance between
practitioner and academic work in brand equity, with consistency in
terminology and with practitioners utilizing foundational academic
measurement approaches and academics using practitioner indices, such
as Interbrand, to ground academic models. Accordingly, in this con-
ceptual work, we utilize the practitioner-focused share of search, brand
awareness, and brand sentiment metrics, described in section 4 and
summarized in Table 3, and create propositions to relate these metrics to

5.2.1. Digital brand equity measurement
We argue that the consumer search for a brand could be a significant
factor that influences its brand value. Customer information search
behavior is directly related to individual customer purchases through
pre-purchase search, so pre-purchase information search should lead to
higher sales and market share (Keller, 1998), but for general searches, it
is strongly related to brand involvement and attachment (Bloch et al.,
1986), which is an indicator of purchases (Park et al., 2010), as well as of
determinants of brand equity, such as recommendations and positive
word of mouth (Japutra et al., 2014). In addition, empirical evidence has
shown that brand searches are positively related to consumer purchases
and are a particularly strong indicator of purchases for consumer du-
rables (Jun & Park, 2016). In summary, consumer search leads directly
to consumer purchases and also indirectly to consumer purchases
through increased consumer attachment and word of mouth, leading to
Proposition 1.
Proposition 1. Proactive customer behavior in seeking information
about a brand leads to higher visibility and word of mouth for a brand,
thereby a higher share of search shows a higher customer interest in a
brand and improved sales and market share, which will lead to higher
brand equity.
Brand awareness is a necessary condition for consumer purchases of
a product, and consumers cannot choose a brand they are unaware of
and that is not in their consideration set. The link from brand awareness
to purchase is mediated by a range of other CBBE factors, including
perceived quality, loyalty, and brand associations (Veloutsou et al.,
2020), and brand awareness shows a positive correlation with overall
brand equity (Huang & Sarig¨ollü, 2012). In addition, high-awareness
brands have several advantages over low-awareness brands. Con-
sumers utilize brand awareness as a choice heuristic (Hoyer & Brown,
1990; Macdonald & Sharp, 2000), choosing brands they know and
associating brands they have heard of with higher quality. This can lead
to a positive feedback loop for brands with high awareness.
Proposition 2. Awareness of a brand is correlated with overall mea-
sures of brand equity and increased brand awareness should result in
increased purchase probability and improved sales, which will lead to
higher brand equity.
Consumer sentiment towards brands can be expressed in person or
through a wide range of digital channels, including online reviews, blog
posts, comments on social media, and other user-generated content.
What brand managers dub “consumer sentiment” or “brand sentiment”
comes under the banner of WOM (word of mouth) research in academic
marketing. Marketing has a rich stream of research on WOM and con-
sumer sentiment from before the internet era, with foundational work
showing that satisfied customers lead to positive sentiment (Swan &
Oliver, 1989) and poor customer experiences and dissatisfied customers
lead to negative sentiment (Richins, 1983). Dissatisfied customers can
be particularly problematic for companies, as dissatisfied consumers are
more likely to provide WOM than satisfied consumers (Anderson, 1998),
and negative WOM sentiment can reduce purchase probabilities (East
et al., 2008), though negative sentiment can be mitigated through strong
service recovery efforts (Maxham III, 2001). Brand attitude and image is
a function of the ability to generate positive WOM sentiment (Day,
1971), and positive WOM sentiment will lead to more favorable con-
sumer product perceptions (Bone, 1995), which can lead to improved
purchase probabilities (East et al., 2008).
WOM research defines sentiment in terms of both volume and
valence, while practitioners will often report aggregate measures of
sentiment valence on a negative to positive scale. There is evidence for a
positive relationship between WOM valence (from negative to positive)
and purchases with WOM gathered from digital platforms (e.g.,
Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006), and this relationship can be moderated by a
range of factors, including social dynamics (Moe & Trusov, 2011),
product category (Yang et al., 2012), reviewer anonymity (Forman
et al., 2008), brand commitment (Chang & Wu, 2014), and the volume
of online reviews (Dellarocas et al., 2007).
Proposition 3. Consumer sentiment affects the reputation and image
of a brand, thereby a positive tone of brand sentiment contributes to
improved brand image, and increased sales and brand equity, while
negative consumer sentiment contributes to decreased brand image, and
decreased sales and brand equity.
The three propositions listed above describe brand equity as a
function of the share of search, brand awareness, and brand sentiment.

6.1. Social media and brand equity
How do practitioners and scholars move forward from the traditional
understanding of brand equity toward digital brand equity? Traditional
brand equity deals with (i) the tangible measurement domain through
financial reports and goodwill excess of the brand performance, and (ii)
the intangible measurement domain through surveys and experiments
where consumers expressed their subjective views and feelings about the
brand performance. Digital brand equity is focused on intangibles and,
almost always, anonymized likes, shares, and sentiment comments that
are hard to link to specific consumer or market contexts. Today, mar-
keting scholars and practitioners do not have a simple and unifying
measurement approach to describe digital brand equity. Instead, they
use the basket of indicators and measurement approaches to understand
the brand value and market performance of brand actions. Marketers
will likely use the current generic approach in the coming years, using
several anonymized consumer behavior indices with low quantifiable
outputs.
Another approach is to focus solely on specific social media plat-
forms, such as TikTok, LinkedIn, or Instagram.5 However, each social
media platform has its own market, consumer base, and indices that are
not necessarily comparable with other social media or across countries.
A key aspect of social media is the complex interplay between con-
sumers, content creators, and brands. On one hand, consumers engage
with content generated by creators who often operate independently
from brands, which allows flexible and organic consumer-creator in-
terconnections. On the other hand, brands try to reach consumers
through traditional advertising and paid sponsorships with creators,
imposing certain limits on brand-consumer relationships. This duality
creates both opportunities and challenges. While consumers gain more
diverse content and exposure to brands on their own terms, brands must
cede some control and risk volatile reactions as consumers and creators
are not bound to the brand. Understanding this nuanced duality is
crucial for brands seeking to effectively leverage social media while
maintaining their value, performance, and reputation.
The most important metrics on TikTok are video views, profile views,
likes, comment shares, and unique viewers. Video views indicate how
often a video has been seen, showing the reach and visibility of the
content creators’ followers. Profile views show how many people have
visited the content creator’s profile (account). Likes measure the
viewers’ likeability of video content. Comment shares reflect active
engagement between viewers and the content. The unique viewers show
the actual number of individuals seeing the videos. For brands, TikTok
created TikTok For Business and provides performance metrics, audi-
ence insights, and various creative tools to connect with content creators
and TikTok users.
Key analytical metrics on Instagram are likes, comments, share of
posts, and followers (Sadowska, 2024). Instagram does not provide
research-based analytics for individual content creators but shows the
results of aggregated interactions (likes, comments, tags, etc.). For
brands, Instagram Insights provides data on engagement, interactions,
reach, and number of impressions. These data describe in detail the
audience, number of profiles visited, and money spent on specific
advertising campaigns.
LinkedIn uses post impressions, followers, profile viewers, and
search appearances as the key performance metrics (LinkedIn, 2024).
Post impressions show how many times content has been displayed on
screen. Followers show the number of people that follow a unique ac-
count, including connections and non-connection followers. Profile
viewers indicate how many people have viewed the account, and search
appearances demonstrate the visibility of a profile in search results.
LinkedIn uses Campaign Manager for brands, which sets advertising
objectives such as awareness, consideration, and conversion.
The sheer range of potential metrics outlined above creates both
opportunities and challenges, due to the heterogeneity and complexity
of different types of data. Modern “big data” are often characterized in
terms of the 4 Vs of volume, variety, veracity, and velocity (e.g., Abbasi
et al., 2016). In the case of digital marketing data, huge volumes of
social data arriving at high velocity are often distilled into summary
metrics, for example, for Twitter, the number of tweets and retweets,
though raw textual data can be collected through APIs. Data include
numeric data, text data, and even video data from sites such as YouTube
and TikTok. These data may vary from key metrics and insights that give
insight into consumer sentiment, to large, noisy data that may be diffi-
cult to analyze without bespoke tools and advanced analytics
knowledge.
The key metrics on each social media platform may provide
invaluable data for users, content creators, and brands to set marketing
goals, benchmark performance, and optimize their strategies. There are
stark differences in performance and value creation between platforms –
TikTok emphasizes video engagement, Instagram focuses on branded
content and shoppable posts, and LinkedIn highlights the content
needed to build professional credibility. Marketers can tailor content
and leverage metrics to maximize impact and resonate with their target
audiences’ values by understanding each platform’s strengths and per-
formance outcomes. For instance, one million followers on TikTok may
have a different meaning and influencer marketing power than ten
thousand followers on LinkedIn.
There is no doubt that with a better conceptual understanding of the
brand equity paradigm in the digital environment and the development
of new marketing metrics and big data analysis techniques, marketers
will be able to create better brand equity models and develop a unifying
approach to the measurement and conceptualization of the digital brand
equity phenomenon. For instance, Google Analytics 4 (GA4) tool pro-
vides a better firm-centric, tailored approach and more accurate data on
ad spending and online users. GA4 provides improved privacy-centric
data performance, more precise advertising investment measurement,
and optimized firm media investment with Google Ads, Display & Video
360, and Campaign Manager 360 (Arnold, 2023).
Lastly, while we provide guidance on how to use current social media
metrics, these are subject to change as social media continues to evolve.
Over the last decade, social media platforms have drastically changed,
and while some have adapted, others have either declined or failed
completely. More importantly, some platforms have changed the way
that customers can interact with the brand. For instance, YouTube
removed the visible count of downvotes to other viewers, impacting the
way that customers interacted. X (formerly Twitter) went a step further
by eliminating the downvote feature from the site, removing a valuable
metric for marketers. This highlights the importance for practitioners
and scholars to adapt and adjust to emerging trends and new social
media platforms. While the current social media platforms seem robust,
past leaders have collapsed and were replaced, and it is likely that the
current leaders will also get replaced over time to leave a place for new
forms of communication. Despite the evolving nature of brand-
consumer interactions, these interactions continue, and our proposed

6.2. Emerging AI technologies and digital brand equity
Although we have previously witnessed the rise and fall of various
social media platforms, the advancement of AI since 2022 has been so
swift that predicting its capabilities in the next five years, let alone
beyond that, is challenging. The rapid rise of AI technologies (generative
AI tools, industrializing machine learning solutions, immersive reality
technologies, quantum technologies, etc.) could directly affect con-
sumer–brand relationships and the shift from a firm–consumer brand
narrative to a technology–consumer brand narrative, which may impact
consumers perception on brand value, i.e., digital brand equity. This
notion is important because AI technologies automate production and
business processes, have continuous learning ability, marketing mech-
anization, and create the intelligence for data-focused analytics and
decision-making (Plangger et al., 2022). According to Goldman Sachs
Research (Goldman Sachs, 2023a,b), generative AI will result in
sweeping changes to the global economy, could increase the global GDP
by 7 % (approximately $7 trillion), and will affect 300 million full-time
jobs in the next ten years. As the digital environment transforms rapidly,
over 90 % of companies are undertaking digital and AI-driven business
changes (Lamarre et al., 2023). Fundamental to this transformation are
people, because new human values, technical expertise, marketing ca-
pabilities, and operational processes are imperative. Simultaneously,
consumer mindsets evolve regarding brand expectations and desired
value propositions. Scholars are exploring the factors that affect
perceived customer value in AI-mediated choice settings (Davenport
et al., 2020; Plangger et al., 2022). Thus, digital transformation requires
synchronized change on both the company and consumer sides. Brands
must cultivate digital fluency throughout their organizations to deliver
renewed values that align with consumers’ digital-first mentalities and
sets of values.
The shift to the technology–consumer brand narrative will have two
profound consequences for firms and consumers. First, all competitors
will offer service and consumer support solutions based on the most
efficient technology rather than human interaction and interference or,
at the very least, some kind of hybrid system that leverages both AI and
human interaction. The cornerstone of the marketing strategy will be to
embody human–machine interaction as a core feature and strengthen
brand value (Kumar et al., 2024). In some way, we have witnessed this
shift for decades because firms have been automating and standardizing
their B2B and B2C processes in an eternal quest for efficiency and cost
control. However, with the increased availability of AI tools, these
trends will be exacerbated, and the role of human interaction and
interference – from an operational point of view – will be much less
important because AI tools will standardize business processes much
more efficiently and lower the possibility of human error. AI technology
has become a core brand value, and future development and marketing
applications will show how much humans depend on it. We cannot as-
sume today how future consumers will value these processes – will they
be inherently price sensitive and highly value cheap and efficient
products, or will they be inherently humanly engaged and prefer high
quality and personalized interactions that cannot be standardized, and
therefore be prepared to pay a premium price for a “humanized” brand?
Companies that have introduced AI into previously human-centered,
creative domains, have faced backlash from consumers. For example,
Dungeons and Dragons books are known for their human-created
artwork, and when a new book used AI to improve the artwork, it
resulted in a backlash from consumers, who boycotted the book and
posted negative reviews online (Wilde, 2023). Yet, if AI is implemented
correctly, the resulting profound change in technology, consumer
behavior, and brand performance could be an excellent opportunity for
marketers to provide higher brand values based on algorithmic effi-
ciency and human, personalized interaction. AI is substituting many
analytical and thinking tasks, and human workers should improve their
empathetic and emotional working performance (Huang et al., 2019).
This implies that digital brand equity will not depend only on technol-
ogy, digital metrics, and efficient AI algorithms, but premium value and
brand performance will also depend on human engagement and the
quality of social interaction.
The second consequence of shifting the technology-consumer
narrative will be decision-making “filtered” through AI algorithms
that will “serve” the solution for a consumer and create brand value. This
will create algorithmic, branding, ethical, and legal challenges for
marketers. Evaluating the impact of AI on brand value will quickly
become a critical component for companies, especially since not all AI
systems are created equal. Their performance, including both successes
and failures, will likely be perceived as part of the brand, thereby
influencing overall brand equity.
The algorithmic challenge lies in the risk of algorithmic fallacy in the
digital environment. AI algorithms can mislead and misuse digital
products and services because of mala fide actions, unintentional coding,
or input errors (Davcik et al., 2025). For instance, data on consumer
preferences and shopping behavior patterns can be misused or incor-
rectly handled, which can vilify an individual without knowing that
their data have been mistreated. A branding challenge is based on a shift
in power from firm-controlled brands to a consumer- and technology-
controlled narrative. Marketers’ tasks in the very near future will not
be to convince the consumer about a brand’s advantages but to feed,
learn, and maintain Large Language Models (LLMs) with information on
brand characteristics, performance, and advantages. An ethical chal-
lenge for marketers is to ensure the fair use and treatment of consumers
and to avoid algorithmic fallacy. The problem lies in the blurred border
conditions of what is considered acceptable ethical and privacy data
handling procedures and appropriate corporate behavior in managing
these challenges. A legal challenge for marketers is addressing various
copyright, privacy, market, procurement, data management, and safety
risks due to different legal jurisdictions and stakeholder interests
because of global reach in a digital environment.
Therefore, the digital brand equity metrics cannot be based only on
social media and digital metrics indicators but must also include the
human side of a brand – such as emotional tone, consumer awareness,
and individual opinions – as well as the technology-consumer
ramifications.
7. Concluding recommendations for academics and
practitioners
The article discusses how marketing practitioners and scholars use
various metrics to assess a brand’s digital brand equity performance.
These metrics include online engagement, social media following, brand
sentiment, brand awareness, the share of search, the share of voice,
mentions on social platforms, time spent on the website, etc. However,
these indicators may not necessarily be good predictors of brand equity,
the value of a brand, or market performance due to the lack of adapta-
tion to the digitally-focused branding environment. Additionally, many
indicators can only provide a descriptive view of a brand or consumer
actions rather than actionable, quantitative, and measurable perfor-
mance outcomes.

For marketing practitioners, the implications drawn from this article

are important because they allow them to better navigate the digital
branding landscape. While metrics, such as online engagement, social
media following, and brand sentiment, are readily available, it is
essential for practitioners to not only rely on those. These metrics,
though informative, may not always serve as reliable predictors of brand
equity or market performance within the digitally-focused branding
environment.
The key takeaway for practitioners lies in recognizing the need for
adaptation. Digital brand equity requires a distinct set of tools and
strategies tailored to this unique landscape. This means leveraging
descriptive views of brand and consumer behavior while seeking out
quantitative and measurable performance outcomes leveraging the
available digital information. In practical terms, marketing practitioners
should use not only data-driven but also qualitative indicators. Metrics
like the “share of search” can gauge consumer interest and demand,
while “brand awareness” and “brand sentiment” provide a nuanced
understanding of reputation and consumer perception. Moreover,
practitioners should seek to gain a better understanding of digital brand
measures. By recalibrating their measurement approaches, practitioners
can effectively leverage the power of these metrics.
We also offer a warning for brands that are trying to jump on the
automation wagon without adequately controlling its impact. While AI
has already drastically changed the branding landscape, and its impact
is only accelerating, automating everything with AI could provoke a
negative customer reaction, especially if its impact on brand equity is
not accounted for. For instance, managers should consider legal, ethical,
and privacy issues when applying AI across brands and their effects on
cross-cultural and cross-border performance.
7.2. Implications for researchers
The implications for academic researchers are two-fold. First, there is
a compelling call for researchers to take charge of developing method-
ologies and metrics to measure brand equity in a digital context. These
efforts should leverage the dense literature on brand equity while also
considering the importance of the digitalization of brand equity.
Second, interdisciplinary collaboration is becoming increasingly
important. The complexities of digital branding necessitate a conver-
gence of expertise from various domains, including marketing, infor-
mation systems, psychology, and data science. Collaborative efforts that
bridge disciplinary boundaries hold the promise of a more comprehen-
sive understanding and theoretical development of digital brand equity.
How can researchers move forward in the theoretical development of
digital brand equity? This paper is an attempt to explain and define a
conceptual framework for digital brand equity. We explain traditional
brand equity concepts in the context of digital transformation, propose
concrete metrics for measuring digital brand equity, and look at how
new digital platforms and technologies and the introduction of AI could
affect the conceptualization and measurement of brand equity. Future
development of digital brand equity will need further expansion of the
concepts and metrics proposed in this paper, including their theoretical
delineation, indices development, definitions of border conditions, and
qualitative testing with industry practitioners and consumers. Theoret-
ical contributions can be established by developing and testing holistic
models that will imbed scholarly and practitioner foundations and have
ramifications on brand stakeholders, performance outcomes, public
policies, and society at large.
8. Conclusions
While it is clear that many powerful tools are available to practi-
tioners and researchers, they all have limitations and only show part of
the overall picture of brand equity and performance. As explained in this
article, including some digital metrics without becoming overly reliant
on them improves the accuracy of brand equity measurement. The share
of search, brand awareness, and brand sentiment are all valuable
measures that can provide deeper insight into a brand’s market perfor-
mance and value. However, marketers must also consider other factors,
such as consumer behavior, changing market conditions due to the
technology (e.g., the use of generative AI tools) or macroeconomic
volatilities (e.g., hyperinflation in many countries), and shift of con-
sumer power (such as UGC effects) to develop effective digital marketing
strategies that will optimize brand equity performance.
Future development of the brand equity paradigm must consider
evolving market conditions, radical technological innovations, and
profound changes in a digital environment and their ramifications for
consumers, brands, and organizations.
