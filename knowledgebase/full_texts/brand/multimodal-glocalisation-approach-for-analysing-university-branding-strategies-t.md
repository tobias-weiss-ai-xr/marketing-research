Abstract
Social media has become central to how universities construct and project their institutional identities. This paper
advances branding and multimodality research by introducing a multimodal glocalisation approach to university
branding on X, integrating discourse-semiotic principles with theories of glocal identity performance. The approach
explicates how institutions align global communicative norms with locally grounded cultural meanings through
coordinated verbal, visual and interactional resources. To demonstrate the value of this conceptualisation, the study
analyses one year of X posts (2,861 posts) from two leading Pakistani universities: the National University of
Sciences and Technology (NUST), a government institution; and Lahore University of Management Sciences
(LUMS), a private university. A multi-method design comprising content analysis, corpus-assisted discourse analysis
and multimodal visual analysis captures the verbal and visual branding strategies, while questionnaires and interviews
provide insight into audience perceptions. Findings show that NUST emphasises national development and research
legitimacy, whereas LUMS foregrounds student experience, social advocacy and community networks. Both,
however, consistently embed Islamic and nationalistic cues that anchor their globally oriented messaging within the
Pakistani socio-cultural context. By conceptualising multimodal glocalisation and demonstrating its empirical
applicability, the study offers a replicable framework for understanding how universities craft culturally resonant yet
globally recognisable identities on social media, with implications for research and digital branding practice.
Introduction
Globally, universities have adopted sophisticated branding strategies to enhance their competitiveness in the inter-
national higher education landscape. Beyond academic reputation, institutions increasingly rely on digital platforms,
alumni networks and carefully crafted narratives to attract students, secure partnerships and strengthen global visi-
bility. Research shows that social media engagement has become a decisive factor in shaping institutional perceptions
and student recruitment outcomes. This shows that branding through digital channels is now a strategic imperative
rather than just a peripheral concern.
Within the global context, the Pakistani higher education context reveals a distinctive institutional pattern. Leading
universities such as the National University of Sciences and Technology (NUST), a public institution, and Lahore
University of Management Sciences (LUMS), a private university, have improved their global rankings despite
lacking systematically articulated brand strategies (Aazam et al., 2024a; Mahmood et al., 2014; Shahnaz et al., 2022).
This situation re ﬂects a gap in the literature, as higher education brand management remains underexamined, with

The concept of ‘glocalisation’ is a valuable lens for understanding how global and local forces intersect. It
originated in Japanese business practices and was later theorised in sociology, anthropology and business studies
(Robertson, 2014; Swyngedouw, 2004). Derived from the Japanese word dochakuka (土着化), literally meaning
‘land,’‘ arrive’ and ‘process,’ this word is often translated as ‘local globalisation ’ or ‘indigenisation’ (Drori et al.,
2014; Giulianotti and Robertson, 2012). Glocalisation describes how global ideas are adapted to local conditions
without erasing cultural speci ﬁcity (Leavitt, 1983). Initially applied in marketing, glocalisation explains how orga-
nisations balance universalising pressures with particularising curated responses to ensure relevance across diverse
cultural settings (Foglio and Stanevicius, 2007; Roberson, 2014). In branding, glocalisation captures this strategic
integration of global norms and local meanings. In higher education, this integration is observed as increasingly
becoming more prominent in universities ’ digital and social media branding practices (Hemsley-Brown and Oplatka,
2006; Knight, 2004). When applied to research in higher education, glocalisation offers a critical lens for analysing
how universities align with global academic norms while embedding local cultural, social and religious values in their
image projection.
Social media further ampli ﬁes glocalisation by functioning concurrently as a tool of global outreach and a site for
the performance of locally situated institutional identities (Maringe, 2006; Maringe and Gibbs, 2008). However,
existing scholarship has yet to adequately explore how universities communicate glocalised identities through digital
branding practices, particularly in the Pakistani context under study here. Although glocalisation has been extensively
theorised in relation to consumer culture and media ﬂows (Robertson, 1995), it has seldom been applied to university
branding, and even less so from a multimodal perspective. Addressing this gap, the present study, which is situated at
the intersection of brand identity and glocalisation, advances an interdisciplinary multimodal approach to higher
education branding in Pakistan. Kapferer ’s Brand Identity Prism, originally developed for commercial brands, is
operationalised here through observable verbal and visual resources in universities ’ X posts, to demonstrate how
brand identity is constructed through both strategic narratives and everyday digital communication (Kapferer, 2012).
By integrating Kapferer ’s and Robertson ’s insights into a multimodal glocalisation framework, this study compares
how leading Pakistani public and private universities (NUST and LUMS respectively) invoke global academic norms
alongside local cultural symbols in their online branding, thereby extending brand identity and glocalisation theories
to the domain of university digital marketing.
Brieﬂy, the study here introduces an interdisciplinary multimodal approach and applies it to an empirical com-
parison of how NUST and LUMS negotiate global visibility and local identity through social media branding. This
comparative focus is particularly salient given Pakistan ’s rising presence in global university rankings, its distinctive
socio-cultural and religious landscape, and the contrasting institutional missions and governance structures of public
and private universities. Accordingly, the study is guided by the following research questions:
(1) How do leading Pakistani public and private universities construct and differentiate their glocal brand identities
on X through verbal, visual and thematic resources?
(2) How do these comparative branding elements demonstrate the explanatory value of a multimodal glocalisation
approach for understanding university branding on X?
More signi ﬁcantly, the proposed multimodal glocalisation framework sharpens existing approaches by reposi-
tioning the object of analysis from outcomes such as brand coherence or successful adaptation to the processes of
semiotic negotiation through which global and local meanings are co-constructed. Unlike multimodal branding,
which privileges strategic coherence in identity projection, this approach examines how verbal, visual and thematic
resources are selectively con ﬁgured and sometimes placed in tension to mediate between global academic imaginaries
and locally grounded cultural logics. In contrast to internationalisation discourse, which remains largely at the level of
institutional narratives and policy orientations, it directs attention to the micro-level orchestration of multimodal

Globalisation of Higher Education and Branding on Social Media
The term ‘globalisation’ broadly refers to ‘all those processes by which the peoples of the world are incorporated into
a single world society, global society ’ (Albrow and King, 1990, p 7). Globalisation has profoundly impacted all
aspects of social life. The higher education sector is also pressured to be more adaptable or risk becoming obsolete.
Globalisation results in rethinking higher education ’s social, cultural and economic roles and their con ﬁguration in
national higher education systems (Enders and Fulton, 2012). Universities worldwide are adopting more business-like
and entrepreneurial approaches in their practices, competing by providing the best facilities and being responsive to
students, who are seen as their clients (Wernick, 1991). These shifts recast universities as competitive market actors,
intensifying tensions between their educational missions and the commodi ﬁcation of higher education under market-
driven imperatives.
Numerous studies have examined promotional discourse in the context of higher education (see Askehave, 2007;
Drori et al., 2016; Fairclough, 1993; Feng, 2019; Teo, 2007; Xiong, 2012; Zhang and O ’Halloran, 2013). Although
various semiotic modes —such as texts, images, and videos —have been examined in relation to universities ’ pro-
motional strategies, written texts across both traditional print and digital formats continue to receive substantial
scholarly attention. This research typically centres on analysing genre structures, lexico-grammatical choices and
discursive representations of social domains. For example, the use of rhetorical moves to establish institutional
credibility and provide incentives to stakeholders in materials such as student prospectuses and advertisements plays a
key role in achieving self-promotional aims (Askehave, 2007; Osman, 2008; Xiong, 2012). Additionally, linguistic
features such as modal verbs, agentless passives, nominalisations, personal pronouns, interrogatives, minor clauses,
contractions and informal expressions have been identi ﬁed as enhancing the persuasive quality of institutional
discourse by fostering a sense of closeness between universities and their intended audiences (Askehave, 2007;
Benwell and Stokoe, 2006; Fairclough, 1993; Teo, 2007). This body of research highlights the strategic deployment
of language and semiotic resources to construct persuasive institutional identities. Yet, at the same time, it also
indicates a gap in the engagement with multimodal and digital aspects, which this study ful ﬁls.
Building on research on promotional texts, research has also shown that higher education discourse frequently
incorporates and blends elements beyond the educational domain (Fairclough, 1993; Xiong, 2012). For instance,
Fairclough ( 1993) highlighted how a British university job advertisement drew on both narrative and advertising
discourses. The narrative elements positioned readers as potential employees within the university, while the
advertising aspects re ﬂected a fusion of consumer appeal, prestige and conventional promotional language for
academic roles. Similarly, Benwell and Stokoe ( 2006, p. 120) identi ﬁed features of corporate discourse on a British
university homepage, where students were simultaneously constructed as ‘customers’ and ‘marketable products ’.
Michelson and Álvarez Valencia ( 2016, p. 254) also found traces of tourism discourse on the introductory webpage
of an American college which aimed to attract students to a Study Abroad programme by evoking feelings of desire,
enjoyment and leisure. These studies foreground that higher education promotional discourse is inherently hybrid,
drawing on multiple semiotic and discursive repertoires. Essentially, they highlight the need for an analytical
framework that is capable of capturing both cross-domain and multimodal practices.
Previous research on the promotional discourse of Pakistani universities has focused on prospectuses, websites,
brochures and advertisements, lacking insights into social media use. For instance, Yousaf et al. ( 2022) examined
Pakistani universities’ websites and found that they were designed with a shared goal of establishing a global identity,
showcasing international standards and promoting a strong reputation among other universities. Arshad ( 2017)

Gradually, a strong online presence becomes crucial for universities to promote and maintain their brand identity.
This points to the growing importance of examining online engagement as a key site for contemporary institutional
branding. As Shahnaz et al. ( 2022) reported, dynamic engagement and communication between students and higher
education institutions in Pakistan becomes essential as these institutions note the growing effects of the commer-
cialisation of higher education. Consistent and strategic use of social media such as X can help universities to build a
recognisable brand (Hemsley-Brown and Oplatka, 2006). The active audience engagements indicate their efforts to be
more accessible, transparent, and student-friendly (McAlexander and Koenig, 2010). Sharing content that re ﬂects the
university’s values, achievements and culture can create a cohesive and appealing online image. Universities can
connect with prospective students, facilitating both local and international student recruitment and signi ﬁcantly
increasing their application rates (Freberg et al., 2011). Case studies on X have shown that the sharing of research
breakthroughs and academic achievements can signi ﬁcantly increase a university ’s visibility among international
audiences (Kaplan and Haenlein, 2010). Hence, these insights demonstrate that X is a highly visible and interactive
social media platform, which merits its suitability for examining how Pakistani universities strategically construct and
communicate institutional identity.
Research on universities ’ engagement with current students on X has also proven its pertinence in building vibrant
and supportive campus communities. For instance, Michigan University has successfully used X to engage with
students and gather feedback on various campus initiatives, enhancing student satisfaction and engagement (Lovejoy
and Saxton, 2012). Here, X provides universities with an effective platform for engaging with students through
interactive content such as polls, Q&A sessions and live events. Real-time communication is a key advantage of using
X, particularly bene ﬁcial for time-sensitive information such as event details, deadlines and emergency noti ﬁcations
to keep the audience informed and engaged (Taneja and Toombs, 2014). Despite its potential as a cost-effective
marketing tool for higher education, the use of X in Pakistani universities remains underexplored. Thus, it is timely to
examine how X supports university visibility, branding, student engagement and the promotion of academic and
campus experiences, making X an ideal site of investigation.
Multimodal Discourse in Higher Education Social Media Marketing
Multimodal discourse analysis (MDA) provides a comprehensive framework for analysing the diverse modes of
communication used in social media, including text, images, videos, sounds and other digital features and effects.
MDA is grounded in the theoretical framework of social semiotics, which examines how different modes of com-
munication contribute to meaning-making.
Kress and Van Leeuwen ( 2020) and Jewitt ( 2009) argued that all modes of communication, including linguistic
and visual, are interconnected and work together to convey messages. As proposed by Kress and Van Leeuwen
(2020), visual grammar refers to the rules and conventions governing the use of visual elements in communication. In
the context of higher education marketing, visual grammar can help universities to create consistent and recognisable
visual identities. This includes the use of colours, fonts, layouts and imagery that align with the university ’s brand
guidelines. Consistent use of the university ’so fﬁcial colours and logo across all social media posts can enhance brand
recognition and trust. Visual grammar also involves strategically using composition, framing and perspective to guide
the viewer ’s attention and convey speci ﬁc messages. Additionally, infographics and visual summaries can make
complex information more accessible and appealing to the audience (Chaffey and Smith, 2022; Holliman and

Rowley, 2014; Yazdanparast et al., 2016). By adhering to visual grammar principles, universities can create visually
appealing and cohesive social media content that effectively communicates their brands (Ledin and Machin, 2015).
Integrating visual and textual elements is critical for effective social media marketing (Wu and Gong, 2023). Visual
content can enhance the appeal and engagement of social media posts, while textual content provides context and
detailed information. Kress ( 2009) highlighted that visual and textual elements can create a more persuasive and
impactful message. For instance, images of campus life, student achievements and university events can evoke
emotional responses and create a positive association with the university. These visuals can reinforce the university ’s
brand identity and attract prospective students when combined with compelling textual narratives. Studies have also
shown that social media posts with a balanced mix of visuals and text tend to achieve higher interactions and
engagement rates through features of likes, comments and shares, compared to text-only posts (Bateman, 2014; Kress
and Van Leeuwen, 1996). This perspective is essential for understanding how universities can use various modes of
social media affordances to convey their brand, values and achievements to engage with the wider audience.
Inﬂuence of Cultural Contexts in Higher Education Marketing
Cultural elements are critical in shaping semiotic choices, affecting how messages are constructed, interpreted and
valued across different regions (Askehave, 2007). Understanding these cultural nuances is essential for educational
institutions aiming to communicate effectively locally and globally, ensuring that their branding resonates with
diverse audiences.
Local cultural elements signi ﬁcantly impact the semiotic choices made by universities in their branding efforts.
Symbols, colours and even typography can have varied connotations depending on cultural backgrounds (Kress,
2009; Moriarty, 1994; Weller, 2002). For instance, a colour perceived as positive and vibrant in one culture may be
associated with mourning in another. Recognising and integrating these cultural speci ﬁcs into branding strategies
helps universities create more inclusive and appealing identities that re ﬂect the values and sensibilities of their local
audiences (Lechner, 2009; Ledin and Machin, 2015).
On the global stage, universities often adopt more universal branding strategies, emphasising values and symbols
with wide-reaching appeal (Marginson, 2004). This involves leveraging aspects of globalisation, such as the English
language and internationally recognised accreditations, to attract a broad audience. The challenge here is to balance
universal appeal with suf ﬁcient local relevance to engage speci ﬁc markets effectively, thereby maximising the
institution’s global visibility and attractiveness.
The dichotomy between local and global branding strategies presents strategic decisions for universities. While
local branding highlights cultural speci ﬁcs and community engagement, global branding seeks to transcend cultural
boundaries to appeal to an international student body (Qiang, 2003; Yang, 2013). Institutions must navigate these
strategies carefully, choosing when to prioritise global standards and when to emphasise local traditions and cultural
heritage to build a compelling and authentic brand presence.
Effective cross-cultural communication requires a keen understanding of semiotics within different cultural con-
texts. This includes language and visuals and adapting marketing messages to align with cultural values and
expectations. For universities, this means tailoring their recruitment materials and digital content to re ﬂect their target
markets’ cultural norms and communication styles, which can signi ﬁcantly enhance engagement and conversion rates
(Hofstede, 1984; Spencer-Oatey and Franklin, 2012). Several universities exemplify successful cultural adaptation in
their branding. For instance, Asian institutions may incorporate traditional motifs and local languages in their
promotional materials when targeting domestic students, while using English and featuring international partnerships
in materials aimed at international students (Bodycott and Lai, 2012; Kim et al., 2011). These case studies highlight
the strategic use of cultural elements to optimise local and global appeal.

Multimodal Glocal Branding Approach
With the fundamentals presented above, this study proposes the Multimodal Glocalisation Approach to examine
glocal marketing efforts through social media communication, and in this context, efforts that are speci ﬁc to uni-
versity marketing. Figure 1 shows how social media communication integrates discourse in various modes with
branding and marketing initiatives. The utility of multimodal resources communicates and develops the establishment
of an institutional or corporate identity, building recognition and trust. The identity is aligned with the balancing of a
glocal perspective and promoted through the marketing discourse via the engagement affordances available on social
media to reach the targeted audience, hence giving an institution its visibility.
Traditional university branding research has often privileged either linguistic or visual elements in isolation, thus
overlooking the dynamic interplay between modes. MDA enables the simultaneous examination of these interacting
layers, revealing how universities visualise their identities through a combination of text, imagery and symbolic cues.
This is particularly valuable for capturing the ways in which global educational narratives are integrated with local
cultural references, natural heritage and context-speci ﬁc aesthetics. Such a perspective makes it possible to identify
how local traditions, landscapes, and socio-cultural values are strategically merged with internationally recognisable
academic symbols, hence producing a glocalised brand identity that resonates across both domestic and global
audiences.
This approach is also informed by Kapferer ’s Brand Identity Theory (2012) and Robertson ’s Glocalisation concept
(1995), which guide the structured analysis of the data. Kapferer ’s framework dissects the six core elements of brand
identity, namely physique (the physical aspect of brand), personality (the character and attitude a brand represents) ,
culture (the brand’s value system, principles and behaviour) , relationship (types such as trust and respect) ,r eﬂection
(consumers’ views of a brand) and self-image (the way the audience see themselves as the brand ’s consumers) within
the context of social media representation. This theoretical approach is crucial for evaluating how various identity
elements are manifested and perceived across different cultural settings, supporting a glocal approach to brand
strategy. Robertson’s glocalisation framework provides the necessary perspective to examine the interaction between
global brand strategies and local cultural adaptations, highlighting how these strategies are tailored to meet local
preferences and cultural norms.

this integrated design serves to operationalise and illustrate the proposed Multimodal Glocalisation Approach, which

Data
The data for this study were drawn from the of ﬁcial X accounts of two leading Pakistani universities: the National
University of Sciences and Technology (NUST) and the Lahore University of Management Sciences (LUMS).
Representing the country ’s top public and private universities respectively, NUST (Pakistan ’s premier public and
military-administered institution) and LUMS (the highest-ranked private university) provide a compelling basis for
comparison, given their divergent governance structures, funding models and strategic orientations. At the com-
mencement of this study, both institutions were ranked within the top 600 in the QS World University Rankings
(2022). By 2025, NUST had risen to 353rd place, while LUMS held the 535th position, underscoring their growing
yet differentiated global visibility. Their active and sustained presence on X during the data collection period further
ensured the availability of rich and comparable datasets for analysing institutional digital branding practices.
Data over the 2021 –2022 academic year (1 September 2021 to 30 September 2022) were collected using NCapture
to gather a complete representation of both universities ’ social media activities in an entire academic cycle. This
culminated in a dataset of 2,861 posts containing 61,185 words. The corpus in this study is not intended for a
comparative corpus linguistics analysis, given the variations in posting frequency and campaign scheduling across the
two universities. Instead, the corpus tool was employed primarily to identify the dominant themes and categories of
posts on X, hence providing a foundation for deeper qualitative investigation. All themes and percentages were coded
separately in NVivo14, with patterns derived from NUST and LUMS datasets being analysed independently rather
than combined. Also, due to space limitation, video data was examined and presented as static images based on
frames.
Interviews were carried out with social media managers from both universities to understand the strategic intent
behind the content posted on X. The interviews comprised ﬁve open-ended questions and were conducted online for
NUST and via written responses for LUMS. Further, a questionnaire using a ﬁve-point Likert scale was administered

Content Analysis of Posts
A comparative analysis of X posts by NUST and LUMS reveals distinct dissemination strategies that re ﬂect each
university’s approach to engagement and communication. While both universities widely used text-only posts to
communicate direct updates and announcements, NUST had signi ﬁcantly more text postings (915) compared to
LUMS (145). NUST also favoured visual content, with 1,102 picture posts, emphasising visual storytelling. Con-
trastively, LUMS utilised a more varied content strategy, with a signi ﬁcant number of videos (97 posts) and external
links (235 posts), suggesting a focus on providing dynamic, informative content that potentially enhances deeper
engagement when the reach is extended to external platforms.
Manual coding using Nvivo14 identi ﬁed 12 prominent content categories. These categories were derived by ﬁrst
referencing the site maps, headings and content classi ﬁcations used on the two universities ’ ofﬁcial websites, as these
reﬂect the areas each institution deems most signi ﬁcant and salient. All posts were coded and tagged accordingly,
after which representative examples demonstrating recurring content patterns across the dataset were selected as
illustrative samples. Figure 3 summarises the key content categories identi ﬁed, showcasing the percentage of likes
received.

Discourse Analysis
Corpus-assisted Discourse Analysis
As mentioned earlier, NUST and LUMS widely used text-based posts. To examine the linguistic nature of the textual
content, Baker ’s( 2020) corpus-assisted technique was employed. By identifying frequent keywords, hashtags, and
pronouns, the corpus technique enabled a systematic exploration of the emerging branding themes through frequently
occurring words and phrases. Table 1 illustrates the selected prominent themes that emerge from the corpus analysis.
Table 1 shows that within the textual/wording choices, the institutional branding and leadership theme is dominant.
NUST relies heavily on hashtags such as #nust (587) and #deﬁningfutures (538), reinforcing a technology-driven,
research-intensive image. LUMS frequently highlights Syed Babar Ali Schoo l and Vice Chancellor , emphasising
institutional prestige and leadership. Academic and research excellence also emerges as a crucial branding tool, with
NUST stressing innovation-driven discourse through terms like Research Showcase and Advanced Studies , while
LUMS focuses on its academic programs through hashtags like # learningwithoutborders and #lumslive.
Table 1 also showcases the extensive use of ﬁrst-person plural pronouns ( we, our) in both universities, which
suggest a collective institutional voice, aimed at strengthening stakeholder relationships. This inclusive approach
enhances audience engagement and promotes a sense of belonging, crucial for universities positioning themselves as
global yet locally embedded institutions. The popular use of second-person pronouns ( you, your) in both universities
shows a direct engagement strategy, where prospective students and stakeholders are actively addressed. This
personalised communication approach aligns with higher education marketisation trends, making the universities
more humanised (Aazam et al., 2024a, 2024b; Hemsley-Brown and Oplatka, 2006; Yu and Feng, 2023).
Beyond academic matters, social advocacy, partnerships and national identity also play signi ﬁcant roles in glo-
calisation branding. NUST highlights national development and industry collaborations through #nustpartnerships
and National Outreach Programme ,r e ﬂecting a research-industry nexus tailored to local needs. LUMS integrates
social responsibility themes through hashtags like #conferenceonrightsofpersonswithdisabilities, aligning with global
discourses on inclusion and equity. These strategies ensure that while both institutions attract international stake-
holders, they maintain local credibility, strengthening their hybrid branding approach (Maringe and Gibbs, 2008).
Returning to Kapferer ’s brand identity prism, the prominence of institutional hashtags, leadership references and
collective pronouns contributes to the construction of organisational physique and relationship, projecting coherence
and stakeholder inclusivity. At the same time, the alignment of globally recognisable academic discourse with locally
salient concerns such as national development and social responsibility re ﬂects Robertson ’s conceptualisation of
glocalisation vis-à-vis the co-presence of universalising and the curation of particularising tendencies.
Discourse Strategies
This analysis examines how discourse strategies (Fairclough, 1993) intersect with branding (Kapferer, 2012) and
glocalisation (Robertson, 1995) to construct university identities. Universities market themselves through corporate
advertising, nominalisation and personalisation, shaping their brand narratives while balancing local and global
engagement to position themselves in the competitive higher education landscape.
Both NUST and LUMS utilise corporate advertising to place themselves as elite institutions, but their branding
strategies reﬂect different glocalisation approaches. As a government and military university, NUST tends to integrate
nationalistic branding, reinforcing its institutional role within Pakistan ’s socio-political and defence sectors through
#DefenceDay and NUST Salutes The Defenders of Pakistan , enacting a national identity. Meanwhile, LUMS, a
private sector institution, employs more globalised narrative, as evident in hashtags like #ImagineYourFuture and
#LearningWithoutBorders, positioning itself as an internationally connected forward-thinking university. This aligns
with Kapferer ’s( 2012) concept of physique, where the tangible visual identity (logos, slogans and hashtags) rein-
forces the brand ’s national or global positioning.

The use of nominalisation in university branding helps to institutionalise achievements, depersonalising con-

institutional success rather than individual researchers. According to Kapferer ’s( 2012) theory, in this expertise
dimension, knowledge, credibility and intellectual contributions reinforce a university ’s brand to compete in the
global academic market while maintaining national relevance.
Engagement with students, alumni and stakeholders is a crucial part of university branding, aligning with Kap-

centred approach, while NUST cultivates a structured, authoritative institutional culture (Fairclough, 1993; Robert-
son, 1995).
Through corporate advertising, nominalisation and personalisation, both universities construct a marketised
identity that blends global competitiveness with local engagement. LUMS positions itself as a global research hub,
while NUST strategically integrates national values into its institutional discourse. As competition in higher education
branding intensiﬁes, universities will increasingly rely on glocalised discourse strategies to maintain relevance in both
the domestic and the international market.

Questionnaire Analysis

University Glocalisation Strategies: Discussion and Recommendations
This study contributes to research on university branding by examining how glocalisation is operationalised through
social media practices, rather than as an abstract positioning strategy. By comparing NUST and LUMS, the analysis
shows that global visibility and local embeddedness are negotiated through patterned alignments of verbal, visual and
interactional resources.
A comparison of NUST and LUMS shows that glocal brand identities are constructed in institutionally differ-
entiated ways. As a public-sector university, NUST foregrounds research productivity, industry alignment and
national relevance, projecting an identity aligned with state narratives of development and technological advance-
ment. LUMS, as a private university, emphasises student experience, alumni engagement and social advocacy,
conveying innovation and cosmopolitanism to diverse local and international audiences. These differences indicate
that glocal branding is contingent and contextually embedded (locally), rather than being a uniform response to global
higher education marketisation.
The divergence in branding strategies between NUST and LUMS can be explained by their contrasting institu-
tional missions, funding models and target audiences. As a state-funded and administered public university, NUST ’s
social media branding is geared towards reinforcing national prestige, projecting scienti ﬁc and technological
advancement and aligning itself with state narratives of progress and security. This orientation re ﬂects its mandate to
serve national development goals and appeal to policymakers, government stakeholders and the broader public. By
contrast, LUMS, as a privately funded university, must actively cultivate its appeal to students, alumni and inter-
national partners. Its branding efforts thus foreground student experience, innovation and global competitiveness,
projecting an image of inclusivity, entrepreneurship and cosmopolitanism that resonates with a diverse set of sta-
keholders. These institutional distinctions fundamentally shape how each university negotiates local identity and
global visibility, again highlighting that glocalisation in higher education branding is not a uniform process but one
that is deeply embedded in the socio-political and economic structures of the institutions themselves.
There are also notable convergences in the cultural elements and values. Both universities, for instance, strate-
gically incorporate national symbols, cultural references and narratives of progress to assert legitimacy and resonance
within the Pakistani context, even as they pursue global visibility (see Aazam et al., 2025). This suggests that glocal
branding is also about shared cultural grounding, where institutions draw on common socio-cultural repertoires to
maintain authenticity while projecting competitiveness on the international stage. Such convergences demonstrate the
role of cultural speci ﬁcity in shaping how glocalisation is performed and interpreted in higher education branding.
Bringing together corpus and visual evidence here further clari ﬁes that glocalisation in university branding is
enacted through coordinated multimodal con ﬁgurations and not merely isolated thematic references. At the linguistic
level, institution-centred hashtags and collective pronouns construct a consolidated corporate voice that projects
organisational coherence and authority, while globally oriented expressions and references to international colla-
borations situate both universities within transnational academic circuits. At the same time, locally anchored lexical
choices, such as references to national events, leadership ﬁgures and the use of Urdu, embed these global orientations
within culturally speciﬁc narratives of national service and institutional heritage. Interpreted through Kapferer ’s brand
identity prism, these patterns indicate how global discourses of excellence contribute to the physique and personality
of the brand, while local semiotic resources anchor culture and self-image.
The discursive orientations are also visually instantiated through consistent representational and compositional
choices. Laboratory spaces, formal academic settings and restrained modern layouts index global academic legiti-
macy. Contrastively, national symbols, culturally marked attire and Urdu typography materialise local af ﬁliation.
Interactive features, including gaze and social distance, further structure the relationship dimension by positioning
audiences as engaged yet institutionally oriented stakeholders. In line with Robertson ’s( 2014) idea, the ﬁndings
demonstrate that global branding norms are recontextualised through locally intelligible multimodal forms, posi-
tioning glocal branding as a structured semiotic process rather than a descriptive co-existence of the global and
the local.
While the above highlights the analytical and cultural frameworks underpinning university branding, it is equally
important to recognise that much of a university ’s marketing through academic content remains relatively standard

and expected, such as promoting programme specialisations or niche expertise. What differentiates institutional
branding, however, is the socio-cultural experience it conveys. Hofstede ’s( 1984) cultural dimensions explicate how
cultural differences shape behaviour and communication. These dimensions include power distance, individualism
versus collectivism, and long-term orientation. In a hierarchically structured or high power distance cultures, mar-
keting messages may need to emphasise the university ’s prestige and authority. Conversely, in individualistic
cultures, marketing strategies may focus on personal achievements and opportunities for growth. Recognising these
cultural dimensions enables universities to adapt their marketing messages to resonate with different cultural contexts
(House et al., 2004; Schlesinger et al., 2023). This adaptability can be made tangible through the interactive
affordances of social media.
Cultural adaptation also involves aligning messaging with the values, preferences and expectations of different
target markets. This is necessary for attracting international students. It includes adjusting language, imagery and tone
to ensure cultural relevance and appropriateness. De Mooij ( 2021) emphasises that culturally adapted messages are
more likely to be perceived as credible and appealing in international marketing. However, balancing cultural
contexts in branding also requires sensitivity, continuous research and engagement with experts to ensure that
branding elements are effective and respectful across diverse cultural landscapes (Amani, 2023; Smith, 2001).
Connecting universities’ cultural adaptation to Robertson ’s( 2013) concept of glocalisation shows that global and
local forces operate simultaneously and relationally. These tendencies are mutually constitutive: global discourses
gain legitimacy through local articulation, and local identities are shaped in relation to global frameworks. Rather
than simply appending local elements or rejecting global norms, universities recontextualise global discourses
through locally salient priorities, producing hybrid brand identities that are both internationally legible and locally
resonant.
From a methodological perspective, these ﬁndings demonstrate the explanatory value of a multimodal glocalisation
approach. By integrating multiple methods comprising content analysis, corpus linguistics, discourse analysis and
visual grammar, the approach encapsulates how global and local orientations are aligned across modes and platforms.
Beyond this theoretical contribution, the study also suggests a practical framework (Table 3) which outlines
empirically grounded parameters for enacting glocal branding on X. Without prescribing rules, these parameters offer

The limitations of the study should be also acknowledged. While the multi-method design provides valuable
insights, the questionnaire and interview components are limited in scope, both in sample size and institutional
coverage. They were interpreted within the speci ﬁc contexts of the participating universities and their student bodies
only. Therefore, they should be viewed as exploratory and illustrative, adding depth to the multimodal ﬁndings rather
than offering comprehensive coverage of stakeholder perspectives. Future research could address these limitations by
including larger, multi-site samples and additional stakeholder groups, as well as expanding the approach to higher
education contexts beyond Pakistan to allow for comparative insights across diverse cultural settings. Longitudinal
analyses could examine how branding strategies evolve over time in response to changing socio-cultural dynamics,
global trends and technological developments. Incorporating big data analytics and sentiment analysis from social
media could further enrich understanding of stakeholder perceptions in real time, strengthening the explanatory and
practical value of the framework.
Ultimately, by conceptualising an interdisciplinary multimodal glocalisation and demonstrating its empirical
applicability, this study provides a replicable framework for understanding how public and private universities craft
culturally resonant yet globally recognisable identities on social media. The framework offers both theoretical and
practical implications, enabling universities to re ﬁne their branding strategies, communicate institutional identity
effectively and strengthen their competitive positioning globally.
