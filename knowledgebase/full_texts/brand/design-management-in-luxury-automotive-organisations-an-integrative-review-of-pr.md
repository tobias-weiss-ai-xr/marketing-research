Abstract
Design management in luxury automotive organisations sits at the intersection of several literatures that have developed
largely in parallel: design management theory, new product development process research, cross-functional integration,
design leadership, luxury brand management, and design value measurement. This paper integrates those literatures
through a structured review of 61 sources, identified through searches of Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect and
Google Scholar covering 1990 to 2026 and t hrough citation chasing, and screened against the inclusion and exclusion
criteria in Section 2.3.
The synthesis organises findings across six domains and identifies three tensions that cut across them: structure against
flexibility in process design, hierarchy against collaboration in decision authority, and heritage against innovation in
brand stewardship. Each tension has been posed in the literature and none has been resolved in a design-intensive setting.
Evidence bearing on all three comes predominantly from high -volume manufacturing and general consumer markets,
leaving open whether established findings transfer to low-volume production with high brand-equity sensitivity.
The review makes a diagnostic contribution. It shows where the six literatures rest on incompatible assumptions,
specifies what evidence would adjudicate each tension, and sets out research gaps together with the designs that would
close them.
Key words: design management, luxury automotive, new product development, cross -functional integration, design
value measurement
1. Introduction
Design management scholarship has developed across disciplinary boundaries with limited contact between its
constituent streams. Process-oriented research (Cooper, 1990; Ulrich & Eppinger, 2015) developed largely in parallel
with brand management scholarship (Aaker, 1996; Keller, 1993) and with organisational capability theory (Teece et al.,
1997; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). Each stream addresses design, and each proceeds on assumptions the others do not
share. Integrating them to explain effective design m anagement in high-stakes, brand-sensitive environments remains
an under-addressed theoretical challenge, and it is the task this review undertakes.
Design management in luxury automotive organisations represents a critical nexus between creative vision, brand
identity, and commercial viability. As organisations navigate market dynamics characterised by technological
disruption, evolving consumer preferences, and intensified global competition (D’Aveni, 1994; Teece et al., 1997), the
effectiveness of design management practices bears directly on competitive advantage and brand differentiation (Borja
de Mozota, 2003; Verganti, 2009). The luxury automotiv e sector provides a particularly rich context for examining
design management because of its combination of high brand equity sensitivity (Kapferer & Bastien, 2012), long
development cycles, substantial capital investments, and the necessity of balancing h eritage with innovation
(Karjalainen, 2007; Ranscombe et al., 2012).
Despite this strategic importance, the scholarly literature exhibits notable gaps. Most empirical studies treat either
broader automotive design (Tovey, 2016; Tovey et al., 2003) or general luxury brand management (Okonkwo, 2007;
Wiedmann et al., 2009), le aving the intersection of design process mechanics, organisational governance, and luxury
brand stewardship underexamined. Theoretical frameworks advanced by Cooper and Press (1995), Borja de Mozota

2. Review Methodology
2.1 Review type
This review follows an integrative approach (Torraco, 2005; Snyder, 2019), appropriate where a topic has been addressed
across literatures that developed independently and where the aim is a new conceptualisation rather than an effect
estimate. A systemati c review in the Cochrane sense is not feasible here: the constituent literatures use incompatible
designs, units of analysis, and outcome measures, so statistical pooling is not possible. The review is therefore structured
and transparent in its search pro cedure, and interpretive in its synthesis. The search and screening are reported in the
manner of PRISMA 2020 (Page et al., 2021), adapted for an integrative review.

2.3 Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Included were peer -reviewed journal articles in English that address design management, design -led product
development, or the role of design in brand formation, and that have a product -form dimension. The database searches
covered 1990 to 2026. Books were  admitted where they constitute field -defining contributions with no journal
equivalent. Thirteen books met this test: Borja de Mozota (2003), Brown (2009), Bruce and Bessant (2002), Clark and
Fujimoto (1991), Cooper and Press (1995), Kapferer and Bastien (2012), Martin (2009), Okonkwo (2007), Tovey

from a practitioner-oriented journal, Borja de Mozota (2006), was admitted as an exception because it is the one design-
specific proposal for measuring design capability in the corpus.
Excluded were practitioner magazines, conference abstracts without an accompanying full paper, and studies of design
in contexts with no product-form dimension.

2.5 Thematic derivation
The thematic structure was developed iteratively during engagement with the literature rather than imposed in advance
or derived through formal coding. Themes formed through repeated reading as recurring concerns became apparent
across sources, were revise d as further literature was retrieved, and stabilised at six domains when additional sources
ceased to introduce concerns the existing structure could not accommodate.
This procedure is consistent with integrative review practice (Torraco, 2005; Snyder, 2019), where the synthesis is
interpretive and the reviewer's engagement with the material is the analytic instrument. It follows the logic of reflexive
thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019, 2021), in which themes are understood as constructed through the researcher's
sustained engagement rather than discovered in the data, and in which coder agreement is not treated as a validity
criterion.
2.6 Limitations of the review
The corpus is drawn overwhelmingly from Western European and North American research settings, which bounds the
transferability of the synthesis. The searches covered 1990 to 2026, and recent developments in vehicle electrification,
software-defined vehicle architecture, and the entry of non -Western luxury manufacturers are underrepresented in the
evidence base. Structural findings on cross -functional integration derive predominantly from high -volume
manufacturers, a limitation examined directly in Section 3.3.
Three further limitations concern sources. Only English-language sources were included, and the books entered through
citation chasing. Chasing also supplied more of the corpus than the database searches did, 33 sources against 28. The
imbalance reflects terminology applied inconsistently across the field, a problem examined in Section 3.1, and it leaves
the corpus dependent in part on the reference lists of the sources retrieved first. One retained source, Borja de Mozota
(2006), appeared in Design Managem ent Review, a practitioner -oriented journal, and was admitted as an exception
because it is the one design-specific proposal for measuring design capability in the corpus. The review treats its balanced
scorecard as a proposal, and it carries less evidential weight than the peer-reviewed empirical studies.
3. Thematic Literature Synthesis
The following subsections synthesise scholarship across six thematic domains. Each aims to establish not only what the
literature reports but where its findings diverge, and whether divergence reflects differing research contexts or genuine
theoretical disagreement.
3.1 Design Management Frameworks: From Operational to Strategic Conceptions
Design management scholarship has moved from early operational emphases towards strategic conceptualisation of
design as organisational capability and competitive resource (Borja de Mozota, 2003; Bruce & Bessant, 2002). Cooper
and Press (1995) established a foundational understanding of design management as the coordination of design resources
to achieve organisational objectives, foregrounding managerial dimensions of what had previously been treated as a
purely creative endeavour. This operational framing  proved insufficient to capture design’s role in product
differentiation and brand formation (Utterback et al., 2006; Borja de Mozota, 2006).
Borja de Mozota (2003) positioned design management across three nested levels — operational, functional, and
strategic — a tripartite model widely adopted in subsequent work (Chiva & Alegre, 2009; Deserti & Rizzo, 2014). At

the strategic level, design functions as an organisational differentiator shaping market positioning, corporate identity,
and long -term competitive advantage (Ravasi & Lojacono, 2005). Ravasi and Lojacono’s (2005) longitudinal case
analysis of Italian desi gn-intensive firms indicates that strategic design management enables firms to sustain
distinctiveness as product categories commoditise.
Contemporary frameworks have emphasised design’s systemic organisational role, though they divide sharply on where
design’s value originates. Martin (2009) positions design thinking as a general business capability, arguing that design-
oriented organisations navigate uncertainty more effectively. Liedtka (2015) proposes a mechanism for this advantage,
arguing that design thinking reduces cognitive bias towards incremental solutions and thereby widens the solution space
a team explores. The claim is a theoretical proposition rather than a tested finding; Liedtka’s contribution is a perspective
essay, and the mechanism it specifies has not been isolated empirically.
Verganti (2009, 2011) advances an incompatible position. Design-driven innovation, on his account, originates in radical
changes to what products mean, and such changes come from interpreters working at a distance from expressed user
demand. Verganti’s arg ument is explicitly a critique of user -centred method: asking users what they want, he holds,
yields incremental outcomes by construction. Verganti and Öberg (2013) develop the point further, describing
interpretive capabilities through which firms anticip ate and shape market meaning. Design thinking, as Martin and
Liedtka present it, places user understanding at the centre of the process. The two positions cannot both be right about
where radical design value originates, and the design management literature has largely declined to adjudicate between
them.
Subsequent scholarship has questioned whether design thinking is coherent enough to test. Micheli et al. (2019) reviewed
the construct across the innovation literature and found its defining attributes specified inconsistently from study to
study. Carlgren, Rauth and Elmquist (2016) documented substantial variation in how firms enact design thinking under
a shared label. Kimbell (2011) argues that the concept rests on an unexamined distinction between designers and non -
designers, which obscures rather than explains where design capability resides. Taken together, these critiques suggest
that the empirical base for design thinking’s strategic claims is thinner than its citation frequency implies — a caution
applying to any framework, including the present review’s, that treats design capability as a measurable organisational
property.
The dynamic capabilities perspective (Teece et al., 1997; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000) offers a further lens. Teece et al.
(1997) position competitive advantage as residing in the firm’s ability to sense opportunities, seize them, and reconfigure
resources — functions design management plausibly supports. The same logic extends to design knowledge held as
organisational routine, which the capabilities literature treats as a durable asset difficult for competitors to replicate (Dosi
et al., 2000).
Empirical work linking design investment to performance is more limited than its citation frequency suggests. Gemser
and Leenders (2001) found that greater design involvement in product development correlated with superior innovation
performance among Dutc h furniture and precision instrument firms. Chiva and Alegre (2009) report a comparable
association with design management as mediator. Both studies are correlational, both measure design emphasis largely
by self-report, and neither can exclude the possibi lity that profitable firms invest in design because they can afford to.
The direction of the relationship remains open.
3.2 Process Organisation and Stage-Gate Models
The organisation of design and product development processes has generated substantial scholarship since Cooper’s
(1990) Stage-Gate model, which formalised structured innovation progression through defined phases separated by
decision checkpoints. Cooper ( 2008) subsequently incorporated spiral development and flexibility provisions in
response to accumulated criticism that early formulations were too rigid to accommodate iterative creative work.
The evidence on structured development processes is more divided than the framework’s adoption rate suggests. Against
Cooper’s position, Sethi and Iqbal (2008) found that strict adherence to stage-gate criteria degraded outcomes for novel
products, and identified the mechanism as learning failure: gate criteria fixed early foreclose the reformulation that novel
projects require, so teams proceed on assumptions the accumulating evidence no longer supports. Nobelius and Trygg

These findings are reconcilable, and product novelty is the variable that reconciles them. Cooper’s evidence base is

Neither literature specifies those conditions explicitly, which is why the dispute persists.
This unresolved boundary matters directly for luxury automotive design. Development programmes in the sector
routinely combine high aesthetic novelty with low technical novelty, since platforms, powertrains, and regulatory
requirements are frequently carried over while form language is expected to advance. Existing evidence does not indicate
which pole governs when the two diverge within a single programme, because the studies establishing the novelty
moderator treated novelty as a project-level property rather than one varying by workstream.
Khurana and Rosenthal (1998) identify the fuzzy front end as the phase where the most consequential decisions are
taken with the least formal support. Their holistic front-end model argues for aligning strategic context, product concept,
and organisational commitment at early development stages rather than imposing sequential structure on an inherently
parallel process. Perks, Cooper and Jones (2005) derived a taxonomy of design’s roles across development projects,
distinguishing design as functional specia lism, as part of a multifunctional process, and as process leader. Their
contribution is descriptive: it maps the configurations design occupies without testing which configuration produces
better outcomes under which conditions.
Research specific to automotive design documents multi-phase development structures with iterative refinement within
phases. Tovey (2016) describes concept exploration, feasibility assessment, detail refinement, and final approval as
distinct but interpene trating activities, with physical and digital modelling serving as integration points. Tovey et al.
(2003) examine sketching and concept development as cognitive and communicative tools, finding that visual
externalisation enables designers to explore solution spaces more effectively than verbal or abstract specification alone
— consistent with distributed cognition accounts (Hutchins, 1995). Both studies describe British practice, both predate
the electrification transition, and the sketching-and-clay workflow Tovey et al. document has since shifted substantially
towards digital methods.
The effect of computational tools on design process organisation remains underexamined. Digital modelling permits
earlier feasibility assessment, which the process literature treats as beneficial, while the fixation literature (Sio, Kotovsky
& Cagan, 2015; Perttula & Sipilä, 2007) indicates that early exposure to concrete representations narrows subsequent
exploration. No study located by this review tests these two effects against one another in a design-intensive development
setting.
3.3 Cross-Functional Integration in Design-Intensive Organisations
Integration of design with engineering, marketing, and manufacturing is among the most consistent themes in design
management and product development literature. Brown and Eisenhardt (1995) synthesise three decades of product
development research to identi fy cross -functional communication and coordination among the most critical factors
distinguishing high -performing from low -performing projects, and specify frequent communication, clear role
definitions, and senior management oversight as structural enablers.
Wheelwright and Clark (1992) establish the distinction between lightweight, functional, and heavyweight team
structures, finding that heavyweight teams with empowered project leaders and cross -functional authority outperform
alternatives in development speed, quality, and strategic alignment. Griffin (1997), reporting PDMA benchmarking data,
confirms cross-functional integration among the strongest predictors of development success across industries.
Structural mechanisms alone are insufficient. Dougherty (1992) demonstrates through case analysis that functional
subcultures — the distinctive interpretive frameworks through which design, engineering, and marketing personnel
understand their work — are a primary source of integration failure. Successful integration requires active management
of interpretive differences across functional boundaries, a finding supported by Nakata and Im (2010). Dougherty’s

The evidence supporting these structural prescriptions is drawn almost entirely from high-volume manufacturing. Clark
and Fujimoto’s (1991) study examined volume producers during the 1980s; Wheelwright and Clark’s (1992) typology

organisations too large for informal alignment — is a function of scale.
Luxury automotive organisations frequently operate at a scale where that premise does not hold. Annual volumes in the
tens of thousands, design teams numbering in the dozens, and long tenure among senior staff produce conditions under
which informal coordination may substitute for formal integration structures. Whether heavyweight team architecture
confers advantage at this scale, or imposes overhead on an organisation already integrated by other means, has not been
examined. The question is not whether cross -functional integration matters, which is well established, but whether the
structural mechanisms identified in volume manufacturing are the mechanisms producing it in small design -intensive
organisations.
Prototypes and physical models function as integration mechanisms in a manner the boundary object literature explains.
Star and Griesemer (1989) describe objects that are simultaneously legible to multiple communities of practice while
retaining distinct m eaning within each, enabling coordination without requiring shared interpretive frameworks. The
full-size clay model in automotive design is a boundary object of unusual clarity: it is simultaneously an aesthetic
proposal, an aerodynamic surface, a packagi ng check, and a manufacturing feasibility test, legible to four functions
sharing no common vocabulary.

3.4 Design Leadership and Decision Authority
Design leadership literature addresses how design authority is distributed within organisations and the mechanisms
through which design influences strategic decisions. Bruce and Bessant (2002) identify centralised versus distributed
design authority as a fundamental organisational choice affecting both design outcomes and organisational dynamics.
A trade-off is widely assumed to follow from that choice: centralised authority is held to produce stylistic coherence at
the cost of creative diversity, distributed authority the reverse. This review located no direct empirical test of the
proposition in a design organisation. It can, however, be derived from adjacent findings. The fixation literature indicates
that exposure to existing solutions narrows the range of subsequent output (Perttula & Sipilä, 2007; Sio et al., 2015),
which predicts convergence where a single authority sets the reference. Amabile et al. (1996) find autonomy-supportive
conditions the most consistent predictor of creative performance, which predicts greater variety where authority is
dispersed. Dougherty (1992) indicates that dispersed authority across interpret ive communities produces divergent
output absent active reconciliation. The trade -off is therefore plausible as a derived proposition and unverified as a
finding.
The role of senior design leadership in automotive organisations — variously titled Chief Design Officer, Design
Director, or Head of Design — has received almost no peer-reviewed treatment. Practitioner and trade accounts describe
the position as combinin g creative direction, quality gatekeeping, executive representation, and cross -functional
coordination, but this review located no empirical study examining how these functions combine, how they are
distributed where they are not held by a single person, or how variation in the role relates to design or business outcomes.
The absence is consequential because adjacent literatures predict the role should matter considerably. Upper echelon
theory (Hambrick & Mason, 1984) holds that organisational outcomes reflect the cognitive frames and values of senior
leaders, an effect that should intensify where decisions rest on judgment resistant to codification. Aesthetic judgment in
luxury automotive design is such a decision class: its criteria are substantially tacit (Nonaka, 1994), the stakes for brand
equity are high (Karjalainen, 2007), and no formal specification substitutes for the judgment itself. Upper echelon theory
therefore predicts strong leader effects in precisely this setting, and the prediction has not been tested.
Johansson-Sköldberg, Woodilla and Çetinkaya (2013) document the evolution of design leadership discourse from
individual creative direction towards organisational capability building. Their review also distinguishes designerly
thinking, grounded in design practice and its scholarship, from design thinking as a management discourse, and is critical
of how the latter travelled into business writing — a critique consistent with the concerns raised in Section 3.1.
Adler, Goldoftas and Levine (1999) identify meta -routines — second-order practices governing how primary routines
are adapted — as a mechanism by which organisations sustain flexibility without abandoning structure. Design
management scholarship has not applied the concept, though design review and approval practices appear to operate in
this way: they govern how design development proceeds rather than performing design work themselves. Whether
review structures function as meta -routines, and whether that f raming predicts anything about their effectiveness, is
unexamined.
Leadership style research provides further context. Amabile et al. (1996) demonstrate that autonomy -supportive
leadership — goal-setting clarity combined with means flexibility — is the most consistent predictor of creative
performance in organisational settings. Taggar (2002) finds that team-level creative performance depends on individual
creative capacity combined with team processes enabling integration of diverse contributions. B oth findings require
qualification before transfer to this setting. Amabile et al. measured perceived work environment via the KEYS
instrument with creativity assessed by self and supervisor report, a different construct from aesthetic quality in a design
output judged against brand fit; Taggar’s teams were undergraduate project groups. The autonomy findings are robust
in their own domain, and their transfer to design-specific creative performance remains an assumption.
3.5 Brand Coherence and Identity Management in Luxury Automotive Design
Literature on luxury brand management emphasises design’s role in maintaining brand identity and visual coherence
across product portfolios. Kapferer and Bastien (2012) articulate the principle that the luxury brand defines the customer
rather than the rev erse, establishing design as the primary vehicle through which brand identity is expressed. Their

substantially shaped by design — drive luxury brand equity.
Keller’s (1993) customer -based brand equity framework positions visual identity as a critical brand asset requiring
systematic management, a position consistent with the wider brand equity literature (Aaker, 1996). In automotive
contexts, design elements function as particularly powerful brand equity carriers because they are directly observable in
everyday use, creating repeated exposure that reinforces identity associations (Karjalainen, 2007). Karjalainen (2007)
demonstrates through systematic visual analysis that automotive design elements function as reliable brand identifiers.
Ranscombe, Hicks, Mullineux and Singh (2012) investigate how aesthetic features influence brand perception through
systematic decomposition of vehicle images, finding that both specific elements — grilles, headlights, silhouette
proportions — and overall gestalt function as brand identity cues with differentiated salience. Two limitations bear on
the present application. Their method measured recognition at a single point in time and cannot address how far a feature
may be transformed before recognition fails. Their stimulus set also predates vehicle electrification, which has removed
the grille as a functional necessity across much of the market. Two of the strongest identifiers in their data are
consequently now styling choices rather than engineering requirements, and whether brand identification survives that
shift is an open question of immediate commercial relevance.
The tension between heritage consistency and innovation-driven evolution has been examined by several scholars. Urde,
Greyser and Balmer (2007) develop the corporate heritage brand framework, arguing that heritage brands must manage
a dynamic tension between continuity and adaptive renewal. Beverland (2005) demonstrates through case analysis that
successful heritage brands treat tradition as a resource for innovation, constructing new products as extensions of the
brand’s historical narrative. Wiedmann, Hennigs and Siebels (2009) find heritage authenticity a primary driver of luxury
consumption, placing organisational pressure on design management to maintain recognisable continuity while pursuing
stylistic evolution.
The literature offers two incompatible accounts of how brand identity persists. Design language systems treat identity
as codified specification — a documented set of formal elements reproducible across products and transmissible to new
personnel (Karjalai nen, 2007). Balmer (2013) advances a different account, treating corporate brand identity as an
ongoing organisational narrative continuously reinterpreted rather than a fixed specification. The two accounts imply
different transmission mechanisms and diff erent failure modes. Codified identity fails through mechanical repetition
producing stale output; narrative identity fails through drift when interpretive authority is dispersed or lost.
Neither account has been tested against the other in a design organisation, and the knowledge management literature
suggests neither is complete. Nonaka’s (1994) distinction between explicit and tacit knowledge implies that codified
design language captures only what can be articulated, while the judgment determining whether a new form reads as
authentic to a brand remains tacit and transmits through apprenticeship rather than documentation. If that is correct,
design language systems are necessary and stru cturally insufficient, and the sufficiency gap is filled by individuals —
which returns the question to the design leadership literature and its documented silence (Section 3.4).
3.6 Measurement and Evaluation of Design Value
Design value measurement remains contested, with ongoing debate regarding appropriate metrics, evaluation
frameworks, and the temporal dimensions of design’s contribution. Hertenstein, Platt and Veryzer (2005) provide the
field’s principal empirical analys is, finding that firms rated design -effective by industry experts outperform matched
comparison firms on multiple financial indicators. Two limitations qualify the finding. The authors acknowledge that
financial metrics capture only a subset of design’s co ntribution and may undervalue long -term brand building. The
independent variable also derives from expert panel judgment, so the result partly reports that experts rate commercially
successful firms as well designed, and halo effects are difficult to exclude.
The limitations of purely financial evaluation prompted broader measurement frameworks. Borja de Mozota (2006)
proposes a balanced scorecard approach integrating financial performance, customer value creation, internal process
efficiency, and organisationa l learning, adapting Kaplan and Norton’s (1996) methodology to design management.

Design value measurement remains fragmented across three literatures that have not been reconciled. Financial
approaches (Hertenstein et al., 2005; Walsh et al., 1992) demonstrate association between design effectiveness and firm
performance but cannot isolate direction. Brand equity approaches (Keller, 1993; Karjalainen, 2007) capture design’s
contribution to identity and recognition without connecting it to financial return. Capability approaches propose balanced
measurement across learning and process dimensions but supply no validated instrument (Borja de Mozota, 2006).
The three operate on different time horizons, which explains why integration has proved difficult rather than merely
neglected. Financial metrics resolve within reporting periods; brand equity accumulates across product generations;
capability development runs longer still. A firm optimising against any one horizon may degrade performance on the
others, and no framework specifies how organisations should weigh them. How firms actually construct, deploy, and act
on design performance data is unexamined.
4. Cross-Cutting Tensions and Research Gaps
The thematic synthesis reveals three tensions cutting across scholarly domains. Each has been posed in the literature;
none has been resolved in a design-intensive setting. Each subsection below states the tension, identifies what remains
unsettled, and specifies what evidence would settle it.
4.1 Structure Versus Flexibility
The most fundamental tension concerns the relationship between organisational structure and creative flexibility. Stage-
gate models (Cooper, 1990, 2008) emphasise structure; design thinking approaches (Martin, 2009; Brown, 2009)
emphasise flexibility. Marc h’s (1991) analysis of the exploration –exploitation trade -off provides the underlying
framework: exploitation and exploration require different organisational conditions and compete for resources and
attention.
Adler and Borys (1996) establish that formalisation is not uniformly constraining, distinguishing enabling bureaucracies
— formal structures designed to support skilled judgment — from coercive bureaucracies that constrain discretion.
Sheremata (2000) develops a related account of centrifugal and centripetal forces, arguing that effective development
organisations must simultaneously enable divergent exploration and enforce convergent integration.
What Adler and Borys do not supply is a criterion for distinguishing the two forms in advance. Enabling and coercive
bureaucracy are identified by their effects, which means the distinction explains outcomes retrospectively without
predicting them. Applied to design development, the unresolved question is which properties of a review structure make
it enabling. Candidate variables are identifiable from the surrounding literature — who sets gate criteria, whether criteria
can be revised mid-project, whether reviews evaluate work or redirect it, and whether authority exists to reopen a settled
decision. None has been tested. Resolving this would require observation of review practice across proj ects varying in
these properties, with design outcomes assessed independently of the process that produced them.
The novelty moderator identified in Section 3.2 gives this tension a specific form in luxury automotive settings.
Programmes there routinely combine high aesthetic novelty with low technical novelty, while the process literature treats
novelty as a project-level property. Which pole governs when the two diverge within one programme is unaddressed.
4.2 Hierarchy Versus Collaboration in Decision Authority
The relationship between hierarchical decision authority and collaborative organisational culture represents a second
persistent tension. Centralised authority is held to enable design coherence and brand consistency while risking
suppression of contribution from experienced designers (Amabile et al., 1996); distributed authority is held to enable
contribution and cross-functional buy-in while risking incoherence and brand dilution (Karjalainen, 2007).

A hybrid — formal authority concentrated at senior levels combined with extensive lateral consultation — is frequently
proposed as the resolution. The empirical record complicates this. Eisenhardt and Bourgeois (1988) found that
centralised power in high -velocity environments generated political behaviour that degraded decision quality, with
executives forming coalitions and withholding information. Their evidence runs against the proposition that concentrated
authority combined with consultation produces informed decisions. Where a hybrid of this kind is claimed to work, the
burden falls on explaining why the political dynamics they documented do not arise.
One candidate explanation concerns the basis of authority. In their sample, executive authority derived from position.
In design organisations, senior authority typically derives from demonstrated aesthetic judgment accumulated over a
career, which may be accepted as legitimate by subordinates in a way positional authority is not. If legitimacy differs,
political contestation may differ. This is a proposition rather than a finding: no study has examined whether expertise -
based authority in creative organisations produces the political dynamics associated with positional authority.
The tension cannot be resolved while the role at its centre remains undocumented. Section 3.4 established that senior
design leadership has received almost no empirical treatment, which means the mechanism through which hierarchical
authority is exercised in design organisations is itself unknown. Upper echelon theory (Hambrick & Mason, 1984)
predicts that individual leader characteristics should matter substantially here, with implications for succession planning
that the literature has not addressed.
4.3 Heritage Versus Innovation in Brand Management
The tension between heritage preservation and contemporary innovation represents a third cross-cutting theme. Urde et
al. (2007) identify this as the defining challenge of heritage brand stewardship: organisations must maintain brand
continuity while adaptively renewing brand expressions. Beverland (2005) documents one resolution, in which tradition
is treated as a resource for innovation rather than a constraint upon it.
Gioia, Schultz and Corley (2000) describe adaptive instability, in which surface changes to identity expression preserve
deeper structural continuity. The framework does not specify where the boundary between surface and deep sits, and in
design that boundary is the entire practical question. Which formal elements a brand may change without ceasing to be
recognisable is decided repeatedly in design organisations and has no theoretical account.
Ranscombe et al. (2012) establish that identification loads unevenly across features, implying the boundary is
empirically discoverable rather than arbitrary. Their cross -sectional method cannot address how far a feature may be
transformed before recognition fails. Longitudinal analysis tracking specific design elements across product generations,
paired with recognition testing, would begin to answer this. Vehicle electrification supplies an unplanned natural
experiment: the grille has lost its cooling function while retaining its identity load, and the industry is currently resolving
in practice a question the literature has not posed.
Organisational memory (Argote, 2013) and tacit knowledge transmission (Nonaka, 1994) are implicated in any account
of how continuity is maintained across generations of personnel, and neither has been systematically incorporated into
brand management theory.

5. Towards an Integrative Reading, and Conclusion
5.1 The domains are not independent
The three tensions identified above each cut across several of the six domains reviewed, which indicates the domains
interact rather than partition the field. The structure–flexibility tension surfaces in process research (Cooper, 1990; Sethi
& Iqbal, 2008), in leadership research through the autonomy findings (Amabile et al., 1996), and in capability research
through the question of what review practice teaches an organisation. The hierarchy –collaboration tension appears in
integration research (Wheelwright & Clark, 1992), in leadership research (Eisenhardt & Bourgeois, 1988), and in brand
research wherever interpretive authority over identity is at issue (Balmer, 2013). The heritage–innovation tension spans
brand management (Urde et al., 2007), process org anisation, and measurement, since the horizon over which heritage
value accrues differs from the horizon on which projects are assessed.
Existing frameworks treat the domains as separable. Borja de Mozota’s (2003) operational, functional, and strategic
levels distinguish design management by scope of application rather than by interacting mechanism, so a tension arising
between two domains has no place in the scheme. The tensions documented here suggest that a framework organised
around interaction would locate problems the level-based framework cannot express.
5.2 Three dimensions as an organising lens
This review therefore proposes reading the six domains as three interacting dimensions. Process architecture covers
phase structure, review mechanisms, and how constraints enter development. Organisational governance covers decision
authority, integration structures, and stewardship of brand identity. Capability development covers talent, learning from
completed projects, and how design performance is assessed.
The grouping is offered as an organising lens rather than a validated model, and it makes no claim to have resolved
anything. Its use is diagnostic. Each tension identified above sits at the intersection of two dimensions — structure and
flexibility between process architecture and capability development, hierarchy and collaboration between governance

5.3 Questions arising
The review identifies gaps that an interview study with senior design practitioners is positioned to address. Four
questions follow.
RQ1. How is the senior design leadership role constituted in luxury automotive organisations, and how are its creative
direction, quality gatekeeping, executive representation, and integration functions combined or distributed?
Section 3.4 established that this role has received almost no peer-reviewed treatment despite adjacent theory predicting
it should matter substantially. The question is descriptive and foundational: the role must be documented before its
effects can be examined.
RQ2. What properties distinguish design review structures that practitioners experience as supporting their judgment
from those experienced as constraining it?
Adler and Borys (1996) distinguish enabling from coercive bureaucracy by its effects, without supplying a criterion
identifiable in advance (Section 4.1). Practitioners who have worked under multiple review regimes are positioned to
articulate the difference from the inside, which is where a predictive criterion would have to come from.
RQ3. On what basis is senior design authority accepted as legitimate, and how does that basis shape how decisions are
contested?
Eisenhardt and Bourgeois (1988) found that concentrated authority generated political behaviour degrading decision
quality (Section 4.2). Section 4.2 proposed that authority grounded in demonstrated aesthetic judgment may operate
differently from positiona l authority. The proposition concerns how legitimacy is understood by those subject to it,
which interviews can address and structural measures cannot.
RQ4. How do practitioners distinguish design elements a brand may change from those it may not, and what governs
that judgment where it is not codified?
Gioia, Schultz and Corley (2000) locate identity continuity beneath surface change without specifying where that
boundary falls (Section 4.3). Section 3.5 set out two incompatible accounts of how the boundary is held, one treating
brand identity as codifie d specification and the other as continuous reinterpretation. Designers decide the question on
every programme, and neither account records the criteria they apply, so the practitioners who apply them are the only
available source.
5.4 Conclusion
This review integrated scholarship across six domains that have developed with limited contact. The synthesis found a
substantial body of relevant theory and a set of disagreements the literature has not settled: whether design value
originates in user understanding or in interpretation away from users; whether structured development processes help or
harm work of high aesthetic novelty; whether integration mechanisms established in volume manufacturing transfer to
small design organisations; and where the boundary sits between design elements a brand may change and those it may
not.
Senior design leadership emerged as the clearest absence. Upper echelon theory predicts strong leader effects where
decisions rest on judgment resistant to codification, and aesthetic judgment under brand constraint is such a decision
class. No empirical s tudy located here examines how the role combines its functions or how variation in it relates to
outcomes. Closing that gap requires access to senior design practitioners and a method suited to eliciting tacit judgment,
which sets the agenda for the interview study proposed in Section 5.3.
