Abstract
This research investigates the impact of circular economy marketing strategies on consumer advocacy, while also
measuring the mediating effect of brand purpose and customer engagement. The paper highlights the need for
sustainability-oriented marketing research that relates circular value communication with pro-brand consumer
behaviour. A simulated academic survey dataset of 300 urban consumers who were aware of sustainable,
reusable, recyclable, repairable or eco-friendly brands was used for quantitative empirical research design. A
total of four constructs were measured using five-item, five-point Likert scales: circular economy marketing
practices, brand purpose, customer engagement, and consumer advocacy. The data was analysed using
descriptive statistics, reliability testing, Pearson’s correlation, multiple regression and bootstrapping mediation.
The study findings confirm the positive relationship of circular economy marketing with consumer advocacy and
how it significantly predicts both brand purpose and customer engagement. There in a consumer’s perception, a
positive framework happens in the presence of brand purpose and other environmental factors. The results of
the bootstrap mediation indicate that both mediators have considerable indirect effects. However, customer
engagement provides a stronger mediation path than brand purpose. The study shows that the persuasive power
of circular economy marketing increases when sustainability claims are reflected in an authentic brand purpose,
and customers are actively engaged in a circular practice. The paper brings together circular economy
communication, purpose-driven branding, engagement, and advocacy within a single explanatory model to
contribute to sustainability marketing. Suggestions for businesses and brands guiding the development of
circular campaigns are offered to build consumer trust and convert sustainable consumption experiences into a
voluntary recommendation and positive word-of-mouth.

1. Introduction
Circular economy marketing is the sum of
communication and value-delivery activities
through which firms market their offering to
support product-life extension, reuse, repair,
recycling, remanufacturing, responsible
consumption, and material circularity. As contrasted
to standard green marketing, which seeks to
convince consumers of environmental friendliness,
circular economy marketing is more systematic
because it invokes consumers to participate in value
retention processes along the product life cycle. In
simple terms, the circular economy is a system
where materials are retained in use, waste is
designed out, and nature regens. This is through
processes such as redesign, reuse, refurbishment,
and remanufacturing, recycling and other related
loops. (Ellen MacArthur Foundation, n.d.; European
Commission, 2020). Recent research claims that
sustainability marketing has shifted away from
climatic marketing towards more work and
questions of circular economy, ethical market,
reduced consumption, consumer participation and
system-level fair improvement (White et al., 2025)
With consumers’ no longer passive receivers of
sustainability communication, this topic is becoming
more and more relevant. Consumers look into the
environmental claims of every brand. They judge
whether the actions of the brand are credible.
Thereafter, they share their verdict with peers
through offline and digital channels. Consumer
advocacy is thus an important behavioural outcome
in sustainability-oriented markets. Brand advocacy
encapsulates more than merely purchasing
intentions or loyalty, it requires voicing at a range of
other levels – endorsing the brand, generating
positive word of mouth, defending the brand and
mobilizing others behind the brand. Advocacy, in
sustainability contexts, is especially important as
credible peer-to-peer endorsement can reduce
uncertainty around green claims and drive
participation in circular practices. Confetto et al.

2. Review of Related Literature
Marketing practices of circular economy.
The circular economy marketing practices are
entrenched in the broader shift from linear
consumption to circular value systems. The
approach of take, make, consume and dispose is
being subjected to increasing criticism because it
tends to escalate the extraction of resources, waste,
emissions and damage to nature. Innovation designs
for a circular economy (19 words)
Circular economy approaches seek to retain
material value by encouraging reuse and repair,
remanufacturing and refurbishment, recycling, and
composting and product-service systems. From a
marketing perspective, brands must communicate
product benefits and the consumer's role in circular
behaviour. The European Commission (2020)
stressed product durability, consumer
empowerment, and information transparency as
ingredients of circular transition. Ellen MacArthur
Foundation (n.d.) stresses eliminating waste,
circulating materials, and regenerating nature as
core principles.
In consumer behaviour research, these choices
relate to buying a product made from a recycled or
upcycled material, accepting a reused material,
repairing a product, returning a product to a take-
back scheme and adopting a sharing or product
service business model. The study conducted by
Coderoni and Perito (2020) on waste-to-value food
purchase intentions revealed that the consumer
acceptance of circular products is affected by
psychological and socio-demographic
characteristics.
A review of circular economy and consumer
behaviour literature was carried out by Vidal-Ayuso
et al (2023). It was found that consumers are
important actors in circular loops, and yet the
literature must do a better job of explaining how
communication, trust, convenience, and perceived
value influence circular decisions. These studies
also show that marketing the circular economy
requires more than just environmental claims
instead it needs credible information, consumer
education, and practical actions to take part.
Circular economy marketing is similar to
sustainable marketing, but not the same. According
to Peterson et al. (2021), sustainable marketing
refers to marketing which helps to further
sustainable businesses and encourages consumers
to choose for environmental and social good.
Marketing for a circular economy can be viewed as a
special operational expression of sustainable
marketing, as it renders the loops of resources
transparent to the consumer. It transforms the
concept of sustainability into practical activities like
returning products, repairing them, reusing them,
minimizing packaging and participating in recycling.
As such, this research regards the marketing
practices of a circular economy as a multi-item
construct which measures the extent to which a

The brand purpose in sustainability marketing
The idea of a brand purpose has become central to
sustainability branding because consumers
increasingly expect brands to justify their social and
environmental relevance. The value of purpose is
distinct from positioning or slogan-based
differentiation because it is a durable organizational
aim that can inform identity, meaning, strategy and
stakeholder relationships. According to Williams et
al. (2022), consumers achieve well-being from a
brand purpose when it facilitiates people in
connecting with nontransactional consumption-
related values. Fernandes and colleagues (2024)
demonstrate that conscientious purpose-driven
marketing enhances brand outcomes when purpose
is branded as meaningful and consistent.
Circular economy marketing requires marketing
strategies with purpose because circular practices
are complex and require trust from consumers. If
consumers doubt greenwashing or symbolic
communication, a recycling claim, repair promise or
take-back scheme may be downgraded. Walter et al.
(2024) found that authentic purpose-driven
branding is elevating brand credibility it is believed
whereas inconsistent or performative purpose
communication harms credibility. Mirzaei et al.
(2022) warn that claims about activism or purpose
may induce scepticism if they lack authenticity. The
findings suggest that circular economy marketing
helps strengthen advocacy when the perception of
the brand’s purpose is clear, authentic, socially
meaningful and compatible with sustainability
objectives.
This study conceptualizes brand purpose as
consumers’ perception of brands having a clear
social and environmental purpose that
communicates values beyond profit-making, as well
as linking business activities to sustainability goals
and strengthening trust through consistent purpose.
Brand purpose is looked at as an intermediary
mainly because it offers an explanation for how
circular economic marketing leads to consumer
activism. The reasoning goes something like this:
circular practices signal environmental
responsibility, brand purpose reads these practices
as authentic and meaningful, and this meaning then
encourages consumers to recommend and defend
the brand.
Engaging the Customers for Sustainable
Consumption
Customer engagement is the behaviour, thought,
and feeling of customers towards a brand.
Engagement in the digital and sustainability context
refers to expressing and interacting with content,
sharing brand messages, taking part in campaigns,
responding to the initiative, and feeling a sense of
emotion with the brand. The research by Lim and
Rasul (2022) reviews studies on customer
engagement and reveals how social media has
heightened customer engagement as a key
marketing idea as customers engage more in brand
communication. For a green brand engagement is a
valuable concept as it shifts customers from being
mere aware to more contributor.
According to researchers, the sustainability values
of consumers can be established marketing
mechanisms of engagement with green brands.
According to Leckie et al. (2021), the perceived
value and self-identity factors concerning green
products can promote customer engagement
behaviour for green brands, which is associated
with favourable brand outcomes. The work of Chen
et al. (2024) shows that interactive green
information can invoke green participation
behaviour of the fashion brands through cognitive
and emotional processes. According to Nadeem et al.
(2025), using new technologies for sustainability
marketing can boost consumer engagement through
a combination of environmental protection, ethical
considerations, social equity and technological
innovation.
The paper therefore treats customer engagement as
a mediator. Circular economy marketing practices
may enhance engagement by providing consumers
with meaningful opportunities for interactions with
sustainability-related content, joining take-back or
reuse programs, sharing circular messages and
responding to campaigns. Most of the consumers
when engaged will start advocating for the brand
because advocacy is the active behavioural
expression of the relationship. The engagement is
considered as a bridge between circular
communication and public support.
Consumer Support for Corporate Entity Brands
Consumer advocacy refers to the voluntary pro-
brand behaviour of the consumer who recommends,
defends, supports or talks positively about a brand.
Broader than loyalty, it entails communicative
support directed at other consumers. Advocacy is
particularly influential in these markets due to the
uncertainty consumers’ face regarding
environmental claims’ reliability. A circular product
looks more credible, accessible and socially
acceptable when recommended by another
customer. Public or organizations’ advocacy may
consist of activities which aim to create public
awareness and connotes promotional activities.
Brand relationship quality and customer
engagement also relates to advocacy. When
consumers feel that a brand’s purpose, values and
actions are important, they will are willing to show
social support to the brand. In sustainable markets,
advocacy can enable brands to overcome the
attitude-behaviour gap by mobilising social

A Purpose of Brand Mentions.
Brand purpose is suggested as a mediator because
circular economy marketing practices can influence
consumers' interpretation of brand motives. A
brand can be viewed as more purposeful if it has a
clear structure revolving around reuse, repair,
recycling, responsible sourcing, and waste reduction
rather than marketing. The purpose of the brand
shows the reason behind their circular practices and
how the brand identity is linked to the sustainability
of operations. This makes customers see the brand
as values-driven rather than just rule-driven.
Empirical research on sustainable marketing shows
mediating brand perceptions matter. Rastogi et al.
(2024) indicate that sustainable marketing
enhances brand image which in turn fosters brand
loyalty in consumer electronics industry. Despite
the brand image and brand purpose not being the
exact same thing, they both work as lenses through
which consumers decipher sustainability efforts.
The current research builds on this reasoning by
examining the relationship between circular
economy marketing and advocacy via brand
purpose as a mediator. When purpose matters,
circular practices become advocacy-generating as
they are read as authentic and meaningful.
2.6 The role of customer engagement as a
mediator.
Customer engagement has been suggested as a
second mediator due to its required participation in
circular economy marketing. Circular economy
marketing differs from traditional marketing in that
it may ask consumers to repair, return, refill, reuse,
resell and recycle products. These practices are
behaviorally demanding and therefore depend on
engagement. When brands offer interactive
information and initiatives to participate in, it
encourages consumers to take action instead of just
being aware.
Research has supported the engagement pathway.
Leckie et al. (2021) show that green brand
engagement results in positive brand outcomes.
Chen et al. (2024) show that a green information
presentation leads to pro-participation behaviour
through internal cognitive and emotional responses.
According to Confetto et al. (2023), the content
about sustainability is associated with advocacy
among Generation Z. So customer engagement is
likely to mediate the circular marketing-advocacy
relationship by turning sustainability
communication into relational and participatory
behaviour.
Research Gap 2.7
A significant growth trend in sustainability
marketing, circular consumption, purpose-driven
branding, and green customer engagement are
found.
However, there are three gaps remaining. Circular
economy marketing practices are often discussed at
the level of business model design or consumer
acceptance, while their effect on consumer advocacy
is less well-developed. Secondly, purpose and
engagement are usually studied separately, yet both
likely operate at the same time in sustainability-
oriented brand relationships. To begin with, loyalty
or the intention to purchase is examined in many
studies. However, advocacy is a better proxy for the
social diffusion of circular consumption messages.
The present study aims to fill these gaps by
developing and providing statistical evidence of a
mediation model in which circular economy
marketing practices influence consumer advocacy
through brand purpose and customer engagement.
This integrated model is advantageous for managers,
as it indicates that circular advertising does not end
with environmental claims. To encourage customer
participation, response, and sharing, businesses
require a real purpose layer and an engagement
architecture.
3. Theoretical Framework
3.1 Stakeholder Theory
The theory of Stakeholders gives a useful foundation
for the SE and CE marketing, because circular value
creation involves multiple parties like consumers,
suppliers, communities, regulators, waste
processors, and future generations. In this work,
consumers buy as well as participate in the circular
economy. Circular economy marketing serves as a
communication channel for stakeholders through
which brands explain how their practices
substantiate environmental and social expectations.
When customers view these practices as being in
line with stakeholder well-being, they can become
more eager to rally behind the brand.

Theory of Signals.
According to signalling theory, a brand
communicates the quality, credibility and values of
its offering to help resolve issues caused by
information asymmetry or signalling. It is hard for
consumers to fully verify sustainability and circular
economy claims, thus mistrust becomes more
probable. Using marketing practices such as
providing sourcing transparency information,
implementing repair programmes, establishing
product take-back systems, and disclosing waste
impact can signal eco-friendliness. When
authenticity steps in, the signal becomes more
persuasive, thanks to the brand purpose. This
indicates what the mediating role of brand purpose
will be.
Theory of Consumer-Brand Relationship
The consumer-brand relationship theory clarifies
the customer's engagement. Through meaningful,
emotionally relevant, and personal value consistent
interactions, consumers build relationships with
brands. Involving customers in sustainability
activities through circular economy marketing can
strengthen these relationships. Customers engage
when they interact with their content and join
circular initiatives and feel their actions matter. As a
result, advocacy is an outward sign of a robust
consumer-brand relationship.
Sustainability Marketing Theory
Sustainability marketing theory argues that, on the
contrary of them focusing on short term
transactions, marketing should create economic,
social, and environmental value. According to White
et al. (2025), sustainability marketing is a broad
topic that now includes distinct circular economy,
consumption reduction, ethics, and systems change
issues. Consumer advocacy is a significant outcome
in this context, reflecting the social diffusion of
sustainability-oriented brand value. The circular
economy marketing practices are antecedents,
brand purpose and engagement are relational
mechanisms and consumer advocacy is the
behavioural outcome in the present model.
4. Theoretical Framework.
The framework suggests that circular economy
marketing practices influence consumer advocacy
directly and indirectly. The direct route suggests
customers may advocate for brands that actively
demonstrate reuse, repair, recycle, waste reduction,
responsible sourcing and circular participation. The
first indirect path proposes that brand purpose can
be strengthened through circular practices that
enhance it. Another indirect way suggests that
circular practices raise customer engagement, and
engagement raises consumer advocacy. As a result,
the model integrates brand purpose with customer
engagement collaboratively in a meaningful manner.

8. Methods to obtain and analysis data.
Design of the Study
The quantitative, cross-sectional, explanatory
research design used on study. The design is
suitable because it seeks to test certain
hypothesised relationships among various
measured constructs and to estimate both direct
and indirect effects. The model is tested in an
empirical consumer study using a survey-based
structure. The analysis focuses on urban consumers
who are familiar with brands that are sustainable,
reusable, recyclable, repairable, or eco-friendly.
Population, Sample, and Sampling Technique
The target population is urban consumers above 18
years of age and who are somewhat aware of brands
communicating sustainability or circular economy.
The sample that was simulated was 300
respondents. The sampling technique adopted is
convenience sampling with purposive inclusion
criteria, as the study requires respondents who can
assess circular economy marketing claims. In a real-
life field study, this design would be implemented
by first screening consumers for awareness of
sustainable or circular brands before the
questionnaire.
8.3 Data Type and Simulation Disclosure.
The dataset utilized in this paper is a simulated
academic survey dataset built just for research
demonstration and statistical illustration. This is not
an indication of real world behaviour and should
not be presented as evidence of actual consumer
behaviour. The simulation was designed to yield
realistic Likert-scale responses and internally
consistent interrelationships among constructs in
order that the paper might demonstrate how one
could analyse the proposed model using common
statistical procedures. Consequently, all the
observed outcomes including correlations,
regression coefficients and mediation effects are
exemplary in nature and not field-validated.
Survey Tool and the Measurement Scale.
The questionnaire framework consists of all the
demographic variables and four multi-item
constructs. Every construct is measured using five
items on five-point Likert scales from 1 = strongly
disagree to 5 = strongly agree. Perceptions of reuse,
recycling, repair, circular communication,
responsible consumption, sustainable sourcing,
waste reduction, and participation measure circular
economy marketing practices. Brand purpose is a
measure of perceived authenticity, social or
environmental purpose, values beyond profit, and
alignment with sustainability goals. Customer
engagement can be measured via interaction,
participation, sharing, emotion and campaign mend.
Suggestions for consumer advocacy measures
include brand defense, encouragement, experience
sharing, and positive word-of-mouth.

10. Results
The data analysis shows that the sample
respondents in the simulation view the circular
economy marketing practices and associated
branding outcomes positively. The mean score of
the circular economy marketing practice is above
the neutral midpoint. This indicates that the
hypothetical brands illustrated in the instrument
are perceived to communicate reuse, recycle, repair,
waste reduction, and responsible consumption. The
purpose development of the brands has also
recorded a positive mean which indicate that
respondents believe that such brands have an
objective which relates to sustainability and besides
profit making. Customer engagement is positive, but
relatively low, suggesting customer participation
would require more active encouragement than
mere awareness or endorsement.
Reliability results suggests that the measures are
internally consistent. The composite mean scores
can be used for the later statistical analysis since all
Cronbach alpha coefficients exceed .70. Due to this,
mediation and regression analysis require
reasonably reliable measures of the constructs. In
real life primary research, reliability should be
supported with a confirmatory factor analysis or
PLS-SEM measurement-model assessment
(composite reliability, average variance extracted,
discriminant validity, and diagnostics for common-
method bias).
The correlation matrix demonstrates that the
constructs have positive relationships. There is a
strong correlation between circular economy
marketing practices and brand purpose and
customer engagement, suggesting that circular
messages can influence both meaning and
engagement. Consumer advocacy shows a strong
relationship with all three predictors, which
indicates that having a circular practice, perceived
brand purpose, and engagement leads to advocacy.
Brand confidence and motivation to speak
positively towards a brand should be positively
correlated with advocacy. This correlation makes
theoretical sense.
Multiple regression analysis indicates that
marketing practices of circular economy, brand
purpose and customer engagement jointly predict
consumer advocacy. Even with the inclusion of
brand purpose (BP) and customer engagement (CE)
in the model, the direct effect of circular economy
marketing practices (CEMP) remains substantial.
According to the analysis, customer engagement
and brand purpose most influence brand equity.
This suggests purpose is meaningful but
engagement is the potentially more immediate
behavioural channel through which circular
marketing becomes advocacy.
The results of bootstrap mediation confirm both
mediation hypotheses. The indirect effect via brand
purpose is positive and statistically significant, as
the confidence interval does not include zero. The
customer engagement indirect effect is also positive
and significant with a relatively stronger magnitude.
Thus, the results are in line with H1 to H7.
According to the model, marketing practices of
circular economy influence consumer advocacy
through three routes–a direct route, a meaning-
based purpose route, and a participation-based
engagement route.

12. Managerial Implication.
First, brands should design circular economy
marketing campaigns around concrete practices
rather than vague environmental language.
Consumers ought to understand how the brand
tackles its waste, extends product life and enables
repair, uses recycled inputs and/or supports reuse.
In order to facilitate consumer involvement,
circularity must be delegitimized in operational
terms within the market.
Moreover, circular economy communication must
connect with genuine brand purpose. Purpose
shouldn't be a marketing message. It must rest on
consistent behaviors, transparent proof, and
internal coherence. When brands communicate
purpose without operational proof, consumers may
interpret this as greenwashing or purpose washing.
A realistic rationale statement should make clear
what broader social or environmental contribution
circular practices make.
Additionally, brands should create customer
engagement in circular campaigns. You can create
engagement via repair workshops, take-back drives,
refill stations, recycling challenges, user-generated
content, loyalty rewards for circular participation,
and transparent updates on environmental
outcomes. These actions encourage consumers to
believe in the efficacy of their actions. It also aids in
creating positive WOM and recommendations.
Brands should promote consumer advocacy too.
You can help make this impact advocacy by making
circular participation shareable, ensuring that
sustainability stories are clear, recognising
customer contributions, and creating community.
Brands need to provide customers with direct,
credible, and easy-to-explain messages so these
customers can become credible ambassadors of the
brand. Fifth, managers should analyze circular
marketing performance beyond sales. Some metrics
can be if consumers are engaging with the circular
content, number of returning products, number of
participating in repairs, number of refill adopters,
customer sustainability content creation, advocacy
intention, referral behaviour, and trust in circular
claims. These metrics offer a better understanding
of how circularity impacts consumers and brands.

The study’s findings contribute to sustainability
marketing theory through the concepts of circular
economy marketing and consumer advocacy. It
shows that advocacy can be conceived of as the
result of circular economy marketing and not just as
a loyalty or communication construct. This
broadens sustainable market outcomes from being
purchase-centred to also include pro-brand
behaviour.

14. Limitations of the Study Above
The dataset is a simulated dataset for educational
purpose, that’s the main limitation. The results
should not be considered as genuine consumer
proof. The relationship observed in controlled
experiments may differ from the real world. A
second limitation is the cross-sectional nature of the
design. Causal inference cannot be made, nor can
changes over time be examined. As consumers
become more familiar with repair, reuse, refill, or
take-back systems, they may participate in a circular
economy.
In addition, the research study uses self-reported
Likert scale measures. Sometimes in actual research
the self-report responses adversely affected due to
social desirability bias, common-method variance,
and limited recall. The proposed sample design uses
convenience sampling, which limits generalisability.
Fifth, the model does not take into account
moderators like those of environmental concern,
scepticism, income, product category, convenience,
perceived price fairness, and prior experience with
circular products. Strength of the proposed
relationships could change through these variables.
15. Future Research Directions.
The proposed model should be tested on real
primary data from consumers in different cities,
product categories and income segments in the
future. Researchers could compare the fashion,
electronics, food, personal care, and consumer
durables sectors as circular economy practices
differ across categories. Repairing and reselling
items may be most pertinent to electronics and
fashion, while refilling and packaging reduction may
be more relevant to personal care and food retail.
It will be interesting to have longitudinal research
on whether circular economy marketing engenders
sustained engagement and advocacy over time.
Experimental designs could test how different
message types (information-based, emotion-based,
and action-oriented circular communication)
influence perceptions of purpose and advocacy.
Future research may also apply structural equation
modelling to assess measurement validity and
evaluate the efficacy of partial mediation and serial
mediation models.
16. In summation
The study explored the link between circular
economy marketing practices and consumer
advocacy, while brand purpose and customer
engagement acted as mediators. The study
proposed that circular economy marketing can
generate advocacy directly through the signalling
effect and indirectly through stakeholder and
consumer-brand relationship perspective. A
simulated dataset of 300 respondents with
academic survey data was utilized to exemplify the
methods of descriptive, reliability, correlation and
regression analyses and bootstrap mediation
analysis.
The findings indicate that marketing practices
adopting the circular economy can positively relate
to consumer advocacy. In addition, it significantly
predicts brand purpose and customer engagement.
Advocacy benefits positively from the brand
purpose as well as customer engagement. According
to the mediation analysis, both brand purpose and
customer engagement have significant indirect
effects. The strength of the customer engagement
pathway indicates that circular economy marketing
may be most effective when customers engage with
brands on sustainability.
According to the study, marketing in the circular
economy should not be confined to product labels
and such other environmental messages. Brands
need to convert their circular practices into a
genuine purpose and meaningful engagement
opportunities for the consumers to create advocacy.
Purpose enables consumers to appreciate the
significance of a brand's circular actions.
Engagement enables consumers to participate and
share. The mechanisms can combine to turn
marketing of circular economy into voluntary
support.
This paper provides an integrated framework for
future empirical research and offers managers a
practical basis on which to design circular
campaigns intended to foster trust, participation,
and advocacy.
