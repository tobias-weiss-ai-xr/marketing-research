Abstract
Purpose – Despite the rapid adoption of artificial intelligence in talent acquisition, its
impact on the dual metrics of recruitment efficiency and effectiveness is characterised
by theoretical fragmentation. This study addresses this knowledge gap by synthesising
the complex interactions between artificial intelligence technologies and resourcing
practices, thereby moving beyond descriptive reporting to uncover the underlying
resourcing paradox in contemporary employee relations.
Methodology – Adhering to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and
Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines, we conducted a systematic literature review of 79
peer-reviewed articles published between 2002 and 2024. We used the antecedents-
phenomenon-impact framework to analyse the literature across multiple levels of
analysis (individual, organisational, and societal), consequently ensuring a rigorous
evidence-informed synthesis.
Findings – This research identifies eight distinct factors – ranging from candidate
experience and algorithmic bias to organisational performance and legal compliance
– that reconfigure the artificial intelligence-recruitment nexus. We identify a critical
tension: while artificial intelligence significantly enhances operational speed and data
processing (efficiency), it risks alienating candidates and introducing ethical ‘black
boxes’ that compromise long-term organisational health (effectiveness).
Practical implications – The study offers human resource practitioners and leaders
a strategic roadmap for navigating the transition to ‘Smart Human Resource
Management’. It underscores the critical role of human-machine collaboration,
proposing that optimal recruitment outcomes necessitate the integration of technical
automation with human-centric judgment to preserve employer brand equity and
uphold ethical integrity.
Originality – This paper contributes to the artificial intelligence-human resource
management literature through the development of a novel process-based
antecedents-phenomenon-impact framework. By addressing a pervasive theoretical
vacuum in current scholarship, it proposes a critical future research agenda centred

1. Introduction
With the advent of the Fifth Industrial Revolution, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has
emerged as a cornerstone of innovation and economic growth across diverse
occupations and industries worldwide (Chang et al., 2024; Felten et al., 2021). As
organisations embrace AI-powered solutions, recruitment processes are witnessing
significant transformations and heightened scholarly scrutiny (Albassam, 2023; Lee
and Cha, 2023); consequently, understanding the impact of AI on this evolving domain
of recruitment practice is imperative (Hammer and Karmakar, 2021). Specifically, AI
has been employed to automate the recruitment process, from the initial sourcing and
screening of CVs to final candidate selection, thus expediting the process (Tian et al.,
2023; Pessach et al., 2020). Leveraging machine learning algorithms, vast datasets
are analysed rapidly to identify optimal candidates based on predefined criteria, hence
streamlining recruitment workflows, optimising hiring decisions, and contributing to the
development of organisational capabilities (Bohmer and Schinnenburg, 2023). The
integration of AI into recruitment processes has yielded substantial benefits for
organisations (Minbaeva, 2020), including facilitating strategic interactions for
decision-makers (Gaessler and Piezunka, 2023), accelerating and optimising
recruitment processes (Reilly, 2018), and enhancing the candidate experience
(Mondragon, 2023). Scholars are increasingly acknowledging the potential benefits
and risks associated with integrating AI into recruitment processes, underscoring its

Despite this growing body of research, a critical ‘resourcing paradox’ has emerged.
While AI expedites recruitment process, the impact of this acceleration on managers
and employees remains largely unexplored, representing a significant gap in current
understanding (Cheng et al., 2022). Similarly, whilst AI enhances the efficacy of
candidate screening and selection, the ethical implications for, and effects on, job
applicants of AI-powered recruitment remain underexamined. This fragmentation stem
from disparate levels of analysis and diverse disciplinary perspectives, resulting in
inconsistent findings regarding the actual impact of AI within this domain.
Consequently, attaining clarity on the precise mechanisms through which AI influences
candidate sourcing, screening, and selection processes is imperative for human
resources (HR) practitioners gaining to optimise their recruitment strategies.
1.1 Problem statement
Despite the proliferation of AI tools in talent acquisition, the extant literature remains
characterised by a profound fragmentation that hinders both academic theory-building
and evidence-based practice. This fragmentation manifests in three principal
dimensions. First, a significant disciplinary divide exists. Research frequently remains
siloed, with computer science primarily focusing on algorithmic accuracy, while human
resource management prioritises social outcomes and employee relations. Second, a
persistent theoretical vacuum is evident, with nearly half of the extant scholarship
(48.1% of our sample) failing to utilise a formal theoretical framework. This leads to a
descriptive trap where studies report what AI does without adequately explaining why
it affects the psychological contract. Third, an imbalance in the level of analysis is
apparent. While the organisational benefits of efficiency and cost reduction are well-

1.2 Research questions and contributions
In response to these identified knowledge gaps, the present study addresses the
following research questions: (1) How does the integration of AI into recruitment and
selection processes impact the dual metrics of efficiency and effectiveness? (2) What
organisational factors facilitate or hinder the successful implementation of AI? (3) How
do candidates perceive and respond to AI-driven recruitment, and what are the
implications for employer branding?
This study offers four substantial contributions to the field of employee relations and
AI-HRM research. By synthesising 79 articles through a PRISMA-compliant lens, this
study presents the following. Firstly, a multi-process theoretical synthesis. This study
reveals how organisational outcomes are improved through AI integration (Mer et al.,
2023; Geetha and Bhanu, 2018). Moving beyond simple categorisation, this study
employs an Antecedents-Phenomenon-Impact (API) framework. The framework
facilitates the mapping of the causal chain of AI adoption, revealing how organisational
antecedents (such as the drive for productivity) translate into phenomena (such as
algorithmic screening) that ultimately impact societal and individual outcomes. This
contributes to knowledge advancement by providing a roadmap for future researchers

2.1 Search strategy and identification
A thorough search was conducted within two primary academic databases: EBSCO
(Business Source Complete) and Web of Science. These repositories were selected
for their comprehensive coverage of peer-reviewed literature in HR, management, and
applied technology. These databases were utilised to identify and verify peer-reviewed
articles, in accordance with a robust identification strategy (Kranzbühler et al., 2018).
To ensure a comprehensive literature review, we employed a sophisticated keyword
search strategy, incorporating terms related to AI and recruitment. The following
Boolean keyword formula was utilised:
(AI OR ‘artificial intelligence’ OR ‘intelligent automation’ OR ‘human-agent
interaction’ OR ‘robot-human interaction’ OR ‘natural language processing’ OR
‘machine learning’ OR ‘algorithms’) AND (recruitment OR ‘talent acquisition’
OR ‘recruitment automation’ OR ‘selection’ OR ‘sourcing’ OR ‘screening’ OR
‘assessment’ OR ‘hiring’ OR ‘candidate experience’ OR ‘workplace impact’)
AND (‘efficiency’ OR ‘effectiveness’ OR ‘performance’).
No temporal restrictions were imposed, with the objective of encompassing studies

2.2 Screening and selection process
The selection process prioritised articles published in academic journals, given their
role in facilitating knowledge advancement (Luo and Zhang, 2016). Consistent with
the methodology established systematic reviews, we restricted our sample to peer-
reviewed journals to ensure methodological rigour (Vrontis and Christofi, 2021). The
screening process adopted a three-stage funnel approach to ensure optimal thematic
alignment with our research questions: Stage 1: Preliminary Screening: Titles and
abstracts were independently reviewed by two researchers to identify articles directly
pertinent to the specified HR sub-dimensions: recruitment, talent acquisition, and
selection. This initial phase reduced the 1,685 unique records to 402 articles. Stage
2: Eligibility Assessment: A more granular relevance screening, predicated on
explicit focus and alignment with the research questions, further reduced the sample
to 153 articles. Stage 3: Full-Text Assessment: A comprehensive assessment of the
remaining 153 articles yielded a final sample of 79 relevant articles published between
January 2002 and November 2024. We implemented quality control checks, including
inter-rater agreement indices, to ensure the reliability and validity of the screening
process (Stumbitz et al., 2018).

and thematic focus: (1) Non-HR Context: Studies primarily addressing general AI
applications, pure computer science developments, or the technical performance of
algorithms, without explicit integration into recruitment practices or human resource

2.4 Data extraction and systematic coding
To transform the raw literature into an analytical synthesis, we employed a multi-step
qualitative coding approach, adhering to the rigour suggested by Danese et al. (2018).
This process addressed fragmentation within the literature through the application of
a structured and reproducible framework. The coding was executed in three distinct
phases: Phase 1: Descriptive Profiling: We documented foundational metadata for
each of the 79 articles, including publication outlet, methodology, industry context,
principal theoretical frameworks, geographic coverage, and author demographics.
Phase 2: Thematic Mapping (API Logic): We mapped the connections between AI
and recruitment using a thematic mapping approach grounded in the API logic (Pisani
et al., 2017). Findings were categorised into Antecedents (drivers of AI use), the
Phenomenon (implementation in the workplace), and the Impact (workplace outcomes
and implications). Phase 3: Synthesis and Categorisation: Recruitment processes
served as the overarching theme, which allowed us to cluster recurring patterns and
relationships into eight unique themes (e.g., candidate experience, bias and fairness,
efficiency). The coding scheme was validated through iterative researcher discussions
and pilot testing on a subset of articles to ensure consistency and accuracy.

3.1.2 Theoretical underpinnings and the ‘theoretical vacuum’
The role of theory is fundamental in social research for understanding the underlying
mechanisms and drivers of a phenomenon (Bryman, 2016; May and Perry, 2022). We
scrutinised how diverse theoretical perspectives – such as the Resource-Based view,
Cognitive Theory, and Social Exchange Theory – inform research questions and the
conceptualisation of AI’s impact on recruitment practices (Balcioğlu and Artar, 2024).
These frameworks facilitate a nuanced consideration of how technological innovations
generate competitive advantage or accentuate existing HR processes. Our analysis in
Table 2 reveals a broad spectrum of theories spanning disciplines such as computer
science, psychology, management, and sociology. However, a significant theoretical
vacuum is evident; nearly half of the sampled articles (38 of 79) lack an explicit
theoretical lens. This absence of theory is particularly prevalent among studies
focusing on organisational and multi-level units of analysis. Such diversity, coupled
with a lack of theoretical consensus, underscores the urgent need for a unified
theoretical framework to facilitate a more comprehensive interpretation of AI-driven
recruitment phenomena.

3.1.3 Methodological landscape
We assessed the appropriateness of various research methodologies – qualitative,
quantitative, and mixed methods – evaluating the impact of these choices on the
validity, reliability, and generalisability of findings (Pan et al., 2022). This approach
offers a comprehensive understanding of the strengths and limitations associated with
each methodological choice within the study of AI-driven recruitment. Empirical studies
constituted the majority of sampled articles (66.2%; n=47), employing a combination
of quantitative (49.3%; n=35), such as surveys and experiments, and qualitative
(16.9%; n=12) designs. Notably, only one study employed a mixed-methods approach,
suggesting a considerable underutilisation of this methodology within this domain.
Non-empirical approaches, comprising conceptual papers and literature reviews,
accounted for 32.4% (n=23). Furthermore, scholarly attention has predominantly
centred on organisational (45.1%) and multi-level (35.2%) units of analysis.
Conversely, the individual and societal levels remain comparatively neglected, which
in turn highlights critical avenues for future research.

disability, and personality (Jackson, 2021). To safeguard fairness, organisations must

4. Discussion
The integration of AI into the recruitment landscape represents significantly more than
a mere technological upgrade; it signifies a fundamental shift in the employer-
employee relationship and the psychological contract that underpins modern
resourcing. While our systematic review of 79 articles identifies eight core factors
driving this change, a deeper synthesis reveals a critical resourcing paradox. This
paradox emerges as organisations gain unprecedented operational efficiency and
predictive accuracy while simultaneously facing new risks of candidate alienation,
ethical black boxes, and a widening theoretical gap, necessitating judicious navigation
by practitioners.
4.1 Theoretical advancement and the knowledge gap
A primary finding of this review, which underscore a critical need for future scholarship,
is the prevalent theoretical vacuum characterising AI-recruitment research. With 38 of
the 79 sampled articles (48.1%) devoid of explicit theoretical grounding, the field
appears to be characterised by an application-first phase wherein practical
implementation significantly outpaces academic conceptualisation. By synthesising
the disparate literature, we argue that the API framework provides a necessary bridge
to transcend purely descriptive accounts. AI adoption is not a linear process but a
recursive one. For instance, while the Resource-Based View conceptualises AI as a
competitive asset for efficiency, it often omits consideration of tensions within Social
Exchange Theory, wherein candidates perceive AI as a breach of the human touch,
thereby impacting subsequent employee performance and retention. Consequently,

4.2 Reconciling Efficiency with Candidate Experience
The review highlights a significant tension inherent in the phenomenon stage of our
framework: AI demonstrates proficiency in automating routine tasks such as sourcing
and screening, this very automation often contributes to algorithmic alienation.
Empirical evidence suggests that AI-driven tools pose a considerable capacity to
streamline recruitment workflows, enhance the accuracy of candidate assessments,
and reduce time-to-hire (Mer et al., 2023; Geetha and Bhanu, 2018). However, this
efficiency introduces a notable trade-off for stakeholders; whilst AI enhances
objectivity, it can lead to the depersonalisation of the recruitment experience,
potentially undermining employer branding (Hu et al., 2019).
Moving beyond the mere identification of the 11.27% research gap in individual-level
studies, our synthesis reveals that such alienation constitutes a fundamental breach
of the psychological contract. When candidates primarily interact with a black box
system, the perceived lack of human agency and interpersonal warmth leads to
reduced organisational attractiveness and lower perceptions of person-organisation
fit. Candidates express profound concerns regarding privacy and fairness, perceiving
the cumulative digital encounters not as an efficient process, rather as an opaque
barrier to authentic organisational entry.

4.3 Ethical integrity and algorithmic fairness
Our findings echo the concerning bias in, bias out’ phenomenon prevalent in the
broader discourse on AI. While AI frequently promoted as a means of mitigating
human prejudice and enhancing the accuracy of assessments, it often, albeit
inadvertently, perpetuates historical biases embedded within its training datasets
(Mondragon, 2023). The potential for such biases raises significant concerns
regarding liability in employment contexts and the ethical ramifications of automated
practices. Research indicates that the pursuit of predictive accuracy is often at odds
with the promotion of equity and diversity. To address this challenge, the
implementation of AI must transcend a purely technical exercise to encompass
multidisciplinary ethical audits involving data scientists, ethicists, and human resource
professionals. Addressing concerns regarding privacy and fairness is not a mere legal
requirement but a strategic necessity to foster trust and acceptance within a global
workforce, which is increasingly sceptical of automated decision-making.

4.4 Practical Implications for HR Leaders
The integration of AI within the recruitment function presents significant implications
for practitioners, owing to the distinct nature of talent acquisition principles compared
to other HRM processes. This distinctiveness stems from their fundamental influence
on workforce quality and overall organisational capability. AI ameliorates specific
challenges, such as the management of substantial data volumes and the optimisation
of efficiency, which manifest with greater criticality and distinctiveness than in
administrative functions like payroll or benefits management. Consequently, it is
imperative for HR leaders to conceptualise AI as a strategic enabler, rather than a
mere replacement for human recruiters.
Optimal outcomes are realised through a hybrid model of human-machine
collaboration, wherein AI undertakes intensive data processing, whilst human
judgment remains crucial for assessing cultural fit and nuanced soft skills. The
operationalisation of this collaboration mandates the implementation by HR leaders of
specific functional mechanisms, as delineated in Table 3. Examples include AI-driven
job description optimisation to mitigate coded bias and predictive vacancy modelling
to enhance strategic foresight. Through the judicious application of these targeted
functionalities, organisations can substantially enhance operational velocity whilst
simultaneously preserving the rigorous human-centric oversight indispensable for
ethical integrity and employer brand stability.
Ultimately, this underscores the critical imperative for proactive strategic foresight and
socio-technical planning to ensure that AI integration is congruent with organisational
values. The upskilling of HR teams in digital literacy and ethical oversight is paramount
to ensure the acquisition of requisite knowledge for making informed, ethical decisions

4.5 Future Research Agenda and Limitations
Despite the rapid expansion of the literature, research remains predominantly
concentrated on organisational-level efficiency. Future studies should investigate the
long-term, individual, and societal impacts of AI on career advancement and labour
market patterns (Desiere and Struyven, 2021). There is a critical need for longitudinal
and comparative studies, particularly those comparing pre- and post-AI interventions,
in order to move beyond the limitations in ecological validity inherent in much of the
current cross-sectional research. Furthermore, the role of organisational readiness
and change management practices (Hradecky et al., 2022; Chowdhury et al., 2023)
deserves deeper exploration. Organisational factors such as culture, leadership, and
technological infrastructure are pivotal in determining the success of AI adoption. We
recommend that researchers strive to develop unified, novel frameworks that account
for the multi-dimensional impacts of AI resourcing, ultimately fostering a recruitment
ecosystem that is efficient, fair, and inclusive for the Fifth Industrial Revolution.
Notwithstanding the rigour of this systematic review, several limitations warrant
acknowledgement to contextualise the findings. First, a language bias arises from the
fact that the study was restricted to full-text articles published in English, potentially
omitting relevant localised insights from other regions. Second, the search strategy
was confined to two primary academic databases, EBSCO and Web of Science; while
these are comprehensive, this selection bias may have excluded pertinent studies
archived in specialised or niche repositories. Third, although we implemented inter-

impact of these technologies on recruitment practices and candidate experiences. By
delineating eight critical factors and the resourcing paradox, our study offers valuable
insights for both management scholars and practitioners. Consequently, sustained
collaboration among researchers, practitioners, and policymakers is imperative to
ensure that AI-powered recruitment not only optimises strategic decision-making but
also fosters human organisational success.
