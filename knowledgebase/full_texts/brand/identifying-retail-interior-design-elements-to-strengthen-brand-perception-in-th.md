ABSTRACT
The rapid growth of e-commerce has intensified showrooming behavior, where customers
evaluate products in-store but purchase them online. For Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises
(MSMEs) with limited physical resources, this poses a significant threat. Furthermore, there is
a lack of practical research addressing this challenge specifically through the strategic lens of
retail interior design. This study aims to identify strategic interior design elements that effectively
strengthen brand perception, awareness, and customer engagement to mitigate the showrooming
phenomenon. Employing a qualitative multiple-case study methodology, data were triangulated
through observations and interviews with customers, practitioners, and academics associated
with three local brands in Bandung (Niion, Humblezing, and Visval). The analysis identified
five primary spatial elements: consistent visual identity, storytelling through interior elements,
flexible display and layout, multisensory experiences, and seamless offline-online integration.
These findings not only provide empirical evidence that strengthens the theoretical framework
of Westermann et al. (2023) and deepens the research of Raja et al. (2023), but also complement
the studies by Wang and Wang (2022) and Frasquet and Ieva (2024), which have not thoroughly
highlighted the spatial aspects. Thus, the primary novelty of this research lies in bridging the gap
between retail interior design studies and the current showrooming challenge in Indonesia by
producing a strategic framework that MSMEs can apply to transform physical retail into a medium
for brand experience and recognition.

Showrooming behavior involves customers assessing products in physical retail
locations before purchasing them online (Kembau et al., 2024; Mele et al., 2024). Though
online shopping continues to rise, physical in-person product evaluation is still desired
(Gong et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2022). In the case of fashion and accessories, which rely
heavily on the senses for decision making, the opportunity to see, touch, and evaluate
items is a vital part of the buying process (Gensler et al., 2017; Rapp et al., 2015; Tang
et al., 2025), The online shopping experience and the lack of in-person contact trick
customers to think they can make a complete purchase without the need for physical
interaction, and the phenomenon of showrooming can be explained by this (Daunt &
Harris, 2017; Holkkola et al., 2023).
The phenomenon of showrooming is becoming an increasingly complex challenge,
especially for micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs) (Chopra, 2018; Febriani
et al., 2025). While some MSMEs have limitations on physical stores for direct customer
interaction (Al-Tayyar et al., 2021; Baldivia & Chowdhury, 2025). It has recently been
reported that most MSMEs, in Indonesia, due to issues around funding, human resources,
and managerial capacity, fail to open physical stores (Sashikirana & Prodjo, 2024).
This issue affects customer's brand perceptions and intimate emotional ties (Bettaieb
& Alsobahi, 2022; Zha et al., 2025). Such emotional ties aid in brand awareness and
customer in engagement.
No physical stores or poor quality physical stores may create customer perceptions
around quality, and negatively impact purchase intent (Brengman & Karimov, 2012;
Mehra et al., 2012; Phamthi et al., 2024). This can be seen within the context of the 7Ps
marketing framework, particularly the ‘physical evidence’ component of the marketing
mix (Alli & Sazwan Mohd Rashid, 2019; Išoraitė, 2021). In a retail context, attest to the
fact that physical evidence constitutes a major factor that shapes customer perceptions
and feelings. In a retail environment, physical evidence manifests primarily through the
design of the space, which includes the layout, the lights, the colors, the arrangement of
merchandise displays, and the overall atmosphere of the store (Dewanti & Nirawati, 2023;
Rahmanto & Ratnasari, 2023).  Aside from the aesthetic considerations, these aspects help
inform perceptions regarding a brand's value and identity (Minerva & Kusumowidagdo,
2024; Park et al., 2015). Therefore, in the showrooming era, the value of the physical
aspects of retailing has diminished.
Beyond transactions, physical retail motivates and inspires customers, strengthens
emotional attachment via interactions with sales personnel, and supports offline-to-online
channel integration (Frasquet & Ieva, 2024). Retail architectures can elicit, and reinforce,
memories and loyalty through multisensory brand cues (Westermann et al., 2023). This
corresponds with marketing literature that details how all five senses can be utilized in

Furthermore, previous studies indicate that integrating visual identity into retail interior
elements such as layout, color, lighting, and furniture effectively shapes positive brand
perception and strengthens consumer emotional engagement (Raja et al., 2023). However,
these studies have yet to link the implementation of such visual and spatial elements as
a psychological strategy to combat the showrooming phenomenon, particularly for local
MSMEs operating with limited physical resources. Consequently, this creates a practical
knowledge gap for MSMEs that are just beginning to implement physical retail strategies.
To help MSMEs gain practical strategies to improve their design and equity, and
engagement in the current Indonesia showrooming context, this study seeks to examine and
understand the basic interior design elements that can assist in this. This study integrated
design best practices from three local retailers in Bandung to reach this goal. Data from
practitioner and academic expert interviews, customer interviews, and observations were
combined and translated into strategic recommendations, theoretically contributing to
the scholarship of showrooming design and, practically, to design guides for MSMEs.

METHODOLOGY
Given the exploratory nature of this study, a qualitative method is the most appropriate.
Understanding the complexities of the meanings, interpretations, and experiences
customers have within the retail environment is multifaceted and, in some cases,
ambiguous, and therefore cannot be captured solely through quantitative means (Baxter
& Jack, 2015; Creswell & Creswell, 2018; Shenton, 2004). There are also dimensions of
the research problem that quantitative approaches are unable to uncover.
More specifically, this research is a collective case study. This type of research was
chosen because the phenomenon being studied concerns the interaction between elements
of interior design and their impact on customer perceptions, and is closely tied to the
real-world context of physical retail and cannot be separated from its environment (Stake,
1995; Yin, 2018). Through case studies, it is possible to conduct a more complete analysis
by examining several cases in the context of physical retail strategies to identify cross-
case patterns and develop new theoretical syntheses that can be applied to other contexts
(Abdussamad, 2015; Eisenhardt, 1989).
Sampling Methods and Selection Criteria
The selection of analysis objects, in the form of case studies and informants, used purposive
sampling, whereby subjects were selected based on their relevance and the wealth of
information they could provide to answer the research questions (Ahmad & Wilkins,
2025; Palinkas et al., 2015; Soewardikoen, 2021). The following are the sampling criteria
used in this study.
Case Study Selection
The selection of case study objects was based on similarities in industry category and
target customers, to ensure the analysis results had contextual validity (Kotler & Keller,
2016).  Out of the vast population of creative MSMEs in Bandung, these three brands
were specifically selected based on four criteria. First, they represent MSMEs specializing
in bag production that embrace an urban lifestyle concept. Second, these brands have
established strong physical retail strategies that serve as effective mediums for strategic
interior design application. Third, they demonstrate high levels of brand awareness and
customer engagement, which is clearly evidenced by their widespread popularity, massive
social media following, and loyal customer base. Fourth, each brand possesses a strong
and distinct visual identity.

This study deliberately limited its focus to exactly three case studies. This decision
aligns with the rationale that a multiple-case design featuring three cases provides an ideal
and sufficient baseline for achieving theoretical replication and identifying consistent
cross-case patterns without reaching data saturation (Yin, 2018). Moreover, restricting
the scope to three brands enables a more profound exploration and understanding of
consumers’ psychological experiences and spatial perceptions (Creswell & Creswell,
2018). The profiles of the selected case studies are presented below.
i. Niion, established in 2013 in Bandung, is known for its functional products and neon
aesthetic visual identity. Its attractive identity is reflected in its physical retail stores,
which are designed to resemble exhibition spaces showcasing a collection of products
from the past 10 years, making its marketing strategy adaptable.
ii. Humblezing, established in 2011 in Bandung with an urban-outdoor concept, has
a physical retail store designed thematically to reflect a consistent brand identity,
strengthening the brand image through the shopping experience and reinforcing the
image of local brands.
iii. Visval, established in 2024 in Bandung, is known for its active collaboration with local
communities and creators. It is also a local brand that applies customer behavior and
brand narratives to strengthen its brand development strategy.

Data Collection
Data collection was conducted using three methods to ensure data depth, as follows.
Systematic non-participant observation was conducted at three case study retail locations
to analyze the application of interior elements. The focus of the observation was based
on theoretical parameters sourced from research by Westermann et al. (2023) on retail
interior elements as visual and spatial indicators that can be physically observed through
the creation of atmosphere and brand image, as well as research by Ustazah et al. (2025)
on spatial experience elements by breaking down the spatial experience into multisensory
indicators consisting of visual, auditory, olfactory, tactile, and spatial layout that play a
role in the potential for increasing brand awareness. Therefore, the subjects observed
include.
i. The physical area of the store includes the facade, main display zone, circulation, and
interaction area.
ii. Customer behavior in the space, movement patterns, and attention focus points.
iii. Visual identity consisting of logos, color palettes, graphic elements, and decorative
elements that represent the brand image.
Observations were conducted thoroughly to record visual and non-visual details, such as
the atmosphere of the space, level of comfort, ease of navigation, and customer responses
to specific interior elements. Interactions between customers and staff were also noted to
assess the role of spatial design in supporting communication and emotional engagement.
Observation times were conducted on both weekdays and weekends. The selection of
these times affects consumer behavior, as the level of consumer density on weekends has
the potential to create differences in circulation patterns, product observation focus, and
social interactions within the retail area.

THEORETICAL CONTRIBUTIONS AND NOVELTY
The five identified elements correlate with one another and provide theoretical contributions
that strengthen and complement previous research. The first finding regarding consistent
visual identity validates the framework of Westermann et al. (2023), proving that brand
cues such as color, materials, and logos serve not merely as aesthetic decorations, but as
crucial stimuli or initial exposure for building brand recall. The subsequent finding, which
highlights interior elements as effective storytelling media, deepens the findings of Raja
et al. (2023) by demonstrating that spatial storytelling is an effective attention-grabbing
strategy in countering the showrooming threat. Furthermore, the findings on multisensory
experiences expand upon Krishna (2012)  research, showing that stimulating the five
senses within a physical retail space acts as a primary differentiator; it emotionally
validates quality perception through a comprehensive experience that cannot be replicated
through digital media.
The next finding regarding space-based offline-online integration successfully
fills the research gap identified by Frasquet and Ieva (2024) concerning showrooming
behavior. While their study tended to view digital and physical integration merely as the
functional application of technology such as QR codes or digital catalogs this research
finds that such digital integration will fail to keep consumers within the brand ecosystem
unless supported by visual consistency (brand fit) between the physical atmosphere
and the digital media. Additionally, these findings complement the work of Wang and
Wang (2022), who emphasized the importance of direct interaction in physical retail to
encourage loyal showrooming, by specifically detailing the spatial elements that shape
brand perception.
Consequently, the novelty of this research lies not only in the identification of spatial
elements, but in the development of a conceptual framework that maps how physical

retail interior design influences consumer perception and behavior. This study offers

As a practical implementation guide, this framework can be applied by MSMEs
through continuous spatial stages. First, by establishing strong initial exposure through
consistent visual identity—such as color, materials, and logo placement—to capture
consumers’ attention upon entering the space. Second, once attention is secured, the
physical space is utilized as a medium for storytelling and multisensory stimulation
to foster emotional attachment to the product’s quality and value. Third, this physical

This allows consumers to transition to the brand’s digital platforms such as e.g., scanning
a QR code or viewing a digital catalog without feeling detached from the physical
atmosphere. Through the application of these practical stages, MSMEs can effectively
transform the threat of showrooming into loyal showrooming.
CONCLUSION
Based on the research questions, this study concludes that to face the showrooming
phenomenon, MSMEs can implement five essential interior design elements: consistent
visual identity, storytelling through interior elements, display and layout flexibility,
multisensory experiences, and offline-online integration. These five elements are proven
to act synergistically to transform the function of physical retail from a transactional
medium into an engaging brand experience space, which directly influences brand
perception positively, strengthens brand awareness, and increases customer engagement.
Overall, this research provides three key contributions. First, theoretically—in
terms of knowledge development—this study enriches the literature on interior design
and consumer behavior by proving that spatial elements are not merely aesthetic, but
act as effective psychological stimuli to combat the showrooming phenomenon. Second,
practically, this research produces a conceptual spatial design framework that can be
measurably applied in designing MSME retail spaces. Third, for the user subjects, namely
MSMEs, these findings offer a strategic solution so that business owners are not trapped
in e-commerce price competitions; instead, they can utilize their physical retail to build
deep consumer loyalty (loyal showrooming).
Despite these contributions, this study has several limitations that should be noted.
The scope of this research is limited to the fashion MSME sector, specifically bag
products in Bandung; thus, the identified design elements may require adjustment if
applied to other sectors, such as culinary retail. Furthermore, the consumer respondents
were predominantly aged 18–25, meaning that the spatial perceptions formed strongly

iii. Testing the application of new technologies, such as Augmented Reality (AR) or the
