Abstract
The dynamic relationship between technology,
communication, and consumption has
transformed contemporary brand identity design,
encouraging the adoption of dynamic identity
systems. This study examines the impact of
dynamic identity design on branding and aims to
contribute a theoretical framework for
understanding its role in contemporary visual
communication. It examines corporate identity,
dynamic identity, and branding concepts.  A
qualitative literature review and analytical case
study approach are employed to examine and
classify selected national and international
examples according to established graphic
design principles. The findings indicate that
dynamic identity systems can strengthen brand
perception by maintaining visual consis tency
while enabling controlled variation across
different communication contexts. The study
further proposes considering colour as an
independent variable within dynamic identity
classification models, contributing to a more
comprehensive understanding of  how variation
mechanisms can support brand recognition,
differentiation, and continuity.

1 INTRODUCTION

In today's complex and constantly evolving business environment, as visual
stimuli proliferate at an unprecedented rate, there is an ever-growing need for innovative
and effective design approaches that can capture and hold human attention. Particularly
over the past decade, as digitalization has accelerated, the necessity for brands to maintain
simultaneous presence across multiple platforms has prompted a fundamental
reconsideration of identity systems. Traditional, static visual identity systems have proven
increasingly inadequate for meeting these demands, largely because they fail to fully
embody the inherent dynamism, fluidity, and transformative nature of contemporary life.
This shift constitutes a strategic imperative. Visual identity extends far beyond logos and
color systems. It constitutes a holistic process aimed at delivering consistent yet dynamic
experiences at every point of contact (Wheeler, 2017). While conventional corporate
identity relies on fixed logos, immutable color palettes, and standardized ty pography,
dynamic identity systems, with their variable and adaptive structures, have emerged as
contemporary visual tools that effectively address modern branding needs. As Rawsthorn
(2007) argued, variability has become an essential component of brand communication.
This study examines the significance of dynamic identity concepts, explores
design methodologies, and investigates its implications throughout the branding process.
Given that dynamic identity remains a relatively nascent area of research, significant
conceptual gaps persist within academic discourse. The central research question is
framed as follows: "How do the variation formats of dynamic identity designs influence
brand perception and visual identity within the branding proces s?" This investigation
draws upon theoretical frameworks developed by Irene van Nes, Ulrike Felsing, and
Martin Lorenz, employing comparative analysis across internationally recognized case
studies.
The objectives of this research are to examine dynamic identity design from a
graphic design perspective, to identify and classify the fundamental variation formats
employed in dynamic identity systems, and to assess their contributions to brand
perception. The study conducts comparative analysis of dynamic identity classifications
that have received limited attention in existing literature, thereby contributing to
theoretical discourse within graphic design.

2 THEORETICAL PERSPECTIVE ON DYNAMIC IDENTITY DESIGN

Brand identity has historically been constructed primarily on principles of stability
and consistency, with the assumption that recognizability is directly correlated with visual
uniformity an understanding that has remained foundational to design practice  for
decades. However, the acceleration of digitalization and the proliferation of multi -
platform environments have fundamentally challenged this established paradigm. Brands
compelled to maintain simultaneous presence across diverse media channels have begun
to recognize an increasingly pressing need not merely for consistency, but for identity
structures that are simultaneously context -sensitive, adaptable, and scalable. As a
consequence of this necessity, a new conceptual framework has emerged within the field
of visual identity: dynamic identity.
Dynamic identity systems are defined as flexible structures in which logos, color
palettes, typography, and visual elements operate within predetermined rule -based
frameworks, continuously interacting with context. It is important to emphasize here that
these systems are not merely concerned with generating visual variety. Rather, what
proves most decisive is the systemic thinking itself that enables the identity to emerge.
The parameter diagrams developed by Lorenz (Figure 1) articulate at a visual level t he
relational structures that characterize the variables constituting visual identity such as
orientation, form, color, texture, and scale thereby grounding the abstract logic of
dynamic identity systems within a tangible framework. Within this approach, i dentity
ceases to be reduced to a single logo and instead becomes a comprehensive structure
capable of continuous regeneration within predefined parameters across different
contexts (Lorenz, 2021).

grounded in controlled, structured, and sustainable diversity. In this capacity, dyn amic
identity systems provide brands with the means to establish agile, interactive, and
meaningful communication languages suited to the demands of the digital age.

3 CORPORATE IDENTITY AND THE BRANDING PROCESS

Corporate identity, which can be defined as the totality of visual and discursive
relations an institution establishes with the external world, extends far beyond a mere
logo or color system. According to Olins, corporate identity functions as a coherent
system of representation encompass ing an organization's values, culture, and
communication practices, serving both internal and external stakeholders. Kapferer, for
his part, examines brand identity through a six -dimensional prism model, arguing that
physical appearance, personality, culture, relationship, reflection, and self-image work in
concert to construct a strong and consistent brand identity. This framework provides an

important reference point for understanding the layered structure of dynamic identity
systems (Kapferer, 2008). Within this context, corporate identity can also be read as a
translation process in which abstract institutional culture is transformed into co ncrete
visual and linguistic instruments (Olins, 2008). Indeed, when examining the historical
development of corporate identity, one observes that this field has progressively assumed
an increasingly strategic and multifaceted character; identity is no longer merely a design
concern but has become a fundamental management tool that determines an
organization's existential positioning (Clifton, 2014).
Branding, by contrast, constitutes a multi -stage process constructed upon this
identity infrastructure, aimed at creating a lasting and meaningful perception in the minds
of target audiences. This process comprises successive and mutually reinforcing stage s,
including target audience identification, competitive landscape analysis, the definition of
brand purpose and value proposition, the identification of critical touchpoints in customer
experience, the implementation of brand strategy, and the establishme nt of feedback
mechanisms (Apaydın, 2014; Tam, 2009). Aaker examines brand identity from four
perspectives product, organization, personality, and symbol arguing that brand equity
must be managed as a long -term and strategic asset. Within this framework, i dentity is
positioned not merely as a visual expression but as a foundational structure that guides
organizational value propositions and communication strategies (Aaker, 1996). Clifton
emphasizes that successful brand construction requires a balance betwe en data -driven
analysis and creative, strategic thinking. The organic relationship between corporate
identity and the branding process constitutes one of the fundamental axes upon which
dynamic identity design is also grounded (Olins, 2008; Clifton, 2014).

3.1 Static and dynamic identity systems

Visual identity systems are fundamentally positioned between two opposing poles
based on their structural characteristics: static systems rooted in principles of
immutability, and dynamic systems grounded in transformability. Traditional, or what we
might call static, identity systems establish corporate identity around a single, fixed
design element. In these systems, logos, colors, and typography are applied without
variation within specific standards, thereby ensuring visual unity and continuity across

all communication materials. However, the limitations of this approach have become
increasingly apparent in today's rapidly transforming digital landscape. For brands
required to function across different platforms, screen sizes, and cultural contexts, a rigid
and unchanging identity system becomes a constraint that restricts communicative
flexibility (Ergüven, 2011).
Dynamic identity systems, by contrast, represent a response to overcoming these
limitations. They are structured as frameworks capable of renewing themselves through
multiple variations across different times and contexts. Visual components such as
backgrounds, colors, and typography can shift within predetermined rules, and the system
can be fed by real-time data. In this way, brand identity transcends its role as a static, one-
directional representation and becomes an interactive communication language th at
updates continuously with audiences and responds to context. As Rawsthorn and Nes
observe, this transformation represents a shift whereby the brand becomes not merely
something that is seen, but something that is experienced and felt (Rawsthorn, 2007; Nes,
2012).

3.2 The potential effects of dynamic identities on brand perception

Contemporary digital and cultural transformations have made it necessary for
corporate identities to evolve beyond traditional static forms toward more flexible and
dynamic expressions. Dynamic identities accomplish far more than simply increasing
visual v ariety. They also trigger feelings of curiosity and loyalty in viewers, creating
conditions for deeper and more sustained relationships with brands. These systems are
particularly favored in creative sectors such as technology, art, and fashion because they
reflect the transformative and innovative nature of the brand (Ergüven, 2011; Nes, 2012).
In this way, dynamic identity transcends its role as merely a visual medium and becomes
a powerful instrument that shapes brand cultural positioning and communication strategy.
As Martin Lorenz emphasizes, the process must begin with clear and detailed
definitions of who the brand addresses, when it communicates, where it does so, and how.
The target audience's communication habits and expectations must be carefully analyzed
to determine the system's visual language and tone. Additionally, it should be clarified in
which media types the flexible systems will be applied, considering the distinct technical

4 RESEARCH METHODOLOGY AND TECHNIQUES

We used a case study approach to understand how dynamic identity designs shape
branding outcomes. This method works well for examining real phenomena in their actual
contexts (Yin, 2018), which mattered here since dynamic identity involves more than just
how things look, it's tied to broader branding efforts and communication approaches.
Six brands formed our sample: Melbourne, AOL, EDP, New Museum, OCAD
University, and Nordkyn. We picked them because they actively use dynamic identity
systems, show different variation strategies, maintain strong digital presence, and show
up frequently in relevant scholarship.
To gather information, we looked at brand websites, corporate identity materials,
social media, and published research. We organized the visual content by key components
including logos, typefaces, colors, backgrounds, shapes, movement, and interaction
elements. Each brand's identity revealed what stayed fixed and what shifted.
We analyzed the material by looking at content systematically and comparing
visuals side by side. We sorted the variations into four types: formal changes, color shifts,
background adjustments, and movement or interaction changes. This helped us see how
the stable parts of each identity worked with the changeable parts, and how well this
balance supported recognition, consistency, adaptability, and message delivery.
The case study method gave us good insight into each system's specific context.
That said, we only examined six examples, and we didn't test these ideas experimentally
or gather user  feedback. So our conclusions apply to these particular cases. To really
confirm what we found, researchers would need to run experiments and talk directly to
audiences.

5 FINDINGS AND DISCUSSION

The six examples examined show that dynamic identity systems work differently
depending on each brand's situation. Rather than following one fixed approach,
Melbourne, AOL, EDP, New Museum, OCAD University, and Nordkyn each adapted
their systems to match their corporate structure, industry, audience, and how they wanted
to communicate. What they had in common was keeping a stable core element for
recognition while allowing other parts to change based on context. This pattern reveals
that these systems aren't random visual changes but follow deliberate design rules.
The variations in these brands weren't just about making things look different.
They served a practical purpose. The systems helped brands stay flexible in today's
communication landscape while keeping things consistent across platforms and building
stronger connections with audiences. When used on digital platforms especially, these
identities let brands adapt quickly while maintaining their overall integrity.
The brands studied demonstrate several variation strategies: changes to form,
color, background, content, user involvement, and real -time data. Each approach shows
how stable and changeable elements can work together to influence how people perceive
the brand and what the brand can accomplish in its marketing efforts.

6 SYSTEMATIC CLASSIFICATION AND PRACTICAL APPLICATIONS IN
DYNAMIC IDENTITY DESIGN

Corporate identity systems do not result from the random assembly of independent
elements. Rather, they achieve coherence through the coordinated functioning of core
components such as logos, colors, typography, graphic elements, imagery, and language
within specific relational structures. According to Nes, when one or several of these
components exhibit variability, they introduce flexibility and reproducibility to the
identity system (Nes, 2012). Within this framework, Nes classifies dynamic identities
under six categories: container, wallpaper, DNA, formula, customized, and generative.
Each category explains which component of the identity system produces variability and
how this variability is structured. These categories provide a systematic answer to t he
question of how recognizability can be maintained despite visual differentiation (Nes,

permutation, and open form -transfer. Felsing's classification particularly clarifies how
flexible design systems can be structured for cultural institutions and organizations

The theoretical frameworks developed by Nes and Felsing establish important
reference points for the classification of dynamic identity systems. However, a notable
gap appears in both approaches: color is not addressed as an independent and determining
design parameter. Yet color represents an integral component of dynamic identity systems
through its direct impact on brand perception, emotional association, and visual
differentiation. This study argues, proceeding from this gap, that color should be
positioned as an independent variable within dynamic identity structures.

6.1 Cases where internal form and color change while typography and shape remain
fixed

One of the most commonly applied approaches within dynamic identity systems
is based on maintaining fixed external boundaries of a form while continuously varying
its interior surfaces through different patterns, colors, and textures. In these structures,
called "container" and "wallpaper" in Nes's classification, graphic variations applied
within a stable form provide flexibility to the identity (Nes, 2012). The "masks and grids"
method developed by Felsing addresses the same principle from a different ang le,
defining the creation of variable internal structures over a fixed external form as a
modular system (Jochum, 2013).
In this approach, while typography and basic geometric structure are preserved as
fixed reference points, the  visual diversity of the identity is enhanced through visual
components positioned on the interior surface. Thus the system becomes a hybrid
structure capable of simultaneously producing both corporate continuity and visual
variety. In this context, the relationship between fixed external form and variable internal
structure is considered one of the fundamental design strategies of dynamic identity
systems.

6.1.1 The Melbourne dynamic identity design

This identity system, developed for the city of  Melbourne by Landor Associates
in 2009, represents a distinctive and successful example of applying dynamic design
principles at the urban scale. The fundamental logic of the system is based on the
combined use of variable patterns, colors, and light -shadow effects within a fixed "M"
letterform. The geometric structure of the logo facilitates its application across different
media and scales, ensuring visual consistency in distinctly different environments such
as print, digital, and outdoor settings. Mean while, color, pattern, and texture variations
on the interior surface reflect the city's multilayered structure, cultural diversity, and
innovative spirit at a visual level (Nes, 2012).

6.2.1 The AOL dynamic identity design

AOL is a web platform providing services at a global scale. In 2009, following its
separation from its former parent company Time Warner, it partnered with the New York
office of Wolff Olins to reconstruct the brand's perception across its various business
divisions. The majority of dynamic designs applied to the background of the AOL identity
consist of work by artists and musicians (Nes, 2012). Wolff Olins conceptualized the
AOL visual identity system not merely as logo design but as a platform for conten t. In
the typographic treatment, the letterforms are consistently rendered in white. This identity
system has been enriched with content encompassing various art forms including
photography, illustration, sketching, graphics, and video (Felsing, 2010). The  AOL
system is grounded in a background-and-layer approach, where typographic elements and
graphic style remain fixed while the background can be refreshed with different content
on each occasion.

6.3 Cases where background, color, and internal form change while typography or
shape remains stable

A significant structural model of dynamic identity systems maintains the
typographic structure or core form as the defining element while secondary visual
components such as background, color, and internal form exhibit variability. This
approach rests on a balanced relationship between a stable visual elem ent forming the
identity's core and dynamic components that diversify around it. Consequently, the
system provides both high recognizability and flexible structures capable of adapting to
different contexts.
Nes explains this type of dynamic identity structure through the concept of
"DNA," noting that a limited number of basic visual units can combine according to
specific rules to generate numerous variations (Nes, 2012). In this framework, the design
system functions much like a genetic structure, gaining operational capacity through
controlled combinations of stable and variable components. Felsing addresses a similar
approach using the concepts of “permutation”, “transformation”, and “modules”
emphasizing t hat dynamic identities are constructed through modular structures that
repeat yet differentiate with each iteration (Felsing, 2010).

In this model, the typography or formal element held constant serves as a reference
point that ensures identity continuity. By contrast, the variable components of
background, color, and internal form enable the identity to adapt to different content,
environments, and communication needs. This maintains balance between continuity and
variability in visual identity systems while supporting the brand in producing a visual
language that is simultaneously stable and contemporary (Wheeler, 2017).
Furthermore, this approach directly relates to modular design and systems
thinking. The capacity to recombine design components within specific rules increases
the scalability of the identity and facilitates its integration across different channels.
Particularly in digital environments, variable color and background allow the identity to
be reshaped according to user interaction, content type, or contextual data. In this respect,
dynamic identity transitions from being a fixed representational tool to become a process-
oriented and generative communication system (Manovich, 2001).
Within this framework, the model structured around a stable core and variable
visual components provides a balanced configuration between control and diversity in
dynamic identity design. The EDP dynamic identity system, one practical application of
this approach, stands out as a noteworthy example in terms of visual diversity produced
through combinations of fixed form with variable color and internal structure. In the
following section, EDP's dynamic identity design will be examined in detail.

6.4 Cases where a single layer exists and shape, typography, and color within that
layer can change

In another structural configuration of dynamic identity systems, variability is
produced within a single design layer through simultaneous transformation of the form,
typography, and color that compose that layer. In this approach, a design language
anchored through specific rules and grid systems ensures both visual and discursive
consistency of the identity (Nes, 2012). In this context, the variability of the design stems
not from multiplicity across layers but from parametric transformation of elements within
a single surface. Through this method, the system achieves a structure capable of
generating substantial diversity with a limited number of components.
In such systems, the transformation of formal elements is not arbitrary but occurs
within predetermined rules. Grid structures, typographic arrangements, and color

6.6 Cases where brand ıdentity changes through real -time data received from the
external world

The most advanced and technology -intensive form of dynamic identity systems
comprises data-driven models in which brand identity establishes direct engagement with
real-time data from the external environment. In this approach, identity ceases to be a
fixed visual representation and becomes a system continuously regenerated according to
environmental, social, and digital data flows. Immediate and unpredictable variables such
as weather conditions, social media interactions, visitor traffic, or economic indicators
function as parameters directly affecting the visual form of identity (Nes, 2012).
Within this context, the design process extends beyond applying predetermined
visual rules to become a dynamic structure operating in continuous data exchange with
the external world. Brand identity is thus redefined not as representing a visual form but
as a communication system that responds immediately to changing conditions and
continuously updates itself. Felsing addresses this situation through the concept of
"Interaction: Transfer, Open Form," emphasizing that through the identity's open form
structure it can interact with external data flows and undergo transformation (Felsing,
2010).

7 CONCLUSION

This study aimed to examine the effects of dynamic identity designs, which are
gaining increasing significance in graphic design, on the branding process. The study
addressed how variation formats influence brand perception and visual identity,
classifying dynamic identity variations into four fundamental categories: formal
variation, color variation, background or contextual variation, and movement or
interaction variation.The analyses demonstrate that variation in dynamic identity systems
is not limited to uniform visual change. Rather, each system develops a distinct variability
model according to its institutional structure, target audience, and communication
strategy. The examined cases of Melbourne, AOL, EDP, New Museum, OCAD
University, and Nordkyn reveal different ways of balancing stable and variable elements
to create unique identity structures. Thus, dynamic identity designs not only produce
aesthetic diversity but also function as structural extensions of brand communication
