Abstract
Retail availability and brand recognition shape the fortunes of regional dairy
brands in India, yet few studies document how routine field activities
contribute to both. This paper examines the market survey, retailer
engagement and brand awareness work carried out for Govind Milk and Milk
Products in Pune during an On -the-Job Training (OJT) assignment from 15
May to 15 July 2026. The four objectives were to document product
availability and visibility, examine retailer requirements, complaints and
lead-generation opport unities, assess consumer responses during society -
based activities, and identify practical challenges and improvements. A
descriptive and exploratory design was used. Primary data came from field
observations, retailer and consumer interactions, Google For ms entries and
GPS-tagged photographs, supported by peer-reviewed literature and official
sources. A practical field-coverage approach, not probability sampling, was
followed. The field team visited 460 outlets (200 grocery shops, 120 medical
shops and 140  bakeries), reported 200+ leads and 90 complaints, and
interacted with 550+ consumers in two residential societies; a separate
analytical base of 220 was used for selected indicators. Ghee, chaas, basundi,
curd and buttermilk were commonly observed as avai lable, curd attracted
notable consumer interest, and shoppers frequently compared Govind with
Amul. Retailer time constraints and limited brand awareness were the main
practical barriers. The paper recommends structured lead and complaint
tracking, a retailer communication kit, availability monitoring, a clear differentiation message and measurable
performance indicators. The findings are descriptive and are not statistically representative of Pune or the Indian dairy
market.
Keywords: dairy marketing, retailer engagement, brand awareness, market survey, FMCG, regional brands
1. Introduction
India is the world's largest milk producer. Official statistics estimate production at 247.87 million tonnes in 2024–25, a
rise of 3.58% over 239.30 million tonnes in 2023 –24, with Maharashtra among the leading producing states (Ministry
of Fisheries, Animal Husbandry & Dairying [MoFAHD], 2025). Scale of supply, however, does not guarantee success
for an individual brand. In fresh and value-added categories such as curd, buttermilk, ghee and traditional desserts, many
brands offer similar basic products, so purchase depends on whether the product is on the shelf, how its price and quality
are perceived, and whether the buyer recognises and trusts the name. Because dairy products are bought frequently from
nearby outlets, the retailer is a decisive point of contact.
Three activities connect these concerns. Market surveys record what is actually stocked, requested and complained
about. Retailer engagement turns a single visit into a working relationship that supports display and recommendation.

Govind Milk and Milk Products is a Maharashtra dairy company with a corporate office in Baner, Pune and a
manufacturing headquarters in Phaltan (Govind Milk and Milk Products, n.d.). Its portfolio spans milk, curd, chaas,
ghee, paneer, lassi, basundi, shrikhand and related products, and its stated positioning is value to farmers and quality to

experience into a structured case study. It reviews relevant scholarship, states the gap the case addresses, describes the
method, analyses the reported figures, and offers recommendations while separating field observation from published
evidence and from the author's interpretation.
2. Review of Literature
2.1 Consumer preferences for dairy products
Studies of dairy choice consistently point to quality, price and availability. A review of more than twenty articles found
that quality and nutrient content were the major influences on dairy choice, with price significant and promotion
following in the ma rketing mix (Parasuraman et al., 2021). A multiple -regression study of Indian consumers likewise
identified quality, health consciousness, price and availability as the strongest influences (Bahety et al., 2024). Research
in a retail setting in Chennai reported that product safety and taste were the main reasons for buying at university outlets,
which ranked third as a source after supermarkets and specialised outlets (Shree et al., 2025). Evidence from Poland
ranked sensory and functional impressions and the use -by date highest while confirming that marketing stimuli also
matter (Grębowiec, 2021), and a logistic-regression study of ghee in Bengaluru linked brand preference to flavour, shelf
life and consumption experience among other factors (Sivaram, 2016).
These studies are useful but limited. Most rely on stated preferences from consumer samples in a few cities, and the
review by Parasuraman et al. (2021) synthesises earlier work rather than collecting new data. They rarely observe what
retailers stock. Awareness research is more suggestive: 53% of 1,112 Bengaluru consumers knew of probiotic dairy
products but only 23% consumed them (Kumar et al., 2026), and a pan-India yogurt study (n = 409) described a push-
marketing market in which friends and relatives were the main information source and supermarkets the main purchase
point (Singh et al., 2026). Both suggest that awareness and purchase can diverge, though their samples differ from the
grocery, medical and bakery outlets covered here.
2.2 Brand awareness, equity, value and loyalty
Keller (1993) places awareness and brand associations at the centre of customer-based brand equity, and Aaker (1996)
and Yoo and Donthu  (2001) operationalised equity through measurable dimensions. For frequently bought goods,
awareness works as a decision shortcut: aware brands are favoured in choice, particularly when other information is
scarce (Hoyer & Brown, 1990; Macdonald & Sharp, 2 000). Zeithaml (1988) showed that perceived value reflects the
trade-off between perceived quality and price rather than price alone, while Oliver (1999) argued that satisfaction is
necessary but not sufficient for loyalty. T hese works are conceptual or measurement-oriented, largely consumer-level,
and none addresses a regional dairy brand competing for shelf space.
2.3 Retailer engagement and channel relationships
Trust and commitment are central to successful long -term exchange relationships (Morgan & Hunt, 1994), and retail
buyers' trust in a vendor shapes long -term orientation (Ganesan , 1994). In Indian food retailing, retailer awareness,
association, perceived quality and loyalty were positively related to purchase intention (Das, 2014). The common
limitation is that these studies measure perceptions through surveys; they do not docume nt the field routine through
which a regional supplier builds trust with small outlets.
2.4 Promotions, sampling and trial
Advertising and trade promotions interact with existing brand loyalty (Agrawal, 1996), and retailer-branded promotions
can raise purchase intent beyond generic offers (Hamlin et al., 2012). Free-sample experiments found measurable sales
effects lasting up to twelve months, although effectiveness varied widely even between brands in one category (Bawa

2.5 Customer relationship management and integrated communication
Payne and Frow (2005) frame customer relationship management as cross -functional processes that use customer
information to create value, and Duncan and Moriarty (1998) argue that consistent, interactive communication across
touchpoints sustains relationships. Both are conceptual. Applied to this case, they imply that leads and complaints should
be recorded, owned and followed up, and that brochures, conversations, stalls and digital reporting should carry one
coherent message.
3. Research Gap
Gap identified from the literature. Within the sources reviewed for this paper, dairy research concentrates on consumer
preference surveys, and channel and relationship research is largely conceptual or drawn from other sectors. None of the
reviewed sources documents, for a regional dairy brand, how retailer-level surveys, availability checks, lead generation,
complaint capture and society-based promotion operate together as marketing execution. A systematic database search
would be needed to confirm that no such study exists.
Assumptions that remain untested.  This paper does not test whether retailer visits raise awareness, whether leads
convert into orders, whether complaint capture improves availability, or whether stall interest, such as that shown for
curd, becomes purchase. These are propositions for future research, not findings.
4. Research Objectives
1. To study the availability and market visibility of selected Govind Milk products across retail outlets in
Pune.
2. To examine retailer requirements, complaints and lead -generation opportunities related to Govind
products.
3. To assess customer responses to Govind products during society-based brand awareness activities.
4. To identify challenges affecting retailer acceptance and consumer awareness and recommend practical
marketing improvements.
5. Research Methodology
Design and setting.  The study is descriptive, because it documents coverage, availability, complaints and consumer
interactions, and exploratory, because it seeks reasons for retailer resistance and consumer questions (Malhotra, 2019).
Fieldwork took place in Pune, Maharasht ra, from 15 May 2026 to 15 July 2026, covering assigned routes and two
residential societies.
Data sources. Primary data comprise field observations, retailer interactions, consumer interactions, reported leads,
complaints and product availability observations. Secondary data comprise peer -reviewed publications, official
company information and government statistics.
Sampling and analytical base.  A practical field -coverage approach was followed; outlets were visited as part of
assigned routes, and no probability sampling was used. Two figures must be kept apart. The field team visited 460
outlets. Separately, the OJT report applies an analytical base of 220 retailer interactions for selected calculations, and it
does not state how those 220 relate to the 460 visits. They are therefore not treated as one sample.
Tools and analysis. Tools were retailer conversations supported by a product brochure, Google Forms, field notes, GPS-
tagged photographs and the company reporting group. A ten-item retailer checklist (Appendix A) structured each visit.
Analysis uses counts, percentages, tables, charts and thematic interpretation of observations. No inferential statistics are
reported.

6.3 Findings by objective
Objective 1: availability and visibility.  Ghee, chaas, basundi, curd and buttermilk were commonly observed as
available. Milk, milkshake, lassi, shrikhand, cheese and paneer were promoted during field activity. Product -wise
availability counts were not recorded in the report, so no availability rate is calculated, and 110 outlets were reported as
non-selling in the specified comparison.
Objective 2: requirements, complaints and leads. More than 200 leads arose from retailer requirements and interest
in stocking Govind products, and 90 complaints were recorded through Google Forms. The report does not classify the
complaints or state how many leads were followed up, so neither conversion nor complaint resolution can be assessed.
Objective 3: consumer responses. Two society stalls produced more than 550 consumer interactions. Curd drew strong
interest, and consumers asked whether Govind was better than Amul and what made it different. Price and promotional
offers, perceived quality, taste, brand familiarity and curiosity appeared to draw attention. No purchase counts,
respondent profiles or satisfaction scores were captured.
Objective 4: challenges. Retailers were often busy and unable to discuss products in detail, some did not stock Govind,
some showed little interest, consumers had limited awareness, and verifying information was difficult. Amul was the
strongest competitive reference, with Gokul and Chitale also observed. These observations come from field interactions,
not a formal competitor study.
7. Discussion
This section separates what was observed from what published research suggests. The links are interpretive and imply
no causation.
Availability and value. Observed: availability varied by outlet, and consumers asked for reasons to choose the brand.
Literature: availability, quality and price are recurring purchase factors (Bahety et al., 2024; Parasuraman et al., 2021),
and perceived value reflects the quality–price trade-off (Zeithaml, 1988). The questions asked at the stalls are consistent
with consumers weighing value, which suggests that a clear value message could matter as much as price.
Awareness. Observed: many consumers and retailers compared Govind with Amul, which the OJT identified as the
strongest competitive reference. Literature: awareness can act as a decision shortcut for repeat-purchase goods (Hoyer
& Brown, 1990; Macdonald & Sharp, 2000), and awareness may exceed consumption (Kumar et al., 2026). Interest in
curd at the stalls is therefore best treated as an early signal, not evidence of purchase.
Retailer relationships. Observed: time pressure limited retailer conversations, and stocking varied. Literature: trust
and commitment underpin lasting exchange relationships (Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Ganesan , 1994), and retailer
perceptions relate to purchase intention (Das, 2014). Brief, repeated and consistent visits are a plausible way to build
such trust, but this study did not test it.
Promotion and follow-up. Observed: stall offers attracted attention, and leads and complaints were logged digitally.
Literature: sampling can have lasting sales effects that vary by brand (Bawa & Shoemaker, 2004), promotions interact

9. Limitations and Future Research
The study used field coverage rather than a probability sample, so results cannot be generalised. The geography is limited
to assigned Pune routes and the period to two months. The 460-outlet coverage and the 220 analytical base are different
figures, the relationship between them is not documented, and leads, complaints and non -selling shops may overlap.
Retailer statements were largely verbal and could not always be verified, no sales or market -share data were available,
and customer-level survey data are incomplete; there are no purchase counts or respondent profiles.
Future work could use structured questionnaires with a representative consumer sample, validated brand -awareness
scales (for example Yoo & Donthu, 2001), retailer interviews on acceptance and stocking decisions, and longitudinal
tracking of lead conversion, complaint closure and availability before and after the proposed interventions.

10. Conclusion
This paper examined how market surveys, retailer engagement and society-based activities supported brand awareness
for a regional dairy brand in Pune. Coverage was broad (460 outlets), activity was substantial (200+ leads, 90 complaints,
550+ consumer interactions), and curd, price-value and the comparison with Amul  stood out at consumer level, while
retailer time and limited awareness were the main barriers. Because the evidence is descriptive, the findings indicate
where attention is needed and not what produced any outcome. For regional dairy marketing, the implication is that field
activity becomes valuable when it is recorded, followed up and measured. For the author, the OJT showed that a market
survey involves route planning, listening, documentation and follow-up, not only a questionnaire.
