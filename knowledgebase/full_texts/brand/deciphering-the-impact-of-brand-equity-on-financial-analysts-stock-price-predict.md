Abstract
Employing a scenario -based behavioral experiment, we test the impact of disclosure of
customer-based brand equity on the analysts’ stock price forecasts. Our results suggest that
financial analysts favorably factor customer -based brand equity information into their stock
price forecasts. We also empirically test our hypotheses using archival data from a panel of
firms in 19 industries from 2007  to 2015. More experienced analysts’ forecasts reflect brand
equity information less efficiently . Hence, more seasoned analysts issue less accurate stock
price forecasts than less experienced analysts. Our findings suggest that firms can benefit from
voluntary disclosure of customer -based brand equity. While challenges such as measurement
standardization and bias persist, advancements in methodologies and data analytics provide a
clear path forward. This research bridges marketing and finance disciplines, offering actionable
insights for firms to enhance transparency, justify marketing expenditures, and align valuation
with customer-driven growth strategies.

Keywords: Marketing-finance, Brand Equity, Marketing Performance , Marketing
Effectiveness

Introduction

Brands account for nearly one -third of the S&P 500's market capitalization,
representing over 30% of companies' stock market value, a figure that stood at $12 trillion as
of 2017 (The Economist, 2014; BAV Consulting, 2017). Almost all positive cash flows can be
traced to customers ( Srivastava et al.,  1998). Much  of the marketing –finance literature
investigates the impact of customer -based assets on firm performance and, ultimately,
shareholder value (e.g., Mizik & Jacobson, 2008; Rego et al., 2009; Rego et al., 2013; Bayer
et al., 2017 ). However, a review of the marketing and finan ce literature suggests that the
relationship between customer -based assets and shareholder value is more complex than
previously understood . First, the middleman effect is significant in understanding whether
customer-based asset information is deemed rele vant in a firm valuation and, if so, how it is
interpreted and consumed by various middlemen or information intermediaries. In particular,
information intermediaries such as sell -side financial analysts function as critical sources of
information for institutional investors and individual investors who ultimately will drive the
demand for a firm's securities (Chang  et al., 2006). Bayer et al. (2017) provide evidence that
the disclosure of customer metrics, such as retention rates and customer satisfaction, reduces
uncertainty among analysts and investors, underscoring the importance of customer -based
metrics for improving financial analysis.  This perspective aligns with our exploration of how
brand equity, as a specific customer-based metric, influences analysts' stock price forecasts.

Financial analysts are responsible for examining a firm's financial and non -financial
information, both public and private, compiling that information, and anticipating its prospects
in terms of future earnings and investment potential. They are of substantial interest to
researchers because of their critical role in analyzing, interpreting, and disseminating
information to capital market participants (Brown et al., 2015). From a marketing perspective,
studying financial analysts can offer insights into how they incorporate customer-based assets
when developing their price expectation models. This knowledge can g uide marketing
managers to communicate more effectively with investors and top management. Much
information excluded from financial reports relates to customer -based assets, such as brands.
Prior research has suggested that financial analysts are expected to make up for the
shortcomings of financial reporting and factor information excluded from financial reports into
their forecasts and investment recommendations.
The literature on financial analysts and their processes has almost exclusively examined
the efficiency of analyst stock price forecasts and recommendations in relation to the
accounting and financial metrics disclosed in annual and quarterly reports. Several studies have
tried to close this gap by examining the relationship between customer -based assets and
financial analysts' stock price forecasts in the marketing literature (e.g., Luo et al., 2010; Ngobo
et al., 2012). These studies have revealed that financial analysts include customer satisfaction
in their stock forecasts and recommendations. However, a knowledge gap exists in
understanding how the disclosure of customer-based brand equity affects analysts' stock price
projections, and not solely the precision of these forecasts. Moreover, limited knowledge exists
regarding how analysts’ skills  affect the extent to which marketing -related information is
incorporated into price formation. We aim to bridge the gap in understanding how brand equity
influences stock price forecasts and the extent to which analyst experience affects their ability
to accurately capture the value of brands in these predictions.
Brand equity’s favorable impact on financial performance, as a key determinant of
marketing productivity and long-term value, is well documented in the literature . In addition,
brands are also a topic of interest among financial community members. Reputable financial
news outlets, such as the Wall Street Journal, have also called for including brand equity
information in annual reports or balance sheets (Gregory, 2011). Thus, understand ing how
financial analysts incorporate customer -based asset information into their forecasts is highly
relevant to marketing scholars and practitioners. Furthermore, top managers communicate with
investors and financial analysts informally and formally in their effort to market their firms,
increase their visibility and coverage, and enhance firm perception among these information
intermediaries (Bushee & Miller, 2012). Therefore, effective communication with information
intermediaries is paramount for firms and directly relevant to their corporate strategies.
By leveraging rich, existing marketing literature, we address  two main research
questions that have theoretical and managerial relevance:

1. Does customer-based brand equity information disclosure influence financial analysts'
stock price forecasts?
2. Are analysts' forecasts efficient concerning changes in brand equity information?

We address the first research question using an experimental study (Study 1). To
explore the second research question, we analyzed a longitudinal dataset (Study 2) that tracked
analysts from 2007 to 2015, leveraging brand equity data from the BAV group available for
this period. Specifically, we examine how customer -based brand equity influences analysts'
target price pre dictions (stock price forecasts over a 12 -month horizon). Additionally, we
investigate how analyst experience affects this relationship by strengthening or weakening the
impact of brand equity on target prices . In summary, our findings from these two studi es

Hypotheses Development

Sell-side financial analysts issue stock recommendations to investors mainly according
to the prospects of future cash flows (Chen & Matsumoto, 2006). Therefore, the better these
prospects are, the greater the probability that analysts will issue favorable  stock
recommendations (Jegadeesh et al., 2004).
The value relevance of non -financial information as an indicator of corporate
management quality and future cash flow prospects has been noted in accounting and finance
literature (e.g., Dhaliwal et al., 20 12). Prior research suggests that sell -side financial analysts
pay attention to non -financial information. Breton and Taffler (2001) found that after
accounting for fundamentals (e.g., earnings per share, management effectiveness), non -
financial informatio n related to customers and product strategies significantly influences
analyst recommendations. Moreover, Amir  et al. (2003) revealed that analysts recognize the
importance of intangibles and compensate for intangible -related information shortcomings in
financial reports. Whitwell et al. (2007) also demonstrated that analysts pay attention to firm
intangibles to enhance the quality of their assessments. These findings suggest that sell -side
financial analysts know the importance of non -financial information related to customers and
intangible assets as drivers of firm performance and firm value.
Luo et al.'s (2010) study examining the relationship between customer satisfaction and
analyst recommendation provides the logical ground for our argument that not o nly will sell-
side financial analysts pay attention to brand equity, but it may also positively affect their
forecasts. Moreover, prior research in marketing finance has consistently connected customer-
based assets such as brand equity and customer satisfa ction to firms' cash flow prospects. For
example, Srivastava et al. (1998; 1999) hypothesized a powerful relationship among customer-
based assets (e.g., brand equity), market performance, and shareholder value. In a related study,
Madden et al. (2006) found that a portfolio of stocks comprising firms with high-value brands
has lower systematic risk. Likewise, Rego  et al. (2009) showed that higher levels of brand
equity are associated with lower risk for the equity and debt holders and significantly lower
firms' capital costs. Therefore, information about customer satisfaction and brand equity should
serve as an indicator of future cash flows.
We expect increases in brand equity to strengthen analysts’ confidence in firms’ future
performance, thereby leading to more favorable target price forecasts. More broadly, analysts
may interpret changes in brand equity as signals of meaningful shifts in c ustomer evaluations
of the firm and its offerings, with important implications for future firm performance.
Naturally, brand equity decreases occur for various reasons, including a lack of
effective marketing strategy, product defects, adverse events, etc. , thus jeopardizing the
prospects of firm performance. Hence, we expect that decreases in brand equity will negatively
affect analysts' target price forecasts . We anticipate that analysts' forecasts will efficiently
reflect adjustments in brand equity. We propose the following hypothesis:
H1: Increases (decreases) in brand equity are positively (negatively) associated with stock price
forecasts.

Moderating Role of Analyst Experience

The accuracy of analysts' forecasts is strongly influenced by their level of experience
(Mikhail et al., 1997; Clement & Tse, 2005). Analyst experience is a critical factor shaping
their forecasting models, choice of inputs, and overall precision. Prior re search in finance and
accounting consistently demonstrates that experienced analysts outperform their less
experienced counterparts in forecast accuracy (Rahman et al., 2019). Maines et al. (1997) found
that experienced analysts produced significantly more  accurate forecasts compared to less
experienced individuals, particularly those with MBA degrees. Similarly, Mikhail et al. (1997)
emphasized the importance of firm -specific experience, highlighting that analysts with in -
depth knowledge of a company's operations and practices are more likely to base their forecasts
on reliable, fact-based information, leading to fewer errors and greater precision.
Further evidence from Clement et al. (2007) underscores the role of task -specific
experience in improving fore cast accuracy, while Barron  et al. (2008) demonstrate that
analysts’ private, experience-driven information effectively reduces errors.
The learning-by-doing theory (Argote & Epple, 1990) provides additional support for
these findings, suggesting that for ecasting accuracy improves as analysts acquire deeper
industry- and company -specific knowledge and develop relationships with corporate
management. This expertise allows experienced analysts to incorporate firm -specific
information, such as  brand equity  and customer satisfaction , into their forecasting models,
rather than relying exclusively on readily available market -level data. Consequently,
experienced analysts are better positioned to recognize the incremental value of brand equity
information and leverage it to enhance the accuracy of stock price forecasts. These findings
collectively s upport the hypothesis that the positive incremental effect of brand equity
information on stock price forecasts strengthens with increasing analyst experience.
Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: The positive incremental effect of the informati on content of brand equity on the stock
price forecast strengthens as analyst experience increases.

Study 1: An Experimental Study

We tested our hypotheses via a scenario-based behavioral experiment. We investigated
whether financial analysts incorporate a firm’s disclosed customer -based brand equity
information into their stock price forecasts.

Sample, Data Collection, and Procedure

Through Qualtrics Panels, we recruited sell-side financial analysts (N = 203) currently
employed in the United States who had at least 6 months of experience when the study was
conducted. More than 20 percent of our sample were Chartered Financial Analysts (CFAs),
and more than 4 0 percent had MBA degrees . Prior research in finance and accounting has
suggested that MBA education may potentially influence information processing, knowledge
acquisition, and skill development, though the results have not been consistently significant
(Dahlin et al., 2005; Fang &  Hope, 2021). Half of our sample had less than 12 years of
experience and covered fewer than 5 firms. Before starting the study, we tested our experiment
with a panel of experts and revised the survey to maximize its fluency and clarity. We also
pretested the experiment on 60 respondents. The pretest results confirmed that the experimental
content is relevant and indicated how long people would need to answer the questions on
average. We included three manipulation checks and one attention check in the fina l survey.
Our final survey included 35 demographic and open -ended questions related to our research

questions. We collected the responses over 2 days, during which no significant macroeconomic
events occurred that might affect survey respondents' perceptions of the market.
Applying a randomized between -subjects design, we assigned each analyst to one of
the between -subjects treatment conditions (increase in brand equity vs. decrease in brand
equity). They read a short scenario about a recent memo from the C EO to the board of a
hypothetical company called Tuft Technology Corporation. This company manufactures
consumer electronics and creates computer software. The CEO discusses his suggestions for
reporting brand equity information for public consumption in t his memo. He also quantifies
the increase and decrease in brand equity. A short description of the methodology to measure
customer-based brand equity was presented as part of the memo. Respondents were provided
with necessary financial documents, including  a 10-K report, key financial statistics for the
firm and its direct competitors, and an industry report. In the control condition, the randomly
assigned participants only received basic financial data without any information about brand
equity. The result s of the manipulation checks confirmed that the conditions demonstrated
significant mean differences (p-value < .05) in the expected directions.

Analysis and Results

For the stock price forecast, we coded predictions: 1 = stock price will go down, 2 = no
change, and 3 = stock price will go up. The between-subjects design included two brand equity
conditions: increase and decrease. We analyzed the effects of the experimental treatment with
an analysis of covariance  (ANCOVA), with the need for cognitive closure  (Webster &
Kruglanski, 1994) as the covariate, to assess the extent to which a person expressed a need for
definite answers rather than ambiguity. Stock price fore casts varied significantly across the
control and treatment groups (F (1,  202) = 4.083, p-value < .05), such that they were
significantly higher (more optimistic) in the treatment group (M treatment = 2.55, M control = 2.15,
p-value < .05). In testing H1, we found that an increase in brand equity results in more optimistic
stock price forecasts (M Increase = 2.72, M Decrease = 2.26, F (1, 202) = 5.87, p-value < .05), in
support of H 1. These results are also robust when we include MBA  as a control variable and
when we do not include any covariates. Figure 1 provides a visual representation of the results.

Model

We estimated a fixed -effects panel regression with AR ( 1) disturbances, which
accommodates serial correlation in the idiosyncratic error term and is applicable to moderately
unbalanced panel data (Wooldridge, 2015). This approach allowed us to control for unobserved
heterogeneity. The autoregressive correlation structure also allowed us to address any serial
correlation concerns. Regular fixed effects estimation assumes strict exogeneity, which means the
covariates at any time s are uncorrelated with the id iosyncratic errors at any time t. Strict
exogeneity often fails and can cause severe inconsistencies in estimation (Wooldridge, 2015).
Furthermore, prior researchers have documented the serial correlation problem (i.e., the
relationship between past and current random errors of covariates) in modeling analyst stock price
forecasts (Bradshaw et al., 2013). Thus, an econometric model that accounts for serial correlation
is required. Accordingly, fixed effects estimation with AR (1) disturbances is appropriate for our
study. Moreover, we obtain a significant test statistic ( p-value < .05) for autocorrelation by
performing Wooldridge's (2010) test. Variance inflation (VIF) and condition indices statistics
remain adequately below standard limits, sugge sting that multicollinearity is not a concern. We

Robustness Checks

Considering the persistent nature of target price as reported in the past literature (e.g., Sinha
et al. , 1997; Brown et al. , 2015), we used dynamic panel difference generalized methods of
moments (GMM) with robust standard errors (Arellano & Bond, 1991) to test the validity and
robustness of our results. The robust option alleviates concerns about heteroscedasticity and serial
correlation. This approach also addresses endogeneity problems. Moreover, we used a forward
measure of target price (Rego  et al., 2013) to establish causality. As a robustness check, we also
treated the independent variables as endogenous, again finding similar results.

Discussion

Our findings from Study 1 and Study 2 underscore the growing need for and interest in
customer-based brand equity information among financial analysts , a critical intermediary with
significant influence over institutional investors and, ultimately, the capital markets. Both studies
confirm that brand equity positively influences stock price forecasts, and our extended analysis
reveals that increases in brand equity are associated with decreased earnings forecast errors.
In Study 1, experimental results show that providing analysts with customer -based brand
equity data leads to more optimistic stock price forecasts compared to when this information is
absent. This  finding highlights the significant impact of brand equity on shaping analysts’
expectations about a firm's future performance. Study 2, a longitudinal analysis, reinforces these
findings by demonstrating a consistent positive relationship between brand equity and stock price
forecasts over time, emphasizing the enduring relevance of brand equity in financial valuation.
Interestingly, our findings challenge assumptions about analyst experience. Contrary to
prior research ( Trueman, 1994)  suggesting that experienced analysts produce  more efficient
forecasts, our results indicate that less experienced analysts are more likely to incorporate brand
equity information into their target price estimates. We observed a statistically significant
moderating effect of analyst experience, where the positive influence of brand equity information
weakened as experience increased. A possible explanation is that less experienced analysts, still
developing their forecasting models, are more open to experimenting with firm-specific data such
as brand equity. Conversely, experienced analysts, whose models may rely more heavily on
market- or industry-level factors, tend to integrate less brand -specific information. This pattern
suggests firms should tailor their communication strategies to different anal yst segments,
particularly as customer-centric approaches gain traction through subscription -based models and
modern business education.
While challenges such as measurement standardization and reporting bias persist,
disclosing brand equity metrics offers a path forward. For instance, although customer satisfaction
and brand equity differ —customer satisfaction reflects past experiences, whereas brand equity
captures past, present, and future customer preferences—they are similar in that both are customer-
centric metrics . Customer satisfaction has already been successfully measured and tied to
executive compensation in industries such as airlines (Bloomberg, 2017), offering a precedent for
developing reliable frameworks for brand equity reporting despite its intangible nature.
We also recognize the susceptibility of self -reported metrics to bias. However, even
regulated metrics such as R&D costs and advertising expenses are often inconsistent, with studies
showing significant underreporting due to Wall Street’s preference for immediate cash flow (Han
et al., 2017). There is also a lack of consensus on how to measure marketing effectiveness
accurately. Analysts often rely on metrics such as R&D, advertising, and selling, general, and

these costs are accurately reported, they do not provide a precise meas ure of marketing
effectiveness. In contrast, disclosing brand equity metrics focuses on outcomes, such as customer
loyalty and brand attachment, rather than just expenditures, thereby reducing the perceived risks
of reporting.
Advancements in digital marketing now allow for precise measurement of customer-based
metrics such as customer lifetime value, churn rate, and brand sentiment. These tools address
historical concerns about marketing’s intangibility, offering actionable insights for assessing and

Conclusion

Our findings extend the work of Bayer et al. (2017), who demonstrated that increased
disclosure of customer metrics reduces investor uncertainty in the telecommunication and airline
industries and analysts' uncertainty in telecommunications. Our results sh ow that disclosing
customer-based assets, such as brand equity, addresses key limitations of traditional financial
reporting. This study underscores the need for CMOs and marketing managers to measure and
communicate brand equity effectively to financial a nalysts, as such disclosures can justify
marketing expenditures and enhance firm valuation. Financial analysts, in turn, can improve their
forecast models by incorporating meaningful drivers of firm value  and enhanced measures of
marketing effectiveness, such as brand equity. Additionally, governing bodies and policymakers
should establish clearer guidelines for including customer-related metrics in financial disclosures.
We focus specifically on brand equity as a central customer -based metric. Our findings
confirm its distinct and measurable effects on financial analysis, providing a strong rationale for
integrating customer-based assets into financial disclosures.
We also acknowledge challenges associated with incorporating brand equity into financial
disclosures, including measurement difficulties and the risk of manipulation. Firms might overstate
their brand equity to appear more favorable, potentially misleading investors. To address these
concerns, we propose adopting a triangulation approach, combini ng third -party validated
methodologies, such as the BAV model that uses customer surveys, with additional data sources
to ensure objective and standardized brand equity measurement. Additionally, advancements in
natural language processing (NLP) could anal yze user -generated content to provide real -time
insights into brand sentiment and customer perceptions.
The financial community has increasingly recognized the importance of customer -based
metrics. In particular, the rise of subscription -based business mod els has made metrics such as
customer lifetime value (CLV) and churn rate standard considerations, with CLV offering insight
into the long -term value of customer relationships and informing both marketing and financial
decision-making (Danao, 2024). Decades of research in marketing have shown brands to be one
of the key drivers of customer retention, customer loyalty, and ultimately, customer equity and
lifetime value (Stahl et al., 2012).
Furthermore, the decision to disclose brand equity metrics requires careful consideration
of its dual effects. While such disclosure can reduce information asymmetry, improve

strategic vulnerabilities or create short-term market pressures. Firms must weigh these risks against
the benefits when deciding on disclosure strategies. Voluntary disclosure of brand equity metrics
can particularly benefit industries such as fashion, consumer electronics, and other competitive,
low-margin B2C sectors by enhancing transparency and clarifying long-term value for investors.
In conclusion, this study bridges the disciplines of marketing and finance by demonstrating
the significant role of customer-based brand equity in financial analysis. While challenges such as
bias and implementation hurdles remain, advancements in measurement techniques and voluntary
disclosure frameworks offer a way forward. Recognizing the importance of customer -based
metrics such as brand equity can enhance investor confidence, justify marketing expenditures,
address gaps in measuring marketing effectiveness, and align firm valuation with customer-driven
growth.
