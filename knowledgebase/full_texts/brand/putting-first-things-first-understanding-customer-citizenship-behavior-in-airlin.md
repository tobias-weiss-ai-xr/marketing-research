Putting first things first: Understanding customer citizenship behavior in airlines through brand
experience, loyalty, and relationship quality
Abstract
The airlines industry is becoming increasingly competitive, necessitating deep understanding of the role
passengers’ brand experiences plays in their citizenship-like behaviors. This study aims to investigate the
influence of service experiences of airline passengers on their voluntary, customer -driven citizenship
behavior. Using purposive and quota sampling, the data for this study was collected from 395passengers
of 20 international airlines operating in Pakistan . Subsequently, the data was analyzed using  PLS-SEM
and necessary condition analysis ( NCA). The results present that consumers' brand experience (BE)
significantly influences customer citizenship behavior (CCB). Moreover, this relationship is mediated by
brand loyalty (BL; B = 0.116), affective commitment (AC), and BL in sequence (B = 0.02), as well as by
brand relationship quality (BRQ) and BL in sequence (B = 0.097). However, AC and BRQ do not mediate
the relationship between BE and CCB individually . On the other hand, NCA manifests that brand
experience, relationship quality,  loyalty, and affective commitment are necessary for CCB – AC
(≥18.83%), BE (≥2.66%), BL (≥82.18%), and BRQ (≥63.86%) are critical threshold levels for achieving
CCB. Besides contributing theoretically to the existing literature, this offers several practi cal
implications for the airlines industry managers and the policy makers working on developing strategies
for passenger experience . Moreover, this study contributes methodologically to the literature,
demonstrating the operationalization of hierarchical component modeling through second-order reflective
formative construct of BE.

1 Introduction
In 20 years, Pakistan's air travel is predicted to increase by 184%, sustaining 786,300 jobs and $9.3

billion in GDP (IATA, 2022). Customers do not buy a product; they buy an experience. An airline journey
may be brief, but it can leave passengers with a lasting impression—especially when they feel genuinely
valued (Faiyetole & Yusuf, 2018). Consumers engage with brands and eventually evolve into inseparable
components of them, fostering a symbiotic relationship (Rather et al., 2024). After that, they help service
organizations and their customers, thereby demonstrating a perfect display of customer citizenship
behavior (CCB) (Grönroos, 2011).
Customer voluntary behavior is gaining more attention in the current business landscape, as indicated
by the rise in customer-to-customer support (Bakır et al., 2024). As good social citizens, customers trust
other customers' opinions and recommendations for buying decisions (van Tonder & Petzer, 2021). As an
element of CCB, customers share their positive memorable experiences with others (Kim & Choi, 2016).
According to Chan et al. (2017), continuity and sustainability  of a service brand depend on customers'
willingness to engage in extra-role behaviors.
Brand experience (BE) refers to the tangible and affective assessment that an individual makes
throughout their journey with a brand (Laming & Mason, 2014). A pleasurable airline BE leaves a lasting
impression, compelling airlines to deliver distinctive and memorable experiences to gain a competitive
edge and differentiate themselves. Consistently providing reliable and enjoyable service allows an airline
to build a strong emotional connection with its passengers, fostering loyalty and trust. In addition,
customers often give positive feedback and become loyal when they have a positive experience (Arguello
et al., 2019; Gong & Yi, 2018). Enjoyable customer experience fosters strong ties between the customer
and the brand through consistent quality (Wu & Kang, 2012).
In turn, brand relationship quality (BRQ), affective commitment (AC), and brand loyalty (BL) are
primary antecedents of CCB (Bartikowski & Walsh, 2011; Wu et al., 2017). Consequently, the mediating
roles of BL, AC, and BRQ are pivotal in linking BE to CCB. Some studies investigated the chain of
effects from service quality to patient satisfaction and then to patient citizenship behaviors (Ulucayli et

examined as intervening variables.
Although social exchange theory (SET) is commonly used in literature as an underpinning theory for
CCB frameworks (e.g., Abdou et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2020), it has not been fully integrated with other
theoretical perspectives. Combining SET with the stimulus-organism-response (S-O-R) model and brand
relationship theory could present a complete understanding of the influence of customer expectations and
confirmations on CCB in the airline services  industry. Such integrative  approaches are currently
underutilized in the literature.
Recent research in the airline industry has identified multiple antecedents that shape CCB. Early work
by Woo (2019) established service quality as a central driver of CCB, showing that both tangib le and
intangible aspects of airline service influence passengers’ perceived value, which in turn stimulates
citizenship behaviors. Hwang and Lyu (2020) found that environmentally friendly perceptions of airlines
positively influence consumer attitudes which ultimately influence CCB. At the same time, some studies
have explored the role of customer satisfaction in CCB (Kim et al., 2020). Han et al. (2021), who observed
that airlines’ eco-friendly initiatives enhance corporate trust, thereby encouraging CCB. On the relational
front, Hassan and Suki (2022) found that relational benefits strengthen relationship quality, which
mediates their effect on CCB. Later, Khan et al. (2024) also found that SERVQUAL dimensions indirectly
promote CCB through behavioral int entions. Similarly, Bakır and Atalık (2024) found that CCB is
influenced by quality of service  and economic value through satisfaction. However, airlines literature
lacks assessment of the serial mediation effects resulting in CCB. A framework with the direct effect of
BE on CCB and an indirect effects mechanism via AC, BRQ, and BL  will enhance the literature on
citizenship behavior.
Brakus et al. (2009)’s seminal study on brand experience identified sensory, affective, behavioral, and
intellectual as core dimensions. In order to adapt to the airlines context , our study  categorizes brand

2.1.2 Brand Experience
Passengers' favorable feelings and impressions of their airline's interactions are referred to as a
positive brand experience. This can include several elements, such as comfort, emotional fulfillment, and
service quality across touchpoints, including reservations, in-flight amenities, and post-flight interactions
(Brakus et al., 2009). More specifically, an airline passenger's experience includes the experiences before,
during, and after the flight. Therefore, in this study, BE is conceptualized with three  dimensions: pre-
flight experience, in-flight experience, and post-flight experience.
2.1.3 Affective Commitment
An individual's emotional attachment, identification, and involvement with an organization or  brand
is called affective commitment (Rizomyliotis et al., 2022). A psychological state, it reflects the emotional
attachment of the consumer with the brand (Rather et al., 2024) . Customers maintain a connection with
the brand because they want to, not because they have to (PhamThi & Ho, 2024).

2.1.4 Brand Relationship Quality
BRQ refers to the intensity of the relationship between customers and brands, reflecting the
strength of the emotional and psychological connection (Rather et al., 2024). BRQ represents the level
to which the relationship between the brand and the customer fulfills the customer's needs (Garg et al.,
2025). The ‘brand relationship’ is defined as an idea that helps brands and consumers relate to each other and
align their expectations of the actors involved.
2.1.5 Brand Loyalty
Loyalty refers to cu stomers’ emotional or behavioral  attachment with a brand and/or company
(Wang & Yang, 2025). The emotional element of the BL makes a customer emotionally clung to the brand
which they believe is good for them and they cannot separate from it (Harsono et al., 2023). However, the
behavioral element of BL is action oriented. The customers keep using the product and recommend the
product to others as well (Ragab et al., 2024; Wang & Yang, 2025). Airline brands are a type of service where
ownership of the product is not transferred to the customer rather, they experience the benefit on each
purchase. Therefore, when a passenger is loyal to an airline’s brand, they will have repeat purchase of the
tickets from the same brand (Ragab et al., 2024; Vuong et al., 2024).
2.2 Underpinning theories
This study integrated SET, the S -O-R model, and Brand Relationship Theory to provide an in-
depth explanation of the nuanced relationships among the constructs  of the study.  SET explains the
reciprocal nature of the relationship between BE and CCB , in which positive experiences translate  into
citizenship behavior. According to Blau’s (1964) social exchange theory, interpersonal connections
develop and persist through a cost -benefit calculus, with individuals striving to optimize gains and
minimize losses in relational exchanges. In marketing, this theory explains the cultivation of relationships
between individuals and brands, akin to interpersonal connections, where brand exp erience reflects the
brand's efforts to enhance passengers' journey experiences, fostering BRQ and cultivating BL. In this
exchange mechanism, the brand must serve as the initiator, with the customer responding only when the

brand consistently delivers a memorable experience. In the airline industry, delivering seamless travel
experiences converts brand promises into credible outcomes. By strategically optimizing touchpoints
across the customer journey—from reservations to post-flight interactions—carriers can garner favorable
online advocacy from engaged travelers (Bakir et al., 2024; Bakır & Atalık, 2024), a vital asset in a
saturated market (Siering et al., 2018). Here, passengers function as collaborative partners through CCB
(consumer citizenship behavi or), including proactive efforts such as  promoting services, providing
constructive feedback, and demonstrating patience during service gaps.
The S-O-R model explains the immediate customer re sponse by framing BE as an external stimulus
that triggers internal emotions of AE, BL, and BRQ, which lead to the  response of CCB. In marketing,
the main idea of the S-O-R theory (Bagozzi, 1986; Eroglu et al., 2001; Sherman et al., 1997; Yu et al.,
2021) is that external stimuli (S) influence internal emotional and cognitive states (O), which in turn drive
consumer behavioral responses (R). In this study, a traveler's experience with an airline serves as a
stimulus, generating an organism like AC, which culminates in CCB as the airline traveler's outcome
behavior.
Furthermore, Brand Relationship Theory explains the long -term phenomenon that underscores how
the relational and emotional bond evolves over time, reinforcing the mediating roles of AC, BL, and BRQ.
2.3 Hypotheses development
2.3.1 BE and CCB
A positive experience with a brand encourages  consumers towards CCB  (PhamThi & Ho, 2024 ).
Customer attitudes and behaviors are greatly influenced by a positive brand experience (Bakır & Atalık,
2024). Even in a corporate environment, the work environment influences employees' c itizenship
behavior (Arokiasamy et al., 2025). Furthermore,  like other industries' customers (Fahim et al., 2021 ;
Rehman et al., 20 21), passengers frequently base their assessments of the airline industry on several
touchpoints, including the purchase of t ickets, the check -in procedure, in -flight amenities, employee

increases trust, emotional attachment, and satisfaction , which play a  role in CCB (Bakır et al., 2024;
PhamThi & Ho, 2024). Based on the above discussion, a hypothesis is proposed:
H1:   Airlines passengers with positives brand experience exhibit CCB.
2.3.2 Mediation of AC
A strong bond and emotional connection form between customers and service providers when they
have a fulfilling service experience (Etemad-Sajadi et al., 2016; PhamThi & Ho, 2024). In the hospitality
industry, customers with an emotional connection to the service provider tend to provide extra -role
services to the service provider, such as brand advocacy, peer support, and tolerance (Lee & Kim, 2022;
Nguyen & Chiu, 2023). Based on this discussion, the following hypothesis is proposed.
H2: Airline passengers with positive BE develop AC, which subsequently leads to CCB.
2.3.3 Mediation of AC and BL
BE of a consumer makes a customer feel more committed towards the brand (PhamThi & Ho, 2024).
Positive experiences during and after interactions with airlines develop a bond between passengers and
airlines (Etemad-Sajadi et al., 2016). This emotional and psychological bond with the brand can lead to a
passenger's repeated business and preference for the airline over rivals (Ragab et al., 2024; Wang & Yang,
2025). BL, in turn, promotes actions that are advantageous to the airline, such as  more word-of-mouth
referrals and support  in the event of minor service issues  (Harsono et al., 2023) . Based on the above
discussion, a hypothesis is proposed:
H3:    Airline passengers with positive brand experience exhibit CCB through the serial mediati on of
AC and BL.

Customers who have a  positive BE develop an affection for the brand (PhamThi & Ho, 2024).

2.3.5 Mediation of BRQ and BL

Passengers with a positive BE subsequently develop a strong BRQ toward the service provider (Rather
et al., 2024). Due to strong relationship quality, Customers consistently choose the same airline over
others and exhibit more brand loyalty (Han et al., 2021; Tegambwage & Kasoga, 2024). Customers with
strong BL often engage CCB (discretionary advocacy efforts) , including promoting the company to

Based on the above discussion, a hypothesis is proposed:
H5:  Airline passengers with positive BE have better BRQ, which leads to BL, and subsequently
exhibit CCB.
2.3.6 Mediation of BRQ

BE enhances the quality of customer relationships with servic e providers (Rather et al., 2024). The
strong relationship between the customer and the brand encourages customers to perform extra -role
behavior, including advocating the brand, assisting other customers, and providing positive feedback
(Bansal & Thakur, 2024; PhamThi & Ho, 2024). Based on the above discussion, a hypothesis is proposed:
H5:  Airline passengers with positive BE have better BRQ and subsequently exhibit CCB.

The voluntary, extra-role activities that consumers take to support a brand, such as advocacy, assisting

H6:  BE, AC, BL, and BRQ are necessary conditions for CCB.

3.1 Population and Sampling
The study’s sampling criteria included individuals aged 18 or older who identified as international
travelers and were residents of Pakistan. Given that more than 70% of international departures from
Pakistan originate in Karachi, Lahore, and Islamabad  (Pakistan Civil Aviation Authority, 2022) , these
three major urban centers were selected as the primary data collection sites. Probability sampling methods
were not feasible because an exhaustive list of international travelers departing from the selected airports
was unavailable. Consequently, the non-probability sampling techniques adopted were the most practical
and contextually appropriate for this study. A hybrid sampling comprising purposive and quota sampling
techniques were utilized.  Purposive sampling was used to ensure that only respondents who met the
criteria for the target population —international travelers currently residing in Pakista n and aged 18 or
older—were included in the study. Quota sampling was then applied to ensure representation from the
three cities in proportion to the actual distribution of international travelers departing from each airport.
This approach allowed the sample to mirror the relative volume of passengers traveling from airports at
Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad.
G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Faul et al., 2009) was used for the power analysis. G*Power estimates that a sample
size of at least 85 is necessary for this  study to be statistically significant. According to Kline (2016),
structural equation modeling requires five to ten observations for each item in the survey. Based on this
guideline, data were collected from 6 respondents for each manifest variable. Since this study includes 65
items, a sample of 390 respondents (65×6) was collected.

This study utilized pre -validated scales from literature.  CCB included two components:  Helping
Customers (4 items, based on Bove et al., 2009; Choi & Lotz, 2016) and Helping the Company (4 items,
derived from Bartikowski & Walsh, 2011; Bove et al., 2009; Choi & Lotz, 2016). BE was operationalized

3.3 Pre and Pilot Testing
Pre-testing and pilot testing of the scales were conducted to ensure reliability and validity. First, face
validity was conducted by language experts from local universities who performed language reviewed. Minor
grammar and sentence structures adjustments were made to suit the comprehension of Pakistani target population.
Next, the adjusted questionnaire was reviewed by a senior Professor from the University of Dammam and
a senior business consultant who finalized the content questionnaire. Afterwards, ten potential respondents
were selected who reviewed the questionnaire to provide feedback on difficulty level of the questions (Hunt et al.,
1982). With a sample of 80 respondents, the pilot testing was conducted after the pre-testing phase to establish
the scale reliability and validity in research context (Whitehead et al., 2016). PF3, PF4, IF2, IF3, and BT6
were removed from behavioral ties, in-flight experience, and pre-flight experience as their outer loadings
lower than 0.708 benchmark (Hair et al., 2021).
Full-scale data collection was conducted through google form link shared online . The questionnaire
was divided into sections to retain respondents' attention . Moreover, missing responses or values were
avoided by setting all the questions that were set on required in the google form . Common method bias

4 Results and Discussion
In this research, 427 out of 550 online questionnaires floated were rece ived, marking a response rate
of 77.6 per cent. At the stage of data screening, 27 responses were discarded as their responses across the
items showed near -zero standard deviation. Subsequently, Mahalanobis  distance with chi -square
probability were used to  identify the outliers. Five responses with probability less than 0.001 were
removed from the data set.  Eventually, a final sample of 395 valid responses was obtained for analysis.
The screened data were tested for multivariate skewness and kurtosis by means of Mardia multivariate
test. The results showed that the data was skewed and kurtotic lending another justification for the use of
PLS-SEM (Nitzl, 2016). A full collinearity test was conducted to test the possibility of CMB based on the
methodology su ggested by Kock and Gaskins (2014). All first order constructs were regressed on a
randomly generated variable . The variance inflation factors (VIFs) of all constructs were less than 5,
assuring absence of CMB in the data (Hair et al., 2017).

4.3 Necessary Condition Analysis
Necessary Condition Analysis (NCA), introduced by Dul (2016), has become a popular management
and commercial practice during the past decade. Therefore, a necessary condition is a barrier, a bottleneck,
that must be cleared to reach an expected outcome variable. There will be no outcome if the requirement
is not fulfilled (Dul, 2016, 2020). To clarify whether an outcome is absent if a specific condition is not
given, NCA does not seek to explain how modificati ons of a factor/determinant influence an outcome
(i.e., additive average effect logic represented in various types of regression or structural equation
analyses). This study hypothesized that BE , AC, BRQ, and BL are necessary conditions for CCB; i.e.,
CCB is not possible without them. The NCA's findings (see Table 11) establish that BE, AC, BRQ, and
BL are meaningful (d ≥0.1).
According to Dul (2016), a modest effect size (d = 0 to 0.1), a medium effect size (d = 0.1 to 0.3), a
large effect size (d = 0.3 to 0.5), and a very high effect size if d is greater than 0.5) and significant if p is
less than 0.05. The bottleneck tables allow for a comprehensive evaluation of all necessary conditions.
Table 12 highlights that to reach a 100% - level of CCB, four cond itions need to be met: AC at no less

4.4 Discussion
The findings of the present study suggest that BE is an important factor affecting CCB, which matches
the results of Tang et al. (2023), who also investigated the dependence between destination BE and CCB.
Nonetheless, there are significant differences between the two studies despite this similarity. To define

BE in the first place, Tang et al. (2023) used BE dimensions of originally suggested by Brakus et al.
(2009). In the context of the airline industry, BE was conceptualized as second-order construct with pre-
flight, in-flight and post-flight experience as dimensions. Second, Tang et al. (2023) did not consider CCB
as a higher -order construct with the two dimensions of HTC and HOC, which are represented in the
present study.
Although this research holds the two dimensions of CCB used in the original research, it is different
in that it builds a higher -order CCB model to analyze structural relationships thus improving analyti cal
rigor. This study represents BE and CCB as second -order constructs, thereby capturing the
multidimensional nature of airline experience in a holistic manner and in this regard, the study is
especially critical in experience service industries such as the aviation industry.
One of the differences in methodology is the selection of statistical instruments. This study is based
on PLS -SEM as opposed to Tang et al. (2023), who applied CB -SEM without considering data
distribution assumptions. The method is a beneficial SEM that is used to deal with non -normal data to
provide high parameter estimation strength. Also, although Gao et al. (2016) explored the connection
between the experiential factors and citizenship behavior using meta-analysis, their study highlighted the
importance of having an empirical validation, which the study fills through its quantitative model.
It was observed that the effect of BL on CCB was significant which is consistent with the study of
Ponnusamy and Ho (2015). Nonetheless, his wor k is more rigorous as he integrates both the purposive
and the quota sampling methods and subjects his on-site sampling rather than e-environment. One of the
main similarities between the present research and that by Nagy and Galal Marzouk (2018) is that BL has
a great impact on CCB, but they focus on different groups of participants (international air travelers and
students, respectively). The results are also consistent with the emphasis of Kim and Choi (2016) on the
quality of customer experience to promote CCB, but in this study, BE is the independent variable. In the
same way, there are also parallels with Qiu et al. (2021), who associate service climate with CCB.

Although their framework incorporates service climate in the air travel experiences, they operationalized
it as a first-order construct. Conversely, this research contributes to the field of method, modeling BE and

Structural analysis supports the fact that BRQ has a major direct influence on CCB in line with Lishan

5 Conclusion, Limitations, and Further Research
This study explained the effect of BE, AC, BL, and BRQ on CCB in the aviation industry. This study
contributed to literature by operationalising BE as second-order construct representing authentic
passenger experience to  fill the  gap in literature  related to multidimensional CCB research . Notably,
integrating necessary condition analysis (NCA) with PLS -SEM reveals that BE, AC, BL, and BRQ
function as necessary conditions for CCB. This provides a more integrated understanding of the conditions
that must be present for airlines to encourage CCB among passengers. The BE-CCB framework shows
that these relational and experiential influences are crucial in the aviation industry.

5.1 Theoretical Contributions
This study offers several theoretical implications.  First, BE construct is operationalized by adopting
service quality touchpoints (pre-flight, in-flight, post-flight) unlike the traditional BE dimensions (Brakus
et al., 2009). The dimensions used in this study align more with the holistic customer experience (Meis,
2018), thereby contributing to broader service and experiential marketing that emphasizes contextualized
and reality-based interpretations of brand experience. Secondly, this study introduces serial mediation
pathways (e.g., BE → AC → BL → CCB) absent in prior CCB literature and validates BRQ’s mediating
role between BE and CCB . This is unique  contribution to the literature of relationship marketing as it
clarifies how a customer’s experience, emotional, and relational mechanisms collectively converts to their
citizenship behavior. Further, this study combines second-order reflective–formative modeling with NCA
and PLS-SEM which takes into account a multidimensional construct’s complexity. This methodological
integration sets an example for the forthcoming research. At the same time , this study has responded to
calls in the service literature for more sophisticated data analysis approaches that can capture multifaceted
constructs and their necessary conditions. In addition, the utilisation of NCA and PLS-SEM uniquely
identified necessary conditions while testing the hypotheses. Thereby, this study has offered a replicable
operationalisation of constructs and methodological approach for future studies.
5.2 Practical Implications
This study has several im plications for marketers and policy makers in the airlines industry. Firstly,
BRQ enhancement should be on the agenda of airlines since it is a critical connection between BE and
CCB. BRQ enhancement ensures that positive BE are well transformed into volun tary CCB and thus
investments in BRQ are strategically significant. The relationships with passengers can be enhanced by
means of mobile POS. Regular communication, and incorporation of feedback, can encourage passengers’
advocacy and collaboration with th e airlines. POS through mobile systems can provide  smooth and
personalized interaction, which creates a sense of care and responsiveness in customers, which directly
leads to BRQ. After the pandemic, hygiene and health safety  should be integrative of BE in accordance

Further, this study is aligned to SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), as the proposed

5.3 Limitations and Future Research
Despite its contributions, this study has some limitations which open avenue for future research. This
study used on-probability sampling design and cross -sectional data  collection which limited the
generalisability of the findings although quota sampling was used to reduce the bias. Future studies should
use probability sampling and other study designs (experiments or longitudinal data collection) .
Replicating the framework in diverse regions or airline types using longitudinal desi gns could validate
findings. This study was situated in post -pandemic era but did not include any construct related to
pandemic. Future studies should explore pandemic -related moderators (e.g., health protocols, isolation
norms). Moreover, later studies ca n utilise PLS prediction-oriented segmentation (PLS -POS), FIMIX-
PLS or other advanced statistical techniques to enhance methodological contributions.
