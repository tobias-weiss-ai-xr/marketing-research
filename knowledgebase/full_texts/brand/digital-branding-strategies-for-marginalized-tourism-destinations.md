Abstract. This study explores whether digital branding can reduce socio-economic disparities
in marginalized tourism destinations. Using a mixed-methods comparative case study of Ostrava,
Košice, and Glasgow, it analyses strategic tourism documents and digital infrastructure via BDC and
RivalIQ tools. Findings identify three mechanisms: visibility alone is ineffective without strategic
links to community benefits; stakeholder co-creation works moderately when structured but lacks
platforms for resident-visitor interaction; ecosystem alignment – combining resident-focused planning,
measurement systems, and workforce programs – is essential. Identical digital tools yield different
outcomes depending on governance models and strategic intent. The study concludes that digital
branding supports equity only when strategies prioritize community benefits, enable authentic local
storytelling, and include comprehensive impact tracking. It extends destination branding theory by
challenging the visitor-centric paradigm, arguing that residents must be the primary beneficiaries. The
research contributes to tourism policy by highlighting the need for inclusive digital strategies that
integrate local voices, measure benefit distribution, and foster long-term socio-economic resilience in
post-industrial destinations.
Keywords: Digital branding, residents, socio-economic disparities, tourism destination, visitors.
1. Introduction
The transformation of industrial regions into attrac-
tive economic centres and tourism destinations means
a fundamental shift in the economic and often social
structure of the region, but it does not necessarily
mean the loss of the region’s identity. The identity
of the region in the form of industrial and cultural
heritage is reflected in the branding strategy of the
tourist destination. The research examines three dif-
ferent approaches to tourism development and digital
brand strategy in terms of addressing regional dis-
parities. Regional disparities represent the unequal
distribution of resources, opportunities, and outcomes
across different groups, regions, or populations [1].
Differences in economic performance between regions
within countries can be large and sometimes even
larger than between countries [2]. In marginalized
regions, these disparities manifest through limited
access to economic opportunities, inadequate infras-
tructure, and reduced quality of life compared to more
developed areas.
A marginalized tourism destinationcan be consid-
ered a peripheral area characterized by economic de-
cline or stagnation, limited economic opportunities,
limited tourism infrastructure and exclusion from
mainstream tourism development, which, however,
also represents an opportunity for socio-economic
transformation of the mentioned regions. The pro-
cess of marginalization is the result of complex power
dynamics [3]. Destinations show economic decline,
abandonment and socio-economic decline [4]. A sig-
nificant finding [5] is that the starting points can be
found in a strategic approach and redirection based on
inclusive approaches that take local communities into
play. The successful transformation of marginalized
destinations remains challenging due to the complexity
of the tourism destination itself and the involvement of
wider political-economic structures and requires indi-
vidual specific strategies activating territorial capital
and community involvement [5].
Digital destination brandingrepresents a key prac-
tice for tourist destination management organisations
(DMOs) built on three pillars: social network pres-
ence, social network content and digital services [6].
Digitalisation, i.e. the rapid development of digital
infrastructure, digital technologies, and artificial in-
telligence, is completely changing not only economic
models, but also transforming society and relation-
ships between people, the way of communication, work
and lifestyle [7–9]. The changes concern the transfor-
mation of value chains and innovative business models
as drivers of economic growth and development [10],
which can help reduce regional disparities. At the
same time, however, a completely opposite trend is
visible, namely the emergence of digital disparities
in terms of access to digital infrastructure, but also
digital literacy and skills [11, 12]. Digital platforms
can help in tourism, but at the same time deepen
local inequalities, demonstrating how digital branding
can inadvertently worsen regional disparities if not
carefully managed.
The goalof this research is to explore the impact
and value of digital branding strategies in reducing
socio-economic disparities in selected regional mid-tier

The concept of value in this research refers to the
long-term strategic value of digital branding strategies,

reducing socio-economic disparities. This concept

(e.g. ROI, click-through rates).
To address the objective, the following research
questions have been formulated.
RQ1: How is digital branding integrated into strate-
gic tourism development documents for addressing
regional disparities in marginalized tourism destina-
tions?
The theoretical basisfor the formulationof the ques-
tion RQ1 Digital branding integration in strategic doc-
uments is represented by Kotler’s [13] identification
of fragmented branding as a “deadly marketing sin”,
combined with Morgan et al.’s [14] argument that des-
tination communication serves as a flexible part of
the brand strategy with “bleed-over effects” on the
attitudes of residents. It will be investigated whether
digital branding is systematically anchored in strate-
gic documents or rather a random ad-hoc activity.
The content analysis method will be used.
RQ2: What role does digital infrastructure (web-
sites, social media) play in bridging the gap be-
tween visitor-oriented tourism marketing and resident-
centered community development?
The theoretical basis for the formulation of the
question RQ2 Digital infrastructure’s role in visitor-
resident bridgingis mainly Buhalis and Sinarta’s [15]
“real-time co-creation and nowness service” framework,
emphasizing technology’s transformation of customer-
brand interaction points, combined with Buonincontri
et al. [16] conceptualization of “destination ecosys-
tem”. According to Buonincontri et al. [16], a brand
is the result of the interaction of residents, busi-
nesses and visitors. The question tests whether dig-
ital platforms function as uni-directional marketing
tools (visitor-centric) or bi-directional engagement
platforms (community-empowering). Quantitative
digital performance metrics and qualitative content
analysis of platform features assessing resident partic-
ipation mechanism will be used.
Based on preliminary research and existing litera-
ture, this study operates undertwo key premises.
P1: Marginalized tourism destinations with com-
prehensive digital branding strategic integration will
demonstrate more substantial progress in addressing
regional disparities than destinations with fragmented
or absent digital branding strategic frameworks, re-
gardless of existing digital infrastructure capacity.
P2: Tourism destinations that implement system-
atic regional disparity reduction assessment mech-
anisms will show more comprehensive outcomes in
reducing socio-economic disparities than destinations
lacking structured disparity reduction monitoring
frameworks.
2. Theoretical background
According to Kotler [13, p. 86], one of the “deadly
marketing sins” is that the company (destination) does
not manage to build a brand and a communication
strategy. Not only do (potential) visitors not have
enough correct information, but the destination brand
is not differentiated enough, and they do not associate
it with any attributes, qualities or value.A brand
represents a product, but one that adds an additional
dimension that differentiates it in some way from other
products designed to satisfy the same need. These
differences can be rational (rational) and tangible,
related to the product implementation of the brand,
or symbolic, emotional or intangible, related to what
the brand represents. Brands themselves are valuable
intangible assets that need to be handled with care [17,
p. 8].
Place brandrefers to the symbolic representation
of a place’s identity, aiming to cultivate a positive
perception both internally – among public, private,
and civil society stakeholders – and externally, includ-
ing groups such as tourists, investors, traders, and
migrants [18]. According to Govers and Go [18, p. 17],
place branding “build a favourable internal (those who
deliver the experience) and external (visitors) image
(brand satisfaction, loyalty, name awareness, perceived
quality, and favourable associations)”. The result is
the final brand equity covering brand satisfaction and
loyalty, brand awareness and other elements.Brand
strategy (branding)can be defined as the process of
developing a brand value proposition and building
a brand. The value proposition is based on the key
products (market opportunities) of the destination,
creating their specific presentation and their value
presentation [19].
Brand building itself is based on a visually processed
logotype and the destination brand name, developing
associations and promises, and managing all contacts
of the destination brand with clients [20, pp. 115–116].
In the broader concept of a brand covers the so-called
brand portfolio consisting of the logotype as a more
permanent and long-term component and advertising,
or other tools of the promotional mix.Destination
communication is a key and flexible part of brand
strategy [14, 21], which has a “bleed-over” effect on
residents’ attitudes and can be used as a public diplo-
macy tool [22]. Brand strategy thus influences the
perception of the destination by the client and helps
shape the destination image and competitiveness. The
effort to increase and maintain the competitiveness
of destinations leads them to carefully consider their
strategic positioning while emphasizing the unique-
ness of the place to visit, live, work and study [23].
Creating a strong brand logically led from passive to
responsive marketing andthe involvement of the brand
manager and the customer in communication [24].
The goal of the brand strategy is to create a positive
relationship between the destination and the visitors
and ultimately increase the client’s loyalty to the des-

While historically the destination brand strategy
was mainly within the competence of the destina-

development of digital technologies that enable the
sharing of experiences between customers contributes
significantly to brand building and reputation [28, 29],
which supports arguments about digital branding’s
capacity to provide visibility for underrepresented re-

Tourism experiences involve active tourist partici-
pation and interaction with“local businesses, visitor
organizers, residents and other travellers as part of
a range of on-site activities”. This creates a desti-
nation ecosystem where brand meaning and value
emerge from collaborative contributions by residents,
attractions, cultural experiences, hospitality and other
local businesses, employees and consumers, and not
just the consumers [16, 30]. Digital branding strategies
for marginalized tourism destinations should facilitate
community involvement and authentic storytelling, as
the destination brand is inherently co-created by the
entire local ecosystem rather than imposed externally
by tourists or marketing professionals.Corporate (or
destination) social responsibilityas well as resident
engagement and environmental sustainability have
a significant influence on destination branding [31, 32].
Digital branding strategiesmust ensure community
involvement and equitable benefit distribution, as resi-
dents are integral brand ambassadors whose attitudes
directly shape visitor experiences and destination per-
ception. Residents are part of the brand strategy in
the destination, acting as place-ambassadors online
and off-line and thus supporting the brand [33–35].
Consumers willingly pay premium prices for excep-
tional and authentic destination brands demonstrat-
ing genuine local character, creating economic bene-
fits that help address socio-economic inequalities in
marginalized tourism destinations.
Digital destination brandingis becoming an inno-
vative emerging process, in which many stakeholders
in the destination, including residents, participate
simultaneously and significantly change the form of
traditional procedures in brand strategy. The main
platforms of the digital infrastructure for destination
branding are not only the official website or social net-
works of the destination, but also many others that
the destination cannot influence in any way. Digital
branding of tourism destinations can be an important
tool to mitigate regional disparities in their various
forms. Disparities refer to the unequal distribution
of resources, opportunities, and outcomes across dif-
ferent groups, regions, or populations. Disparities in
digital infrastructure and education can be a signifi-
cant handicap [1, 36]. Tourism has the potential to
foster inclusive growth and reduce regional dispari-
ties by channelling investment to less developed areas
and creating opportunities for local entrepreneurship
(residents).
3. Data and methodology
3.1. Theoretical framework and
research design
This study employsa multiple-case study design[37]
based on the mixed methods approach, both grounded
in destination branding theory [14, 18] and digi-
tal transformation frameworks[15] to examine how
marginalized tourism destinations integrate digital
branding strategies into policy and practice. The
case study method was selected to provide contextual
understanding of complex phenomena across differ-
ent regional settings. The research design is based
on explanatory case study approach and seeks to ex-
plore “how” and “why” certain phenomena occur in
a particular environment, similarly to the intersection
of destination strategic intentions and technological
infrastructure [37]. The research design combines
qualitative content analysis of strategic documents
with targeted quantitative assessment of digital in-
frastructure performance. Destination selectionwas
based on comparable post-industrial transformation
contexts, and varying levels of digital branding matu-
rity to enable meaningful comparative analysis. The
research timeline focuses on strategic documents from
2020–2030 to capture contemporary digital transfor-
mation approaches while ensuring policy relevance
and implementation feasibility assessment.
Three cases were selected according to the “literal
replication” logic [37], sharing critical contextual char-
acteristics (post-industrial transformation, peripheral
positioning, regional disparities) to enable cross-case
pattern identification, namely Ostrava (Moravian-
Silesian region),Košice (Košice region) andGlasgow
(Glasgow region and Scotland), Glasgow serves as
a benchmark case following Eisenhardt’s [38] recom-
mendation to include extreme or polar cases for the-
oretical elaboration. The research primarily focuses
on cities, but in some aspect the regional dimension
cannot be ignored, especially when analyzing strate-
gic documents, where documents for a wider region
(region) are also the subject of analysis. The reasons
for choosing the three destinations are as follows. The
choice of two regions Ostrava (Moravian-Silesian re-
gion) and Košice (Eastern Slovakia) was made because
of their similar position in evaluatingthe percentage
of people at risk of poverty, where Moravian-Silesian
region reaches 15.7% and Eastern Slovakia 21.9% [39].
Glasgow (Glasgow region) was included as a bench-
mark region that underwent a dramatic transforma-
tion during the 1980s. All three regions share the

3.2. Data collection and analytical
procedure
The research employed asequential mixed methods
design [41] where qualitative content analysis informed
quantitative data collection priorities. The research
can be divided into two basic phases.
Phase 1: Strategic document analysis
(March–June 2025) In the preparation of thean-
alytical protocol[42], individual phases were carried
out, namely data familiarization, deductive coding
scheme development based primarily on the cate-
gories digital branding integration, brand strategy
components, stakeholder engagement mechanisms,
disparity reduction objectives, digital infrastructure.
Next, inductive code refinementand expansion with
new themes (tourism-mobility integration, participa-
tory planning mechanisms, public-private partnership
model) took place, followed by systematic coding.The
thematic synthesisfocused onthree analytical dimen-
sions, namely digital branding integration assessment,
destination digital infrastructure and disparity reduc-
tion focus assessment. Following Yin’s “explanation
building” technique [37, pp. 196–200], propositions
were iteratively refined through within-case analysis
followed by cross-case comparison to identify conver-
gent/divergent patterns.
The followingdocuments were identified as relevant
and selected for content analysis based on selection
criteria: official status (government-approved), tem-
poral relevance (2020–2030 planning horizon) and
tourism/mobility focus.
• Tourism Management Strategy of the Moravian-
Silesian Region 2021–2025 [43].
• Priorities of Measures Including KPIs – Tourism
Management Strategy of the Moravian-Silesian Re-
gion 2021–2025 [44].
• Action Plan for the Development of Tourism in the
Ostrava Region in 2025 [45].
• Ecotourism Development Strategy in the Košice

• Sustainable Tourism Development Strategy in

• Cycling Transport and Cycle Tourism Development

• Košice Region Tourism Annual Report 2024 [49].
• Glasgow 2030 Tourism Strategy [50].
• Scotland Outlook 2030 – Responsible Tourism for

The necessary analytical rigor [37] is based oncon-
struct validity(multiple data sources addressing the
same constructs),internal validity (pattern matching
between strategic documents and operational practice
– digital infrastructure),external validity(replication
logic across three cases with shared contextual charac-
teristics) andreliability (detailed case study database
maintained including coding decisions).
Phase 2: Digital infrastructure assessment
(June–July 2025) Website analysis (BDC tool [52])
and social media analysis (RivalIQ tool [53]) are used
to empirically verifyhow strategic objectives in the
field of digital branding are fulfilled, so that the
requirement for convergent evidence from multiple
sources is met [37]. In the case ofwebsite analysis,
this involves sampling, metrics (composite scores) and
analysis performed by the BDC tool. Similarly, for
social media analysisfocused on official destination
accounts (Facebook, Instagram) for a 30-day period,
metrics (audience size, post frequency, engagement
(likes + comments + shares), engagement rate) and
the resulting analysis (cross-channel composite indi-
cators, engagement rates) obtained by the RivalIQ
tool. Digital metrics are interpreted through lens of
strategic document analysis.
The study incorporates targeted quantitative ele-
ments, including baseline tourism statistics published
by Eurostat and national statistical offices, and digital
platform analytics, to complement the primary quali-
tative analysis. Digital infrastructure assessment was
conducted using publicly available information (web,
social media) as well as specialised analytical tools.

3.3. Methodological justification and
scope of digital analytics tools
The selection of the free-tier tools [52, 53] is linked
to the research focus on publicly accessible digital
branding assessment, ensuring research replicability
and evaluating the transparency of destination dig-
ital infrastructure from a perspective accessible to
all stakeholders, including smaller DMOs with lim-
ited budget. This approach is based on emphasis on
“purposeful selection” of data sources that align with
research questions while maintaining methodological
rigor [37, p. 127].
Specific concernsregardingthetoolsusedaremainly
as follows. For the BDC website evaluation tool, there
is ascope limitation, as only five pages (the homepage
and others selected by the tool) are analyzed. Ad-
ditionally, there is a depth limitation, since the tool
provides composite scores (0–100) across five dimen-
sions (accessibility, popularity, design, speed, SEO)
without detailed technical metrics. The tool is suffi-
cient for a comparative assessment of front-end user
experience, which serves as the primary touchpoint for
visitors and residents. A detailed technical analysis
was beyond the scope of this study, which focused
more on the integration of strategic documents rather
than on technical performance evaluation. The results
reflect the destination-level digital presence, making
them suitable for cross-case comparison, but not for
in-depth technical benchmarking.
For theRivalIQ social media analytics the temporal
limitation lies in the fact that the tool was available
for free use only for 30 days (13 June – 12 July 2025).
The metric limitation refers to the provision of engage-
ment metrics (posts, likes, shares, comments) with-
out sentiment analysis or demographic segmentation.
Nevertheless, the tool offers a snapshot assessment
approach, providing basic insights into digital perfor-
mance. The study did not aim to examine longitudinal
trends in social media activity. The impact of these
limitations on the results is not considered significant,
as the data reflects current operational practices, par-
ticularly whether long-term branding objectives are
being met.
These tool constraints do not compromise the
study’s primary objective –evaluating the strategic
integration of digital branding in policy documentsand
assessing whether the digital infrastructure exists to
operationalize the stated strategies. As Creswell and
Creswell [54, p.184] note, mixed methods research
prioritizes the “adequacy and appropriateness” of data
for answering specific research questions over exhaus-
tive data collection. The selected tools thus provide
sufficient evidence for comparative case analysis, while
maintaining transparency regarding methodological
boundaries.

5. Conclusion
This research examined the role of digital branding
strategies in addressing socio-economic disparities
across three marginalized tourism destinations: Os-
trava (Czech Republic), Košice (Slovakia), and Glas-
gow (Scotland). The comparative analysis reveals
significant variations in digital branding integration
maturity and disparity reduction effectiveness among
post-industrial transformation contexts.
RQ1: Digital Branding Integration in Strategic
Documents The study shows that how destinations
use digital branding differs. This backs up Kotler’s
idea that products (places) without clear branding
and communication plans commit a major market-
ing sin. Glasgow has the strongest plan, with clear
strategies and focus on both being green and using
digital tools to get things done. The Scotland is Now
campaign shows how to use digital positioning well by
communicating with visitors and helping overcome re-
gional disparities, which fits with Govers and Go’s [18]
view of place branding. Ostrava has a less developed
branding concept. It misses key parts of brand build-
ing idea and uses scattered digital methods instead of
a total framework. Košice is somewhere in the middle,
putting effort into digital tech and being sustainable,
but it doesn’t have one solid brand concept and uses
varying words in its planning papers.
RQ2: Digital Infrastructure and Community
Development The analysis of digital infrastructure
and the potential for resident engagement to bridge
visitor-oriented marketing with resident-centred com-
munity development varies significantly. The research
findings support Buhalis and Sinarta’s [ 15] argu-
ment for technology’s fundamental transformation
of customer-brand interaction points. The most ad-
vanced stage in the digitalisation of branding strategy
with a “people first” philosophy and mechanisms for
communityempowermentisevidentinGlasgow, which
follows the path from passive to responsive marketing
described by Brown et al. [24]. The study confirms
that resident engagement in destination branding and
communication is growing [26]. However, none of the
destinations effectively connect domestic visitors with
residents through digital platforms, representing a sig-
nificant missed opportunity for authentic experience
sharing and community engagement as conceptual-
ized by Buonincontri et al. [16] and Garibaldi and
Pozzi [30].
The findings show that three key elements – first,
resident-centred strategic planning, second,participa-
tive digital platform building, and third,robust impact
assessment systems– will assist to create successful
digital branding for community involvement. This
alignment supports continuous improvement of the
participatory strategy by demanding sophisticated
measurement systems able to follow cause-and-effect
connections between digital branding efforts and local
outcomes.
Final practical recommendations can be set out
in particular towardsdestination managers, policy-
makers and community organisations. Given the
ever-increasing importance of branding in market-
ing and marketing communication [13, 62, 63] and
the dynamics of technology and digital infrastructure,
destination managersshould focus on comprehensive
brand strategies to build a coherent and competitive
brand. Research shows that technological solutions
without strategic integration of digital branding and
co-creation of the brand do not necessarily mean suc-
cess.
The role of destination managers has changed signif-
icantly. As a result, they should implement resident-
centred digital platforms that facilitate authentic sto-
rytelling and community participation in tourism de-
velopment processes, recognizing that tourism experi-
ences involve active participation and interaction with
local businesses, residents, and other travellers [16, 30].
An integral part of a strategic approach to digital
branding and its role in reducing socio-economic dis-
parities is investment in sophisticated measurement
systems capable of tracking causal relationships be-
tween digital branding activities and community out-
comes addressing the transformation from passive to
responsive marketing approaches [24].
Digital branding strategies must be part of broader
tourism strategies prepared bypolicymakers with the
aim of reducing inequalities, not supporting them.
The development of digital infrastructure and edu-
cation in marginalized regions is particularly impor-
tant, also regarding the local community and its in-
volvement in tourism development planning and the
distribution of the economic effects. The research
has policy implications for destination managersin
the form of the need to prioritise governance design
over technological investment. Glasgow’s success is
based on aresident-centred strategic architecturethat

involvement of residents in digital branding can in-

brand, creating economic opportunities and effects, as
customers are willing to pay premium prices for excep-

genuine local character. However, the implementa-

tination branding [31, 32]. This approach recognizes
that destination brand meaning and long-term value
emerge from collaborative contributions by residents,
attractions, cultural experiences, and local businesses
rather than external imposition.

extends the theory of destination brandingto include
a community-centred frameworkand its relevance for
digital destination brandingin the context of address-

creation through active involvement of wider networks.
Digital branding is changingthe role of destination
management organizations (DMOs), as they can func-
tion as a tool for social and economic transformation
rather than merely visitor attraction. The shift from
the traditional role [20] of organizations (DMOs) to
broader stakeholder participation in brand creation
and communication is evident. Comparative analysis
provides a certain methodological basis for evaluating
digital branding across different post-industrial trans-
formation contexts incorporating Morgan et al.’s [14]
and Chen and Shih’s [21] insights about tourism com-
munication as a flexible component of brand strategy
with “bleed-over” effects on resident attitudes.
Fromtheoretical perspectivethe cross-case analysis
showed that digital branding can help reduce dispari-
ties inthree different ways, depending onthe gover-
nance configurations. First, all three destinations have
comparable digital infrastructure (websites, social me-
dia), but the results differ. This fact supports Buhalis
and Sinarta’s [15] argument thatthe mere existence of
technology does not necessarily mean transformation
or reduction of disparities. The example of Ostrava
shows that digitalisation alone without including dig-
ital visibility and community benefits objectives in
strategic documents cannot help to reduce inequalities.
Digital visibility is necessary, but without a strategic
framework it may not be effective towards achieving
the goal. Second,the mechanism of stakeholder en-
gagement, including residents, and co-creation of the
brand is visible in the case of Košice thanks to the
comprehensive ETIS monitoring system and struc-
tured approach to collaboration, which corresponds
to the theory of co-creation of the brand according
to Boyle [25]. However, the possibilities for reducing
disparities are rather limited due to the lack of interac-
tion between residents and visitors. This means that
internal communication with stakeholders is different
from public co-branding, where residents act as brand
ambassadors. This type of co-creation is relatively
effective, but it requiresspecific digital infrastructure
(e.g. community forums). Third, the highly effective
coordination ofthe destination brand ecosystemin the
case of Glasgow, which has gradually integrated the
digital ecosystem at several levels, in particular at the
level ofstrategic documents(creating value for Glas-
gow residents), digital infrastructure (high organic
search), the STEAM systemtracking the distribution
of economic impacts andworkforce development pro-
grams using digital platforms to connect residents and
employers in tourism. This holistic concept corre-
sponds to the concept of the destination ecosystem of
Buonincontri et al. [16], wherevalue is created through
collaboration, not unilateral marketing.
The findings challenge the generally accepted the-
sis that digital technologies reduce inequalities. The
results point to the fact that the reduction of inequali-
ties is a consequence of the way in which technologies
are applied ina governance model. The same technolo-
gies can have different effects on reducing disparities
depending on the way in which they arestrategically
integrated and implemented. The case ofdigital frag-
mentation in Ostrava, in particular investments in
digital infrastructure without a clear brand strategy,
shows thattechnologies without strategic grasp and
governance can worsen the situationby concentrating
the benefits among a narrow group of digitally literate
actors. On the other hand, inthe case of Glasgow,
where the strategy and its implementation are focused

Limitations and further research The mixed
methods approach, which combines analysis of strate-
gic documents with examination of digital infrastruc-
ture, provides a replicable framework for assessing
digital branding’s disparity impact. In terms of as-
sessing current limitations and possibilities for further
research, it is necessary to state that research into the
relationship between brand equity and its impacts on
resolving regional disparities is in its early stages. Re-
search is currently limited primarily bya lack of data,
but also by the veryabsence of clear branding strate-
gies in two of the three destinations studied (Ostrava,
Košice) and the inconsistencies between city and re-
gional strategies. Concerning the tools used(BDC,
RivalIQ), the above-mentioned time limitations, limi-
tations in terms of depth of analysis and limitations
in terms of data granulation must be included when
evaluating the results. At the same time, the use of
publicly available tools increases research replicabil-
ity and reflects the analytical capacity available to
resource-constrained DMOs – particularly relevant for
marginalized destinations.
Further research in the form oflongitudinal stud-
ies should focus on the causal connection between
digital branding and economic and other effects, as
well as on qualitative research among the main actors,
especially residents and visitors of the destination. Re-
search in the post-industrial transformation context
can bring valuable insights that can be used for prac-
