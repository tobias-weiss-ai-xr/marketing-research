ABSTRACT
Background
 Despite the 1998 ban on paid product
placement by the tobacco industry,
 branded tobacco
products continue to appear in media.
Methods
 Using the popular streaming series
Stranger
Things as a case study, this paper identifies potential
motivations of content producers for including branded
cigarettes in their work by analysing how the products
function in the series.
Results
 T
hese include enhancing verisimilitude,
character development and serving as action props to
anchor and advance a narrative.
Conclusions
 Since the use of br
anded tobacco
products appears to be firmly entrenched in Hollywood
as a storytelling device, policymakers should consider
mitigating the negative effects of repeated tobacco
depictions on young people by requiring advisory
warnings and or promotion of cessation resources before
and after movies or series episodes that depict tobacco
use. In addition, raising critical public awareness of the
issue may encourage content creators to reconsider
the necessity of tobacco depictions and explore more
creative alternative narrative-
 adv
ancing devices rather
than reifying smoking behaviour.
INTRODUCTION
The depiction of cigarette smoking in entertain-
ment media normalises use of deadly and addic-
tive products, and the 2012 US Surgeon General’s
Report concluded these exposures cause youth to
initiate smoking.1 If the particular cigarette brand
being smoked is identifiable by the audience, the
depiction also provides promotional exposure for
the brand. In the USA, the tobacco industry used the
marketing communication practice ‘product place -
ment’ systematically in radio, television, theatre and
motion pictures throughout the 20th century.i Ciga-
rette manufacturers either provided free products
to media producers through paid intermediaries,
or, less frequently, paid a cash fee directly to the
producers to have their products featured in media
content.2
Recognising the important role cigarette
product placement has played in fueling the
tobacco epidemic, and especially in smoking
initiation among young people, the Master
Settlement Agreement (MSA) banned the prac -
tice in 1998. 3 Branded cigarettes and other
tobacco products, however, have not disap-
peared from screens. While it did not examine
branded tobacco depictions explicitly, a recent
report shows that in 2023, tobacco use (branded
and unbranded) was frequently featured in the
top movies, music videos and nearly half of the
most popular streaming media shows among
15–24 year-
olds. 4
The objective of this study is to identify the
narrative roles branded cigarettes perform in
storytelling, which make their use attractive for
filmmakers even if they receive no compensa -
tion from the tobacco industry. The paper sits at
the intersection of media studies, marketing and
semiotics, informed by a broad range of academic
work. ii While prior studies have examined how
brands and branding construct meaning, we
focused specifically on how branded cigarettes
function in the context of filmed entertainment.
As a case study, we selected Stranger Things
(2016–2026). iii Created by Matt and Russ
Duffer, aka the Duffer Brothers, for Netflix, the
streaming series became an overnight sensation
and remained immensely popular throughout its
10- ye
ar run. The show has had over 1.2
 b
illion
views globally and received over 70 awards and
230 nominations, including Emmy and Screen
Actors Guild Awards. 5 Equally important,
WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
 ⇒ Tobacco depictions in entertainment media
are a common and persistent problem, and
exposure to smoking in movies causes young
people to start smoking. Studies document the
quantity of tobacco depictions in media, but
few address the potential motivations for these
depictions.
WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
 ⇒ This case study of branded cigarette depictions
in the popular show, Stranger Things,
demonstrates how and why branded cigarettes
serve narrative roles, supporting motivations to
include them that may operate separately from
paid product placement deals.
HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH,
PRACTICE OR POLICY
 ⇒ Even when policies prohibit paid cigarette
brand placement, entertainment media creators
may be motivated to include branded tobacco
products to serve narrative purposes. In
addition to comprehensive policies prohibiting
tobacco product placement, increasing public
awareness of the harms of this practice may
decrease producer motivations to include
branded tobacco products in media content.
Policies requiring anti-
 tobacco advertisements
be run before media with tobacco depictions
could mitigate pro-
 tobacco effects

METHODS
All 42 episodes of Stranger Things were watched by the first
author multiple times to select a number of scenes with
the most prominent branded cigarette exposures. Multiple
screenshots were taken during the viewing, which were then
reviewed iteratively to consider the elements of the scene
and the role of the branded exposure. A branded expo-
sure was defined as that a name, logo or design of a ciga -
rette brand was recognisable in at least one shot in a scene
(various shots within the same location are considered by
filmmakers to constitute a scene). Partially visible identifi -
cation was included if the branding was recognisable. The
number of shots depicting tobacco was not counted. Other
studies have done this type of quantification, but it was not
germane to the study’s purpose, which focused instead on
the narrative arc.
If a character’s choice of brand was already clearly estab -
lished in a particular scene, consequent scenes when they
smoked were considered branded exposure even if branding
was not discernible. Since smokers tend to stick with one
cigarette brand, 11 once a character’s choice is established,
the audience is likely to assume that said character keeps
smoking the same brand. Scenes involving primary and
secondary characters were prioritised. The selected scenes
were subjected to close textual analysis, a method borrowed
from literary and media studies that critically examines
written, visual or spoken messages to understand their
deeper meanings, themes and structure, and how their
components work together. v
ANALYSIS
Cigarette brands appearing in Stranger Things
Stranger Things features what appears to be product placement
for cigarette brands. Characters who smoke buy cigarette packs
and cartons in retail establishments; handle cigarette packs with
exposed or partially exposed branding; cigarette packs with
exposed or partially exposed branding are shown on coffee
tables and nightstands. These depictions may not be as promi-
nent as those of other branded products in the series, but they
are conspicuous enough to establish which brand is smoked by
which character.
The first season appears to contain exclusively R. J. Reynolds
products. Joyce Byers (Winona Ryder), the adult female protag-
onist, smokes Camel Lights, as does another female supporting
character. Jim Hopper (David Harbour), the male adult protago-
nist smokes regular, aka straight, or unfiltered Camels (figure
 1
).
In Season 2, Philip Morris’s Marlboro brand is introduced by
Billy (Dacre Montgomery), a new arrival to the local high school.
In Season 3, a renegade Soviet scientist Alexei (Alec Utgoff)
also smokes Marlboro, while journalists of the local newspaper
smoke yet another Reynolds product, Winston, the time-
 period’s
second best-
 selling
brand. The mother of a supporting character
smokes Kool in Season 4, a brand manufactured by Brown &
Williamson. One of the outcasts in high school smokes Camel
Filters.
In Season 2 and 3, a wide variety of cigarette brands are on
display behind the counter in various gas stations, accompanied
by point-
 of
-
 sale
advertising. Major brands and brand extensions
of all six members of the tobacco oligopoly are clearly identi-
fiable: Camel, Winston, Salem (RJR); Marlboro, Parliament
(PM); Pall Mall (American T obacco); Kool (B&W); L&M Filter
(Liggett & Myers); and Newport (P . Lorillard).vi
Branded cigarettes for the sake of verisimilitude
One possible explanation for the inclusion of branded ciga-
rettes in the series is to lend verisimilitude to the story—the
most frequently mentioned justification for product placement
in general. 12 Hollywood places great value on having a story,
character and mise-
 en-
 scène
that appear similar or true to real
life. ‘Appear’ is the key: Verisimilitude is not realism. Realism is
what is actually real, while verisimilitude is the appearance of
being real. Verisimilitude exists only in fiction. Even a story with
supernatural elements can achieve a high degree of verisimili-
tude if those elements appear ‘realistic’ within the story world
constructed for the film, that is, if a monster, for example, looks
and behaves according to genre conventions.

Hollywood insiders often argue brand name products are
necessary to create this illusion of reality. ‘If a movie is to accu -
rately depict today’s America’, explained legal scholar Steven
Snyder, ‘it also must depict the commercialization that has seeped
into every corner in our society’.13 The two alternatives to using
branded products—creating fictional brands from scratch, or
‘greeking out’ the recognisable branding on the package of a real
brand—are both counterproductive because they produce unfa-
miliar objects that shatter this illusion of realism. ‘You don’t see
cars driving around with their logos blocked out or labels over
the logo in real life’, maintained a product placement agent. 14
‘Everything is a brand’, added another agent. ‘You product place
roses, almonds. You can do roofing, shingles. Refrigerators are
full of real products, and you want that to be realistic.’15 If film-
makers insist on using real brands for every prop, they will insist
on using real cigarette brands too.
Using historically appropriate packaging, storefront designs,
advertisements and point of sales material also helps filmmakers
to establish the time period in which the story takes place. In
Stranger Things , the branded products are an integral part of
the producers’ strategy to achieve ‘total authenticity and fidelity,
manifest in efforts to meticulously reproduce the 1980s through
set design and props’. 16 One of the most praised aspects of the
series is how faithfully it portrays the Reagan years.17 The imme-
diately recognisable aesthetic may evoke feelings of nostalgia in
the audience. For older generations, these are recollections of
actual events in their own lives. For young viewers who were
born after the time period, the experience is mediated—they
remember the joy of watching movies and television series
depicting the 1980s.18 Nostalgia for times before they were born
has been noted to be a powerful marketing tactic to appeal to
Gen Z.19
Branded cigarettes for character development
Another reason for the creators to include cigarette brands
appears to be to exploit the well-
 established ‘personalities’
of those brands for character development. Brands in Holly -
wood have been long considered a ‘narrative shorthand’,
and
are deployed to quickly convey contextual information that
would otherwise take additional screen time. As a seminal 1959
marketing text noted, ‘People buy things not only for what
they can do, but also for what they mean’. 20 In our consumer
society, ‘the social function of goods’ has changed ‘from being
primarily satisfiers of wants to being primarily communicators
of meanings’."21
T oday, almost every commodity is associated and promoted
with a set of easily identifiable, symbolic properties—a brand
personality that may be eerily similar to the people who buy
them.22 It is unsurprising that many customers choose brands
based on how the brand’s identity reflects their own personality
traits. Using particular brands helps their buyers manifest who
they think they are, or who they aspire to become—sending
signals both to themselves and their social environment. 20 Few
commodities are better suited for self-
 expression than cigarettes.
‘Outside
the clothes and jewelry you wear’, noted Leo Burnett,
the founder of the eponymous advertising agency that repre-
sented Marlboro for decades, ‘a cigarette package is your most
frequently exposed possession. Every time you expose it, it says
something about you’.23
‘Cigarettes are a badge product’, explained Richard Pollay. 24
People who smoke carry them around all the time, and display
the branded packages whenever they light up. When they go out
and sit down somewhere—and if smoking is socially acceptable
at the location—they place their cigarette packs on a nearby
flat surface. Because the act of smoking involves an intimate
relationship with cigarettes (they put them in their mouth,
and inhale their smoke into their lungs), they tend to develop
greater brand loyalty than what a high-
 turnover
, low-
 cost, repeat
purchase item like cigarettes would normally warrant. 25 This
means that most people who smoke stick with a brand for long
time periods, sometimes decades, and are known socially as a
Marlboro, Camel, Kent, etc, smoker.
This social conditioning has an important side effect. When a
filmmaker, novelist or any artist makes one of their characters
smoke a certain brand, they can assume that the audience will
conflate the personality of the brand with that of the character
who smokes. Jim Hopper, the male adult protagonist in Stranger
Things, is a battle-
 hardened veteran of the V
ietnam War who can
handle all types of weapons and excels in hand-
 to-
 hand combat.
Smoking non-
 filter
Camel, the strongest member of the brand
family, may serve to underline Hopper’s ‘toughness’. Camel, in
general, skewed toward older, male, less educated, blue-
 collar
smokers in the 1980s. 26 Thus, the police chief’s brand pref -
erence also appears to demonstrate his contempt for his own
middle-
 class
status afforded by the prestige and salary of his job.
Smoking ‘regular’ Camels also amplifies Hopper’s self-

destructive, nihilistic behaviour
. When the audience first meets
him, he is waking up in his dilapidated trailer following an
apparent all-
 night bender
. Surrounded by empty beer cans and
an ashtray full of cigarette butts, his first act is to light up. In the
subsequent scene, he is getting ready in the bathroom, smoking
again. In his next scene, he arrives at the police station with a
cigarette hanging from his mouth.
It is not just that Hopper chain-
 smokes—he
chain-
 smokes an
unfiltered cigarette.
After the link between smoking and cancer
first became known in the 1950s, people who smoked began
abandoning unfiltered cigarettes in favour of brands or brand
extensions with filters.vii Filtered cigarettes were advertised first
explicitly, and later implicitly as ‘healthier’ than their unfil-
tered counterparts—unfortunately, even the strongest critics of
tobacco use shared this erroneous belief. 27 By the mid-  1980s,
when Stranger Things takes place,
 the combined market share of
unfiltered cigarettes in the USA was a minuscule 2.5%. 28 They
were considered by most consumers ‘as old-
 fashioned, very
strong and very dangerous’.29
At the same time, smoking a type of cigarette that hardly
anyone smokes anymore may also signify that Hopper is stub-
bornly independent and, instead of following trends, does things
his own way. The audience learns early, for example, that despite
working in law enforcement, Hopper distrusts authority. His
contempt for the establishment is shown by his terse, strained
interactions with State T roopers, the town mayor and the
employees of the secret laboratory.
Branded cigarettes are also used in Stranger Things  to rein-
force personality traits of other important characters. Billy, the
macho high-
 school student, for example, smokes Marlboro. His
appearance,
behaviour—and not least, his Chevrolet Camaro—
all mesh with the long-
 established constructed image

 tip
for decades. 31 However,
this is a different type of masculinity than that of Hopper and
straight Camels. On the socially constructed cigarette manli-
ness scale, Marlboro smokers are pretenders compared with the
police chief. If Hopper is a true, old-
 fashioned ‘
American male’,

Branded cigarettes as action props
Stranger Things  also features several scenes in which cigarette
brands play a role in propelling the narrative forward—‘action
props’ in Hollywood lingo.33 In Season 2, Episode 2, Joyce and
Hopper are discussing her son, Will, and his ongoing treatment
for trauma he experienced in the previous season. Sitting at the
kitchen table, Joyce wants to light up but realises her Camel
Lights pack is empty. She asks Hopper for a drag of the unfil-
tered Camel he is smoking. The smoke of the straight cigarette is
harsher than what she is used to. She coughs and removes shreds
of loose tobacco from her lips. Her facial impression indicates
puzzlement at how Hopper can smoke unfiltered cigarettes
(figure

3).
Hopper and Joyce both laugh, much-
 needed comic relief
for
the sustained tension they, and thereby the audience, have been
experiencing so far in the episode. The conversation becomes
personal: Nostalgically, Hopper recalls how they used to sneak
cigarettes in high school.
HOPPER: Brings me back to old times.
JOYCE: What?
HOPPER: [sighs] Sharing my cigarettes between…
JOYCE: Fifth and sixth period.
HOPPER: Yeah, under the steps. Mr. Cooper caught us that time,
remember? He was like, [gruffly] “Hey, assholes”.
JOYCE: We ran. We just ran.
At this point, Hopper and Joyce stare at each other, with
palpable sexual tension, revealing not only the probability of a
teenage ‘hook-
 up’, but also foreshadowing a romantic relation
-
ship that begins in the final episode of Season 3.
In Episode 9, smoking together—in the past and the pres-
ent—is used again to strengthen the emotional bond between
the two adult protagonists. While the kids are inside at a dance,
Hopper arrives at the school parking lot to commiserate with
Joyce, whose Season 2 boyfriend was killed.
HOPPER: Thought I might find you out here.
JOYCE: Will wanted me to give him some space, so…I’m giving
him a few feet. [chuckles]
HOPPER: [showing her his Camel pack] What do you say? I’m
pretty sure that Mr. Cooper retired in the ’70s, so…we might be
okay.
JOYCE: Give me that…
Joyce goes through the motions again—smokes, coughs,
grimaces, smiles. After sharing the cigarette throughout their
heart-
 rending
conversation about grief, Hopper puts his arm
gently around Joyce’s shoulders.
Another instance using cigarettes as action props happens in
Episode 5, Season 2. Hopper enters a complex underground
labyrinth. T o find his way out, the police chief breaks his beloved
Camels in two and drops pieces along the route. The markers
help the rescue party to find and free him from captivity. In
Episode 8, Season 1, Hopper’s cigarettes are used as a confi-
dence building measure in the ‘truce’ negotiation after he and
Joyce are captured trespassing in the secret laboratory. Threat-
ened with execution, Hopper makes a last-
 ditch proposal for

an agreement. Signalling his willingness to entertain the offer,
the head of the laboratory enters the makeshift interrogation
room, and without uttering a word, hands Hopper the pack
of Camels the arresting officers presumably took away from
him (figure

4). Hopper lights up and the two thrash out a deal
(off-

screen).
Hopper’s cigarettes are also used as narrative bookends to
create a sense of completion. viii At the beginning in Episode 1,
Season 1, the police chief arrives at the station, smoking. The
motherly secretary plucks the cigarette from his lips and puts it
out in an ashtray (figure

5). The same action happens again at
the end of the last episode of the season. In the traditional Holly-
wood narrative structure, after the climax, equilibrium needs to
be restored. The easiest way to convey the return of normalcy
is to repeat a routine activity that took place in the setup before
the conflict was introduced. Indicating that everything is still all
right, Episode 1, Season 2, features yet again the identical action
at the police station.

DISCUSSION
This qualitative analysis of Stranger Things  illustrates how film-
makers may use tobacco depictions to enhance their storytelling.
In this series, cigarettes help create verisimilitude, serve character
development and advance specific narrative arcs. Cigarettes are
so-
 called badge products
that come with carefully crafted, firmly
established brand identities—the tragic legacy of the tobacco
industry’s highly successful marketing bonanza in the previous
century. Writers, directors, producers and actors take advan-
tage of this ‘to develop or reveal some aspect of’ their protag-
onists and antagonists. 34 Cigarettes, handy and ubiquitous, are
also well-
 suited to be
used as action props. The characteristics
associated with cigarettes in the series—rebellion, masculinity,
freedom, sexual attraction, trust and normalcy—both reflect and
reinforce marketing messages known to appeal to youth.
While the 1998 MSA prohibited payments to promote tobacco
products in media, such as movies, television shows, theatre,
music and video games,35 this analysis demonstrates how unpaid
depictions not covered by this policy nonetheless reinforce
marketing messages. Considering that the depiction of neither
branded cigarettes nor that of smoking is illegal in the USA what
can be done to mitigate the damage caused by such depictions?
After a prolonged political struggle, India enacted compre-
hensive regulations aiming to reduce tobacco imagery in films
and television. Since the measures’ systematic implementation,
depictions of tobacco use in films have significantly decreased,
and public exposure to anti-
 tobacco messages has increased. 36
Under the so-
 cal
led Cinematograph Act, movies and television
programmes produced after 2012 also have to have strong
editorial justification to show tobacco use; anti-
 tobacco
health
spots must be shown at the beginning and during the middle of a
film/television programme; and a static health warning message
must appear onscreen each time the tobacco product is depicted.
Movies produced before 2012 with tobacco consumption must
contain anti-
 tobacco
health spots and the static health warn-
ings.37 But given that the US Food and Drug Administration has
repeatedly failed to implement updated health warning labels on
cigarette packs since 2009, 38 placing effective health warnings
in media showing tobacco depictions is unlikely to be feasible.
A strategy that might address the problem is to adopt poli-
cies to run warnings and or promote cessation resources before
and after movies or series episodes that depict tobacco use. ix
Networks and streaming providers could also agree to run free
anti-
 tobacco public service announcements, preferably close to
content containing tobacco
depictions. There is strong evidence
that running anti-
 tobacco
advertisements prior to movies
depicting tobacco use counteracts the pro-
 tobacco effects,
‘inoc-
ulating’ the viewer. 39 This practice could mitigate the adverse
impact of tobacco exposure without infringing on the film-
makers’ creative freedom. While Netflix has a system of ‘matu-
rity ratings’ used to inform parental controls, standard tobacco
specific warnings that lead to an ‘MA ’ rating for all streaming
platforms could disincentivise depictions of tobacco use in
streaming media. Giving movies with smoking an ‘R’ rating has
been promoted as a financial disincentive for over 2 decades. 40
Streaming platforms should also be held accountable to report
on the impact of rating systems on youth exposure to tobacco
use.
Finally, efforts to educate filmmakers about the impact of
tobacco depictions—branded or unbranded—could be benefi-
cial. Filmmakers may not be aware of the detrimental effects
of onscreen tobacco use, especially on youth, and might be
persuaded to explore alternative methods to achieve their
narrative goals. Here, Stranger Things can serve as a case study
as well. When the T ruth Initiative’s 2019 ‘While You Were
Streaming’9 report called out Netflix for featuring excessive
tobacco use in a series popular with adolescents, USA Today41
and Variety42 picked up the story, putting pressure on the series’
producers. It appears that the attention worked, at least to a
certain extent. Stranger Things did not eliminate all tobacco use
depictions, but their number in Season 4 significantly decreased
compared with previous seasons. 43 Winona Ryder’s character,
for example, stopped smoking altogether. In Season 5, the
depictions of tobacco use have further diminished—even Jim

Limitations
This study has limitations. It is not possible in this type of anal-
ysis to disentangle fully the potential roles of external pressures
versus creative motivations. T extual analysis is inherently subjec-
tive, as are many types of qualitative, interpretive studies. Just as
in ethnographic studies, where different researchers may make
note of or focus on developing an analysis of very different
aspects of the same places or situations, textual analysis uses
the researcher as the ‘instrument’. The value of an account is
assessed by whether it is reasonably representing aspects of the
text as a narrative whole and valid and useful in illuminating
various more detailed aspects of the phenomena under study.
CONCLUSION
Content producers appear to have incentives to feature branded
cigarettes in their shows even absent financial support from
tobacco companies. Understanding why filmmakers put branded
cigarettes in their content may foster more effective communica-
tion with the entertainment industry about ending the practice.
A constructive dialogue may also help to increase the willingness
of filmmakers and their employers to include ‘tobacco warn-
ings’ and anti-
 smoking commercials
to mitigate the detrimental
impact of depicting tobacco use.
