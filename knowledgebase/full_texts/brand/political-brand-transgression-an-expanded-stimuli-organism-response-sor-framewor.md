Abstract
This study examines political brand transgression (BT) using the Stimuli-Organism-
Response (SOR) framework. It explores how voters and opponents perceive politi-
cal BT and respond through anti-brand behaviors. Understanding these dynamics
is essential for political entities to manage their brand and sustain voter support.
Employing a qualitative case study with netnographic practices, this research ana-
lyzes perceived political BT by Taiwan’s Democratic Progressive Party, particu-
larly regarding migration policies and unmet domestic promises. It identifies key
stimuli—expectation disconfirmation and relationship norm violations—that trig-
ger perceptions of transgression. The findings highlight core belief misalignments
and performance failures as common drivers of political BT, leading to voter dis-
appointment, switching intentions, and opposition-led criticisms, word-of-mouth
damage, and efforts to unseat the party. This study extends the SOR framework
by outlining the stimuli, process, and outcomes of political BT while identifying
factors that moderate post-transgression behavior. Practically, it offers insights for
political brand owners on maintaining self-consistency, assessing their governance
capabilities, and curbing excessive zeal among supporters. By bridging brand man-
agement and political branding, this research provides a novel perspective on how
political entities can navigate transgressions and maintain brand integrity, ultimately
strengthening electoral resilience.

Most research on BT focuses on businesses, not political contexts. It remains unclear
when voters perceive a political BT and how they might react, while researchers have
highlighted differences between political and corporate brands. For example, politi-
cal brands are often marketing a set of ideological values and policies, while corpo-
rate brands are marketing offerings of products and services (O’Shaughnessy &
O’Shaughnessy, 2000). In addition, political brands operate in a competitive and often
adversarial environment, with rival parties or candidates directly opposing one another
(Pich et al., 2020). On the other hand, corporate brands compete in the market but may
also collaborate for mutual benefit in terms of, for example, co-branding (Blackett &
Russell, 2000) and cross-domain brand extensions (Au et  al., 2024a). These differ -
ences, in turn, emphasise the importance of testing existing brand management litera-
ture in political branding contexts.
Political brands (like a party’s name) can influence voter choice, especially when
voters lack time to review all candidates’ materials (Ahmed et al., 2017). In extreme
cases of political brand transgression, a political brand may be abandoned, as seen in
2016 when South Korea’s Saenuri Party renamed itself into Liberty Korea Party in
February 2017 after the scandal involving President Park Geun-Hye (StraitsTimes,
2017). Indeed, this case may also suggest the importance of branding in political con-
texts, as indicated by the performance of the party candidate of the 2017 South Korean
Presidential election (Hong Jun-Pyo), from less than 1% according to a public opinion
poll in mid-January 2017 to 24.04% in May 2017 (the election results). Conversely, a
political brand may be more able to prevent and manage the consequences by studying
the stimuli and moderators of political BT.
Our study examines political brand transgression using the Stimuli-Organism-
Response (SOR) Framework to analyze how Taiwan’s Democratic Progressive Party
(DPP) lost support by imposing migration barriers on Hongkongers, failing to support
Ukrainians, and not fulfilling domestic promises, which led to anti-brand responses.
Our research questions (RQs) are:
(1) What stimulates voters and opponents to perceive political brand transgression?
(2) How will voters and opponents respond to political brand transgression?

2  Literature review
2.1  Brand transgression
Brand transgression (BT) refers to breaches of implicit or explicit guidelines gov -
erning consumer-brand relationships (Aaker et  al., 2004). Past studies identify
two types: performance-based, related to core product/service functions, and
value-based, connected to brand values and self-image symbolism (Pullig et  al.,
2006). Brand transgression occurs when a brand fails to meet customer expecta-
tions through intentional actions or neglect, encompassing behaviors that violate
the norms of the consumer-brand relationship. Such transgressions can manifest
in various forms, including poor customer service, underperforming or defective
products, and ethical scandals (Khamitov et  al., 2020; Magnusson et  al., 2014;
Sayin & Gürhan-Canlı, 2015). In today’s increasingly polarized world, consumers
may react strongly against a brand when they disagree with its stance on morally
charged issues, leading to condemning emotions and anti-brand behaviors (Costa &
Azevedo, 2024; Wannow et al., 2024).
Upon perceiving transgressions, customers may retaliate, shun the brand, or
engage in harmful actions against it (Grégoire et al., 2009; Yang et al., 2022). One
common reaction to brand transgression is boycott participation, particularly dur -
ing the initial “heat-up” phase (Lasarov et al., 2023; Viscomi, 2023). Additionally,
consumers may switch to competing brands offering similar products or services
(Zhou, 2015). In some cases, brand transgression leads to brand hate (Bayarassou
et al., 2020; Costa & Azevedo, 2024), which can drive negative eWOM behaviors,
including criticism (Burgess & Jones, 2021), cyberbullying and harassment (Costa
& Azevedo, 2024; Grégoire et al., 2009). However, some consumers prioritize main-
taining their relationship with the transgressing brand, seeking to replace negative
emotions with positive or neutral ones, particularly when a strong attachment exists.
In human brand contexts, post-transgression support depends on factors such as the
consumer’s fondness for the celebrity, moral reasoning, and prior perceptions of the
individual (Park & John, 2018). Over time, avoidance tends to increase, while the
desire for revenge diminishes (Grégoire et al., 2009).
However, some research gaps remain. First, few studies examine transgression in
political brand contexts, suggesting that applying brand theories more broadly could
be valuable (Khamitov et al., 2020). Second, responses to BT vary, creating theoreti-
cal ambiguity. This may result from differing boundary conditions, necessitating fur-
ther research to understand the emergence and management of brand transgression.
2.2  Political brand
A political brand represents an identifiable and distinct pattern linked to a political
entity, such as artefacts, names, policies, sentiments, or symbols associated with a
party (Nielsen, 2017; Van Steenburg & Guzmán, 2025). Factors such as increased
valence issues, voter volatility, and the adoption of political marketing have encour-
aged viewing politics through a branding lens (Ahmed et al., 2017; Bennett et al.,

2019). Political branding has since become widespread, extending from consumer
marketing to politics (An, 2025; French & Smith, 2010). Comparing corporate
and political brands, corporate brands focus on delivering products or services to
consumers, emphasizing quality, reputation, and customer satisfaction. In contrast,
political brands revolve around ideologies, leadership, and governance, aiming to
gain public trust and voter support through policy positions, symbolic messaging,
and emotional appeal (Nielsen, 2017). Furthermore, in corporate brand contexts,
consumers’ decisions can be made across multiple days, but in political brand con-
texts, all voters make their choices on the same day (Lock & Harris, 1996).
Political branding is inherently complex. Every political brand should aim to be
seen as authentic, cohesive, engaging, trustworthy, and straightforward to a diverse
range of stakeholders, ultimately increasing their chances of electoral success (Pich
& Armannsdottir, 2021). Researchers are now exploring factors influencing political
brands and their functions (see Table 1).
Several research gaps exist in current political brand literature. Firstly, most stud-
ies concentrate on European contexts, so exploring political brands in more diverse
settings would be beneficial. Secondly, many researchers have examined political
brands before the emergence of global ideological polarization. The political brand-
ing concept remains underdeveloped, with limited attention to how voters’political
choices relate to brands (Ahmed et  al., 2017). For example, Banerjee and Goel
(2020) highlighted that in political marketing contexts, there is a lack of under -
standing of the impact of brand hate on voters. Sharma et al. (2023) suggested that
ideological incompatibility with the political party leads to political brand hate but
solely based on a survey in a single country context focusing on voters, leaving the
mechanism and the role of the political opponent brands unexplored. Therefore, it
would be advantageous to advance or revise our theoretical understanding of politi-
cal branding, possibly by identifying the boundary conditions of existing implica-
tions. In response to our research questions, we posit that certain factors may serve
as stimuli for voters’perceptions of brand transgression, leading to various outcomes.
Consequently, we review prior literature on the Stimulus-Organism-Response (SOR)
framework to establish a theoretical foundation for our study.
2.3  Stimulus‑organism‑response (SOR) framework
The stimulus-organism-response (SOR) framework offers a structured approach to
understanding human behavior in various settings (Mehrabian & Russell, 1974).
Stimuli are external influences on individuals, while organisms include internal
factors like emotions and perceptions. Responses are the observable behaviors or
reactions elicited by these stimuli. This framework is widely applied across different
fields, revealing the intricate dynamics that shape human experiences and behaviors
in diverse situations.
Stimuli primarily relate to artefact attributes in the context, such as design, con-
tent (Floh & Madlberger, 2013), Informativeness (Gao & Bai, 2014), features (Guo
et al., 2021), trust (Mim et al., 2022) and source credibility (Qiu et al., 2023). Com-
mon organism variables include perceived value (Guo et  al., 2021), attachment

3  Research method
The case research method was chosen for its effectiveness in exploring emerging
phenomena and processes closely tied to their contexts and addressing"how"research
questions (Pan & Tan, 2011), all relevant to our study. Given the multidimensional
nature of our focal phenomena (social and political aspects), their inherent complex-
ity makes a purely objective approach challenging. Instead, examining the phenom-
enon from the shared understandings among relevant stakeholders is more appropri-
ate (Klein & Myers, 1999).
We selected Taiwan’s Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), one of Taiwan’s most
prominent political parties, which governed from 2000 to 2008 and has been in
power since 2016. However, in the 2024 presidential election, the DPP saw a 17%
drop in vote shares, surpassing those of many ruling parties in other countries when
defeated. In other words, DPP is a political brand that experienced substantial suc-
cess but later declined significantly. Studying based on DPP allowed us to develop
theoretical implications grounded in established contexts (Pan & Tan, 2011).
3.1  Data collection
We collected the data between February 2024 and May 2024. Observing signifi-
cant online political discourse in Taiwan, we employed netnography, which involves
studying cultures and communities emerging from online communications. It helps
uncover insights from underexplored experiences (Langer & Beckman, 2005) and
can be integrated with qualitative methods (Kozinets, 2010). To reduce biases and
distortions in informant accounts, we gathered data from multiple sources to con-
firm our key findings (Klein & Myers, 1999), along with triangulation and caution.
These data sources include (1) DPP’s (including their politicians) official websites
and social media outlets, (2) the official websites and social media outlets of opposi-
tion parties (including their politicians), (3) pro-brand and anti-brand communities,
YouTube channels and Facebook pages, (4) Online news portals, and (5) websites
and reports of independent polling organisations and thinking tanks. Appendix 2
provides more details and examples of these data sources.
We emphasise the importance of caution given that our preliminary data col-
lection suggested that there is a significant ideological polarisation in Taiwan, and
collected data are more likely to be subject to bias. Therefore, researchers should
be more cautious than in other research contexts. For example, researchers should
reflect on the stance of the stakeholders who made certain viewpoints. Especially

3.2  Data analysis
Data analysis occurred concurrently with data collection to leverage the flexibility
of the case research method (Eisenhardt, 1989). In general, we manually coded our
data without using specific software. First, we created a chronological timeline of
the development of DPP, other political parties, and the broader Taiwanese socio-
political environment, along with data collection. This would give us a clearer over -
view of the related critical milestones and events (See Fig.  1 for an example of the
timeline of major events and milestones of DPP).
Second, we extracted a set of aggregate theoretical dimensions and second-
order themes (Gioia et al., 2013) from existing literature on brand transgression,
political brand and SOR framework. These constructs served as the framework
for guiding both data collection and analysis (see Appendix 3 ). The collected
data underwent coding using open, axial, and selective coding techniques
(Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Initially, open coding applied conceptual labels to rel-
evant excerpts from interviews and news articles, forming first-order concepts
(see Van Maanen, 1979). These first-order concepts were then organized into
second-order themes through axial coding. If a first-order concept matched an
existing second-order theme, it was directly assigned to that theme. Otherwise,
adjustments were made to existing themes, or new themes were created. Coding
continued based on these adjustments. Selective coding abstracted the second-
order themes into aggregate dimensions. In Fig.  2, we provide an example of

4  Case description
4.1  About Taiwanese election systems
Except for unconventional recall elections and referendums, Taiwanese elections fall
into local and general categories. Taiwan is divided into twenty-two districts (cit-
ies or counties) for local governance, each with a local council and a city mayor (or
county magistrate). Local elections involve selecting council members and mayors/
magistrates on separate tickets, consolidated on the same date since 2014. General
elections involve choosing 113 parliament (Legislative Yuan 1) members and the
president on separate ballots, consolidated on the same date since 2012. Among the
113 parliament members, 73 are elected by non-indigenous voters in single-mem-
ber constituencies using the first-past-the-post system. In contrast, indigenous vot-
ers elect six in two three-member constituencies with single, non-transferable votes.
Additionally, 34 members are elected through party-list proportional representation
(with a 5% electoral threshold) based on nationwide votes.
4.2  About DPP , its allies and rivals
The Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) is one of Taiwan’s two major political par-
ties, notably distinct from its main rival, the Kuomintang (KMT)2 by promoting Tai-
wan’s independence. The DPP gained power in the presidential and legislative elec-
tions in 2016 and 2020. However, it suffered a 17.08% drop in vote share and lost its
parliamentary majority in 2024.
The DPP leads the pro-Green Coalition, which includes smaller like-minded par -
ties such as the Taiwan Statebuilding Party (TSP), Social Democratic Party (SDP),
Green Party Taiwan, and Taiwan Solidarity Union (TSU). The New Power Party
(NPP), a more minor pro-independence party with similar beliefs to the DPP, was
initially part of the pro-Green Coalition but has become more critical of the DPP
on domestic issues since 2018. In contrast, the KMT leads the Pan-Blue Coali-
tion, which typically advocates for closer ties with Beijing. The Pan-Blue Coalition
consists of smaller parties with similar ideologies, including the New Party (NP),
People First Party (PFP), Non-Partisan Solidarity Union (NPSU), and the Unionist
Party.
Additionally, since 2020, the Taiwan People’s Party (TPP) has emerged as the
largest third party in Taiwan, promoting big-tent politics and serving as an alterna-
tive to the major parties.

5  Results
Our results suggested that the process of political brand transgression may be
explained using the Stimuli-Organism-Response (SOR) Framework, along with dif-
ferent moderating factors on organisms’responses (See Fig. 3).
Perceived transgressions by political brands, like (1) performance incapabili-
ties or (2) core belief misalignment, triggered both supporters’ and opponents’
responses. These transgressions cause disappointment and disagreement among sup-
porters, leading to (1) open criticism and (2) switching intentions. Meanwhile, oppo-
nents exploit these issues through (1) WOM-damaging behaviours and (2) unseating
efforts, potentially catalysing supporters’ responses. Yet, supporters’ responses are
moderated by factors like (1) brand size, (2) effective alternatives, and (3) remedy
actions. Collectively, the result is a weakened political brand, evident in (1) ally
departures, (2) declined electoral performance and (3) opponents’ continuous ero-
sion of the brand’s core value.
5.1  Stimuli: Perceived transgression
Our findings show that political brand transgressions largely stem from perfor -
mance incapabilities that impact a broad population. A notable example was the
2016 changes to labour holiday regulations, mandating one compulsory day off
weekly, a second negotiable day, and the cancellation of seven public holidays.
Despite the DPP’s 2016 election platform commitment to improving labour rights
(Pich et al., 2020), some labour rights groups saw the proposal as a compromise,
accusing the DPP of favouring wealthy businesses. Despite triggering massive pro-
tests, the proposal was passed owing to the DPP’s parliamentary majority.
Pedestrian safety became another pressing issue in Taiwan, with"pedestrian
hell"gaining attention in foreign media in 2022 due to numerous accidents at cross-
walks resulting in deaths or injuries. An online sentiment analysis by the Taiwan

5.4  Moderators on supporters’ responses to transgression
And yet, the switching intention and open criticisms of original supporters due to
disagreement and disappointment may be moderated by a range of factors, such as
(1) the perceived size of the political brand, (2) the availability of effective alterna-

Regarding the perceived size of the political brand, the DPP is one of the two
largest political parties in Taiwan, although its members and parliamentarians may
hold differing views on certain issues. On the Hongkonger issue, some DPP mem-

Hongkongers, despite their dissatisfaction with unfriendly immigration policies, did
not switch to support the DPP’s opponents.

5.5  Outcomes of political brand transgression
Amid perceived brand transgression, the DPP faced ally departures and electoral
performance decline. For instance, minor pro-independence groups like the NPP
and TSU, which endorsed the DPP in 2020, withheld support in the 2024 presiden-
tial election, avoiding the DPP (Grégoire et al., 2009). The TSU even partnered with
the Green Party to criticize the DPP for reneging on its pledge to lower the electoral
threshold for national seats, disadvantaging smaller parties. In the 2022 local elections,
DPP won only three magistrate seats and two mayorships, its poorest showing since
2014, prompting Tsai to resign as party leader. In 2024, despite winning the presiden-
tial race due to failed KMT-TPP ticket negotiations, the DPP’s vote share dropped by
17%, securing only 40.1% of votes. It also lost ten parliamentary seats, losing its par-
liamentary majority, with Taichung’s 2nd constituency witnessing KMT’s Kuan-Heng
Yen unseat DPP’s Ching-Yi Lin, with Yen setting a record for the highest number of
votes since 2008. The decline continued in local by-elections in April 2024, where the
DPP lost a safe seat in Yilan County. Moreover, in June 2024, a recall election against
Kuo-liang Hsieh (Mayor of Keelung from KMT), backed by some DPP local council-
lors, ended with 86,014 votes for Hsieh to remain in office and 69,934 for his recall.
Lastly, DPP’s tolerance towards pro-Beijing figures’calls for unification spurred
opponents’ continuous erosion of the brand value of DPP (e.g., pro-independ-
ence). Former Taiwanese president Ying-Jeou Ma of KMT visited mainland China
and opposed Taiwan’s independence several times in 2023 and 2024. In 2024, these
visits and oppositions were endorsed by the China tour of the KMT parliamentary
caucus, which was appreciated by former KMT president Hsiu-chu Hung. Addition-
ally, the New Party and the Unionist Party persisted in their political and electoral
endeavours in 2024, advocating for Chinese unification.
6  Theoretical implications
6.1  Brand transgression and its process in political brand contexts – an expanded
stimuli‑organism‑response (SOR) framework perspective
Our first theoretical implication is the contextualisation of BT in political brand
contexts. More specifically, based on the definition of brand transgression made

533
Political brand transgression: an expanded…
by Aaker et al. (2004) and our findings, we define political brand transgression
as actions, behaviours, or decisions by political brands that violate the expecta-
tions, values, or trust of their supporters (e.g., the expectations of Hongkongers
and Ukrainians in Taiwan in our case), allies (e.g., NPP) and or other stakehold-
ers. These transgressions can lead to a perceived breach of the brand’s identity
or promises, resulting in a range of undesirable consequences, such as WOM-
damaging behaviour, supporters’ switching intention, departure of allies, declined
electoral performance (as indicated by partial loss of endorsement from previous
supporters such as Sam Yang and Jen-to Yao) and opponents’ continuous erosion
of brand’s core value (closer relationship between KMT and Beijing government
in our case). Our identification of the loss of supporters and the emergence of
anti-brand behaviours in political BT contexts is akin to previous BT literature
(e.g., Costa & Azevedo, 2024; Riaz & Khan, 2016). However, previous BT lit-
erature that discussed BT’s outcomes largely focused on customers’ reactions. On
the other hand, we argue that brand transgression in a political context may also
lead to undesirable reactions from allies, such as criticisms and departure.
Our second theoretical implication is a political brand transgression process
model framed through an expanded stimuli-organism-response (SOR) perspective.
From the original supporters’ perspective, performance failures (e.g., egg supply
crisis) can lead to expectation disconfirmation (Khamitov et al., 2020; Magnusson
et al., 2014), while core belief misalignments (e.g., promise violation by DPP) may
violate relationship norms (Sayin & Gürhan-Canlı, 2015). Both lead to perceived
brand transgression, sparking disappointment and disagreement (Zhou, 2015). Such
emotional responses often drive supporters to criticize the political brand (e.g.,
Hongkongers criticizing DPP’s immigration policies) and sometimes to consider
switching to brands that better align with their beliefs, as seen in public figures like
Jen-to Yao endorsing NPP for party-list seats. This supports the arguments of Riaz
and Khan (2016) and Zhou (2015) on transgression-driven switching intentions.
However, supporter reactions to transgression may be influenced by factors like
the perceived size of the political brand, effective alternatives, and the brand’s
remedy actions (to be discussed further below). Additionally, political opponents
use these transgressions to amplify negative discourse, seizing on perceived flaws
to heighten criticism. Actions from both groups—supporters and opponents—
lead to anti-brand behaviours (Grégoire et al., 2009; Viscomi, 2023), including
public criticism (e.g., Chiao-Hsin Hsu’s KMT critique during the egg crisis) and
the rise of anti-brand communities (e.g.,"Impolite Taiwan"and"Smart Minister").
The primary outcome is a weakened political brand, demonstrated by electoral
performance declines like DPP’s drop from 57.1% to 40.1% in vote shares, loss
of parliamentary majority in 2024, and ongoing defeats in local by-elections in
April 2024. This mirrors consumer switching behaviour due to brand transgres-
sions in non-political contexts (Zhou, 2015). Additionally, some allies (e.g., NPP,
Green Party Taiwan, TSU) may distance themselves despite shared political val-
ues, aligning with literature on avoidance as a brand transgression response (e.g.,
Grégoire et al., 2009). Finally, opponents may intensify their actions against the
core values of the political brand. In our case, the DPP’s limited actions against
pro-Beijing politicians allow their Beijing alignment to continue, which could

534 C. H. Au
erode Taiwan’s independence—a value the DPP upholds nominally. In the long
term, maintaining the original brand values will likely become more challenging.
The identified impact of core belief misalignments is indeed akin to the argu-
ments of Pich and Armannsdottir (2021), who argued that political brands should be
trustworthy for electoral success. We may indeed understand core belief misalign-
ments as a lack of trustworthiness, which, conversely, leads to electoral failures.
Yet, we emphasise the term"perceived"here. In political branding, brand owners
face constraints, such as needing a parliamentary majority, time limitations, and bal-
ancing stakeholders’interests, which can limit a ruling party’s control. For exam-
ple, DPP’s response to the egg supply crisis was constrained by import policies, and
pedestrian safety issues also involved non-DPP local governments. However, these
can still be perceived as service failures of a brand, contributing to DPP’s brand
transgression (Khamitov et  al., 2020; Magnusson et  al., 2014). Rather than com-
paring DPP’s stated platforms with actions, this study focuses on voter perceptions,
responses, and outcomes.
Political brand transgressions that lead to supporter disappointment and declin-
ing electoral performance can be observed beyond our Taiwan case when viewed
through a political marketing lens. A good example was the Liberal Democrats in
UK, who became part of the Conservative-led coalition government in 2010, but
lost a large portion of support owing to notable policy reversals (Cutts & Russell,
2015; Dommett, 2013). In Malaysia, the Malaysian Chinese Association (MCA) was
perceived to be compromising the rights of Malaysian Chinese in the coalition gov -
ernment and eventually lost all but one seat (out of 222 seats) in the lower house
of the parliament in 2018 (Chin, 2001, 2010). Other examples can also be found in
Indonesia (see e.g., Chew, 2019; Setiawan & Taufik, 2022), Greece (e.g., Fallon,
2023; Souvlis, 2020) and possibly other countries. Appendix 4 listed similar cases,
which covered individual and organisational political brands.
6.2  Factors of moderating post‑transgression behaviour in political brand
contexts
We also identified moderators on supporters’ post-transgression behaviour in politi-
cal marketing contexts, including (1) the perceived size of the political brand, (2) the
availability of effective alternatives and (3) the brand’s remedy actions.
Regarding the perceived size of a political brand, a prominent brand may
include numerous politicians who share similar yet distinct political visions and
missions. Viewpoint differences among brand owners, supporters, or represent-
atives may arise concerning issues not presented during the brand’s inception.
In our case, the DPP is one of Taiwan’s two largest political parties, established
to advocate for democracy and independence. Within the party, viewpoint dif-
ferences on economic development, public health, and other issues have existed.
Despite DPP transgressions (e.g., misinformation spread by DPP parliamentar -
ian Ching-Yi Lin against Hongkongers), Hongkongers did not vehemently oppose
the DPP, recognising the party’s diverse politicians, some of whom support their
cause. In contrast, the Liberal Democratic Party (LibDem) in the UK has never

535
Political brand transgression: an expanded…
been a major political force that can become the sole ruling party since its incep-
tion in 1988, as evidenced by its seat counts in various general elections (Cutts
& Russell, 2015; Dommett, 2013). The public may perceive the LibDem as a
smaller entity with fewer viewpoint differences among its members, leading to
limited moderation of the perceived brand transgression’s impact.
For the availability of effective alternatives, a two-party system has emerged
in many established democracies (Riker, 1982), and third parties often struggle
to secure significant votes or seats. Voters may resort to tactical voting to avoid
unfavourable outcomes (Au & Ho, 2019; Dellis, 2013). Consequently, some may
refrain from anti-brand behaviours or voting for alternatives even when perceiv -
ing a brand transgression. In our case, voters prioritizing Taiwan’s independence
may find no viable option beyond DPP in presidential elections. Kin-man Chan, a
sociology scholar from Hong Kong, expressed dissatisfaction with DPP but ulti-
mately voted for them, deeming them a lesser evil compared to KMT and TPP,
thus self-mediating the brand relationship (Park & John, 2018). While Johnson
et al. (2011) argued that having competitors offering alternatives tends to reduce
anti-brand actions (aside from switching), we contend that in political marketing
contexts, a lack of effective alternatives diminishes the likelihood of switching
behaviour in response to the transgressions of a previously preferred brand if vot-
ers perceive switching as choosing a less acceptable brand, potentially leading to
more undesirable outcomes (e.g., supporting a more pro-Beijing party that threat-
ens Taiwan’s independence).
Moreover, we identified that political brand owners may recognise their dropping
popularity and public discontent, thus taking some remedy actions. However, the
effectiveness of these remedies can vary. For instance, in response to the egg supply
crisis, Chi-Chung Chen resigned, acknowledging his limitations, which somewhat
eased public discontent. In addition, this aligns with previous literature on service
failure recovery’s role in brand transgression (e.g., Khamitov et al., 2020) and pre-
vious literature about leaders’ resignations to mitigate ongoing adverse effects of a
political brand (French & Smith, 2010). Nevertheless, Chi-Chung Chen was indeed
an independent politician recruited into the government led by the DPP. Accord-
ingly, we argue that for a brand of political party that is in power (i.e., like DPP), it
is not only the party leaders but also the senior government officials that may influ-
ence the political brand (regardless of their political affiliations). In addition, Tsai
responded to the criticisms over the 2016 labour holiday regulation changes in an
interview, stating her pain in making such a decision and balancing the rights of
SMEs and those of labourers. This aligned with previous literature on how leaders
shape a political brand (Phipps et al., 2010; Smith & French, 2009).
Yet, some remedial actions might be considered offensive. While some scholars
may suggest that developing online communities may foster the relationship with
supporters and thus contribute to the overall success of political brands (Smith &
French, 2009), the DPP faced widespread accusations (from, for example, Chiao-
Hsin Hsu, Chia-Chien Yu and Wen-je Ko) of deploying internet-water-army to
counter criticisms. Despite the DPP’s denials, these allegations exacerbated dis-
satisfaction, mirroring the negative aspects of consumers’ online brand defense,
as discussed by Au et al., (2021a, 2021b). This may be explained by the ongoing

536 C. H. Au
ideological polarisation in Taiwan (Albana & Fiori, 2024), whereas highly engaged
supporters may sometimes defend the political brand in ways perceived as aggres-
sive by others to attack political opponents, as suggested by previous literature (e.g.,
Au & Ho, 2021; Au et al., 2021a, 2021b). Accordingly, instead of simply suggest-
ing the positive, contributory function of online communities in building politi-
cal brands, we argue that the extent of ideological polarisation may influence such
function and lead to the extra attention required to manage such political brand
communities.
7  Practical implications
Our case study offers implications for political brand owners. First, they must
acknowledge constraints that limit their ability to uphold their values, especially
when becoming the ruling party or joining a coalition. They should consider these
constraints when making promises to avoid overcommitting. Failure to meet these
commitments may result in perceived transgressions, leading to allies’departure,
public anti-brand actions, or even a loss of power. Second, prioritizing consistency
in core beliefs is essential. Inconsistencies may be perceived as brand transgressions,
inviting criticism and the departure of allies. While some departing allies may be
minor, their influence can sway outcomes, especially in tight races. Political brand
owners may face decisions that conflict with their core beliefs, requiring careful
consideration of short-term gains against potential compromises to long-term brand
integrity and development. Thirdly, political brand owners must curb excessive zeal
among their supporters, particularly online. Overly zealous advocates may employ
aggressive tactics that could provoke backlash and criticism against the brand.
8  Conclusion
We conducted a case study on political brand transgression. Theoretically, we estab-
lished and extended the SOR framework to explain the stimuli, process, and out-
comes of political brand transgression. Common stimuli of political brand transgres-
sion include core belief misalignments and performance failures. These may lead
to disappointment and disagreement, resulting in supporters’ switching intentions,
opponents’ criticisms, WOM-damaging behaviours and unseating efforts, and the
erosion of the core values of the political brands. We have also identified a range of

537
Political brand transgression: an expanded…
factors that moderate post-transgression behaviour in political brand contexts. Prac-
tically, we offered insights for political brand owners on maintaining self-consist-
ency and assessing their capabilities to realise the value when assuming public office
and highlighted the importance of curbing excessive zeal among their supporters.
Our study has limitations. Firstly, it relies on a single case study of a ruling party,
raising concerns about generalizability (Walsham, 2006) though single-case stud-
ies can be valid (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). We contend that our findings can be
viewed as generalizable per the principles of analytic generalizability (Yin, 2003).
For instance, we identified anti-brand behaviour as a response to brand transgres-
sion, consistent with previous literature (e.g., Au et al., 2024b; Costa & Azevedo,
2024). We also compared the cases in the UK, Malaysia, Greece, and Indonesia
with those in our Taiwan case study, contributing to our analytical generalisation.
Nevertheless, future research, particularly on opposition parties, could statistically
validate our propositions to further clarify our model’s boundaries. Some exam-
ples of hypotheses which we can statistically test include but are not limited to: (1)
Performance incapabilities of political brands can lead to supporters’ disagreement
and disappointment, (2) Supporters’ disagreement and disappointment may lead to
switching intentions and open criticisms. Secondly, our data collection may be con-
strained by data availability. Some information regarding perceived transgression
may remain undisclosed or confidential. Similarly, we encourage further studies on
political brand transgression using qualitative and quantitative methods.
The DPP government, especially former Interior Minister Kuo-Yung Hsu,
adopted a contradictory stance. While restricting Hongkongers’ immigration appli-
cations, citing national security concerns, he was highly lenient toward radical pro-
Beijing influences. When many arrested pro-democracy figures have backgrounds
as faculty members in public universities, rejecting Hongkonger applicants based
solely on their past employment in public universities and treating them as linked to
the Hong Kong government or potential national security threats lacks justification.
In Taiwan, KMT chairperson Eric Chu was once a faculty member at the public-
funded National Taiwan University during the tenure of DPP Ing-wen Tsai as presi-
dent, but no Taiwanese would regard Eric Chu as a DPP supporter owing to such an
employment relationship. In addition, the dissemination of fake news against Hong-
kongers by Ching-Yi Lin should be condemned. However, other issues lie not solely
with the DPP but with local regulations and global supply chain constraints.
Our paper hopes to advance theoretical understandings of brand transgression
and political marketing so that political brand owners will be more informed about
managing their brands.

Country: United Kingdom
Political Brand: Liberal Democrats (LibDem)
LibDem entered power as a junior coalition partner with the Con-

Country: Malaysia
Political Brand: Malaysian Chinese Association (MCA)
Since its establishment, MCA promised to speak on behalf of the
ethnical Chinese in Malaysia but was perceived to be compromis-
ing the rights of Malaysian Chinese in the coalition government.
When a more effective choice emerged (Democratic Action Party)
in 2008, MCA began suffering from electoral performance decline.
From being the largest junior partner within the governing coalition,
MCA lost all but one seat (out of 222 seats) in the lower house of
the parliament in 2018
(Chin, 2001, 2010)
