Abstract
Place brand management has traditionally focused on constructing a desirable place brand identity often overlooking the
diversity of meanings that stakeholders attach to a place. These meanings may become visible in multiple situations, where
stakeholders actively express, defend, and negotiate what a place means to them. This study addresses the question: how
are place identity perceptions expressed and structured?

The article presents an exploratory qualitative study of the public debate surrounding the proposed Guggenheim Museum
in Helsinki. Rather than focusing on the project itself, the context triggered the public articulation of multiple, often latent,
place identity perceptions of Helsinki across media platforms. Through thematic analysis of naturally occurring discourse,
the study identifies eight dimensions of place identity that structure these expressions.

The study’s main contribution is the development of an analytical framework of place identity dimensions that can support
the understanding of situations in which place identity becomes salient in public discourse. By focusing on spontaneous
stakeholder participation, the study also contributes to participatory place branding research by showing how place identity
is continuously shaped outside formal processes.

The findings offer a structured approach to interpreting diverse place identity expressions and anticipating stakeholder
tensions in place brand management.

but as a triggering context that activated a wide range of

This study contributes to place branding research by
developing an eight-dimensional framework of place iden -
tity, derived from the analysis of public discourse in an urban
development context. The framework provides a structured
way to interpret how stakeholders express and negotiate
meanings attached to place. In addition, the study extends
existing perspectives on participation by conceptualizing
uninvited spontaneous stakeholder engagement as a central
mechanism through which place identity becomes visible
and evolves. These contributions advance understanding of
place identity as a dynamic and context-dependent phenom-
enon and provide a basis for analyzing similar processes in
other settings.
Traditionally, place brand management has prioritized
the development of a coherent and strategic brand identity
intended to promote the place. Even when stakeholders are
included in the branding through a participatory approach
(Kavaratzis and Kalandides 2015; Stoica et al., 2022), the
underlying objective typically remains the construction of a
unified brand narrative to be externally communicated. Yet,
when the multiplicity of stakeholder place identities is con-
sidered, including identities shaped by personal background,
lived experience, goals, interests, and affective ties to the
place, a fundamentally different set of challenges emerges.
Tøttenborg et al. (2023), for example, emphasize that local
groups, businesses, and residents increasingly expect, if not
demand, that place branding efforts reflect their needs and
aspirations, generate tangible value for them, and include
their voices in how the place is presented.
In response to such expectations, a participatory approach
to place brand management has emerged, wherein stake -
holders are invited to co-create the strategic brand iden -
tity (Klijn et al. 2012; Braun et al. 2013; Kavaratzis and
Kalandides 2015). However, Botschen et al. ( 2017) found
that, in practice, stakeholder involvement often proves com-
plex and contentious. Diverging views among stakeholders
regarding what should be promoted as the place brand can
lead to conflict, compromise, and, ultimately, fragile con -
sensus. Even after a formal branding decision is reached,
stakeholders typically retain their own place identity per -
ceptions, which may become activated when they feel those
identities are misrepresented or ignored, particularly in the
context of public decisions or investments.
A key challenge, therefore, is how to meaningfully iden-
tify, interpret, and compare stakeholders’ place identities,
and how to act proactively in situations where those iden -
tities may come into conflict. Although the participatory
approach has dealt with differences among stakeholders’
place identity perceptions there is a need to understand in
more detail the character of these differences. The purpose
of this paper is to empirically explore the research question:
How do various stakeholders perceive a place’ s identity ,
and can they be organized according to identity dimensions?
Identity dimensions are elements of an identity perception,
and potentially different stakeholders may prioritize dimen-
sions differently. Mapping stakeholder positions across such
dimensions can provide public administrators with a practi-
cal framework both for understanding stakeholders and for
anticipating potential tensions between stakeholders and the
administration.
This article focuses empirically on the dimensions of
identity that emerged during the public debate surround -
ing Helsinki’s proposal to host a Guggenheim Museum.
The six-year-long Guggenheim-Helsinki project ultimately
failed, and the museum was not built. However, the pro -
cess attracted considerable media attention, both nation -
ally and internationally, and revealed latent place identity
perceptions among stakeholders that became publicly and
politically salient. The case provides a compelling example
of how stakeholders’ place identities can become activated
in response to public decision-making and underscores the
necessity for public administrators to engage with these
dynamics.
It also responds to calls for more empirical research on
place branding that incorporates a broad range of stake -
holder perspectives (Taecharungroj 2019).
The study reveals the diversity of place identity percep -
tions among stakeholders, thereby contributing to the field
of place brand management by proposing a categorization
of place identity dimensions that can inform continuous
monitoring of place identity dynamics. This understanding
facilitates a more nuanced appreciation of the evolving and
sometimes conflicting identities associated with a place.
Furthermore, the paper contributes to the participatory turn
in place branding by examining a previously underexplored
phenomenon: stakeholders’ spontaneous and uninvited par-
ticipation in public discourse about place identity.
As an empirical case, Guggenheim-Helsinki is intrigu -
ing for the broader place brand context, considering that
the establishment of the Guggenheim Museum in Bilbao
had a positive, revitalizing effect on that city’s place brand
identity. However, as Hannigan (2003) and Franklin (2016)

The Guggenheim-Helsinki process has caught many
researchers’ interests. For example, Linko (2020) discusses
the museum project’s failure to persuade stakeholders that
the Guggenheim brand would strengthen the City of Hel -
sinki’s brand, particularly given the project’s high costs.
Ponzini et al. ( 2020) compared two similar Guggenheim

cal and planning terms in Abu Dhabi, UAE and Helsinki,
Finland. The comparison shows that the democratic gov -

involved multiple stakeholders, and allowed critical evalua-

ing the site-specificity, and bargaining for the city’s benefit.
Moreover, Alshawaaf and Lee’s (2021) study explored how
cultural democracy is achieved by collective mobilization.
Furthermore, Ritvala et al. ( 2021) longitudinal explanatory
case study explored the failure of Guggenheim-Helsinki,
showing that multinational organizations increasingly face
strong resistance to their market entry by some local audi -
ences, reflecting growing ideological divisions and popu -
lism in societies. Taken together, these studies reflect the
will of stakeholders to engage in debate when faced with
a controversial proposal that corresponds to their sense of
place identity.

Theoretical views on place identity and place
brand management
One of the main goals of place brand marketing is to influ -
ence stakeholders’ views of a place (Keller et al. 2012).
Since identity has long held a prominent role in brand man-
agement literature (Melewar and Karaosmanoglu 2006;
Balmer 2008) it likewise has a prominent role in the place
branding context (Botschen et al. 2017), as residents and
other stakeholders of a place hold views of what a place
represents for them.
A traditional view within place brand management
has been to distill complex place identities, reflecting the
question ‘who we are as a place’, into a singular brand
message designed to differentiate the location within
a competitive landscape (Ren and Blichfeldt 2011).
Botschen et al. ( 2017) argue, however, that place brand
identities are better understood as combinations of socio-
cultural meanings, and they propose a structured, stra -
tegic approach to developing such identities. While this
communication-based model may offer clarity for exter -
nal branding, it risks becoming a barrier if the emotional
and symbolic meanings that residents and other stake -
holders attach to a place are overlooked (Pedeliento and
Kavaratzis 2019; Hay et al., 2022). Furthermore, stake -
holders may express their unique opinions and experi -
ences of places to other residents, media, and service
providers on various platforms such as virtual communi -
ties, blogs, social networking sites, and tourism reviews
(Oliveira and Panyik 2015). The outcome may there -
fore be a brand that is distinctive but disconnected from
the lived experiences and identities of its inhabitants
(Botschen et al. 2017; Pedeliento and Kavaratzis 2019).
For place brand marketers, it is essential to recognize
such stakeholder views on places, yet they are not easy to
depict in simple identity claims (Ntounis and Kavaratzis
2017) - as Skinner ( 2008) articulates, “places do not have
single identities (…) Places may have different attrac -
tions and different meanings to various target markets and
various groups of stakeholders” (p. 916). In accordance,
Zavattaro (2013) urges urban governance to move beyond
traditional marketing campaigns and focus on meaningful
policy changes and mutual relationships among stakehold -
ers. To identify stakeholder views around a place’s iden -
tity, scholars suggest place brand marketers to adopt a
stakeholder-focused view (Martin and Capelli 2017) and
consider the diversity (Hankinson 2004; Hanna and Row -
ley 2011; Maffei 2024), and dynamic nature of place iden -
tities (e.g., Feng et al. 2023; Kavaratzis and Hatch 2013;
Kavaratzis and Kalandides 2015; Warnaby and Medway
2013). Moreover, since place identity is not necessarily
coherent or unified, but a bricolage of diverse identities in
constant formation over time, Kavaratzis and Kalandides
(2015, 1379) suggest that place brand marketers should
work with “many open-ended trajectories, and ‘celebrate’
their [stakeholders’] diversity, allowing for their interaction
through participation and openness.”
Moreover, place brand management within a public
administration context is embedded in intricate political
and institutional structures, shaped by cultural, economic,
and societal factors. As such, even policy decisions not
explicitly linked to place branding may affect stakeholder
perceptions of identity and trigger public response. This
underlines the importance of understanding the multi -
plicity of place identities and the various interpretations
of what Helsinki means to different people.

Place identity dimensions
Only few marketing studies have focused on gaining an
understanding of what specific dimensions constitute stake-
holder perceptions of place identities. As one example, Peng
et al. ( 2020) distinguish between two dimensions of place
identity; the external looks and internal thoughts of the
place’s people, and the place itself. Also, Feng et al. (2023),
as well as Wang and Chen ( 2015) highlight the social and
spatial dimension of place identity. Stakeholders can also
perceive the place identity through their own self-image and
roles in relation to the place – thus, place identity can be
local and residential (Erickson and Roberts 1997; Lee et al.
2016), but it can also evoke meanings among tourists (Dan-
dotiya and Aggarwal 2023; Wang and Chen 2015) and other
stakeholders not local to the place. Indeed, understanding
dimensions of identity in a place marketing context is com-
plex and challenging due to the multiplicity of stakeholders,
like local governments, residents, tourists, media, compa -
nies, and other organizations operating in the area on one
hand, and place brand marketers on the other, who direct
activities towards prospects in many of these groups. Fur -
thermore, Strandberg and Ek Styvén (2021) emphasize that
the same individual may also describe place identity differ -
ently depending on role perspectives - for instance, a resi -
dent may not only consider their perceptions of a place and
their relation to it, but they may also consider views of other
residents, tourists, and other stakeholders. This highlights
the need for priming to activate a specific lens on place iden-
tity when gathering stakeholder perceptions on identity and
designing place brand management campaigns (Strandberg
and Ek Styvén 2021).
Stakeholder involvement in place brand
management
Literature on place brand management has taken an interest
in how place brand marketers may involve stakeholders in
the co-creation of place brand management (Oliveira and
Panyik 2015). This participatory approach with invited par-
ticipants to place brand management involves residents and
other stakeholders in the development of place brand man -
agement (Braun et al. 2013; Klijn et al. 2012; Kavaratzis
and Kalandides 2015). Stakeholder participation in place
brand management may have various benefits. Aitken and
Campelo (2011) argue that stakeholder involvement in the
place brand management process is crucial to achieving
authenticity, stakeholder commitment, and brand sustain -
ability. Furthermore, Klijn et al. ( 2012) suggest that stake -
holder involvement may have positive effects on the clarity
of the place brand as a concept. The legitimacy of the place
brand can also be enhanced if public managers who engage
in place brand management empower stakeholders to “par -
ticipate in every stage of the place brand management pro -
cess, starting with its creation” (Martin and Capelli 2017, p
839). Storytelling is further proposed as a ‘genuine’ form of
participation, in that residents (or other stakeholders) may
be invited to share their stories and convey their personal
view on the place identity in place brand management cam-
paigns (Hay et al., 2022).
Wæraas et al. (2015) emphasize that different brand man-
agement strategies may be employed depending on the tar -
get audience in focus; for instance, with a tourist focus, it
may promote local attractions, or with an investor focus, it
may emphasize economic potential. Furthermore, although
place brand management has economic goals, place identi-
fication and the involvement of local communities (Alex -
ander and Hamilton 2016) also constitute pertinent goals of
place brand management (Zenker 2011; Zenker et al. 2017).
Notably, place identification grows gradually with the dura-
tion of residence (Fleury-Bahi et al. 2008).
A participatory approach, however, also has its backsides.
Stoica et al. ( 2022) propose that stakeholder participation
in place brand management projects can intensify disputes
and negative stakeholder discourse if people’s needs are not
properly addressed afterwards. Ripoll González et al. drew
from network management theory and found that the choice
of network management strategy led to differing outcomes
and levels of involvement, and that different fluid strategies
are needed in different points of time in the process (2025).
Further, Casais and Monteiro ( 2019) found that residents
may not be satisfied with participating in place brand man -
agement that is designed to attract tourists rather than rein -
force residents’ place attachment, which raises the questions
of whom place brand management is developed for, and
whom to involve in participation. For example, Kavaratzis
and Kalandides ( 2015) conducted a study on participatory
place brand management in Bogotá, where many stakeholder
groups—though not the poorest residents—were invited to
take part in the city’s place brand management process. This
may however result in the previously mentioned challenge
of promoting a brand that is unique yet detached from its
multiple identities (Pedeliento and Kavaratzis 2019), and
the reality that, for example, tourists face when visiting the
place.
In conclusion, while place identity, and its co-creation
is emphasized in recent place branding literature, (Ripoll
González et al. 2025) few studies have focused on explor -
ing the variety of dimensions that construct a place’s
identity – even though recognizing the place identity is
essential for successful place brand management for public
administration.

Methodology
This study is exploratory and inductive in its approach and
focuses on how people express their views on the place
brand identity of Helsinki through activities and discussions
in various media, in the context of the Guggenheim-Hel -
sinki museum proposal, which triggered discussions around
Helsinki and its identity as a place. In doing so, the study
reveals similarities but also contrasts between stakehold -
ers’ and place brand marketers’ views on the place identity.
Place identity construction involves dynamic and relational
complexity, which manifests in discursive practices among
individuals in society (Wodak et al. 2009). Thus, identities
may be expressed in discussions among stakeholders, where
they express their unique views, while influencing each oth-
er’s perceptions of situations (Cooren 2015).
Empirical case: The Guggenheim-Helsinki process
Helsinki is the capital of Finland and a site where the Solo-
mon R. Guggenheim Foundation proposed to build an art
museum. In 2011, the Helsinki City Council announced the
start of negotiations with the Guggenheim Foundation (City
of Helsinki, 2011). Thus, 2011 represents the starting point
for our study, as indicated in the timeline in Fig. 1. In the
following years, the activities and discussions surrounding
the museum were abundant and fervent, resulting in active
pro- and anti-museum movements. It also prompted the for-
mation of numerous committees, events (e.g., discussion
forums), and exhibitions. An open, international architec -
ture competition, funded by private sources, started in 2014.
The winner was announced in June 2015, and the Helsinki
City Board decided, at a meeting on November 21, 2016,
to propose that the City Council approve the Guggenheim-
Helsinki, with a vote of 8–7. The City Council (Helsinki’s
highest decision-making body) then voted on the proposal
on December 1, 2016, and chose not to build the museum.

Data
The collected data covers the whole timespan of the Gug -
genheim-Helsinki process (Fig. 1). In this study, naturally
occurring discourse (Phillips and Hardy 2002; Wodak and
Meyer 2016) that reveals stakeholders’ views of the place
brand identity is of particular interest. The collected data
consists of news articles from nationwide domestic media,
official press releases and reports from the City of Helsinki
and the S. R. Guggenheim Foundation, and individual user
comments on social media about the Helsinki-Guggenheim
process. As Silverman ( 2011) recognizes, such naturally
occurring textual data have the advantages of being rich,
culturally relevant, and readily accessible.
The data were collected by conducting online searches
with the keywords “Guggenheim” and “Helsinki”, revealing
103 texts from various nationwide media outlets, and 247
pages of text from organizational websites about Guggen -
heim-Helsinki. Furthermore, user comments were searched
on social media posts with the criterion that the posts should
relate directly to the empirical context. The search revealed
3401 social media comments, which appeared on pages
hosted by the following national media sources: Helsingin
Sanomat, the national main newspaper in Helsinki; YLE,
the national broadcasting company; and Kauppalehti, a daily
business newspaper in Finland. Furthermore, as supporting
data, comments were also identified on the Facebook page
of an influential person within the communication industry
with 2995 followers.

Findings
The identity dimensions will be presented in a longitudinal
way, to describe how the discussions unfold and change in
interaction with events and how multiple actors - the city of
Helsinki, Finnish politicians, representatives of arts orga -
nizations and professions, business communities, citizens
- participated in the public debate during 2011–2016. This
public debate around the Guggenheim-Helsinki Museum
proposal revealed views of Helsinki’s identity. The vari -
ety of these identities were grounded in past, present, and
future views on what Helsinki was, is, and could (or should)
become. During the process, not only dimensions of Hel -
sinki’s identity were revealed but also dimensions of the
Finnish national identity merged into views of Helsinki’s
identity, as will be explicated next.

Identity dimension on patriotism
The political climate in Finland in these years was influ -
enced by European nationalism (Nieminen 2015). Various
stakeholders expressed patriotism and anti-Americaniza -

Museum project in Helsinki.
Discussion
This paper focuses on discussing dimensions that constitute
a place’s identity and examining the implications of this
understanding for place brand management. Our contribu -
tion lies in proposing eight place identity dimensions that
can be continuously monitored and further developed by
public administration, particularly in their capacity as com-
missioners of place brand management.
Theoretical implications
The empirical findings indicate that the identity of a place
is shaped by multiple dimensions, including international,
identity attachment, political, spatial, aesthetic, socio-cul -
tural, economic, and environmental dimensions. Previous
studies have primarily emphasized the spatial and social
aspects of place identity (Feng et al. 2023) and the distinc-
tion between a place and its inhabitants (Peng et al. 2020).
This study extends that research by highlighting the multi -
faceted nature of place identity. Fig. 3 summarizes the eight
dimensions identified in this study.

Figure 3 presents the eight-dimensional framework of
place identity derived from the analysis. The framework
conceptualizes place identity as a set of interrelated dimen -
sions through which stakeholders interpret, evaluate, and
communicate meanings attached to a place. Rather than rep-
resenting fixed attributes of a specific location, these dimen-
sions capture analytical categories of meaning that can be
activated across different contexts.
Each dimension reflects a particular way in which place
identity may be articulated—for example, in economic,
socio-cultural, spatial, political, or symbolic terms. Impor -
tantly, the framework does not assume that all dimensions
are equally relevant in every situation. Instead, different
contexts may activate different combinations of dimensions,
depending on the nature of the issue under debate and the
stakeholders involved.
The dimensions are not mutually exclusive; rather, they
often overlap and interact, and the same dimension may
be interpreted differently by different actors. For instance,
stakeholders may agree on the relevance of a particular
dimension (e.g., economic or aesthetic) yet diverge signifi -
cantly in how they define or prioritize it. This highlights that
place identity is not only multidimensional but also inter -
nally contested within each dimension.
The framework is intended as an analytical tool that can
be applied to examine how place identity becomes expressed
in situations where it is brought into question—for example,
in urban development projects, policy decisions, or brand -
ing initiatives that provoke public debate. By identifying
which dimensions are activated, how they are articulated,
and where tensions arise, the framework enables a struc -
tured interpretation of complex and often fragmented place
identity expressions.
Not all eight dimensions of place identity are necessarily
shared by all actors—such as politicians, place representa -
tives, arts organizations, business communities, and citizens.
Different groups may emphasize distinct dimensions; for
example, societal equality and cultural vitality (socio-cul -
tural dimension) may be salient in one context, while attrac-
tiveness to tourists (international or economic dimension)
may dominate in another. These dimensions often overlap
and influence one another. Even within the same dimension,
actors may hold divergent views. As Cooren ( 2015) notes,
identities can be challenged or contested through interaction.

For instance, in the Guggenheim–Helsinki case, place brand
marketers and the pro-Guggenheim movement portrayed
Helsinki’s art scene as culturally vibrant and internationally
recognized, whereas other stakeholders viewed it as strong
yet secluded and introverted.
Public discussions can reveal aspects of place identity that
may be overlooked by place brand marketers. Nonetheless,
such dialogues offer valuable insights, as understanding a
place’s identity in one context can inform interpretations in
others (Rindell and Iglesias 2014). For instance, during the
Guggenheim–Helsinki architecture competition, differences
between the City Council’s and the Guggenheim Founda -
tion’s perceptions of Helsinki’s identity became evident
when the winning design was announced. Many stakehold -
ers rejected the proposed black museum building, believing
it conflicted with their vision of Helsinki as “The White City
of the North,” characterized by its low waterfront silhou -
ette. This example illustrates how a place’s historical sig -
nificance may hold importance in some contexts, while in
others, attention may shift toward its future development.
The present study aligns with prior research on the Gug-
genheim–Helsinki project in several ways. Our findings
support Ponzini et al. ( 2020) argument that democratic
governance fosters collective intelligence, engages mul -
tiple stakeholders, and enables critical evaluations that can
reduce risk, emphasize site specificity, and promote the
city’s interests. This suggests that in democratic contexts,
stakeholder involvement and awareness of stakeholders’
views on place identity are essential. Similarly, consistent
with Alshawaaf and Lee ( 2021), cultural democracy in the
Guggenheim–Helsinki process could have been realized
through collective mobilization. The study also reinforces
Ritvala et al. ( 2021) conclusion that multinational organi -
zations increasingly encounter strong resistance to market
entry, here examined through an identity-based lens.
Further, in contrast to the participatory view of place
brand management (Klijn et al. 2012; Braun et al. 2013;
Kavaratzis and Kalandides 2015), which emphasizes
invited participation, this study introduces uninvited par -
ticipation as an important extension of that perspective.
Uninvited participation occurs continuously in today’s
digital environment, where stakeholders freely express their
views about a place’s identity on social media platforms.
Consequently, identity formation becomes an ongoing and
open process among multiple stakeholders. We propose that
these engaged stakeholders constitute a valuable resource
for understanding place identity and that their interactions
can be analyzed using the proposed place identity dimen -
sion framework (see Fig. 3). This approach contrasts with
traditional, managerially driven models of place brand man-
agement, in which the brand promise may not reflect stake-
holders’ evolving perceptions of place identity. For instance,
in Kavaratzis and Kalandides’s (2015) study, residents con-
tributed to the construction of Bogotá’s place identity, but
the poorest citizens were excluded, creating discrepancies
between tourists’ experiences and the city’s brand identity
claims. Therefore, we advocate adopting a broader view of
stakeholders and their diverse place identities. With these
insights, we present a generic framework of place identity
dimensions (Fig. 3), suggesting that understanding a place’s
identity over time emerges at the intersection of multiple
discourses and contexts. Applying this framework could
also have yielded valuable insights during the Guggen -
heim–Helsinki process.
Managerial implications
From a managerial perspective, the framework can support
the systematic monitoring of public discourse by helping
practitioners identify which dimensions of place identity
are most salient in each situation, and where potential mis -
alignments between stakeholders and decision-makers may
occur.
As in all areas of branding, understanding stakeholders’
perceptions of identity is a fundamental principle of brand
management, including in place branding. However, as Cerda-
Bertomeu and Sarabia-Sanchez ( 2016) note, place brand
developers - such as politicians, public managers, and business
consultants - often hold divergent views of what the place brand
represents and aims to achieve. Moreover, dialogue among
these actors does not necessarily lead to consensus (Tøtten-
borg et al. 2023). Within public management research on place
branding (Hereźniak et al., 2021; Klijn et al. 2012; Tøttenborg
et al. 2023), Tøttenborg et al. (2023) found that in Tasmania’s
Go Behind the Scenery campaign, brand marketers struggled
to mobilize local support. They argue that a public manage-
ment perspective can strengthen place brand management by
(1) emphasizing negotiation and persuasion to sustain local
engagement, (2) balancing the costs and benefits of participa-
tion, and (3) ensuring fairness in determining who participates.
The present study extends this discussion by suggesting that
a place identity lens can help marketers recognize and under-
stand the diversity of place identities in practice by monitoring
public debate. Observing such naturally occurring stakeholder
participation may enable marketers to adapt their strategies to
align more closely with stakeholders’ varied perspectives. For
instance, Zelenskaya and Elkanova (2021) advocate a portfolio
of city sub-brands tailored to different target groups.

The generic framework of place identity dimensions (Fig. 3)
can assist management in continuously evaluating how stake-
holders express their perceptions of a place across various con-
texts. While not all dimensions are relevant in every situation,
the framework provides a foundation for assessing whether,
and to what extent, managerial decisions align with stakeholder
views on a place’s identity.
Place brand marketers may relate to place brand identity
differently from other stakeholders. Managing place brand
identity involves diagnosing its past and present states among
stakeholders and setting targets for its future development.
Selecting, emphasizing, and prioritizing specific dimensions,
such as regional devotion, in alignment with city strategies
are therefore central tasks in place brand management. This
is particularly relevant in democratic societies, where politi-
cal decision-making is grounded in freely elected city coun-
cils. Stakeholders, however, experience place identity from
their own perspectives and interests, resulting in a diversity
of perceptions. Recognizing this diversity presents market -
ers with the challenge of navigating multiple interpretations
of place identity while pursuing strategic goals.
In summary, this study contributes to place brand manage-
ment research by identifying dimensions of place identity that
warrant ongoing consideration within managerial practice.
Our findings show that while place brand marketers adapted
partially to stakeholders’ financial demands, the architecture
competition revealed increasing incongruence between stake-
holder groups. The analytical framework proposed here could
assist in recognizing which dimensions are most relevant to
both marketers and other stakeholders engaged in related dis-
courses. Finally, we suggest that place brand marketers need
not always act as facilitators of the place identity process;
rather, their role can be that of collectors and interpreters of
identity expressions. Through social media, stakeholders inde-
pendently engage in identity-related activities and interactions
that collectively shape the evolving place identity.
From the perspective of public administration, as the commis-
sioning authority for place branding initiatives, identity-related
complexities may surface unexpectedly and complicate branding
efforts. To effectively recognize and proactively address situations
in which place identities become pivotal to public administration
decision-making represents an alternative and broader interpreta-
tion of place brand management. What may initially appear unre-
lated to a place’s identity can, upon closer examination, reveal
underlying identity dynamics. From the perspective of public
administration, this necessitates an awareness that any policy
decision may influence, reflect, or disrupt diverse stakeholder
perceptions of place identity, potentially provoking reactions.
Limitations and future research
While this study provides valuable insights into place brand
management, several limitations should be considered when
interpreting the findings. First, the research is exploratory and
focuses on a single case—the Guggenheim–Helsinki Museum
proposal—which activated multiple dimensions of place iden-
tity in public discourse. Future studies could focus on these
dimensions and find new sub-items within each dimension, as
has been proposed by, for example Melewar and Karaosmano-
glu (2006).
Although this case is particularly illustrative, additional
dimensions of place identity may emerge in other contexts.
Future studies could examine how place identity develops in
different types of public discourses and locations, considering a
broader range of stakeholders such as tourists, residents, place
brand managers, and other actors.
Further, the nature of naturally occurring data and research
design presents certain limitations. In the analysis of this data,
researcher bias was mitigated by having two independent
analysts, one of whom was born in Helsinki and has lived
there for her entire life. The researchers discussed the results
on multiple occasions to reach a deeper understanding of the
topic. However, the empirical analysis relied solely on pub-
licly available data, including national print and online media
articles, opinion pieces, and social media discussions among
stakeholders. These media types may overrepresent certain
particularly active and visible stakeholders’ viewpoints, while
other, more silent stakeholders’ views, may remain unrepre-
sented. Furthermore, discourse in news media doesn’t only
reflect people’s views, but it is also framed selectively, and it
actively influences different audiences’ opinions on the topic.
In social media platforms, despite their potential and impor-
tance for marketing research (Patino et al. 2012) additional
types of issues may emerge, such as algorithmic bias, trolling,
and bot activity, making these platforms challenging research
environments. All in all, these naturally occurring data are not
representative of all Helsinki’s stakeholder perspectives and
place identities.
The dimensions identified in this study can be further
refined and expanded from a temporal perspective in future
research, contributing to the emerging field of place identity
diversity studies. The dynamic and context-bound nature
of place brand identity from a heritage perspective could
recognize that place identities often have deeper historical
roots than most corporate identities. Such investigations
could provide valuable insights into the temporal dimension
of both place and corporate identities.
