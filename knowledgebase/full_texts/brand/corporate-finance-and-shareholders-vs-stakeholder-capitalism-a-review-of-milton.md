Abstract
Milton Friedman argues that the responsibility of business is to maximize profits for shareholders, and that managers
should not divert company resources toward social responsibility because that role belongs to government and
individuals, not the corporation. Friedman’s 1970 paper became the foundation for shareholder primacy for decades
and remains the default position that stakeholder-theory and ESG advocates argue against today. Since the 1970s, the
business environment has changed substantially: corporate rhetoric has shifted toward stakeholder inclusion, ESG
investing has grown sharply in scale, and new disclosure rules require companies to report ESG-related information.
This review evaluates what these changes actually demonstrate, distinguishing evidence of changing norms and
rhetoric from evidence about financial performance, and argues that Friedman’s framing is too narrow for the present
day even though the empirical relationship between ESG and firm value remains genuinely unsettled.
Keywords: Shareholder Primacy; Stakeholder Theory; ESG; Corporate Social Responsibility; Milton Friedman;
Greenwashing
1. Introduction
This paper matters because it became one of the most influential arguments in business ethics, shaping
the rules in companies around the idea of shareholder primacy. This paper was published in 1970, when
environmental issues weren’t widely known to affect businesses and when ignoring social issues did not
hurt the image of companies. This raises the question: is a business’s responsibility only to generate
profit for shareholders, or do businesses also need to address social and environmental issues that affect
their finances and image? The purpose of this review is to determine whether the social responsibility
of corporations to only focus on generating profit for shareholders still holds fifty-six years later. This
paper evaluates whether the assumptions, methodology, and conclusions remain valid in the light of

2. Summary of Original Paper
Question: Is a business’s responsibility only to generate profit for shareholders, or do businesses also
need to address social and environmental issues that affect their finances and image?
Sample: Philosophical essay; Friedman presents a logical argument rather than an empirical study.
Method: No method. Friedman’s reasoning for his argument was that managers are employees of
shareholders, so spending money on social and environmental issues was spending someone else’s money
without their consent.
Conclusion: Businesses should focus on generating the most profit while obeying the law and
following basic ethics. Social responsibility belongs to individuals and government.
Contribution: Foundation for shareholder primacy when corporations shape rules and reference points
for arguments in business ethics.
3. Literature Since Publication
After Friedman’s view on the social responsibility of business, his paper became one of the most referenced
points when arguing business ethics. People increasingly challenged Friedman’s narrow view on a
business’s responsibility. Freeman and McVea (2001) argue in “A Stakeholder Approach to Strategic
Management” that businesses succeed by creating value for all stakeholders, and not just shareholders.
Freeman believes that to make profit, you need to create value for every single person that is connected to
the company, whether it’s employees, community, government. They believe that businesses depend and
rely on many groups, and if corporations don’t have these people, the company will not have value. Jensen
(2001), in “Value Maximization, Stakeholder Theory, and the Corporate Objective Function,” positions
himself between Friedman and Freeman. Jensen tries to combine both of their ideas in his paper. The
paper’s main idea is that companies should maximize long-term value, but they must pay attention to
stakeholders to achieve this goal. He proposes the “enlightened value maximization,” which means to
maximize the long-term value of the corporation and support stakeholders who help create that value.
These responses show that business is not just based on what is going on statistically or financially, but
outside forces like social issues also shape a corporation’s long-term performance. That is not to say that
corporations should give their full attention to social or environmental causes over profit, but they should
have a balance between financial goals and stakeholder relationships because that is what really creates
long-term value for companies.
Beyond this theoretical debate, scholars have tested directly whether stakeholder-oriented business
practices are associated with financial performance. Friede, Busch, and Bassen (2015) conducted a
large-scale meta-analysis combining more than 2,000 individual empirical studies on ESG and corporate
financial performance, published in the Journal of Sustainable Finance & Investment . Their results found
a statistically significant positive association between ESG performance and financial performance overall
— an empirical finding about correlation, not proof that ESG causes stronger performance, but one that
lends some support to Jensen’s claim that attending to stakeholders can help long-term value. Li, Tang,
and Li (2024), in a study published in Frontiers in Sustainability , looked at 792 listed companies in
environmentally significant industries between 2019 and 2021, using multiple regression analysis, and

Al-Tarawneh, Tayeh, Iskandrani, and Obeidat (2024) studied 188 non-financial firms listed in the UK

financial performance is not settled, and likely depends on sample, time period, and how ESG is measured.
Researchers studying this question have noted that using inconsistent ESG measurement systems across
studies makes it difficult to compare results and draw firm conclusions about how ESG affects financial
performance.
4. What Has Changed
As of 2026, several things have changed regarding the responsibilities of corporations, though each
type of change demonstrates something different and should not be treated as interchangeable evidence
for the same conclusion. First, there has been a clear shift in corporate rhetoric and stated purpose.
In 2019, the Business Roundtable (2019) publicly redefined the purpose of a corporation to include
stakeholders alongside shareholders, challenging the shareholder-primacy model Friedman described.
This statement demonstrates that corporate leaders’ stated values and public commitments have shifted
since 1970; it does not, on its own, demonstrate that stakeholder-oriented management produces better
financial outcomes than shareholder primacy did. Second, the scale of ESG-oriented investing has grown
substantially. According to Bloomberg Intelligence, global ESG assets under management grew from
roughly $22.8 trillion in 2016 to around $41 trillion in recent years, nearly doubling in less than a decade.
This growth demonstrates that investors are increasingly choosing to allocate capital using ESG criteria; it
does not by itself demonstrate that ESG-oriented investing produces superior returns, since investors can
act on a belief or preference regardless of whether that belief is empirically justified. Third, regulatory
attention to ESG has increased. The EU’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive and the U.S. SEC’s
climate risk disclosure rules now require many companies to formally report ESG-related information
(though it is worth noting the SEC has since proposed rescinding its climate disclosure rules, showing
this regulatory attention is not fixed or one-directional). This demonstrates that regulators increasingly
treat ESG-related information as material enough to require disclosure; it does not demonstrate that the
underlying ESG performance being disclosed actually predicts financial outcomes. Fourth, and separately
from all of the above, empirical studies asking directly whether ESG performance is associated with
financial performance have produced mixed results, as discussed in the previous section. This is the only
category of evidence that speaks directly to whether attending to stakeholders improves firm value, and it
remains genuinely unresolved. Together, these four types of change show that ESG has become more
central to how businesses talk about themselves, how investors allocate money, and how regulators oversee
disclosure, but they do not, on their own, resolve whether Friedman’s core empirical assumption (that a
firm best serves everyone by focusing narrowly on profit) is wrong.

5. Counter-Evidence
While the previous section shows that Friedman’s separation of business and social responsibility no
longer matches how markets, regulators, and corporate leaders talk about the firm, this does not mean
his underlying argument has been empirically disproven. Not all studies support a positive link between
ESG performance and firm value. Al-Tarawneh et al. (2024), studying 188 non-financial UK firms from
2015–2023 using panel regression with GMM estimation to control for endogeneity, found a significant
negative relationship between ESG scores and Tobin’s Q in that sample. This directly contradicts the
positive findings from Friede, Busch, and Bassen (2015) and Li, Tang, and Li (2024) discussed earlier.
A separate challenge concerns whether ESG disclosures accurately reflect real corporate behavior
in the first place. This is called greenwashing, where companies make their environmental and social
efforts seem better than they really are in order to impress investors and the public (Yu, Luu, and Chen
2020). If some firms’ ESG scores or disclosures do not accurately reflect their actual practices, then
studies relying on those scores may be measuring perception or disclosure quality rather than genuine
stakeholder-oriented performance. This matters for how the mixed empirical findings above should be
interpreted. Some of the positive associations between ESG scores and financial performance could
partly reflect that better-resourced companies produce more polished ESG reporting and disclosure, not
necessarily that their underlying practices create more value. This review returns to greenwashing as an
interpretive limitation later, since accurately measuring it is a serious methodological challenge in its own
right.
ESG and stakeholder capitalism have also faced political opposition in the United States. Since 2021,
Republican-led state legislatures have introduced many anti-ESG bills that limit the use of ESG factors
in public pension funds and state contracts. In 2023, about 165 anti-ESG bills were introduced across
37 states, and 14 states passed new laws (Lichtenstein et al. 2024). These laws mainly require public
pension funds to make investment decisions based only on financial considerations, echoing Friedman’s
argument that investment decisions should focus only on financial returns. This shows that Friedman’s
shareholder-primacy theory still has real political and institutional support today, and that the shift away
from his framework is contested rather than settled.
6. Critical Evaluation
Milton Friedman’s paper has many strengths, but the biggest one is its influence. It became one of the
most-referenced points in business ethics and is still used and responded to decades later, as shown by
Freeman and McVea (2001) and Jensen (2001) each building their own theories partly in response to
it. Friedman’s argument is also internally logical: he treats managers as employees of shareholders and
assigns them a single, measurable objective (profit) rather than the more complex, harder-to-evaluate set
of obligations that stakeholder theory can create.
Friedman’s paper also has clear limitations when evaluated against today’s evidence, though these
limitations are narrower than “Friedman has been proven wrong.” Jensen (2001) criticizes Friedman’s
narrow framing directly, arguing that ignoring stakeholder relationships can destroy long-term profit rather
than protect it. This is a theoretical critique, not an empirical refutation, since the empirical evidence on
this exact question remains mixed, as shown above. Separately, Friedman’s assumption that social and
environmental issues are wholly separate from firm value is harder to defend today given that markets,
through the scale of ESG investing, and regulators, through mandatory disclosure rules, now treat these
issues as financially material rather than as external concerns. Friedman’s paper also does not anticipate

7. If This Paper Were Written Today
The studies reviewed in this paper show mixed results. Some large reviews and recent studies (Friede,
Busch, and Bassen 2015; Ho, Nguyen, and Dang 2024; Biju et al. 2025) found that ESG is associated
with better firm performance. Other studies, including Al-Tarawneh et al. (2024), found ESG has little
or even a negative association with firm value. One reason for these mixed findings is that many studies
use a single overall ESG score, even though the environmental, social, and governance factors may affect
firms differently. This study proposes examining that possibility directly, while treating greenwashing as
an important interpretive caution rather than as a variable the study attempts to directly measure.
Research Question
Among S&P 500 firms from 2015–2026, does the environmental (E) dimension of ESG perfor-
mance predict long-term firm valuation (Tobin’s Q) more strongly than the social (S) or governance (G)
dimensions?
Hypotheses
H1: Firms with higher ESG performance will have higher firm value, measured by Tobin’s Q, among
S&P 500 firms.
H2: The environmental (E) pillar will have a stronger positive association with firm value than the
social (S) or governance (G) pillars.
Sample
S&P 500 firms, 2015–2026 (panel dataset).
Data
ESG pillar scores (environmental, social, and governance) from MSCI ESG Ratings. Financial
performance measures: Tobin’s Q (main measure), with ROA, ROE, and stock returns as additional
measures, using data from Compustat and CRSP. Control variables: firm size, industry, leverage, and
R&D intensity.
Methodology
Panel regression with firm and year fixed effects, to account for differences between firms that do not
change over time (such as industry or reputation) and events that affect all firms in a given year (such as the
COVID-19 pandemic or interest rate changes). System GMM estimation to address reverse causality and
endogeneity, specifically, the possibility that firms with higher Tobin’s Q or stronger financial performance
have more resources available to invest in ESG, rather than ESG performance causing higher firm value.
System GMM uses lagged variables as instruments to help identify the direction of the relationship,
following the approach used by Al-Tarawneh et al. (2024) and Zhang, Kong, and Hu (2024).
8. Findings and Discussion
Expected Results
Based on the mixed findings in previous research, the overall relationship between ESG and firm
value is expected to be positive but relatively weak (partially supporting H1). When the ESG pillars are
examined separately, the environmental pillar is expected to have the strongest positive association with

9. Limitations
ESG pillar scores differ across rating agencies (such as MSCI, Sustainalytics, and Refinitiv), which means
results could vary depending on which agency’s scores are used. Correlation between ESG and financial
performance does not establish causation, even with GMM estimation, which addresses reverse causality
but cannot rule out every alternative explanation. Changes in ESG regulations and political attitudes,
including anti-ESG legislation and the SEC’s proposed rescission of its climate disclosure rules, may
affect ESG reporting and data availability during the study period in ways that are difficult to fully control
for.
A further limitation concerns greenwashing, which this study treats as an interpretive caution rather
than a directly measured variable. If some firms’ ESG disclosures overstate their actual practices, then the
ESG scores used in this study, and in the broader literature reviewed above, may partly reflect disclosure
quality or public relations effort rather than genuine underlying performance. Reliably distinguishing
greenwashing from simply incomplete or imprecise reporting is a serious measurement challenge: it
would require a clear definition of what counts as an ESG claim, an independent and validated benchmark
of actual firm performance to compare it against, and a validated method for quantifying the gap between
the two. Existing attempts to do this using NLP-based textual analysis of corporate disclosures are still
developing and have not been widely validated against independent ground truth. Rather than propose an
unvalidated measurement approach, this study treats greenwashing as a plausible explanation for why the
ESG–performance relationship remains mixed across the literature, and future research using validated
greenwashing measures would be a valuable extension of the pillar-level analysis proposed here.
10. Conclusion
Milton Friedman believed that the responsibility of businesses is only to maximize profits for shareholders,
not to get involved with social and environmental concerns, because he saw those as the responsibilities
of government and individuals rather than corporations. This review finds that Friedman’s argument is
partially right rather than fully correct or fully wrong. His paper still holds up in one respect: a single,
measurable objective gives managers clear accountability, a point Jensen (2001) raises in critiquing
Freeman’s broader stakeholder theory, since trying to satisfy every stakeholder at once can leave managers
without a clear priority.
At the same time, several forms of evidence show that Friedman’s clean separation between business
and social responsibility no longer matches how markets, corporate leaders, and regulators actually
operate. Corporate purpose statements like the 2019 Business Roundtable statement show a rhetorical
shift toward stakeholders. ESG-oriented investing has grown to roughly $41 trillion globally, showing a
major shift in how capital is allocated. New disclosure rules, such as the EU’s CSRD and the SEC’s climate
disclosure rules (now themselves facing proposed rescission), show that regulators increasingly treat

ESG information as material. None of these three facts, however, proves that ESG performance actually
improves financial outcomes. That question depends specifically on the empirical literature reviewed
above, which remains genuinely mixed. Friede, Busch, and Bassen’s (2015) meta-analysis of over 2,000
studies found a positive association between ESG and financial performance, while Al-Tarawneh et al.’s
(2024) GMM-based study of UK firms found the opposite.
Greenwashing further complicates this picture, since some of the reported positive associations be-
tween ESG and financial performance may partly reflect disclosure quality rather than genuine differences
in underlying practice, though reliably measuring greenwashing remains a significant methodological
challenge. Meanwhile, the anti-ESG movement in the United States, with more than 165 bills introduced
across 37 states in 2023, shows that Friedman’s shareholder-primacy view still carries real political and
institutional support today rather than being simply an outdated idea from the 1970s. Friedman’s paper
remains important for its argument that businesses need a clear and accountable objective. However, his
assumption that social and environmental issues can be treated as fully separate from financial performance
no longer matches a business environment shaped by ESG-oriented capital, mandatory disclosure, and
public scrutiny, even though whether attending to stakeholders actually improves financial performance,
rather than simply reflecting a changed set of expectations, remains an open empirical question.
