Abstract: Local tea culture is frequently mentioned in agricultural produ ct branding, but taking Pu'er tea as an example, its
packaging design is often unsatisfactory. Symbols are overused, visual identity is unclear, and regional characteristics are difficult
to reflect. This study focuses on Bingdao Pu'er tea, exploring how to integrate local tea culture into packaging and brand design.
First, we reviewed the ancient tree resources related to this tea, the traditions of the Tai and Lahu ethnic groups, and the history
of the Tea Horse Road. Based on this, we proposed three design methods. First, we don't simply paste symbols, but delve into
local symbols. Second, we construct a coherent brand visual sys tem, rather than a collection of scattered elements. Third, we
cleverly integrate information and functionality around the actual user experience. Our research found that cultural empowerment
is not simply a matter of piling up materials, but only when sy mbol extraction, brand structure construction, and experience
integration form an interconnected cycle can it truly be effective. We believe this framework can provide practical reference for
building other agricultural product brands using local cultural resources.
Keywords: Regional Tea Culture; Bingdao Pu'er; Brand Visual Design; Packaging Design; Cultural Transformation.

1. Introduction
The Pu'er tea market has been booming in recent years, yet
packaging designs seem stuck in a rut. Generic Chinese
motifs—misty mountains, lucky clouds, brushwork
characters, red seals—keep turning up on wrapper after
wrapper. Walk into any tea shop, and you'd be hard pressed to
tell whether the cake in front of you is from Bingdao,
Banzhang, or Yiwu just by looking at the package. This
overuse of "symbolic shorthand" does two things at once: it
blurs brand identities and drains the regional distinctiveness
out of the tea itself. If regional culture is truly going to
empower a product, it can't mean slapping a few clichéd
"Chinese-style" visuals on the outside. It has to start from the
ground up—with the specific ecology, the local people's ways
of life, the historical layers that make one place different from
another—and turn those into a visual language that actually
tells a story.
Bingdao Pu'er makes for a particularly telling case. The
area still holds cultivated ancient tea trees that are centurie s
old. The Dai and Lahu communities along the Lancang River
have passed down their own tea customs for generations. And
the place itself once sat on a key stretch of the Yunnan-Tibet
Tea Horse Road. All of that adds up to rich material for
design—yet if you look at the Bingdao Pu'er packaging out
there today, you'll find the same old problem: the name
"Bingdao" gets thrown around loosely, and genuinely site-
specific designs are few and far between. That gap is exactly
what this study sets out to address. The core question is
straightforward: how can the regional tea culture tied to
Bingdao Pu'er be systematically translated into packaging and
brand visuals? And what kind of step-by-step path does that
translation actually need to follow?

3. Analysis of Local Symbols of
Bingdao Pu'er
3.1. Natural Symbols: Ancient Tea Trees and
the Landscape
Bingdao Village is located in Shuangjiang County, Lincang
City, Yunnan Province, at an altitude of approximately 1600
to 2200 meters. It is home t o several ethnic minorities,
including the Dai, Lahu, Wa, and Bulan peoples. The region's
most unique natural heritage is its centuries-old tea trees.
Their robust trunks, gnarled branches, dense foliage, and bark
covered in moss and lichen are breathtaking. The tea trees are
not only the source of tea leaves  but also symbolize "time"
and "nature." From a design perspective, these twisted
branches, layered leaves, and weathered bark can serve as
primary materials for graphic design.
Beyond the tea trees themselves, the entire ecosystem of
the Bingdao tea-producing reg ion also embodies a deeper
natural symbolism. High-altitude mists permeate the forests,
ferns and moss cover the ground, and the soil's color changes
from reddish-brown to grayish-brown—these elements
collectively constitute the "natural landscape" of Bingdao
Pu'er tea. When choosing a color scheme, these natural tones
more accurately reflect the essence of Pu'er tea than popular
color schemes.
3.2. Ethnic Symbols: Visual Traditions of the
Dai and Lahu
The Dai and Lahu peoples have lived in Bingdao and its
surrounding areas for genera tions, cultivating their own
unique visual traditions through centuries of tea cultivation.
Let's first look at the Dai. Their most distinctive patterns ar e
found on brocade. These patterns feature elephant motifs,
peacock patterns, diamond pa tterns, wave patterns, and
serrated edges, exhibiting a strong sense of rhythm and often
arranged in two- or four-way repetition. Their use of color is
also bold, frequently employing vermilion, bright yellow,
emerald green, and deep indigo—colors that strongly reflect
the climate of their tropical river basin.
On the other hand, the Lahu people possess a different
visual language. Their traditional clothing features cross
patterns, eight-petaled flowers, and canine-tooth-shaped
bands, with strong color combinations, primarily using a
contrast of black, red, and white. However, the Lahu's
connection to tea goes far beyond  patterns. They are one of
the oldest tea-cultivating ethnic groups in the Lancang River
basin. According to a legend, the Lahu's ancestors originated
from the tea tree. This identity as "tea ancestors" provides
designers with a captivating narrative perspective.
These ethnic visual elements should not be regarded as
ready-made decorative materials that can be simply cut and
pasted. They are cultural resources that need to be truly
understood and carefully interpreted. For example, the
orderly logic of Thai brocade weaving can inspire grid
structures in packaging design. Lahu patterns can be
developed into auxiliary graphic systems. In addition, the
color perceptions of the two ethnic groups can be combined
to create a unified overall tone. [5]

3.3. Historical Symbols: The Ancient Tea
Horse Road and Caravan Culture
Lincang, the origin of Bingdao tea, was once a vital hub on
the Ancient Tea Horse Road connecting Yunnan and Tibet.
Historically, Bingdao tea departed from Mengku, passing
through Lincang, Dali, Lijiang, and Zhongdian (present-day
Shangri-La), transported by horse to Tibet. This route was not
only a transportation channel for tea but also a conduit for
cultural, religious, and commercial exchanges across the
plateau. The material traces lef t along the way—hoof prints
on stone slabs, copper bells of caravans, bamboo tea utensils,
post stations, and piles of Mani stones—carry multiple

meanings: migration, trade, hardship, and hope. In the
packaging design, these traces of caravans are transformed
into a visual narrative structure, guiding the viewer's eye from
"harvesting" to "processing," then to "transportation," and
finally to "tasting," much like a mule caravan traversing
mountains.
3.4. Hierarchical Relationships and
Transformation Potential of the Three
Symbol Types
The three categories mentioned above (natural, ethnic, and
historical) are not merely interchangeable decorative
elements, but a system of meaning with a clear internal logic.
Natural symbols connect the product to material reality and
indicate the tea's origin; ethnic symbols connect it to cultural
life, showcasing the tea growers and drinkers; historical
symbols give the product temporal depth, demonstrating the
geographical and temporal spread of tea. Together, these three
construct a complete narrative arc about place, people, and
time. In actual design, each plays a crucial role. Natural
symbols enhance the product's authenticity, telling consumers
that this is genuine, ancient tea from Bingdao; ethnic symbols
create uniqueness, giving the packaging an inimitable or
counterfeit visual identity; historical symbols evoke
emotional resonance, allowing drinkers to feel the
sedimentation of time and the charm of stories in every sip of
tea. The skillful combination of these three avoids
monotonous design. In monotonous design, simply piling up
one type of symbol can numb the visual senses, leading to a
loss of meaning.

4. Design Positioning
4.1. Brand Value Proposition
Based on the above, this study proposes the brand value
proposition: Tracing the origin of the product and tasting the
spiritual world behind the tea. This proposition aims to
respond to consumers' thirst for exploring the various
symbols represented by "Bingdao Pu'er," moving from simple
tea tasting to a higher pursuit of the spiritual world.
Specifically, the brand value proposition includes three
levels. At the functional level, through "Bingdao" (a
transliteration of the local dialect), consumers learn about the
thousand-year-old ancient tea trees, trace their origin,
visualize the form and origin of the ancient tea trees, and
establish a deep trust connection with consumers. At the
emotional value level, it conveys a sense of "gift of nature,"
allowing consumers to empathize with the vigorousness of the
ancient tea trees, the sound of caravan bells, and the warmth
of ethnic culture. At the cultural value level, it adheres to pure
natural, pollution-free, and hand-picked practices. The tea
leaves also carry the warmth of traditional tea pickers. Thus,
the brand gains emotion, becoming not only a seller of tea but
also a storyteller and disseminator of the natural and
humanistic stories of Bingdao.
4.2. Target User Positioning
The first category is seasoned tea enthusiasts, aged 25-55.
As core users, they are the main consumers. They are
predominantly male, have considerable knowledge of tea
culture, have a habit of drinking tea, and have a deeper pursuit
of spiritual levels. For them, tea tasting involves not only the
tea itself but also the culture behind it, resonating with thei r
own experiences. They have some understanding of Bingdao
Pu'er tea, pursue the drinking experience of authentic
mountain charm, are tired of homogenized packaging, and
hope to see designs that more reflect the essence of the tea
and cultural depth.
The second category is culture-exploring middle-class
consumers, as secondary users. For them, tea serves as a
status symbol, reflecting elegant personal taste. They are aged
30-45, with a balanced male- to-female ratio. For them,
experienceable cultural narratives and aesthetic lifestyles are
highly attractive. The unique and interesting stories behind
"Bingdao" in the design appeal precisely to their tastes.
The third category is gift purchasers, who represent a
distinct scenario-based user group. These include corporate
buyers and individual consumers purchasing for high-end
gifting occasions, whose pr imary concerns are the
packaging's presentability, distinctiveness, and cultural
depth—they want the gift to convey thoughtfulness and good
taste.
4.3. Design Strategy Framework
Strategy 1: A Thorough Transformation of Regional
Symbols – From Appropriation to Conversion
This strategy aims to address the widespread misuse of
symbols and cultural misunderstandings in Pu'er tea
packaging design. Instead of simply pasting common Chinese
patterns and ethnic motifs, we employ a systematic
transformation method to convert symbols representing
Pingdao's unique natural landscape, ethnic culture, and
history into a design language with modern aesthetic
sensibilities. This study proposes a four-stage transformation
model: extraction, abstraction, reconstruction, and metaphor.
In terms of color, we break away from the traditional Pu'er

42
tea packaging design's dominant color scheme of gold, brown,
and white. The main colors are directly extracted from the
visual characteristics of the ancient tea trees themselves: the
grayish-brown of old branches, the deep green of mature
leaves, and the yellowish-gr een of new buds. These three
colors constitute the main color scheme, accounting for
approximately 70% of the overall tone, giving the concept of
"ancient trees" visual credibility. The secondary colors are
inspired by the ochre and gold of Dai brocade, and the deep
black and vermilion of Lahu clothing. The use of color is
perfectly balanced, with reduced  saturation, adding a subtle
touch of ethnic charm without being overpowering.
In terms of design, it doesn't directly imitate the patterns of
Dai brocade or Lahu embroidery, but rather extracts and
abstracts their inherent structural logic—the repetition and
variation of diamond geometric patterns in Dai brocade, and
the symmetry and rotation of the eight-petal flower pattern in
Lahu embroidery—and integrates them into a set of auxiliary
graphic systems and a layout grid. Furthermore, the
brushstrokes of the brand logo are inspired by the winding
lines of ancient tea tree branches, while the embossing on the
packaging surface is inspired by the concave surface of a
horseshoe.
In terms of materials and stru cture, the outer packaging's
binding system uses the binding techniques of bamboo ropes
and leather containers used on the Ancient Tea Horse Road.
Additionally, by incorporating the woven patterns of Thai
bamboo houses into the embossing on the packaging surface,
consumers can experience traces of regional culture through
touch when opening the package. Through these three steps,
the unique regional symbols are elevated from decorative
stickers to an inherent formal language of the design itself.
Strategy Two: Building a Brand Visual System – From
Fragmentation to Holism
This strategy aims to address the fragmentation and lack of
consistency prevalent in current Pu'er tea brand presentations
– namely, the use of different visual languages for logos,
packaging, and promotional materials, resulting in a lack of a
unified brand image. We will build a complete visual identity
system from the core logo to its application deployment.
Regarding the core logo design, we propose two feasible
future development options. The first option is to use a unique
font for the characters "Bingdao", incorporating the shape of
ancient tea tree branches int o the strokes, making the
characters themselves a mediu m for visually representing
natural symbolism. The second option is to extract key
structural units from Thai b rocade and Lahu patterns to
maintain a high degree of brand visual consistency and
recognizability across various media.
Strategy Three: Experience-Driven Integration of
Information and Functions
This strategy aims to addres s the challenges of "single-
function packaging" and "information overload,"
transforming packaging from a  mere tea container into an
integral part of the overall experience. Information is divided
into three levels. The first level is the product name Bingdao
Pu'er Tea, presented in the most prominent way. The second
layer contains basic product information, such as origin, year,
and net weight. The third layer, printed in smaller print on the
back of the packaging, tells the story behind the tea and its
production process.
The packaging's narrative structure creates an experiential
path, like "an open book." Upon opening the outer box, the
inner box unfolds a series of continuous illustrations and
scroll-like structures, telling the complete story from the
harvesting of ancient tea leaves to handcrafted processing,
caravan transport, and finally, the careful enjoyment of the tea.
The tea cake itself is cleverly placed at key points in the
narrative, allowing consumers to naturally experience the
progression of the visual story as they remove it.
5. Conclusion and Discussion
This study takes Bingdao Pu'er tea as an example to
systematically explore how regional tea culture influences
agricultural product packaging and brand visual design. The
study found that empowering regional culture is not simply
piling up elements. Effective design begins with in-depth and
multi-layered investigation of the unique natural, ethnic and
historical symbols of a specific region, and the construction
of a three-dimensional semantic network of "place-people-
time". On this basis, true empowerment is not just the isolated
piling up of decorative elements, but requires collaborative
design on three levels: extraction of cultural symbols,
construction of brand system and integration of functional
experience. The case of Bingdao Pu'er tea shows that when
these three levels work together, regional culture transcends
surface decoration and becomes the structural basis of brand
visual identity, giving the product authenticity and narrative
depth, thereby arousing consumer resonance.
