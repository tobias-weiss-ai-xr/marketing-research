Abstract
This article extends Williams, Escalas, and Morningstar’s (2022) framework of brand purpose and consumer eudaimonic
well-being through an in-depth analysis of Resmed, a restorative health technology company whose mission is to create
products “that people love.” We conceptualize brand purpose as organizational architecture rather than simply external
positioning, showing how purpose operates through identity integration, emotional activation, and structural integration
across stakeholders. We examine brand love as a relational outcome that consolidates purpose-driven mechanisms in
contexts characterized by vulnerability and stigma. In restorative categories, love may emerge not from aspiration but
from sustained functional restoration and dignity-preserving design. Second, we expand the framework to incorporate
employee eudaimonic well-being, as Resmed reveals that purpose internalization shapes employees’ authentic pride, coor -
dination, and enactment quality, reinforcing consumer flourishing through reciprocity loops. We further identify structural
conditions, including clarity, authenticity, vulnerability intensity, and stigma visibility, that stabilize emotional pathways
and strengthen durable commitment. Finally, we outline measurement implications for assessing purpose-driven systems.
Together, these contributions illuminate brand purpose as a multi-level system linking consumer and employee flourishing
with organizational strategy and generate interesting avenues for future research.

primarily as consumer persuasion (e.g., shaping attitudes

In this article, we explore the brand purpose framework
in the context of Resmed, a leading health technology com-
pany focused on sleep and breathing. Resmed’s stated vision
is a world where every person can achieve their full poten -
tial through better sleep and breathing with care delivered
at home. This vision is paired with a mission statement that
sets a demanding experiential standard for a health technol-
ogy company: “We create life-changing health technologies
that people love” (Resmed website). While Resmed does
not have a stated purpose per se, the combination of the
company’s vision and mission statements can be considered
the company’s purpose, and indeed in the interview, Puck -
nat confirms this. Combined, these statements delineate a
long-term and central aim that is a core part of a Resmed’s
identity and meaning structure. Additionally, the company
explicitly frames its role in terms of lived outcomes and
human potential, key aspects of eudaimonic well-being.
Our interview with Katrin Pucknat of Resmed illustrates
how the company’s mission and vision organize strategic firm
behavior and resource allocation. Additionally, many of the
mechanisms that lead to eudaimonic well-being, such as iden-
tity/meaning, relationships/community, and emotions, are dis-
cussed in the interview, as is the importance of brand trust and
the difficulty in measuring customer eudaimonic well-being.
A new conceptual contribution of this article is the elaboration
of brand love as a relational mechanism through which pur-
pose-driven identity integration, gratitude, and commitment
may consolidate over time. While love was not central in the
original WEM framework, the Resmed case suggests that in
restorative contexts, love can be a conscious goal for brands
and emerge as an outcome of sustained restoration and inter-
dependence and may function as a mediator between purpose
enactment and durable flourishing. A second contribution to
the model is the extension in scope to include employee well-
being and cross-stakeholder spillovers. This article also out-
lines future research opportunities on (i) how purpose becomes
internalized and enacted as organizational architecture, (ii)
how purpose-driven experiences foster identity, meaning,
and positive relationships, (iii) how brand love is developed
and maintained in restorative product categories such as sleep
and breathing health, (iv) the role of discrete emotions within
purpose systems, (v) employee well-being and spillovers, and
(vi) measurement and research design approaches for studying
eudaimonic impact beyond standard brand metrics.

The mission to change people’s hatred for wearing a

completely rethinking the product to create something con-

things that are radically different, even things that look out-

their mission to change hate to love. The company recently
ran a campaign around Valentine’s Day called “Love is in

dard shift within the organization—a design, experience,
and dignity benchmark—rather than as a more formal theo-

purpose and its implications for eudaimonia below.
Self-acceptance, meaning and relational pathways
in a restorative context
Self-acceptance
Consumption of brands with purpose may help consum -
ers achieve self-identity goals, constructing and reinforcing
their self-concepts. Brands with purpose may become incor-
porated into consumers’ self-concepts as they use the brand
to construct and/or strengthen their self-identity and give
meaning in their lives. Eudaimonic well-being results from
self-determination, self-knowledge and acceptance, and per-
sonal growth (Ryan & Deci, 2001; Ryff, 1989). In the case
of Resmed, CPAP equipment gives consumers autonomy
and better health outcomes, enabling them to achieve their
fullest potential. Based on this positive effect on customer
eudaimonic well-being, a meaningful connection between
the consumer and the brand may grow, engendering the brand
love Resmed hopes to evoke. Thus, self-brand connections
form with Resmed, as the company helps consumers achieve
their true self (Escalas, 2004). These sentiments are echoed
by Pucknat in the interview, where she describes love in terms
that are often identity related. For example, her love for her
iPhone is described as being “part of me” and an “extension
of myself,” and Resmed aspires to this level of connection.
Meaningful relationships
Love is not only a consumer emotion; it can be a relation -
ship and a community glue. It invites a “we/us” orientation.
Brand purpose can foster positive relationships and com -
munity as pathways to eudaimonic well-being. Nearly all
the theories of psychological well-being include interper -
sonal relationships as an important component (e.g., Ryan
& Deci, 2001; Ryff, 1989). Positive relationships consist of
warm, satisfying, trusting relationships with others; concern
about others’ welfare; being capable of strong empathy,
affection, and intimacy. In order to thrive, people need to
possess a sense of belonging that arises from meaningful
relationships.
Resmed’s product affects couples and daily routines.
Relational outcomes are salient, so much so that the “Love
is in the Air” advertising campaign focused on restoring
relationships for loving couples distanced by sleep apnea.
By centering love in its mission, Resmed can actually pro -
vide consumers with tangible outcomes such as relational
repair. Sleep products aren’t only “functional”—they affect
partners, intimacy, and daily functioning.
Another form of consumption-based relationship is brand
communities. A brand community is a “specialized, non-
geographically bound community, based on a structured set
of social relationships” (Muniz & O’Guinn, 2001, p. 412).
Purposeful brands are a likely center around which a brand
community can grow because they provide consumers with
purpose and meaning. Muniz and O’Guinn ( 2001) found
that communal interaction provides wider social benefits to
brand community members, and, in some cases, these com-
munities actually serve to strengthen family and other inter-
personal ties giving rise to eudaimonic well-being.

Future research
The Resmed case illustrates a number of interesting future
research questions. How does purpose function differently
when baseline consumer response includes avoidance or
shame? Do the relevant aspects of eudaimonic well-being
(e.g., self-acceptance, meaningful relationships, etc.) change
in importance across different product categories? To what
extent do the other mediators of brand purpose in the WEM
model play a role in Resmed’s strategy? For example, do
consumers share Resmed’s purpose, which might lead to
advocacy to reduce sleep apnea stigma, or do they simply
benefit from the CPAP product?
Brand love, interdependence, and
eudaimonic well-being
To this point, we have focused our analysis of the Resmed exam-
ple within the lens of the WEM (2022) framework, examining
how purpose contributes to eudaimonic well-being through
identity integration, self-acceptance, and positive relationships.
The Resmed case introduces love as an explicit organizational
standard and relational aspiration, which was not originally
considered in WEM (2022). We examine brand love to under-
stand how purpose-driven identity and emotional mechanisms
may consolidate into a durable relational configuration. The
Resmed case suggests that brand love is not a substitute for the
mechanisms articulated in WEM (2022), but is the relational
bond that Resmed explicitly seeks to cultivate through iden-
tity and eudaimonic-relevant experiences and purpose driven
decisions. In this sense, brand love may be understood as the
outcome and amplifier of purpose-driven eudaimonic design
when an organization structures products and experiences to
advance autonomy, self-acceptance and relational functioning.
We turn first to the prior research on brand love, which pro-
vides a framework for understanding how durable relational
bonds form between consumers and brands.
Conceptual foundations of brand love
The brand love literature has evolved beyond metaphori -
cal uses of “love” to articulate a structured construct that
captures the depth and durability of some consumer–brand
bonds. Rather than conceptualizing love as intense liking
or affective enthusiasm, recent research treats brand love as
a higher-order relational configuration that integrates cog -
nition, emotion, identity, and behavior. Batra et al. ( 2012)
define brand love as a prototype composed of multiple inter-
related elements, including self–brand integration, passion-
driven behaviors, positive emotional connection, long-term
relationship orientation, anticipated separation distress, and
strong global attitudes. This multidimensionality is theoreti-
cally significant because it positions brand love not just as a
discrete feeling but as an enduring relational structure.
At its core, brand love reflects a connection between
brand meaning and deeper aspects of a consumer’s sense
of self. Loved objects often play a role in resolving ten -
sions within identity narratives, contributing to coherence
between who individuals are, who they aspire to be, and
how they make sense of their lived experiences (Ahuvia,
2005). In this view, brand love is not merely attachment to
a consumption object but a symbolic and functional inte -
gration of that object into one’s life story. The loved brand
becomes a vehicle through which self-actualization, rela -
tional connection, and existential meaning are pursued or
stabilized.
Emotional attachment research further clarifies the
structure of this bond. Attachment is conceptualized as the
strength of the bond connecting the brand to the self, char -
acterized by brand–self connection and brand prominence
in everyday cognition (Park et al., 2010). It is distinguished
from attitude strength, with attachment reflecting emotional
bonding rather than evaluative extremity (Thompson et al.,
2005). When integrated with the brand love framework,
attachment can be understood as a foundational component
of love, but not synonymous with it. Brand love captures
the broader relational configuration that includes identity
integration, motivational investment, and anticipated loss.
These perspectives collectively suggest that brand love is
identity-relevant, emotionally infused, and behaviorally
consequential in ways that extend beyond loyalty or satis -
faction. Yet despite its relational depth, the brand love litera-
ture has rarely been explicitly situated within a eudaimonic
framework. The constructs most often linked to brand love,
such as passion and commitment, are typically examined
in terms of attitudinal strength or purchase behavior rather
than consumer flourishing.
While identity integration and emotional attachment
explain what brand love looks like, they do not fully
explain how such bonds deepen and persist over time.

Interdependence theory and the Investment Model of Com-
mitment offer a structural account of relationship durability
that complements the brand love framework (Rusbult et al.,
1998). In the investment model, relationship commitment
level reflects both psychological attachment and intent to
persist, and it is strengthened by three factors: satisfaction
level (the degree to which important needs are fulfilled),
quality of alternatives (the perceived desirability of compet-
ing options), and investment size (resources that would be
lost if the relationship were discontinued, including time,
effort, routines, and identity-linked resources). Importantly,
commitment mediates the relationship between these struc-
tural inputs and persistence-related behaviors (Rusbult et
al., 1998). Durable relationships emerge not simply because
they are liked intensely, but because valued outcomes
become contingent on them and because investments accu -
mulate over time. Applied to brand love, this perspective
suggests that love is not only an affective configuration but
a developmental outcome of increasing interdependence
between consumer and brand.
Viewed within our model of how purposeful brands can
contribute to consumer eudaimonia, however, brand love
can be understood as a relational mechanism through which
consumers pursue identity coherence, value alignment, and
meaningful participation. If a brand becomes integrated into
the self because it supports self-actualization, reinforces
central aims or values, or stabilizes life narratives, then
brand love may contribute to eudaimonic well-being. A the-
oretical question is not whether brand love predicts loyalty,
but whether it reflects and reinforces individual flourishing.
In the human love literature, close relationships promote
thriving by restoring functioning under vulnerability and
supporting growth, autonomy, and identity coherence (Fee-
ney & Collins, 2015, Ryff and Singer, 1998). If brand love
operates analogously, it may contribute to well-being not
merely by intensifying attachment but by stabilizing align -
ment between values, relationships, and daily practices.
This opens a bridge between the brand love literature and
purpose research. If purposeful brands support value align -
ment, they may also create conditions under which con -
sumer attachment deepens into love. In such cases, brand
love is not merely a marker of affective intensity but an
indicator that purpose has deeply embedded in identity and
relational structure.
Having outlined how brand love has been conceptualized
in prior research, we now return to the Resmed case to exam-
ine how brand love may develop when a brand in a restor -
ative health context builds bonds of love with its consumers.
In this case, love is not treated as a spontaneous consumer
reaction, but as an explicit organizational aspiration that is
embedded in design choices, innovation priorities, market -
ing communications, and everyday user experience.
Resmed’s love-as-purpose and an inversion of the typical
brand love trajectory
Resmed’s decision to frame its mission as creating life
changing health technologies that people love is therefore
theoretically provocative. Love is rarely articulated as a
purpose in healthcare, particularly in a category such as
CPAP therapy that is typically stigmatized. In the interview,
Katrin Pucknat emphasized that love was chosen deliber -
ately and ambitiously. She characterized love as intuitive
integration into a consumer’s daily life, similar to tech -
nologies that become extensions of the self and embedded
within everyday routines (Mick & Fournier, 1998). This
language echoes central components of the brand love lit -
erature, particularly self–brand integration and long-term
relational orientation (Batra et al., 2012). The framing also
offers a way to integrate brand purpose, brand love, and
consumer eudaimonia. However, it goes beyond that as
the Resmed context challenges conventional assumptions
about how brand love develops.
This aspiration becomes more concrete when we exam -
ine how love is translated into product experience and
everyday use. In the interview, Pucknat describes functional
components of love such as creating value and being easy
and intuitive. She describes love as a high bar—closer to a
product and experience standard than to a marketing com -
munications strategy. In this category, love implies usability
(frictionless routines, comfort, intuitive design), dignity and
discretion (reduced embarrassment and privacy-respecting
experiences), and identity integration (a sense that this is
part of the consumers’ life rather than a symbol of illness).
These features link directly to eudaimonic pathways such
as agency, self-acceptance, and meaningful relationships,
rather than merely to persuasion outcomes such as attitudes
or recommendation intentions.
The emphasis on changing hate to love further clarifies
the distinctiveness of this case. When people see CPAP
equipment, they might react with avoidance or shame. Love,
here, is positioned as a deliberate response to psychological
resistance. As Pucknat notes, the “biggest barriers are often
psychological barriers.” In this sense, love functions as a
stigma-transformation target: reducing identity threat and
reframing the experience from illness management to dig -
nity, autonomy, and restored relationships.
Prior research suggests that long term relationships between
consumers and loved brands often begin with hedonic attrac-
tion and voluntary adoption of brands that signal identity,
lifestyle, or self-expression. Love emerges as rush of emo -
tion, aesthetic resonance or symbolic alignment. Over time,
utilitarian value becomes increasingly important (Huber et al.,
2015). As consumers gain experience, functional performance
stabilizes and rationalizes the bond. The Resmed case appears

to invert this progression. In this case, it seems the pathway
to love does not begin with aspiration or symbolic desire, or
hedonic appeal, but with vulnerability and functional neces-
sity. CPAP therapy is typically adopted under conditions of
health risk, fatigue, and perhaps embarrassment. The rela -
tionship may begin with aversion, stigma, and reluctance.
Functional restoration likely precedes emotional bonding. But
physical relief produces self-restoration and reliance. If love
emerges in this context, it must do so not from initial passion,
but from sustained experience of life improvement.
The investment model provides a theoretical lens for
understanding restorative healthcare trajectories. In such
contexts, satisfaction derives not from hedonic pleasure but
from regained functioning. Improved sleep restores rela -
tional harmony and occupational competence. As these out-
comes accumulate, perceived alternatives may diminish, not
merely because of market structure but because few alter -
natives replicate the same degree of restored well-being.
Investment size increases through nightly use, behavioral
adaptation, routine formation, and identity-linked restruc -
turing of daily life. As Rusbult et al. ( 1998) emphasize,
commitment strengthens when satisfaction is high, alterna -
tives are poor, and investments are substantial, and commit-
ment directly mediates persistence behaviors. In restorative
contexts, adherence may therefore reflect not only medical
compliance but relational commitment in the investment
model sense. This deepening attachment may be accompa -
nied by increasing commitment as accumulated investments
and reduced perceived alternatives strengthen the intent
to persist in the relationships (Rusbult et al., 1998). From
an attachment perspective (Park et al., 2010), the brand
becomes central to life projects such as sustaining rela -
tionships and maintaining health. Moreover, nightly CPAP
use increases brand prominence as brand related behaviors
become embedded in everyday routines. As prominence
increases, attachment likely deepens. Love may develop
through increasing interdependence, as valued life projects
become contingent on sustained engagement with the brand.
Instrumental attachment grounded in improved flourishing
may therefore scaffold the emergence of brand love.
This inversion becomes theoretically richer when inte -
grated with Ahuvia’s ( 2005) insight that loved objects
derive emotional power from helping resolve conflicts
within consumers’ identity narratives. Sleep apnea fre -
quently generates painful tension between the desired self
and the experienced self, as chronic fatigue undermines the
ability to enact valued roles of partner, parent, colleague, or
friend. CPAP therapy does not merely symbolize identity,
it reduces lived identity dissonance. Self-brand integration
in this setting is restorative rather than expressive (Escalas
& Bettman, 2015). This may offer a meaningful theoreti -
cal extension to the brand love literature. While expressive
integration projects a desired identity outward, restorative
integration stabilizes or recovers an impaired identity.
This distinction extends identity-based conceptualizations
of brand love and suggests that healthcare contexts may
involve unique restorative pathways to attachment and love.
In restorative healthcare contexts, the integration of brand
into the self may follow a trajectory from resistance to reli -
ance to appreciation. When therapy restores sleep, relational
connection and occupational performance, the brand may
gradually shift from an external medical device to an inter -
nalized support structure. The description of love as “intuitive
integration into daily life” captures this transition. Love, here,
is not exuberant affect but seamless incorporation. It reflects
the point at which the brand becomes part of the consumer’s
ongoing life narrative rather than a disruptive intervention.
Integrating interdependence theory further suggests that
commitment may mediate not only persistence but flour -
ishing. As commitment deepens, individuals become moti -
vated to maintain and protect the relationship. In restorative
contexts, maintenance behaviors such as adherence sustain
restored autonomy and competence. Commitment may
therefore function as a bridge between functional restora -
tion and long-term eudaimonic outcomes. The brand rela -
tionship becomes part of the infrastructure through which
autonomy, competence, and relationships are maintained
(Ryan & Deci, 2001).
At the same time, the interview makes clear that Resmed is
not content to rely solely on restorative necessity as the path-
way to love. Pucknat describes ongoing R&D efforts aimed
at creating devices that reduce stigma, improve design aes -
thetics, and enhance user experience. This ambition suggests
an attempt to cultivate dimensions of love more commonly
associated with discretionary categories, including delight,
emotional resonance, and pride of ownership. In this sense,
the restorative pathway need not replace traditional passion-
based models of love but can coexist with them. Functional
restoration may initiate reliance and commitment, while
design innovation and stigma reduction may lessen emo -
tional resistance and enable more expressive forms of identi-
fication. The resulting brand love for Resmed’s new product
innovations may therefore be hybrid in nature, combining
instrumental indispensability with elements of passion asso-
ciated with aspirational consumption. Rather than viewing
these pathways as competing, the Resmed case suggests that
restorative integration and affective appeal may reinforce
one another in the development of durable love.

to generate gratitude as consumers come to experience

competence. Rather than a hedonic to utilitarian maturation,
this reflects a utilitarian to emotional and relational transfor-
mation, with gratitude as a potentially central mechanism.
Functional efficacy does not merely stabilize love (Huber, et
al., 2015); it initiates the pathway toward it.
Gratitude emerges as a developmental mechanism within
this architecture. Psychological research demonstrates that
gratitude strengthens and stabilizes relationships over time
(Algoe, 2012; Emmons & McCullough, 2003). Relationship
marketing research shows that gratitude mediates relational
investment and loyalty behaviors (Palmatier et al., 2009).
In healthcare contexts, gratitude may arise when consumers
perceive that the brand has restored valued capabilities and
reduced identity tension, as enhanced physical restoration
produces perceived life improvement and thus gratitude.
These feelings then reinforce restorative self-brand inte -
gration and increase brand prominence. As gratitude accu -
mulates, attachment consolidates into brand love. Rather
than treating gratitude as only a consequence of love, the
Resmed case suggests that gratitude precedes and deepens
love by linking the brand to identity repair and flourishing.
When consumers experience improved sleep and relational
connection and attribute that improvement to sustained ben-
efits of Resmed’s products and purpose, gratitude may situ-
ate the outcomes within an ongoing relationship rather than
one-time functional success. The improvement is no longer
simply that the device works, but rather something that the
brand has done for them over time. The separation distress
component in the Batra et al. ( 2012) brand love model
also takes on distinct meaning in this setting. Brand love
includes distress at the thought of losing access to the brand
(Batra et al., 2012). In discretionary contexts, such distress
may reflect symbolic loss. In healthcare contexts, separa -
tion distress may reflect anticipated regression into dimin -
ished physical function. Vulnerability may intensify both
the emotional intensity and existential meaning of separa -
tion distress, signaling not merely attachment but perceived
indispensability in sustaining restored well-being.
At the same time, Resmed’s emphasis on creating devices
that are compelling and reduce stigma suggests that sepa -
ration distress may not be a purely functional reponse. As
design innovation enhances aesthetic appeal and social
acceptability, the device may also acquire symbolic and
identity-expressive value. In such cases, anticipated loss may
reflect not only fear of diminished functioning but also loss
of a technology that evokes pride, aesthetic appreciation, and
even admiration. Separation distress in restorative contexts
may therefore combine indispensability grounded in restored
autonomy with elements of pride and identification more
typical of traditional passion-based brand love. This dual
structure reinforces the idea that restorative and expressive
pathways to love can converge rather than compete.
From a eudaimonic perspective, these threads suggest
that brand love may arise when a brand facilitates sustained
identity coherence under conditions of personal vulnerabil -
ity. In such cases, self–brand integration is not primarily
expressive but restorative. The brand becomes woven into
daily routines and relational practices in ways that stabilize
meaning and self-continuity. This context therefore chal -
lenges the implicit assumption in much of the brand love
literature that love emerges from aspirational identifica -
tion alone. It suggests an alternative pathway in which love
develops through repeated confirmation that the brand sup -
ports flourishing as its central purpose.
Integrating these insights with the WEM ( 2022) frame-
work linking brand purpose to eudaimonic well-being
produces novel insights. Brand purpose can articulate a
commitment to improving lives. When purpose is embed -
ded in product design and operational practice, experienced
functional restoration may reduce identity conflict and pro -
duce perceived life improvement, which evokes gratitude.
Gratitude may strengthen brand attachment and restorative
integration, evolving into brand love characterized by long
term relational commitment and anticipated separation dis -
tress. Brand love thus may emerge from and contribute to
eudaimonic outcomes such as restored autonomy, improved
relationships, and identity coherence. In this model, brand
love might mediate the relationship between purpose and
flourishing, while gratitude mediates the relationship
between restoration and attachment.
Future research
This integrated view also suggests a number of potentially
interesting questions. For example, under what conditions
can functional reliance evolve into relational attachment,
and when does attachment consolidate into love? Address -
ing this question requires integrating emotional mediation,
identity repair, and structural moderators into a unified
account of brand love as a potential contributor to eudai -
monic well-being rather than merely an outcome of affective
intensity. How does brand love develop over time in stigma
laden healthcare contexts characterized by identity conflict?
Can gratitude mediate the relationship between perceived
life restoration and brand attachment? Does restorative self-
brand integration differ structurally from more expressive
integration? How does vulnerability shape the intensity
and meaning of separation distress? Which dimensions of
attachment and love best predict sustained adherence and
long-term well-being? Addressing these questions may
extend brand love theory into developmental, vulnerability
informed, and flourishing oriented domains.

Emotions, purpose, and eudaimonic
well-being: Moral elevation, gratitude,
admiration, inspiration, and authentic pride
WEM (2022) propose that one way purposeful brands may
contribute to eudaimonic well-being lies in the activation
of other-praising, or self-transcendent, emotions, including
moral elevation, gratitude, admiration, and inspiration. In
their framework, these emotions serve as mediating mecha-
nisms that connect purpose articulation to meaning, value
alignment, and relational connection. The preceding sec -
tion examined how restorative integration and interdepen -
dence may consolidate brand love as a relational outcome
of purpose. The present section turns more directly to the
emotional processes identified in the WEM ( 2022) model,
asking how these discrete states are elicited, interpreted, and
sustained within lived organizational contexts.
The Resmed interview provides an unusually concrete
setting in which to examine how these emotional mecha -
nisms operate within a purpose-driven organization. Rather
than treating moral elevation, gratitude, admiration, and
inspiration as abstract psychological responses, the inter -
view reveals how they are elicited through identity-relevant
outcomes, visible brand decision making, and the daily
enactment of purpose. Restoration of sleep, improvement in
relational presence, and enhancement of occupational com-
petence create experiential conditions under which gratitude
and elevation are likely to arise. Decisions that prioritize
safety and integrity over expediency make moral commit -
ments visible and thus potentially elevating. Product inno -
vations aimed at reducing stigma and creating compelling
devices may evoke admiration and inspiration, reinforcing
positive identification with the brand.
At the same time, the Resmed case invites an extension
of the original WEM ( 2022) framework, which empha -
sizes other-praising emotions primarily from the consumer
perspective. The interview highlights an additional emo -
tional dynamic: authentic pride among employees who
perceive their work as contributing to restored well-being.
Employees are described as being proud when they hear
about the downstream impact of their efforts. This suggests
that pride, particularly pride grounded in meaningful contri-
bution rather than status or superiority, may operate along -
side other-praising emotions as a mechanism through which
purpose fosters flourishing. Incorporating pride allows the
analysis to extend from consumer response to organizational
internalization, thereby capturing how purpose shapes well-
being across stakeholder groups.
Below, we examine the conditions under which moral
elevation, gratitude, admiration, inspiration, and authentic
pride are likely to arise in response to Resmed’s purpose.
Drawing on a functional account of emotion (e.g., Frijda,
1986; Keltner & Haidt, 1999), these states are understood
not merely as feelings but as action-oriented responses
that shape attention, motivation, and relational orienta -
tion. By grounding the WEM ( 2022) emotional pathways
in this organizational context, and by situating them within
emerging evidence on shared purpose and clarity, the sec -
tion shows how discrete emotional responses can reinforce
relationships such as brand love and contribute to sustained
eudaimonic well-being for consumers and employees.
Moral elevation
Moral elevation is a feeling that occurs when an individual
witnesses acts of virtue or moral goodness, including char -
ity, gratitude, fidelity, generosity, and other strong displays
of virtue (Algoe & Haidt, 2009; Haidt, 2000, 2003; Keltner
& Haidt, 2003). It is experienced as warmth and expansion
and is often accompanied by affection, and sometimes love,
for the actor whose exemplary behavior is being observed.
Elevation motivates self-transcendence by prompting action
tendencies to open up to and care for the needs and interests
of others (Haidt, 2000, 2003). Consistent with a eudaimonic
account of flourishing, research finds that moral elevation
increases prosocial motivation, charitable giving, emulation
of moral role models, and the pursuit of self-transcendent
goals (Algoe & Haidt, 2009; Huta & Ryan, 2010; Thomp-
son & Siegel, 2013). Corporate Social Responsibility
(CSR) research further suggests that exposure to a compa -
ny’s responsible initiatives can elicit elevation (Romani &
Grappi, 2014).
In the context of purpose-driven brands, elevation may
be especially likely when purpose is not merely commu -
nicated but enacted as an integral and identity-defining
commitment. The Resmed interview highlights how moral
integrity becomes legible through decisions that impose
real constraints. Pucknat describes halting a clinical

The evaluative force of that statement lies in its refusal to

Moral elevation also has implications for brand rela -

brand’s values, it may increase self-brand connection and
potentially contribute to the longer-term relational configu -

brand love (Batra et al., 2012). Elevation may additionally
motivate self-transcendent action beyond the brand rela -
tionship. A central theoretical question concerns the speci -
ficity of such emulation. Stakeholders may be motivated to
pursue self-transcendent goals in domains closely aligned
with the brand’s purpose, or elevation may generalize into
broader prosocial orientation. Individual differences should
further shape these effects. Aquino et al. ( 2011) show that
moral identity centrality predicts the intensity of elevation
and the accessibility of moral exemplars. In purpose-driven
contexts, moral identity may shape which consumers notice
purpose cues, interpret them as morally meaningful, and
experience elevation in response.
Future research
These dynamics suggest several avenues for further inves -
tigation. If moral elevation arises in response to a brand’s
visible moral constraint and principled action, then one crit-
ical question concerns the conditions under which purpose
articulation elicits elevation rather than skepticism. Eleva -
tion may be more likely when purpose is enacted through
visible, costly, constraint-imposing decisions rather than
symbolic declarations, but the boundary conditions for this
effect remain underexplored. There are also questions about
when and how brands reveal moral decision making in the
marketplace and when and how consumers become aware
of it. In addition, if elevation strengthens moral alignment
with brand values, it may function as an emotional pathway
linking perceived moral authenticity to self-brand connec -
tion and, ultimately, to relational commitment. Understand-
ing whether elevation accumulates through repeated
exposure to consistent moral action, or attenuates as such
action becomes expected, would clarify its durability within
purpose systems. Finally, whether elevation motivates
domain-specific emulation aligned with the brand’s purpose
or broader prosocial orientation remains an open question
with implications for consumer behavior and eudaimonic
flourishing.
Gratitude
Gratitude is triggered by an appraisal that one is the ben -
eficiary of another’s intentionally and voluntarily provided
benefit (McCullough et al., 2001). This appraisal structure
distinguishes gratitude from generalized positive affect and
from satisfaction. Satisfaction evaluates whether perfor -
mance meets expectations (Oliver, 1997), whereas gratitude
reflects recognition of benevolence and relational invest -
ment. Gratitude promotes relationship strengthening with
responsive others, motivates reciprocation and the desire to
“give back,” increases willingness to spend time with the
benefactor, and can prompt paying generosity forward even
to unrelated others (Fredrickson, 2004; McCullough et al.,
2001). In doing so, gratitude can shift relationships beyond
exchange toward more communal forms (Algoe & Haidt,
2009). Witnessing gratitude can also provoke moral eleva -
tion (Schnall et al., 2010), suggesting that gratitude may not
only bind but also amplify moral learning.
Work on consumer inference and marketplace morality
suggests that gratitude toward brands depends on perceived
effort and motive. Morales ( 2005) shows that consumers
reward firms for high effort, including when benefits are
generalized rather than uniquely experienced, but espe -
cially when the effort is perceived as controllable by the
firm and undertaken from moral responsibility rather than
persuasion. Related work indicates that consumers may feel
gratitude when they benefit from prosocial behavior by a
social actor (McCullough et al., 2001) and in response to
CSR initiatives that are perceived as authentic (Romani et
al., 2013).
The Resmed interview suggests that gratitude is not
merely a theoretical possibility in purpose-driven health -
care contexts. At Resmed purpose is articulated as enabling
flourishing rather than delivering transactional satisfaction.
They believe that if they can help customers to sleep bet -
ter, they can be “the best version” of themselves. When
improved sleep restores daily functioning and relational
stability, consumers experience meaningful benefits that are
identity-relevant. In such settings, gratitude is likely to arise
when the benefit is attributed to intentional and sustained
organizational commitment.
Crucially, the interview also indicates that gratitude is
experienced, expressed and received. Pucknat describes
how patient stories travel through the organization reveal -
ing that expressions of appreciation are not confined to the
consumer’s private experience. Leaders and employees
receive gratitude from beneficiaries, and that received grati-
tude becomes an internal resource that reinforces their iden-
tification and commitment. Organizational research shows
that gratitude expressions increase prosocial motivation and
strengthen relationships within organizations (e.g., Grant

In restorative healthcare contexts, gratitude is not sim -

when consumers interpret improved functioning as the
result of intentional, sustained, and benevolent action.
When enhanced sleep restores relational harmony, occupa -

improvements are attributed to the brand’s ongoing com -
mitment, gratitude situates the benefit within a relational
frame rather than as an isolated functional success. Unlike
satisfaction, which reflects evaluative comparison to expec-
tations, gratitude reflects perceived beneficence. It encodes
restoration as something that has been done for the con -
sumer, thereby strengthening the relational meaning of the
experience.
This distinction becomes particularly salient under
conditions of vulnerability. When restoration resolves a
perceived identity-relevant disruption rather than minor
inconvenience, gratitude may be experienced as relief born
from regained normalcy. The restoration of a valued capa -
bility such as restful sleep may reduce tension between the
desired self and the experienced self. Gratitude, in this con-
text, reinforces the perception that the brand has contributed
meaningfully to the stability of everyday life. If gratitude
mediates the relationship between perceived restoration
and attachment more strongly than satisfaction, particularly
under vulnerability, then models of brand love and adher -
ence may need to incorporate gratitude explicitly as a struc-
tural driver rather than as an emotional byproduct.
Attribution processes are therefore central. Gratitude
depends on inferences about motive and responsibil -
ity. When consumers perceive that purpose-driven effort
reflects authentic commitment rather than persuasive
intent, gratitude is likely strengthened. When restoration
is attributed to marketing tactics, regulatory obligation, or
opportunistic positioning, gratitude may weaken or fail to
emerge. Understanding how consumers infer whether brand
purpose is authentic versus strategic self-interest is critical
for explaining when gratitude consolidates attachment and
when it does not. Perceived authenticity and visible con -
straint in organizational decision making may therefore
function as antecedents to gratitude by shaping attributional
interpretation.
Gratitude also has behavioral consequences that extend
beyond internal appreciation. Research demonstrates that
gratitude strengthens relational bonds, motivates reciprocity,
and increases investment in relationship maintenance
behaviors (Algoe, 2012; McCullough et al., 2001; Palmatier
et al., 2009). In restorative contexts, these dynamics may
manifest as continued adherence, positive word-of-mouth,
and expressions of appreciation. However, in stigma-laden
categories such as CPAP therapy, the expression of gratitude
may be constrained or strategically managed. While clearly
some consumers are expressing gratitude to Resmed, per -
haps many are more reluctant. Consumers may feel deep
appreciation for restored functioning while remaining hesi -
tant to publicly affiliate with the device. As design improve-
ments reduce stigma and enhance aesthetic and experiential
appeal, gratitude may become more openly expressed, shift-
ing the device from tolerated necessity to valued technol -
ogy. Such visible expressions of appreciation can reinforce
commitment by making the relational bond socially legiti -
mate rather than privately experienced. Over time, repeated
cycles of restoration and gratitude, particularly when stigma
is mitigated, may shift the consumer’s orientation from epi-
sodic relief to durable relational commitment, thereby con -
tributing to the consolidation of brand love.
The reciprocal circulation of gratitude warrants equal
attention. When consumer gratitude is expressed and
received internally, it may strengthen employee identifica -
tion and purpose enactment. Evidence from organizational
research suggests that contact with beneficiaries and expres-
sions of gratitude can significantly increase employee effort
and persistence. In a field experiment with university fun -
draisers, brief exposure to scholarship recipients who ben -
efited from their work substantially increased subsequent
performance, underscoring the motivational power of per -
ceived impact (Grant et al., 2007). Similarly, expressions of
gratitude have been shown to increase prosocial behavior in
workplace settings (Grant & Gino, 2010). Employees who
perceive that their work restores meaningful aspects of oth-
ers’ lives may therefore experience affirmation of purpose
and increased commitment to its enactment.
In this way, gratitude operates not only as a consumer-
level mechanism but as a relational bridge across stake -
holder groups. Consumer gratitude reinforces employee
pride and identification; employee commitment strengthens
consumer trust and perceived authenticity. Understanding
how gratitude operates both externally and internally may
clarify how purpose-driven relationships stabilize over time
and how emotional processes contribute to relational dura -
bility across the ecosystem.

Admiration
Admiration is typically elicited by witnessing exceptional
skill, achievement, or competence, and it motivates learn -
ing, aspiration, and emulation of excellence (Algoe &
Haidt, 2009; Keltner & Haidt, 2003). In purpose-driven set-
tings, admiration may be particularly relevant when organi-
zational competence is deployed in service of meaningful
human outcomes. Admiration differs from gratitude in that
it does not require the one feeling it to be a beneficiary, and
it differs from elevation in that the appraisal target is compe-
tence rather than moral virtue. Nevertheless, admiration can
contribute to a purpose system by strengthening perceived
credibility and capability, thereby supporting the appraisals
required for gratitude and elevation to stabilize rather than
collapse into skepticism.
In addition, admiration may contribute to identification
processes distinct from love. Whereas love often reflects
personal integration and relational depth, admiration can
operate at a distance, motivating aspiration and affiliation
even before direct experiential benefit occurs. For clinicians,
partners, or prospective consumers who are not yet benefi -
ciaries, admiration for technical competence or innovation
may precede gratitude. In restorative categories, this may
be especially important: admiration for clinical excellence
may reduce psychological resistance and legitimize adop -
tion, laying groundwork for later emotional consolidation.
Future research
A key question that emerges from our discussion is whether
admiration operates as an early-stage stabilizer in purpose
systems, particularly in domains where vulnerability height-
ens scrutiny of competence. Admiration may also serve as a
mechanism of expansion of our purpose system framework
to include peripheral stakeholders into the structure, such
as caregivers and family-members. Future research could
explore this and other aspects of adminiration.
Inspiration
Inspiration is characterized by transcendence, evocation, and
motivation (Thrash & Elliott, 2003, 2004). Although it is not
always classified as a discrete emotion, it is emotion-laden
and often co-occurs with, or follows from, elevation, admi-
ration, and gratitude. Being inspired by reflects appreciation
of the intrinsic value of an elicitor and entails transcendence
and evocation. The transcendence is experienced as coming
from beyond the self, in the sense that the person feeling
it attributes the uplift to the elicitor rather than to personal
agency. Being inspired to involves approach motivation and
a desire to transmit or actualize the qualities exemplified by
the elicitor in future action (Thrash & Elliott, 2004; Thrash
et al., 2010). This by-to linkage suggests that inspiration is
especially relevant to purpose because it translates present
appraisal into future enactment.
In purpose-driven brand systems, inspiration may be elic-
ited by purpose in the abstract, but it is likely to be strength-
ened by concrete acts, outcomes, and narratives that make
purpose tangible. WEM ( 2022) suggest that brand stories
may be an effective route through which purpose inspires
stakeholders (Bublitz et al., 2016). The Resmed interview
provides an internal analog: patient stories circulate within
the organization. Such narratives can function as elicitors
of being inspired by, because they foreground intrinsic
value and better possibilities. They can also produce being
inspired to, motivating employees to extend purpose into
daily practices, innovation, and customer orientation.
The interview also implies that consumer gratitude
is itself inspirational for employees. When beneficiaries
express appreciation, employees not only feel affirmed
but may experience renewed motivation to carry the val -
ued qualities forward. This dynamic aligns with evidence
that CSR perceptions can increase employee identification
and customer orientation (Korschun et al., 2014), and with
the view that purpose operates as shared beliefs about the
meaning of work that require internal clarity to translate into

performance (Gartenberg et al., 2019). Inspiration may help
explain how purpose beliefs become motivationally active,
while clarity may shape whether inspiration becomes sus -
tained enactment rather than episodic uplift.

Inspiration may extend present appraisal into future-oriented
enactment, translating purpose recognition into behavioral
engagement. Yet the conditions under which purposeful

Authentic Pride
Although WEM ( 2022) emphasize emotions that praise
excellence in others, the Resmed interview underscores a
complementary internal emotional process that is central to
purpose enactment: employee pride. Pride is a self-conscious
emotion that arises when individuals appraise themselves or
their in-group as responsible for valued outcomes (Tracy &
Robins, 2007). The literature distinguishes authentic pride
from hubristic pride. Authentic pride is grounded in effort,
persistence, and meaningful accomplishment, whereas
hubristic pride is grounded in attributions of superiority and
is associated with defensiveness and arrogance (Tracy &
Robins, 2007). In organizational contexts, authentic pride
should be particularly relevant to eudaimonia because it
reinforces identity coherence through valued contribution
and sustains engagement over time (Weidman et al., 2017).
In the Resmed interview, pride is explicitly linked to
impact. Employees feel proud when they see the impact
their technology has on people’s lives. The appraisal target
is not competitive success but contribution to human well-
being. This form of pride is therefore plausibly authentic,
grounded in effort and in perceived alignment with mean -
ingful, purposeful standards. Pride also interacts with grati-
tude and elevation. When employees receive gratitude from
beneficiaries, pride is reinforced. When they witness moral
integrity in company decision-making, elevation strength -
ens moral alignment with organizational values. Together,
these emotions may help sustain purpose as lived practice
within the organization rather than simply aspirational
storytelling.
CSR internalization research suggests additional path -
ways. Korschun et al. ( 2014) show that CSR perceptions
strengthen organizational identification and customer ori -
entation, leading to improved performance among frontline
employees. Authentic pride may function as an affective
mechanism that deepens identification and sustains enact -
ment. The durability of pride, however, should depend
on clarity regarding how daily work connects to purpose.
Gartenberg et al. (2019) show that purpose predicts perfor -
mance when paired with clarity, suggesting that employees
must be able to locate their contributions within a coherent
purpose system to sustain authentic pride rather than drift
toward frustration or cynicism.
WEM (2022) position other-praising and self-transcen -
dent emotions as mediating mechanisms through which
brand purpose contributes to eudaimonic well-being. The
Resmed interview has allowed for an examination of those
mechanisms in the concrete setting of a restorative health
brand. Rather than treating moral elevation, gratitude,
admiration, and inspiration as abstract emotional states, the
Resmed case shows how these emotions are elicited through
identity-relevant restoration, visible brand decision making,
and repeated enactment of purpose in product design and
organizational practice.
Within the WEM (2022) framework, gratitude functions
as a response to perceived benevolence and life improve -

action; admiration reinforces perceptions of competence;
and inspiration extends present appraisal into future-ori -

are not peripheral reactions. They structure how purpose
is interpreted and integrated into ongoing brand relation -
ships. Gratitude, in particular, links functional restoration
to relational commitment, reinforcing the interdependence
processes discussed in the preceding section. Elevation
and admiration reinforce alignment with the brand’s moral
and technical standards. Inspiration motivates continued
engagement. Together, these emotions support the consoli -
dation of brand love as a durable bond.
At the same time, the Resmed case suggests an exten -
sion to the WEM ( 2022) model. The original framework
emphasizes consumer emotional responses. The interview
highlights the role of authentic pride among employees who
experience purpose enactment as meaningful contribution.
Pride grounded in contribution rather than status strength -
ens employees’ identification with a brand’s purpose and
reinforces organizational coherence. Incorporating such
feelings of pride expands the emotional architecture of pur-
pose to include internal stakeholders and connects employee

Expanding the scope of brand purpose:
Employee eudaimonic well-being
The preceding analysis demonstrated how purpose activates
emotional pathways among consumers and, in the ResMed
case, among employees as well. Yet employees’ roles extend
beyond experiencing pride as one additional emotional
response. If purpose functions as organizational architecture
rather than external messaging, then employee flourishing is
not peripheral to the model. It is constitutive of how purpose
systems are enacted, stabilized, and sustained over time.
Purpose as internal coordination architecture
Part of the rationale for examining ResMed is that, even
without a formalized “purpose statement,” its mission and
vision clearly organize strategic behavior and resource allo-
cation. In the interview, purpose appears less as an external
promise and more as an internal standard. Managers and
employees invoke it to prioritize, make tradeoffs, and evalu-
ate success.
Pucknat is explicit that the mission is meant to “inspire
the people inside the company to rally them around a
shared purpose” and she describes how quickly it became
something employees could remember. This is not simply
culture or morale. It can function as a coordination mecha -
nism that directs behavior like innovation and resource allo-
cation, for example. A mission such as “life-changing… that
people love” operates as a constraint that steers R&D pri -
orities, partner selection, and definitions of what counts as
excellence. This is a stronger claim than saying employees
“like” the mission. Purpose becomes a decision screen that
filters innovation options and justifies investments that may
not optimize short-term financial metrics.
This architectural role aligns with organizational research
suggesting that shared purpose enhances coordination,
reduces ambiguity, and increases collective efficacy (Gar -
tenberg et al., 2019). When purpose is clear and central, it
becomes more likely to be invoked in consequential deci -
sions, increasing consistency across functions and time.
Employee eudaimonic well-being as outcome and
mechanism
Extending WEM (2022) beyond the consumer level requires
recognizing employees as stakeholders in purpose systems.
Research on meaningful work shows that employees expe -
rience greater well-being, motivation, and resilience when
they perceive their work as contributing to something larger
than themselves (Damon et al., 2003; Rosso et al., 2010).
Similarly, self-determination theory suggests that autonomy,
competence, and relatedness support eudaimonic function -
ing (Ryan & Deci, 2001). When employees see how their
work contributes to restoring patient dignity and relational
functioning, purpose may amplify how their work lives ful-
fill these psychological needs.
Purpose contributes to employee eudaimonic well-being
in at least three interrelated ways:
1. As an outcome:  Employees experience authentic pride
grounded in contribution rather than status. Authentic
pride, associated with effort and achievement rather than
superiority, has been linked to persistence and adaptive
functioning (Tracy & Robins, 2007; Weidman et al.,
2017).
2. As a mechanism:  Employees who internalize purpose
may enact decisions differently. Research on beneficiary
contact shows that employees who directly perceive the
impact of their work demonstrate greater persistence
and performance (Grant et al., 2007). Purpose-aligned
employees may therefore enhance consumer experiences
through higher-quality enactment.
3. As part of a reinforcing feedback system:  Employee
flourishing strengthens enactment quality, which
improves consumer outcomes, which in turn gener -
ates gratitude and reinforces employee pride. These

2024). Similarly, McKinsey ( 2023) reports that alignment
between individual and organizational purpose is associ -
ated with higher engagement, commitment, and retention.
Gallup’s, 2025 State of the Global Workplace report further
finds that employees who strongly agree that their organi -
zation’s mission or purpose makes them feel their job is
important are substantially more likely to be engaged and
to report thriving in their overall lives. These large-scale
findings suggest that perceived purpose alignment is associ-
ated with greater vitality, resilience, and sustained commit-
ment at work. In the Resmed case, where purpose operates
as a decision standard rather than simply a communication
device, such evidence strengthens the argument that inter -
nalized purpose may contribute to employee flourishing and
to the quality and consistency of purpose enactment.
Resmed’s example also raises important questions about
what makes purpose “usable” inside organizations. Clarity,
actionability, and role relevance determine whether purpose
becomes operational rather than symbolic. Organizational
infrastructures, such as rituals, recognition systems, feed -
back loops, governance processes, and design principles,
translate rallying into behavior. Emotionally powerful sto -
ries, such as bringing “the patient into the building,” can
inspire employees. Yet such narratives must be institutional-
ized through systems that connect daily tasks to downstream
impact. Otherwise, purpose remains episodic rather than
architectural. Research on meaningful work suggests that
structured reflection and role alignment enhance internal -
ization (Rosso et al., 2010). Thus, infrastructure, not inspi -
ration alone, determines whether employee eudaimonic
well-being becomes durable.
The interview highlights employee pride and received
gratitude, including stories of customers thanking or hug -
ging employees. These episodes illustrate that purpose
effects are not solely consumer-side phenomena. They
include employee eudaimonia and reinforcing feedback
loops. Measuring only consumer outcomes misses part
of the system. Measuring only in-the-moment emotional
responses misses the accumulation process that unfolds
through routines, identity integration, and relational repair.
Reciprocity loops connect employee flourishing and con -
sumer flourishing: when employees witness the down -
stream impact of their work, pride reinforces enactment;
when enactment improves, consumer outcomes strengthen,
generating further gratitude. Such dynamics suggest that
purpose systems should be analyzed as cross-level pro -
cesses rather than one-directional influence chains.

Including employee well-being suggests a number of future

Under what conditions does “purpose pressure” create
strain, burnout, or moral injury rather than flourishing?
How can employee eudaimonia be measured systemati -

Addressing these questions would extend purpose research

Structural conditions that stabilize purpose-
driven eudaimonic well-being
The prior sections drew on WEM’s (2022) account of other-
praising and self-transcendent emotions to explain how pur-
poseful brands can activate discrete emotional responses that
motivate relationship strengthening and self-transcendence
and extended the original model to examine employee well-
being in addition to consumers. However, these mediation
paths alone are not sufficient for durable eudaimonic well-
being. The mechanisms we have described are stabilized or
destabilized by structural conditions that shape attribution,
credibility, and the accumulation of commitment over time
(WEM., 2022). In this section, we revisit the moderators
emphasized by WEM ( 2022), and incorporate additional
structural conditions surfaced by the Resmed interview, par-
ticularly vulnerability intensity, stigma visibility, and cross-
stakeholder reciprocity loops.

condition for purpose-driven eudaimonic outcomes. Trust -
ing beliefs can be organized around ability, integrity, and
benevolence (Mayer et al., 1995). The Resmed interview
speaks directly to each dimension. Ability is grounded
through sustained R&D investment and continuous prod -
uct refinement. Pucknat framed the objective as develop -
ing life-changing health technologies that reliably improve
lived experience, noting that if Resmed seeks to help users

This ties technical competence to flourishing rather than to
performance alone, making ability-based trust integral to
eudaimonic impact.
Integrity is signaled in decision making. Pucknat
described a case in which leadership halted a clinical initia-
tive because “if it’s one person that could be endangered
because of our technology, it’s one person too many.” Such
acts can be consequential. Stakeholders are more likely
to interpret restoration and related prosocial outcomes as
grounded in principled commitment rather than oppor -
tunistic persuasion. In the interview, Pucknat talks about
Resmed’s values in the context of doing “the right thing.”
For example, Resmed focuses much of its communication
on health literacy and education to combat misinformation
prevalent in healthcare. Together, trusting beliefs support
the accumulation of gratitude, elevation, and commitment
by reducing uncertainty about how the organization will
continue to enact its purpose when tradeoffs arise.
Credibility complements trust by capturing whether
stakeholders perceive purpose claims as realistic, sup -
ported by evidence, and sustained over time. The interview
repeatedly acknowledges the difficulty of the ambition. This
enhances credibility by positioning love as a demanding
standard rather than a promotional claim. In a restorative
category where adoption is difficult and stigma is common,
credibility is not merely reputational. It shapes whether con-
sumers treat the brand’s purpose as a reliable basis for ongo-
ing reliance, whether they attribute improved functioning
to a durable system of competence and care, and whether
gratitude consolidates into commitment.
The sustainability of purpose storytelling is also a signal
of commitment. In purpose-driven systems, emotional com-
mitment depends on repetition and reliability. A large-scale
campaign that amplifies purpose, followed by a reduction
in narrative reinforcement due to budget contraction, may
recalibrate stakeholder inferences about long-term com -
mitment. When storytelling is episodic, stakeholders may
interpret purpose as campaign-based rather than architec -
tural, potentially weakening credibility even if operational
commitments remain intact. At the same time, if purpose is
deeply embedded in product design, safety decisions, and
employee enactment, emotional mechanisms may persist
independently of communication intensity. Resmed’s mar -
keting communications budget is limited, so the Valentine’s
Day loving couples campaign Pucknat discussed in the
interview was a short-term experiment, rather than a long-
term engagement. Nevertheless, it emphasized the love
component of their mission and demonstrated eudaimonic
benefits for customers. Furthermore, Resmed’s mission is
ostensibely deeply embedded in everything the company
does, reinforcing its mission commitment.
Future research
The interview suggests a number of potential future research
questions. Under what conditions do ability, integrity, and
benevolence interact to stabilize eudaimonic outcomes, and
do different configurations yield distinct emotional profiles
(Mayer et al., 1995; WEM., 2022)? How should purpose-
driven decision constraint be made visible and when does
it increase credibility more than symbolic communication
versus fail to do so? How does credibility evolve longitudi-
nally as purpose is enacted across product cycles, regulatory
environments, and competitive shifts, and how do disrup -
tions in credibility change the accumulation of gratitude,
attachment, and commitment? Does sustained narrative
reinforcement strengthen the accumulation of gratitude and
brand love beyond what operational experience alone can
achieve? Under what conditions does reduced communica -
tion erode authenticity inferences, and when does embodied
experience compensate for diminished storytelling? Norma-
tively, organizations should recognize that communication
investment can function as emotional infrastructure, rein -
forcing reciprocity loops and pride, but only when aligned
with credible enactment. Storytelling volume without struc-
tural alignment risks accelerating skepticism; structural
alignment without narrative reinforcement risks invisibility.
Brand authenticity: Value authenticity and brand
self-authenticity
WEM (2022) distinguish value authenticity from brand self-
authenticity as structural moderators of purpose impact.
Value authenticity concerns whether stakeholders infer that
actions are intrinsically motivated and morally grounded
rather than strategically instrumental. Brand self-authentic -
ity concerns whether purpose and behavior are consistent
with the brand’s enduring identity across time. Foundational
work conceptualizes authenticity as perceived alignment
between claims, identity, and enacted behavior (Beverland,
2005; Napoli et al., 2014).
The Resmed interview provides cues that support both
dimensions. Value authenticity is reinforced when purpose
functions as a decisional frame, as in safety decisions that
prioritize well-being over short-term gain. In other words,
the organization demonstrates that its purpose is not merely
a narrative overlay but an operating principle that shapes
tradeoffs. Brand self-authenticity is reinforced by continu -
ity in the way Resmed frames its purpose and by sustained
investment in design and R & D aimed at improving daily
lived experience. Pucknat’s description of love as “intuitive
and easy to use” positions love as an outcome of consis -
tent enactment rather than a campaign objective, which is
consequential because self-authenticity requires temporal

coherence. In a category defined by long-term adherence,
authenticity is tested not by a single interaction but by
repeated exposure to decisions, product experiences, and
service encounters.
The major rebrand that Resmed has recently launched
(Resmed, 2025) represents a critical moment for brand
self-authenticity. Because brand self-authenticity con -
cerns perceived continuity between enduring identity and
enacted behavior over time (Napoli et al., 2014; WEM
2022), a rebrand can either reinforce or destabilize authen -
ticity inferences. If stakeholders interpret the rebrand as a
superficial repositioning or abrupt identity shift, percep -
tions of self-authenticity may weaken, even when value
authenticity remains intact. Conversely, if the rebrand is
framed as clarifying and making visible an already endur -
ing commitment, it can strengthen perceptions of continuity
and deepen trust. In the Resmed case, a rebrand centered
on love and lived experience must therefore be evaluated
not only for aesthetic coherence but for identity articulation
and continuity. Furthermore, the way that the mission reso-
nates with employees, and the pride it evokes, reveals an
inherent brand self-authenticity. Research should examine
how stakeholders differentiate between clarifying rearticu -
lation and opportunistic repositioning, and how perceived
brand identity discontinuity moderates the accumulation of
gratitude and brand love. Normatively, organizations should
treat rebranding as an identity alignment exercise rather
than a symbolic refresh, ensuring that visual and narrative
shifts are tightly coupled to operational commitments that
make continuity observable.
Future research
Some future research questions informed by this living
example include: How do stakeholders distinguish value
authenticity from sophisticated persuasion when purpose
communication is highly polished (Napoli et al., 2014;
WEM., 2022)? Does brand self-authenticity moderate the
durability of gratitude and commitment under conditions
of rising dependence? How do authenticity inferences dif -
fer across stakeholder groups, including patients, caregiv -
ers, providers, and employees, and how do those differences
shape the visibility and social legitimacy of emotional
responses to purpose?
Brand purpose proximity
WEM (2022) note that purpose effects should be stronger
when there is tight alignment between what the brand does
and what its purpose claims. The Resmed case suggests the
need to distinguish two forms of proximity embedded in
the articulated purpose. Resmed’s stated purpose includes
creating technologies that people love, while the eudaimonic
alignment emphasized in the interview is enabling people to
be their best selves through restored sleep and functioning.
Proximity between product performance and flourishing
is high in the Resmed context. Improved sleep can directly
support relational presence, occupational competence, and
daily self-regulation. This tight coupling reduces ambiguity
about whether purpose is being delivered in lived experi -
ence, and it clarifies the appraisal basis for other-praising
emotions. When restored functioning is experienced as
identity-relevant rather than merely convenient, the benefits
are more likely to be encoded as meaningful and to contrib-
ute to eudaimonic outcomes.
By contrast, proximity between a medical device and
love is developmental rather than immediate. Love is not an
inherent attribute of CPAP therapy, especially given initial
stigma and resistance. The interview describes investments
intended to increase the felt proximity to love over time,
including design work aimed at reducing stigma and creat -
ing more compelling devices. This design ambition can be
interpreted as an effort to bridge a gap between functional
indispensability and experiential desirability. In this fram -
ing, proximity to flourishing is foundational, while prox -
imity to love is cultivated through experiential design and
identity-safe usage contexts.
Future research
Several potential research questions emerge from our dis -
cussion of brand-purpose proximity: How do the two forms
of proximity, flourishing proximity and love proximity,
differentially predict gratitude, admiration, and long-term
commitment? Under what conditions can high flourishing
proximity compensate for low love proximity, producing
adherence without affection, and when does cultivated love
proximity add incremental explanatory power for the devel-
opment and maintenance of brand love?

Bettman, 2005).
The Resmed context suggests that congruence may oper-
ate through values tied to caregiving, responsibility, and
the pursuit of a functional, relational self. When consumers
interpret restored sleep as enabling valued roles, the purpose
feels identity-relevant rather than merely beneficial. The

Vulnerability intensity and stigma visibility as
amplifiers
The Resmed interview surfaces a pair of structural con -
ditions that are not explicitly developed as moderators in
WEM, but that appear central in restorative health contexts:
vulnerability and stigma. Attachment and close-relationship
research suggest that vulnerability can amplify the salience
and consolidating force of relational bonds, particularly
when a partner provides safe haven and supports thriving
(Feeney & Collins, 2015). In sleep apnea, diminished func-
tioning can undermine valued roles and relational stability,
making restoration existentially meaningful. This vulner -
ability may amplify the emotional meaning of restored
functioning and strengthen the likelihood that gratitude and
commitment will accumulate over time.
At the same time, stigma shapes whether vulnerability is
socially visible. Stigma research emphasizes that individu -
als manage socially devalued identities through conceal -
ment and impression management (Crocker et al., 1998;
Goffman, 1963). CPAP therapy can be stigmatized because
the device can be visible and associated with illness. This
implies that vulnerability may intensify private gratitude
while stigma visibility may constrain public expression of
that gratitude. In practical terms, consumers can feel deeply
grateful for restored functioning while avoiding public iden-
tification with the technology that enabled it. This constraint
matters because the visibility of emotion can itself become
a consolidating mechanism. Public expression of gratitude
can strengthen commitment by making the relational bond
visible, socially validated, and reinforced through support -
ive responses.
The interview’s emphasis on design innovation and
reducing stigma can be interpreted as a structural interven -
tion that increases emotional visibility. As devices become
more acceptable and perhaps less embarrassing, gratitude
might be expressed more openly, which may strengthen
commitment through social validation and reinforcement.
In this sense, vulnerability and stigma may operate as ampli-
fiers of the outlined mediational pathways. Vulnerability
may increase the stakes and meaning of restoration, while
stigma may influence whether that meaning can be socially
affirmed. Product design may become a lever through which
the organization can reduce the suppressive effects of stigma
and allow vulnerability-driven gratitude to consolidate into
durable attachment and love.
Future research
Several potential research questions emerge around these
two amplifiers: How do vulnerability and stigma, separately
and jointly, shape the transition from resistance to reliance
to appreciation in restorative integration? Under what con -
ditions does stigma suppress public expressions of gratitude
and identification even when private gratitude is strong? Do
design interventions that reduce stigma increase the visibil-
ity of gratitude and thereby accelerate the consolidation of
brand love and adherence? How do these dynamics vary
across social contexts, such as intimate relationships, work-
place settings, and clinical encounters where devices may be
visible to others? When design change is not possible, could
attempts to change the public narrative to reduce stigma (for
example, through corpporate communications, public rela -
tions or lobbying) be an effective method to enhance con -
sumer well-being?
Reciprocity loops and cross-stakeholder emotional
reinforcement
The Resmed interview highlights a dynamic that extends
beyond static moderation: reciprocity loops link consumer
experiences to employee meaning making. Purpose sys -
tems may become more durable when emotions circulate
between stakeholders. Consumers experience restoration
and gratitude when experiencing the impact of Resmed’s
technology, and when gratitude is expressed through testi -
monials, feedback, or visible adherence, employees become
recipients of that gratitude.
Work on beneficiary contact suggests that seeing the
impact of one’s work on beneficiaries can increase per -
sistence and performance, and gratitude expressions can
strengthen prosocial motivation (Grant & Gino, 2010; Grant
et al., 2007). Within a purpose system, these dynamics
imply feedback effects. Consumer gratitude can strengthen

Purpose internalization, employee identification,
and customer orientation
WEM ( 2022) emphasize that purpose effects depend on
organizational enactment. The Resmed interview under -
scores that enactment is carried by employees whose identi-
fication with purpose shapes how consistently it is delivered.
Research in marketing indicates that CSR can strengthen
employee job performance through customer orientation
and identification processes (Korschun et al., 2014).
Pucknat’s discussion of pride and downstream impact
suggests that employees interpret their work through a pur -
pose lens when they can connect tasks to human outcomes.
This provides a pathway through which purpose-driven
eudaimonic well-being can operate internally. Employees
may experience meaning and authentic pride when they
view their work as contributing to others’ flourishing. The
same internalization likely shapes how employees commu -
nicate with patients and providers, influencing perceived
benevolence and integrity externally. Importantly, this inter-
nal path aligns with the expanded account of reciprocity
loops, because employee pride is not merely an internal out-
come. It is also a driver of enactment quality, and therefore
a stabilizer of consumer experience.

Purpose clarity and interpretive consistency
Clarity makes purpose actionable. Empirical work on cor -
porate purpose suggests that purpose is most effective when
paired with clarity regarding direction and expectations
(Gartenberg et al., 2019). In the Resmed interview, love
is framed as a standard that guides design, R & D, safety
decisions, and messaging, offering a unifying interpretive
frame and specifying how purpose should shape decisions
and actions.
This emphasis resonates with WEM’s ( 2022) discus -
sion of human purpose as characterized by scope, strength,
and awareness. Drawing on the broader human purpose lit -
erature (Damon et al., 2003; McKnight & Kashdan, 2009;
Ryan & Deci, 2001; Ryff, 1989), WEM ( 2022) note that
purpose exerts organizing power when it spans meaningful
domains (scope), is likely to be invoked in relevant domains
(strength), and can be consciously recognized and under -
stood (awareness). While articulated at the individual level,
these dimensions translate to brand purpose. For a firm,
scope might concern the range of decisions and functions
that purpose is expected to guide. For Resmed, that scope
is intended to be quite wide. Strength reflects the likelihood
that purpose consistently shapes tradeoffs in R&D, safety,
design, communication, etc., versus more one-off instantia-
tions. Awareness within an organization might refer to how
many internal and external stakeholders understand the pur-
pose and recognize its impact. Can people who work out -
side marketing for example, articulate Resmed’s purpose?
If love truly functions as a standard, it should be predict -
ably invoked in design deliberations, safety decisions, and
innovation prioritization, for example. If, instead, love is
mentioned selectively or primarily in marketing contexts, its
strength is weaker, even if awareness is high. A purpose may
be broadly scoped, centrally valued, and widely recognized,
but if stakeholders lack a shared understanding of how it
should guide specific decisions and tradeoffs, its strategic
coordinating power remains limited.
When stakeholders can anticipate when and how purpose
will shape tradeoffs, they are more likely to interpret out -
comes as consistent with identity-defining commitments.
Clarity also supports internal alignment by reducing local
reinterpretation that could erode authenticity. In a purpose
system, clarity is therefore a stabilizer of both external trust

and internal enactment. Moreover, clarity can facilitate the
conversion of emotional experiences into durable commit -

excellence.

How does purpose clarity interact with integrity-based trust

Configurational stabilization and a research agenda
beyond WEM (2022)
Taken together, the Resmed interview suggests that the
structural conditions in WEM (2022) operate configuration-
ally. Trusting beliefs, authenticity, credibility, proximity,
value congruence, internalization, and clarity interact with
additional conditions surfaced here, vulnerability intensity,
stigma visibility, and reciprocity loops. These conditions do
not independently generate eudaimonic well-being. They
jointly influence the pathways through which restoration
becomes emotionally meaningful, socially expressible, and
durable over time. In restorative healthcare contexts, vulner-
ability can amplify gratitude and commitment, while stigma
can suppress emotional visibility unless design and orga -
nizational structures counteract it. Reciprocity loops can
then reinforce enactment by making consumer impact vis -
ible internally, strengthening employee pride and purpose
internalization. This configuration clarifies how purpose can
generate eudaimonic well-being not only by activating emo-
tions but also by stabilizing the interpretive and organiza -
tional conditions that allow emotions and commitments to
accumulate rather than dissipate.
Future research
Future research questions should examine how configura -
tions of structural conditions jointly predict durable eudai -
monic outcomes across consumers and employees. For
example, high vulnerability may increase the importance of
integrity and clarity, while high stigma may heighten the
role of proximity to love and design-based dignity cues.
Similarly, models of brand purpose may benefit from incor-
porating dynamic feedback processes, such as reciprocity
loops, rather than treating moderators as static boundary
conditions.
Measuring purpose-driven eudaimonic well-
being
The Resmed interview highlights both the promise and the
complexity of measuring brand purpose and its implica -
tions for consumer and employee flourishing. Although the
interview revealed many potential indicators of well-being
and purpose enactment, measuring eudaimonic well-being
in the field remains challenging for practitioners. When we
asked Pucknat how Resmed measures the outcomes of its
mission and vision, she discussed both favorable marketer
outcomes and consumer eudaimonic well-being. Metrics
of favorable marketer outcomes include attribution mea -
surement on customer surveys as well as measuring how
many people Resmed converts onto therapy as a result of
demand generation. Pucknat also mentioned metrics such
as usability, ease of use, comfort, and fit. These measure
product satisfaction and potentially predict brand loyalty,
but they do not measure brand love, which is considered
to be a deep emotional attachment to a brand. In healthcare
contexts, consumers may remain loyal because alterna -
tives are limited or because adherence is medically neces -
sary, not because love or gratitude have consolidated into
durable bonds. Pucknat admits that Resmed “measures love
by the willingness of users to recommend us.” This metric
may both underestimate brand love for a stigmatized prod -
uct (e.g., someone may truly love Resmed, but hesitate to
recommend CPAP equipment to others) and overestimate
brand love in general (e.g., not everyone who recommends
a product feels a deep emotional attachment to the brand). In
WEM (2022), the ultimate outcomes are eudaimonic (mean-
ing, self-acceptance/authenticity, flourishing via relation -
ships, etc.), not just persuasion outcomes. But firms often
fall back on metrics like satisfaction, Net Promoter Score
(NPS), or adherence (in health contexts).
The brand love literature provides instruments, but they
may be unknown among practitioners. Validated scales
measure self-brand integration, anticipated separation dis -
tress, passion-driven behaviors, and long-term commitment
(Bagozzi et al., 2017; Batra et al., 2012). Emotional attach-
ment measures assess affection, connection and prominence
in daily routines (Park et al., 2010; Thompson et al., 2005).
In health care contexts, interdependence measures such as
investment size, perceived alternatives, and commitment
level (Rusbult et al., 1998) may be diagnostic of adher -
ence and durability. However, relational measures alone
cannot capture whether purpose is generating eudaimonic
flourishing.

Discrete emotional mediators such as gratitude, eleva -
tion, admiration, inspiration, and pride can be measured
using established scales. Yet in stigmatized contexts, mea -
surement might also need to account for emotional vis -
ibility. And even though Resmed seems to generate at least
some public expressions, consumers may privately expe -
rience gratitude while avoiding its public expression. As
Ferraro et al. ( 2011) suggest in their discussion of pos -
session loss and identity domains, visibility and symbolic
meaning can shape how attachments are expressed and
interpreted.
Resmed’s reliance on tools like NPS to measure the suc-
cess of their purpose statement does not capture the richer
potential of their purpose approach. We recommend that
Resmed incorporate some additional measurement scales
among consumers, such as scales measuring brand love
(Bagozzi, et al., 2017), self-brand connections (Escalas,
2004), or possibly a scale designed specifically for measur-
ing eudaimonic well-being (e.g., Scaes of Psychological
Well-Being; Ryff, 1989). Items such as “This brand helps
me be the person I want to be,” “I feel grateful for what this
brand has done for me/my life,” and “This brand is part of
my daily routine” may capture dimensions not fully assessed
in existing scales. Resmed is already conducting consumer
surveys. These scales could easily be incorporated into their
marketing research.
The investment model perspective (Rusbult, et al., 1998)
also clarifies the limitations of relying solely on measures
like NPS, which does not capture perceived alternatives,
investment accumulation, or commitment level. Especially
in restorative contexts, measuring these structural anteced -
ents may provide earlier and more diagnostic indicators of
whether brand love is being developed or sustained. Assess-
ing routine integration, perceived irreplaceability, accumu -
lated investments of many different kinds, commitment to
continued use, and gratitude may reveal whether the rela -
tionship is becoming interdependent rather than merely sat-
isfactory. These measures may capture the extent to which
the brand is embedded in users’ daily lives, whether they
anticipate loss if the relationship were disrupted. Investment-
based indicators may detect relational connection before it is
visible in overt advocacy or recommendation behavior.
It is important to note that Resmed’s sleep apnea device
category might make some measurement difficult. The cate-
gory carries a certain degree of stigma, which contributes to
the initial hatred response from consumers. In the interview,
Pucknat explicitly notes that consumers may initially react
negatively (“when people see it they hate it.”). However,
even a small movement away from hatred and disgust may
have a significant impact on Resmed’s sales and equally
importantly, on consumer eudaimonic well-being.
Internally, we believe measurement should extend
beyond anecdotal narratives, though it’s clear the anec -
dotes are internally meaningful. The interview highlights
employee pride and received gratitude, including custom -
ers thanking or hugging employees. These stories illustrate
that purpose effects are not solely consumer phenomena
but rather include employee eudaimonia and feedback
loops. Measuring only consumer outcomes would miss
part of the broader architecture. Employee eudaimonic
well-being may be assessed using measures of meaning -
ful work (Rosso et al., 2010), autonomy and competence
(Ryan & Deci, 2001) and authentic pride (Tracy & Robins,
2007; Weidman et al., 2017).
Conclusion
The Resmed case demonstrates that brand purpose can
operate as organizational architecture rather than merely
symbolic positioning. Building on WEM (2022), this article
has extended the original framework in several ways. First,
we examined brand love as a relational construct that may
drive purpose-driven identity integration, emotional activa-
tion, and commitment in consumers. Second, we expanded
the scope of eudaimonic impact to include employees as
stakeholders whose pride and internalization reinforce pur -
pose enactment. Third, we identified contextual amplifiers
not fully theorized in WEM ( 2022), including vulnerabil -
ity intensity, stigma visibility, and reciprocity loops which
are in evidence here. These contributions also suggest that
assessing purpose-driven flourishing requires measurement
that includes relational, emotional, employee, and struc -
tural considerations rather than solely relying on traditional
brand or market metrics.
Together, these extensions suggest that purpose-driven
eudaimonic well-being is not generated by isolated mecha -
nisms but by systems of coordinated activation and stabi -
lization,. Emotional responses such as gratitude, elevation,
and pride may energize purpose. Structural conditions such
as trust, authenticity, clarity, and proximity can stabilize or
prevent those responses. In restorative healthcare contexts,
vulnerability and stigma intensify both risk and potential
impact, making clarity and integrity particularly consequen-
tial. Figure 2 illustrates a cross-stakeholder purpose system,
with stakeholders in the circles, their responses in upright
type, directional effects shown as arrows, and key interac -
tive influences in italics.

multi-level system linking consumer, employee, and clini -
cal stakeholder flourishing within organizational coordina -
tion systems. Practically, it suggests that purpose seeking
