Abstract

User-generated

content

(UGC)

has

restructured

how

destination

brands

are

formed,

but

the

tourism-marketing

literature

has

largely

theorised

this

shift

in

the

context

of

mainstream,

high-volume

destinations,

leaving

the

branding

dynamics

of

responsible

tourism

(RT)

destinations

under-conceptualised.

This

paper

addresses

that

omission

by

developing

an

integrative

conceptual

framework

explaining

how

UGC

contributes

to

destination

branding

in

Kerala's

RT

destinations.

Rather

than

treating

relevant

theories

in

isolation,

the

framework

fuses

Source

Credibility

Theory,

electronic

word-of-mouth

(eWOM)

theory,

destination

branding

theory,

and

brand

co-creation

theory

into

a

single

explanatory

chain.

It

positions

UGC

credibility

and

UGC

authenticity

as

the

qualities

that

make

peer-generated

eWOM

persuasive;

it

treats

responsible

tourism

attributes

—

community

participation,

cultural

authenticity,

environmental

sustainability,

local

products,

and

ethical

tourism

practices

—

as

the

contextual

brand

meanings

that

credible,

authentic

content

renders

believable

in

a

domain

otherwise

vulnerable

to

greenwashing

scepticism;

and

it

models

destination

brand

image

as

the

mediating

asset

that

converts

co-created

content

into

travel

intention.

Six

propositions

specify

the

relationships

among

these

constructs

and

are

justified

through

the

integrated

theoretical

logic.

The

paper

contributes

a

theory-grounded

account

of

how

tourist-authored

content

co-creates

a

responsible

destination

brand,

extends

credibility

and

co-creation

reasoning

into

the

sustainability

domain,

and

offers

destination

management

organisations

guidance

on

curating

authentic

advocacy

rather

than

manufacturing

it.

INTRODUCTION

The

authority

to

define

a

destination

brand

has

migrated

from

destination

management

organisations

(DMOs)

to

travellers.

Where

brand

meaning

was

once

broadcast

through

curated

campaigns,

it

is

now

assembled

from

the

photographs,

reviews,

and

narratives

that

visitors

publish

across

review

platforms

and

social

media.

Lund,

Cohen,

and

Scarles

(2018)

capture

the

consequence

bluntly:

destination

brands

are

increasingly

the

product

of

people's

shared

stories

rather

than

of

marketing

strategy.

For

most

destinations

this

is

a

communication

challenge;

for

responsible

tourism

(RT)

destinations

it

is

an

existential

one,

because

the

very

qualities

such

destinations

trade

on

—

fair

community

benefit,

cultural

integrity,

ecological

care

—

are

credence

attributes

that

a

prospective

visitor

cannot

verify

before

arrival

and

often

cannot

fully

verify

even

after.

Kerala

is

an

instructive

setting

for

this

problem.

The

state

adopted

responsible

tourism

as

its

principal

tourism-development

strategy

and

piloted

it

at

four

destinations

—

Kumarakom,

Kovalam,

Thekkady,

and

Wayanad

—

with

Kumarakom

subsequently

recognised

nationally

as

a

model

initiative

built

on

local

sourcing,

community

enterprise,

and

participatory

governance

(Chettiparamb

&

Kokkranikkal,

2012).

Kerala's

long-running

“God's

Own

Country”

positioning

has

given

the

state

unusual

brand

equity,

yet

the

responsible-tourism

layer

of

that

brand

depends

on

claims

about

how

tourism

is

conducted

rather

than

merely

on

what

is

seen.

When

such

claims

are

communicated

by

the

destination

itself,

they

invite

the

scepticism

that

now

attaches

to

sustainability

marketing

generally.

When

they

are

communicated,

credibly

and

authentically,

by

fellow

travellers,

they

acquire

an

evidential

quality

that

official

messaging

cannot

manufacture.

This

asymmetry

motivates

the

study's

central

question:

how

does

user-generated

content

contribute

to

destination

branding

in

Kerala's

responsible

tourism

destinations?

Answering

it

requires

more

than

importing

existing

UGC–destination-image

models.

Those

models

were

developed

to

explain

attraction

and

revisit

behaviour

at

conventional

destinations

and

rarely

engage

the

specific

communicative

burden

of

responsibility

claims.

This

paper

argues

that

a

satisfactory

account

must

integrate

four

theoretical

traditions

that

are

usually

invoked

separately:

Source

Credibility

Theory,

which

explains

why

peer

sources

persuade;

eWOM

theory,

which

explains

how

their

messages

circulate

and

are

adopted;

destination

branding

theory,

which

specifies

the

brand

asset

being

built

and

its

behavioural

payoff;

and

brand

co-creation

theory,

which

reframes

the

tourist

from

message

recipient

to

brand

producer.

The

contribution

is

not

a

survey

of

these

traditions

but

their

synthesis

into

one

framework

in

which

UGC

credibility

and

authenticity

feed

perceptions

of

responsible

tourism

attributes,

those

perceptions

shape

destination

brand

image,

and

brand

image

drives

travel

intention.

The

remainder

of

the

paper

reviews

the

relevant

literature

critically,

articulates

the

integrated

theoretical

foundation,

identifies

the

specific

gap

the

framework

fills,

presents

the

framework

and

a

detailed

account

of

each

relationship,

advances

six

propositions,

and

closes

with

theoretical

contributions,

practical

implications,

and

directions

for

empirical

work.

2.

LITERATURE

REVIEW

2.1

User-generated

content

and

eWOM

in

tourism

User-generated

content

—

reviews,

ratings,

images,

and

travel

narratives

produced

by

consumers

rather

than

firms

—

has

become

a

primary

input

to

pre-trip

decision-making,

functioning

as

a

contemporary

form

of

electronic

word-of-mouth

(Litvin,

Goldsmith,

&

Pan,

2008).

Its

influence

rests

on

a

perceived

independence

from

commercial

interest:

because

peer

authors

are

assumed

to

lack

a

selling

motive,

their

testimony

is

treated

as

more

diagnostic

than

sponsor-controlled

communication

(Filieri

&

McLeay,

2014).

Yet

the

literature

has

grown

increasingly

cautious

about

treating

all

UGC

as

uniformly

persuasive.

The

proliferation

of

fake

and

incentivised

reviews

has

made

credibility

a

contested

and

consequential

judgement

rather

than

a

default

assumption

(Filieri,

2016),

and

empirical

work

shows

that

only

content

perceived

as

trustworthy

is

adopted,

while

suspect

content

is

discounted

(Filieri,

Alguezaui,

&

McLeay,

2015).

Two

evaluative

qualities

recur

as

the

pivotal

antecedents

of

UGC

influence.

The

first

is

credibility

—

the

perceived

believability

of

the

content

and

its

source,

typically

decomposed

into

expertise

and

trustworthiness.

The

second

is

authenticity

—

the

sense

that

the

content

reflects

genuine,

first-hand,

unmanufactured

experience.

Kim

and

Kim

(2020)

demonstrate

that

the

perceived

authenticity

of

online

reviews

drives

trust

both

in

the

platform

and

in

the

destination,

positioning

authenticity

as

an

antecedent

of,

rather

than

a

synonym

for,

credibility.

These

constructs

are

related

but

distinct:

a

review

may

be

authentic

(evidently

sincere)

yet

judged

low

in

credibility

(the

author

lacks

relevant

expertise),

or

credible

in

form

yet

suspected

of

being

staged.

The

present

framework

therefore

treats

UGC

credibility

and

UGC

authenticity

as

separate

independent

constructs

rather

than

collapsing

them.

2.2

UGC,

destination

image,

and

travel

intention

A

substantial

stream

links

UGC

to

destination

image

and

downstream

behaviour.

The

consistent

finding

is

that

UGC

shapes

the

cognitive

and

affective

components

of

destination

image,

which

in

turn

influence

behavioural

intentions

(Filieri

&

McLeay,

2014).

Afshardoost

and

Eshaghi's

(2020)

meta-analysis

confirms

destination

image

as

a

robust

and

sizeable

predictor

of

behavioural

intentions

across

studies,

establishing

image

as

the

central

mediating

asset

between

information

exposure

and

travel

decisions.

Country-

and

destination-image

research

reaches

the

same

conclusion

for

cross-border

travel

intention

(Chaulagain,

Wiitala,

&

Fu,

2019),

and

eWOM

specifically

has

been

shown

to

build

destination

trust

that

translates

into

intention

to

travel

(Abubakar

&

Ilkan,

2016).

What

this

stream

has

largely

not

examined

is

which

brand

meanings

UGC

transmits.

It

typically

models

image

at

a

generic

level

—

favourable

versus

unfavourable,

cognitive

versus

affective

—

without

asking

whether

UGC

is

equally

capable

of

communicating

experiential

and

ethical

brand

content

such

as

sustainability

or

community

benefit.

This

is

a

meaningful

silence,

because

responsibility

attributes

are

precisely

the

meanings

most

difficult

to

convey

through

official

channels

and

most

dependent

on

peer

verification.

2.3

Destination

branding

and

brand

co-creation

Destination

branding

theory

holds

that

a

destination

brand

is

a

bundle

of

associations

and

perceived

value

that

differentiates

a

place

and

shapes

expectations

(Cai,

2002;

Blain,

Levy,

&

Ritchie,

2005),

with

destination

image

operating

as

the

perceptual

core

of

brand

equity

(Qu,

Kim,

&

Im,

2011).

Classical

accounts

of

image

formation

emphasised

the

interplay

of

organic

(uncontrolled),

induced

(marketer-controlled),

and

autonomous

information

sources

(Gartner,

1994;

Baloglu

&

McCleary,

1999).

UGC

disrupts

this

taxonomy:

it

is

neither

the

DMO's

induced

messaging

nor

a

wholly

independent

news

source,

but

a

hybrid

that

is

simultaneously

organic

in

origin

and

brand-constituting

in

effect.

Brand

co-creation

theory

provides

the

vocabulary

for

that

hybridity.

Building

on

the

shift

from

firm-centred

value

creation

to

interactive,

experience-based

value

(Prahalad

&

Ramaswamy,

2004)

and

the

service-dominant

logic

in

which

value

is

co-created

with,

not

delivered

to,

the

customer

(Vargo

&

Lusch,

2004),

tourism

scholars

have

shown

that

value

and

brand

meaning

are

co-produced

on

destination

online

platforms

(Zhang,

Gordon,

Buhalis,

&

Ding,

2018).

Lund,

Scarles,

and

Cohen

(2020)

extend

this

by

demonstrating

that

co-creation

is

double-edged:

the

same

storytelling

mechanisms

that

build

brand

value

2.4

Responsible

tourism

and

its

communicative

problem

Responsible

tourism

is

best

understood

not

as

a

fixed

product

category

but

as

a

discourse

and

practice

oriented

to

minimising

negative

impacts

and

maximising

local

benefit,

sitting

close

to

but

distinguishable

from

sustainable

tourism

(Goodwin

&

Francis,

2003;

Mihalič,

2016).

Its

operative

attributes

are

experiential

and

relational:

community

participation

and

empowerment

(Scheyvens,

1999),

cultural

authenticity,

environmental

stewardship,

the

sourcing

and

consumption

of

local

products,

and

ethical

conduct

toward

hosts.

Kerala's

model

gives

these

attributes

concrete

form

—

local

vegetable

supply

chains,

community

enterprise

groups,

and

participatory

local

governance

were

built

into

the

Kumarakom

initiative

(Chettiparamb

&

Kokkranikkal,

2012).

The

communicative

problem

is

that

each

of

these

is

a

credence

attribute.

A

visitor

cannot

inspect

“fair

community

benefit”

the

way

they

inspect

a

beach.

Authenticity

theory

clarifies

why

this

matters

and

why

it

is

contested:

authenticity

in

tourism

is

not

a

simple

property

of

objects

but

is

variously

object-related,

constructed,

and

existential

(MacCannell,

1973;

Wang,

1999),

and

consumers'

judgements

of

it

are

actively

negotiated

rather

than

passively

received

(Kolar

&

Žabkar,

2010).

Local

food,

for

instance,

functions

as

a

carrier

of

destination

authenticity

precisely

because

it

is

read

as

a

genuine

expression

of

place

(Sims,

2009).

When

responsibility

attributes

are

asserted

by

the

marketer,

audiences

may

treat

them

as

constructed

for

consumption

—

the

essence

of

greenwashing

suspicion.

When

they

are

evidenced

in

credible,

authentic

peer

content,

they

can

be

read

as

object-related

or

existential

authenticity

that

the

audience

discovers

rather

than

is

sold.

This

is

the

hinge

on

which

the

proposed

framework

turns.

3.

THEORETICAL

FOUNDATIONS:

AN

INTEGRATED

LOGIC

The

framework's

premise

is

that

no

single

theory

explains

the

UGC–RT-branding

relationship,

but

that

four

theories,

read

together,

form

a

continuous

explanatory

logic.

They

are

integrated

here

rather

than

reviewed

in

parallel.

●

Source

Credibility

Theory

originates

in

persuasion

research

showing

that

message

acceptance

depends

on

the

perceived

expertise

and

trustworthiness

of

the

source

(Hovland

&

Weiss,

1951),

later

operationalised

as

a

multidimensional

credibility

construct

(Ohanian,

1990).

In

the

UGC

setting

the

“source”

is

a

fellow

traveller,

and

credibility

is

inferred

from

cues

such

as

demonstrated

experience,

detail,

and

the

absence

of

commercial

motive

(Filieri,

2016).

Source

Credibility

Theory

thus

supplies

the

micro-mechanism

—

it

explains

why

some

peer

content

persuades

and

other

content

does

not.

But

it

stops

at

persuasion;

it

does

not

explain

circulation

or

brand

accumulation.

●

eWOM

theory

supplies

the

channel

and

process.

It

models

how

consumer-authored

evaluations

are

produced,

motivated,

and

diffused

across

platforms,

and

how

they

are

adopted

or

rejected

by

readers

(Hennig-Thurau,

Gwinner,

Walsh,

&

Gremler,

2004;

Cheung

&

Thadani,

2012).

Crucially,

eWOM

theory

positions

credibility

and

authenticity

not

as

ends

but

as

the

properties

that

determine

whether

a

message

is

adopted

and

passed

on

(Filieri

et

al.,

2015;

Kim

&

Kim,

2020).

Source

Credibility

Theory

and

eWOM

theory

therefore

interlock:

credibility

is

the

quality,

eWOM

is

the

system

through

which

that

quality

produces

influence

at

scale.

●

Destination

branding

theory

supplies

the

outcome

architecture.

It

identifies

the

asset

that

adopted

eWOM

builds

—

a

structured

set

of

brand

associations

whose

perceptual

core

is

destination

image
