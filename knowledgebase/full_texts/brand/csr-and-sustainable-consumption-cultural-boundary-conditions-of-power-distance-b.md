Abstract
Corporate social responsibility (CSR) is widely recognised as a key sustainability governance mechanism
shaping ethical consumption behavior, however empirical evidence on its effects on repurchase intention
remains inconsistent, making it diﬃcult for ﬁrms and policymakers to anticipate whether CSR signals
reliably translate into responsible consumer behaviour across culturally diverse markets. This
inconsistency suggests a speciﬁcation issue in prior research, where power distance belief (PDB) is
typically modelled as a uniform moderator rather than a pathway-speciﬁc factor. To address this issue,
this study develops a dual-pathway model in which CSR inﬂuences ethical repurchase behavior both
directly and indirectly through perceived brand equity, and proposes that PDB differentially moderates
these mechanisms, exerting inﬂuence on the direct CSR–repurchase pathway but not on the indirect
pathway mediated by brand equity. Using survey data from 630 Chinese consumers in the ethical food
sector, a market central to the country's broader transition toward sustainable food systems, the
empirical results support the proposed model: CSR signiﬁcantly enhances repurchase intention through
both mechanisms, PDB strengthens the direct effect, and the brand equity-mediated effect remains
stable across cultural value conditions, indicating that cultural values operate as pathway-speciﬁc rather
than global boundary conditions. This study contributes to the sustainability and CSR literature by
reframing CSR as a sustainability governance signal and demonstrating heterogeneous cultural effects
across behavioral pathways, with implications for differentiated CSR communication and sustainable
supply chain governance in high-power-distance, emerging-market contexts.
1. Introduction
Corporate social responsibility (CSR) functions as a key sustainability governance signal through which
ﬁrms seek to inﬂuence consumer participation in the transition toward sustainable consumption.
However, whether such CSR signals consistently translate into sustained consumer responses remains
empirically uncertain, particularly across culturally diverse markets [1, 2]. Although survey evidence
suggests that consumers express strong willingness to support corporate sustainability initiatives [3, 4],
behavioural evidence is mixed: stated preferences for CSR do not always translate into actual
repurchase behaviour, and prior studies report both strong and negligible CSR effects on consumer
outcomes [5, 4]. This inconsistency limits the ability of ﬁrms and policymakers to anticipate when CSR
governance signals will translate into stable sustainable consumption responses.
Prior research has identiﬁed power distance belief (PDB) as an important cultural value that shapes
consumer responses to CSR-related signals by inﬂuencing perceptions of hierarchy, legitimacy, and
status [6, 7, 8]. In parallel, CSR studies consistently show that brand equity serves as a key mechanism
linking CSR activities to consumer behaviour across contexts [1, 9, 10]. However, existing research
typically assumes that cultural values such as PDB exert uniform moderating effects across all CSR-
related pathways. As a result, it remains unclear whether cultural moderation applies equally to the direct
behavioural response to CSR and the indirect pathway operating through brand equity. This limitation
may partly explain the inconsistent ﬁndings reported in the literature.
Page 2/24

To address this gap, we develop a dual-pathway model of CSR-driven sustainable consumption that
distinguishes between a direct behavioural route and an indirect cognitive route through brand equity.
The model posits that CSR inﬂuences repurchase intention both directly and indirectly via brand equity,
while power distance belief differentially moderates these pathways. The direct pathway is expected to
be more sensitive to individual-level cultural values, whereas the brand equity-mediated pathway reﬂects
more stable cognitive evaluations of CSR signals. This logic is consistent with dual-process perspectives
on consumer decision-making [11] and signalling theory [12, 13], which explains how CSR communicates
unobservable ﬁrm attributes that accumulate into evaluative judgments of ﬁrms.
We test our hypotheses using survey data from 630 Chinese consumers in the ethical food sector
collected via Sojump.com. The ethical food context is particularly relevant because CSR attributes are
embedded in product characteristics such as organic certiﬁcation, fair trade sourcing, and eco-friendly
production, making CSR signals highly salient in consumption decisions. This setting provides a
theoretically meaningful context in which to examine how CSR governance signals are translated into
consumer responses under conditions of high personal relevance.
This study contributes to sustainability and CSR research by demonstrating that cultural values operate
as pathway-speciﬁc moderators in CSR-driven sustainable consumption, rather than as uniform
boundary conditions. Speciﬁcally, power distance belief moderates the direct CSR–repurchase
relationship but does not signiﬁcantly inﬂuence the indirect pathway through brand equity. This
distinction clariﬁes when and how CSR governance signals translate into stable sustainable
consumption responses and reﬁnes current understanding of cultural heterogeneity in CSR effectiveness
across markets.
2. Conceptual Background and Hypotheses
2.1 CSR, Stakeholders, and Brand Equity Mechanism
Corporate social responsibility (CSR) operates as a key sustainability governance signal through which
ﬁrms communicate ethical commitments and inﬂuence consumer responses. From a stakeholder
perspective, consumers represent primary stakeholders whose evaluations of CSR shape behavioural
intentions in consumption contexts [14, 15]. CSR creates value for consumers through functional,
symbolic, and trust-based mechanisms, which are reﬂected in brand-related evaluations and behavioural
outcomes [16, 17]. Functional value arises when CSR-related attributes, such as certiﬁed ethical sourcing
or environmentally responsible production, signal product quality and reduce perceived purchase risk
[16]. Symbolic and trust-based value arise separately, as consumers use CSR engagement to express
personal values through their consumption choices and to form judgments about ﬁrm benevolence and
integrity that extend beyond the product itself [17, 18].
A central mechanism through which CSR inﬂuences consumer behaviour is brand equity, which reﬂects
aggregated consumer perceptions, associations, and trust toward a brand [19, 20]. CSR signals provide
Page 3/24

information about ﬁrm integrity and stakeholder orientation, which consumers use to update brand
evaluations under conditions of information asymmetry [21, 22]. When CSR signals are perceived as
credible, they strengthen cognitive and affective dimensions of brand equity, including perceived quality,
brand associations, and emotional trust [23, 24], which in turn reduces perceived risk and strengthens
behavioural commitment toward repurchase. Accordingly:
H1a: Perceived CSR positively inﬂuences brand equity.
H1b: Brand equity positively inﬂuences consumer repurchase intention.
H1c: Brand equity mediates the relationship between perceived CSR and repurchase intention.
2.2 Power Distance Belief and Dual-Pathway Moderation
The mediated pathway established in H1a–H1c, however, does not account for why CSR effects vary
across cultural contexts. Empirical evidence on cultural moderation remains directly contradictory: Le et
al. documented that power distance transforms CSR impacts from negative to positive effects, with the
direction varying across CSR dimensions, whereas Al-Absy et al. found null moderation effects,
suggesting that PDB's inﬂuence may be conﬁned to speciﬁc outcomes rather than operating uniformly
[25, 26]. We argue this contradiction reﬂects a speciﬁcation issue: existing research tests PDB at the
level of overall CSR effects without distinguishing the pathways through which those effects occur.
Power distance belief (PDB) reﬂects the extent to which individuals accept hierarchical social structures
as legitimate [27]. Prior research shows that PDB shapes how consumers interpret authority, legitimacy,
and status signals embedded in corporate actions, thereby inﬂuencing responses to CSR-related
information [28, 29].
We propose that CSR operates through two distinct pathways: a direct behavioural route and an indirect
cognitive route through brand equity. These pathways differ in their susceptibility to cultural inﬂuence
[30]. The indirect pathway reﬂects systematic evaluation processes through which CSR signals are
translated into brand equity via credibility assessment and associative learning. These cognitive
processes are relatively stable across individuals, suggesting limited sensitivity to PDB variation. In
contrast, the direct CSR–repurchase pathway is more heuristic in nature, relying on interpretive shortcuts
in evaluating corporate legitimacy signals, which are more sensitive to cultural value orientations [30].
Accordingly, PDB is expected to differentially moderate these two pathways rather than exert uniform
inﬂuence across all CSR effects.
H2: The indirect effect of CSR on repurchase intention through brand equity is invariant across levels of
power distance belief.
H3: Power distance belief positively moderates the direct effect of CSR on repurchase intention, such
that the effect is stronger at higher levels of PDB.

3. Methods
3.1 Participants and procedures
The study recruited Chinese ethical food consumers through Sojump.com’s online platform. From the
655 questionnaire submissions collected over a seven-day period, 630 were valid for analysis.
Restricting the sample to consumers with ethical food purchasing experience ensures that CSR
perceptions are grounded in direct consumption experience. Therefore, participates ﬁrst answered two
screening questions: (1) Have you purchased any ethical or social responsible food products (e.g.,
organic, fair trade, eco-friendly, cruelty-free) in the past six months? (Yes/No) and (2) How often do you
purchase ethical or social responsible food products (1 = never, 7 = very frequently). Only respondents
who answered "Yes" to the ﬁrst question and scored above 1 on the second question were invited to
continue the main survey.
Furthermore, to ensure a common evaluative context across CSR, corporate–consumer identiﬁcation
(CCI), and loyalty measures, all eligible respondents were instructed to identify a company they had
purchased from recently and perceived as engaging in socially responsible practices. A brief instruction
was provided: “Please think of a company you have recently bought from that you believe engages in
socially responsible activities. When answering the following statements, please keep this speciﬁc
company in mind.” Respondents then proceeded to the main questionnaire, which employed a seven-
point Likert scale (1 = "strong disagreement," 7 = "strong agreement"). Socio-demographic data collected
in the ﬁnal section. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 24 and AMOS 26.
Demographic data (Table 1) showed a gender distribution of 55.4% female and 44.6% male respondents,
all aged 18 or above. The sample featured predominantly college-educated individuals, with household
income levels showing broad distribution.

4. Results
4.1 Measurement model
Prior to structural model assessment, exploratory and conﬁrmatory factor analyses (EFA and CFA) were
conducted to evaluate construct psychometric properties [38]. Factor loadings, Cronbach's Alpha, and
Composite Reliability (CR) values all exceeded the 0.5 threshold [39], with CR values between 0.636 and
0.884 and Cronbach's Alpha ranging from 0.631 to 0.882 (in Table 2), conﬁrming internal consistency
and reliability.
Construct validity assessment included both convergent and discriminant validity. Average variance
extracted (AVE) values ranged from 0.370 to 0.718, remaining below Composite Reliability scores,
establishing convergent validity [40]. While AVE values for CSR (0.370) and Brand Equity (0.450) fall
below the commonly cited 0.50 threshold, we retained these measures for three reasons. First, both
constructs are inherently multidimensional: CSR encompasses environmental, social, and governance
dimensions, while brand equity comprises awareness, associations, perceived quality, and loyalty. For
such heterogeneous constructs, slightly lower AVE values are acceptable when other validity criteria are
met [41]. Second, Composite Reliability values (0.636 for CSR, 0.718 for Brand Equity) exceed the 0.60
threshold, indicating adequate internal consistency [40]. Third, discriminant validity was veriﬁed as AVE
values exceeded the average shared variance (ASV) across all constructs, conﬁrming construct
distinctiveness [39].
Model ﬁt indices (in Table 2) aligned with recommended thresholds from [42]: chi-square/df < 3.0,
RMSEA < 0.07, CFI ≥ 0.9, SRMR < 0.07, and GFI ≥ 0.9, indicating strong model representation of the data.
Given that predictors and criteria were rated by the same respondents, we tested for common method
bias (CMB) [43]. Harman's single-factor test showed that the maximum variance explained by any one
factor was 32.864% (in Table 3), well below the 50% threshold, suggesting CMB was not a signiﬁcant
concern [44].

4.4 Testing Competing Models: Universal vs. Culturally
Contingent Mechanisms
To test competing theoretical models of cultural moderation (Model A: Uniform Cultural Moderation vs.
Model B: Differential Pathway Moderation), we employed PROCESS macro Model 8, which
simultaneously tests moderation on both the direct pathway and examines moderated mediation [46].
The analysis generated bias-corrected 95% conﬁdence intervals using 5,000 bootstrap samples.
H2 predicted that the mediated pathway (CSR-Brand Equity-Repurchase Intention) shows stability across
individual-level PDB variation, operating through universal systematic processing mechanisms. To test
this hypothesis, we examined two critical indicators. Analysis results shown in Table 6.
First, the CSR×PDB interaction effect on brand equity was non-signiﬁcant (β = 0.019, p = 0.588),
indicating that PDB does not moderate how CSR builds brand equity. Second, following recommendation
[46], we examined the index of moderated mediation as the formal inferential test of whether the indirect
effect varies across PDB levels. The index was not statistically signiﬁcant (Index = 0.006, SE = 0.013, 95%
Page 11/24

CI [-0.019, 0.033]). Because the conﬁdence interval includes zero, we conclude that the strength of the
mediated pathway does not vary signiﬁcantly by PDB.
The conditional indirect effects provide additional descriptive insight. The indirect effect showed slight
numerical variation across PDB levels: low PDB: β = 0.157, 95% CI [0.111, 0.209]; mean PDB: β = 0.163,
95% CI [0.122, 0.209]; high PDB: β = 0.170, 95% CI [0.122, 0.223]. This 8% increase from low to high PDB
does not constitute statistically signiﬁcant moderation according to the formal inferential test. All
indirect effects remain signiﬁcant across PDB levels, conﬁrming that brand equity consistently mediates

(Index = 0.006, SE = 0.013, 95% CI [-0.019, 0.033]), indicating that the indirect effect of CSR on repurchase
intention through brand equity does not vary signiﬁcantly across PDB levels. We interpret this as
evidence of pathway stability consistent with systematic processing operating through cognitive
mechanisms that function similarly across individual-level cultural value variation. The results support
H2.

4.5 Interpretation of the PDB Main Effect
The analysis revealed a signiﬁcant negative main effect of PDB on repurchase intention (β = -0.149, p <
.001). We note that this main effect was not hypothesised a priori. We offer this interpretation as
exploratory. The pattern is consistent with CSR scepticism research showing that consumers who
maintain higher baseline scepticism toward commercial claims also respond more strongly when
credible legitimacy signals are present [47]. High-PDB consumers may maintain greater baseline
scepticism toward commercial entities, reﬂecting heightened expectations for hierarchical legitimacy
that many brands fail to meet [8].
The negative main effect indicates that high-PDB consumers exhibit lower baseline repurchase
intentions when controlling for CSR perceptions and brand equity. The signiﬁcant positive interaction (β 
= 0.065, p = .034) demonstrates that CSR effectively overcomes this baseline scepticism through
legitimacy signalling. This compensatory mechanism shows that high-PDB consumers are more
demanding in baseline terms but more responsive to appropriate legitimacy signals. We recommend
future research test this compensatory mechanism as an explicit hypothesis.
The pattern is consistent with CSR scepticism research showing that consumers who maintain higher
baseline scepticism respond more strongly when appropriate legitimacy signals are present [47, 8].
We ﬁnd strong support for Model B (Differential Pathway Moderation) over Model A (Uniform Cultural
Moderation) (shown in Fig. 3). The systematic processing pathway through brand equity shows stability
across PDB levels, and the heuristic processing pathway shows cultural contingency. This pattern aligns
with dual-process theory predictions that central-route processing remains stable across contexts and
peripheral-route processing varies by cultural values [11].
The ﬁnding that PDB moderates the direct pathway but not the mediated pathway suggests that cultural
values inﬂuence how CSR affects behaviour (through which psychological route) rather than uniformly
amplifying all CSR effects. High-PDB consumers do not form brand equity differently from low-PDB
consumers when systematically evaluating CSR, but they do respond more strongly to CSR as a
peripheral decision cue. This mechanism differentiation advances understanding of when and how
cultural values create boundary conditions for CSR effectiveness.
5. Discussion and Conclusions
This study examines how individual cultural values, particularly power distance belief (PDB), shape the
effectiveness of CSR as a sustainability governance signal in inﬂuencing consumer participation in
Page 14/24

sustainable consumption. It addresses three interrelated questions: how CSR translates into consumer
behaviour through brand equity, how individual-level PDB moderates this translation, and whether such
moderation operates uniformly across direct and indirect behavioural pathways.
The ﬁndings demonstrate that CSR strengthens brand equity, which in turn drives repurchase intention,
conﬁrming brand equity as a key mediating mechanism in CSR-driven sustainability governance,
consistent with the strong positive effect of CSR on brand equity reported in prior research [18, 48]. This
supports the view that CSR operates not only as a reputational signal but also as an input into
accumulated cognitive and affective brand structures that guide subsequent consumption decisions. In
addition, PDB signiﬁcantly moderates the direct CSR–repurchase relationship, with high-PDB individuals
showing stronger behavioural responsiveness to CSR signals, consistent with prior evidence of a
stronger CSR response among high-PDB individuals [49]. However, PDB does not signiﬁcantly moderate
the CSR–brand equity pathway, suggesting that cultural values primarily inﬂuence immediate evaluative
responses rather than the underlying cognitive accumulation processes through which brand equity is
formed. The study also extends prior work by demonstrating signiﬁcant within-culture variation in PDB, in
contrast to Hofstede's (2001) societal-level analysis [50].
Taken together, these results suggest that CSR-driven sustainable consumption transitions are not
culturally uniform, but depend on the behavioural pathway through which CSR is processed. From a
governance perspective, this implies that cultural variation does not uniformly distort CSR effectiveness,
but instead selectively shapes heuristic interpretation while leaving cognitive accumulation mechanisms
relatively intact.
Importantly, the results also reveal a compensatory cultural mechanism that extends prior CSR-
consumer behaviour research. PDB exhibits a negative main effect on repurchase intention but a positive
interaction with CSR. This pattern indicates that high-PDB consumers are characterised by elevated
baseline scepticism toward CSR claims, consistent with legitimacy-based processing under hierarchical
acceptance norms [51, 52], yet display heightened responsiveness once CSR is interpreted as a credible
and institutionally legitimate signal, consistent with scepticism research showing that consumers who
maintain higher baseline scepticism respond more strongly when appropriate legitimacy signals are
present [47, 8]. Within a dual-process perspective, this suggests that PDB simultaneously lowers
baseline reliance on CSR cues while increasing sensitivity to validated authority signals. Rather than
functioning as a uniform ampliﬁer, cultural values therefore operate as a conditional activation
mechanism that reshapes the reference point from which CSR signals are evaluated.
5.1 Theoretical Implications
This study contributes to CSR and sustainability governance research in three ways. First, it develops a
dual-pathway model of CSR effectiveness that distinguishes between a cognitively mediated route
through brand equity and a heuristic direct route to behavioural intention, drawing on dual-process theory
[11, 53]. This mechanism-based framework helps explain inconsistent ﬁndings in prior research by
Page 15/24

showing that variation in CSR effects reﬂects differences in underlying processing pathways rather than
contextual instability. Accordingly, the central analytical question shifts from whether CSR is effective to
how CSR operates through distinct behavioural mechanisms in sustainability-relevant consumption
contexts.
Second, the ﬁndings reﬁne understanding of cultural moderation in sustainability-related decision-
making by demonstrating that power distance belief (PDB) does not operate as a uniform moderator
across CSR outcomes. Instead, its inﬂuence is concentrated in the heuristic processing pathway, while
exerting limited impact on the cognitively mediated formation of brand equity. This pathway-speciﬁc
moderation perspective offers an explanation for why prior studies report conﬂicting evidence on
cultural moderation of CSR effects: studies ﬁnding signiﬁcant PDB moderation [7] may have examined
outcomes inﬂuenced through heuristic processing, while studies ﬁnding null effects [26] may have
examined outcomes requiring systematic processing. Within this pathway-speciﬁc structure, the study
identiﬁes a compensatory moderation pattern in which PDB exhibits a negative main effect on
repurchase intention alongside a positive interaction with CSR. This asymmetry suggests that cultural
values do not simply strengthen or weaken CSR effects, but reshape the baseline reference point from
which sustainability signals are evaluated. This represents a theoretically underdeveloped mechanism in
CSR research, whereby cultural orientation affects not only effect size but also evaluative baseline
structure. Unlike prior research examining power distance as a societal-level cultural orientation [27],
measuring PDB at the individual level allows this within-culture variation to be captured directly.
Third, the results position brand equity as a relatively stable mechanism in CSR-driven sustainability
governance. The non-signiﬁcant moderation of the CSR–brand equity pathway suggests that brand
equity formation is driven by cumulative cognitive and affective processes—such as associative learning,
credibility assessment, and trust accumulation [24, 10]—that are comparatively robust to individual-level
cultural variation. This stability highlights brand equity as a structurally reliable mechanism through
which CSR-based sustainability signals are translated into behavioural outcomes across heterogeneous
consumer populations. We acknowledge an important limitation here: we treated brand equity as
unidimensional, whereas contemporary frameworks recognise multiple dimensions, including
awareness, associations, perceived quality, and loyalty [20]; the unidimensional treatment may mask
differential PDB effects across speciﬁc equity dimensions. Future research should also extend the dual-
pathway model to multi-country designs, as cultural and economic context shapes which CSR
processing pathways operate most strongly across consumer populations [54].
5.2 Practical and Governance Implications
The ﬁndings have implications at both organisational and sustainability governance levels. At the
organisational level, the stability of the brand-equity pathway suggests that ﬁrms can rely on
substantively delivered CSR practices—such as veriﬁable certiﬁcation, transparent sourcing, and credible
environmental practices—to build brand equity consistently across culturally diverse markets. These
mechanisms operate through cognitive trust-building processes that appear relatively robust across
individual cultural differences.
Page 16/24

However, the direct pathway exhibits strong cultural contingency. In higher-PDB contexts, CSR also
operates as a legitimacy and authority signal that directly inﬂuences behavioural intention independent
of brand equity formation, consistent with research showing that high-PDB consumers rely more heavily
on legitimacy heuristics and authority-based cues [8, 55]. This implies that ﬁrms operating in such
contexts face a dual requirement: maintaining substantive CSR investment while ensuring that CSR
signals are visible, recognisable, and institutionally framed as legitimate.
At the governance level, the ﬁndings extend beyond ﬁrm strategy to the design of sustainability
infrastructure. Certiﬁcation systems, ESG disclosure frameworks, and sustainability labelling schemes
are typically designed as culturally neutral mechanisms. Our results suggest that while the underlying
credibility function of these systems may generalise across contexts, their behavioural impact depends
on how they are interpreted within different cultural value structures. In particular, in higher-PDB
contexts, the visibility and authority framing of certiﬁcation may be as important as its informational
content in shaping consumer response.
This implies a meaningful distinction between (1) the standardisation of CSR-related information
content, which supports comparability and trust formation, and (2) the contextualisation of signal
presentation, which inﬂuences behavioural uptake—a distinction that may be particularly relevant for
improving the effectiveness of sustainability transitions in culturally diverse and emerging-market
contexts where high-PDB orientations are more prevalent.
The compensatory mechanism identiﬁed in this study further suggests that the absence of visible CSR
signals carries a disproportionate behavioural cost in high-PDB contexts, while credible CSR
communication can generate stronger marginal effects once legitimacy thresholds are met. This
asymmetry implies that the behavioural effectiveness of sustainability communication and governance
interventions is likely to be non-linear across cultural contexts, rather than uniform.
5.3 Limitations and Future Research
This study has several limitations that suggest directions for future research. First, the sample is limited
to Chinese ethical food consumers, meaning that pathway stability is tested only within a single cultural
context. This restricts the generalisability of conclusions regarding cross-cultural sustainability
governance. Future research should replicate the model across culturally distinct national contexts to
examine whether the observed pathway stability holds under broader institutional variation.
Second, brand equity is treated as a unidimensional construct, whereas it comprises multiple
dimensions (awareness, associations, perceived quality, loyalty) that may respond differently to CSR
signals and cultural orientations [20]. Future research should examine whether the observed stability of
the mediated pathway persists at the dimensional level.
Third, although the non-signiﬁcant moderated mediation index suggests pathway stability, this does not
constitute formal equivalence. Future studies should employ equivalence testing or Bayesian
approaches to more rigorously assess invariance across cultural conditions [56].
Page 17/24

Fourth, the operationalisation of PDB at the individual level raises conceptual questions regarding its
stability versus contextual sensitivity [34]. Future research should explore additional cultural dimensions,
such as uncertainty avoidance or long-term orientation, within the dual-pathway framework.
Fifth, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference regarding the temporal ordering of CSR
perception, brand equity formation, and behavioural intention. Longitudinal or experimental designs
would strengthen causal claims.
Finally, the study focuses on repurchase intention rather than actual behavioural outcomes. Future
research should integrate behavioural trace data or experimental purchase settings to validate the
external validity of the proposed mechanisms.
