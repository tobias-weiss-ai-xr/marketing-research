 This study examined the influence of hospital branding on healthcare
reputation and patient loyalty using the proposed Customer -Based
Brand Equity (CBBE) framework. A quantitative explanatory research
design was employed using Partial Least Squares Structu ral Equation
Modeling (PLS-SEM). Data were collected from 150 patients at private
hospitals using a structured Likert -scale questionnaire and analyzed
with SmartPLS software through a two -stage evaluation of the
measurement and structural models. The resul ts showed that hospital
branding had a significant positive effect on healthcare reputation (β =
0.72, p < 0.001) and patient loyalty (β = 0.30, p < 0.01). Furthermore,
healthcare reputation significantly influenced patient loyalty (β = 0.65,
p < 0.001) an d partially mediated the relationship between hospital
branding and patient loyalty, jointly explaining 52% and 68% of the
variance in healthcare reputation and patient loyalty, respectively.
These findings highlight the strategic importance of branding in
strengthening hospital competitiveness and patient retention. This study
contributes to the healthcare marketing literature by extending the
application of CBBE to healthcare services and provides practical
guidance for hospital managers seeking to develo p reputation -driven
loyalty strategies in an increasingly competitive healthcare market in
developing countries.

INTRODUCTION
In the modern healthcare industry, hospitals are increasingly evaluated not only based
on clinical outcomes but also on intangible assets, such as brand image and institutional
reputation. The growing competition among healthcare providers, particularly in  the private
hospital sector, has encouraged hospitals to adopt marketing strategies similar to those used in
the corporate sector, especially branding strategies aimed at differentiating hospitals from
competitors in the perceptions of prospective and existing patients (Górska-Warsewicz, 2022)
. As patients gain greater access to information and a wider range of healthcare provider
options, hospital brand equity has emerged as a distinct area of empirical research. Recent
systematic reviews have identifi ed brand loyalty, perceived quality, brand awareness, and
brand associations as the most frequently examined determinants of hospital brand equity in
the global literature (Górska-Warsewicz, 2022).
Digital transformation has further accelerated this develo pment. (Al-Hasan, 2024)
demonstrated that hospitals’ social media marketing activities, including educational content,

patient stories, and the dissemination of recent research findings, contributed to higher brand
equity and improved financial performa nce (Brandão & Ribeiro, 2023)  . This evidence
suggests that hospital branding is no longer merely a communication activity but has become
a strategic asset that influences how patients perceive, trust, and maintain loyalty toward
healthcare providers.
Alongside branding, institutional reputation plays a critical role in healthcare due to the
high-risk and uncertain nature of medical services. (Nguyen & Leblanc, 2001a)  argued that
corporate image and reputation significantly influence custo mer retention decisions across
service industries. This influence is particularly relevant in healthcare, where patients often
cannot fully assess service quality before receiving treatment and therefore rely heavily on
reputational signals. Recent evidenc e from Turkey further supports this perspective: (Amarat
& Ünal, 2022)   found that both doctor reputation and hospital reputation positively affected
patient loyalty, with hospital reputation demonstrating a stronger influence than individual
doctor repu tation. This finding highlights the institutional dimension of trust fo rmation in
healthcare settings.
The theoretical foundation commonly used to examine these relationships is (Keller,
1993) Customer-Based Brand Equity (CBBE) framework, which conceptua lizes brand equity
as the differential effect of brand knowledge on consumer responses. Although CBBE has been
extensively applied in consumer goods, hospitality, and tourism contexts (Aaker, 1996;
Baalbaki & Guzmán, 2016; Han & Hyun, 2017; Lee & Back, 200 8), its application to
healthcare services, particularly hospital branding in developing countries, remains relatively
underexplored (Wu, 2011).
This research gap is particularly evident in Indonesia. Although a growing body of
Indonesian research has examined hospital brand equity and its behavioral consequences, such
as studies by (Mandagi et al., 2024)   in Manado, (Srivastava & Shocker, 1991; Syah et al.,
2024)  in Tangerang, and (Triastuti & Basabih, 2023)  on patient-based hospital brand equity,
most studies have focused on isolated relationships, such as service quality and satisfaction or
brand image and revisit intention. These studies have rarely tested a comprehensive, theory -
driven CBBE model that simultaneously incorporates branding, reputation, and loyalty within
a single structural framework. Furthermore, the mediating role of healthcare reputation in the
relationship between hospital branding and patient loyalty remains empirically underexplored,
despite theoretical arguments that rep utation serves as a mechanism through which branding
investments are transformed into behavioral loyalty outcomes (Amarat & Ünal, 2022; Shie et
al., 2022).
This study addressed these gaps by proposing and empirically testing an integrated
CBBE-based model in which hospital branding influences patient loyalty both directly and
indirectly through healthcare reputation. The study used data collected from patients of private
hospitals in Indonesia and analyzed them using Partial Least Squares Structural Equatio n
Modeling (PLS-SEM). The novelty of this study lies in three aspects: (1) examining healthcare
reputation as a mediating mechanism rather than merely as an outcome variable; (2) providing
empirical evidence from a developing -country private hospital conte xt that remains
underrepresented in international CBBE research; and (3) applying a rigorous two-stage PLS-
SEM evaluation procedure to assess both measurement and structural model quality.

Based on this background, this study was guided by the following research objectives:
(1) to examine the effect of hospital branding on healthcare reputation; (2) to examine the effect
of healthcare reputation on patient loyalty; (3) to examine the direct effect of hospital branding
on patient loyalty; and (4) to examine whether healthcare reputation mediates the relationship
between hospital branding and patient loyalty. Accordingly, the study addressed the following
research questions: Does hospital branding significantly influence healthcare reputation? Does
healthcare reputation significantly influence patient loyalty? Does hospital branding directly
influence patient loyalty? And does healthcare reputation mediate the relationshi p between
branding and loyalty?
Theoretically, this study contributes to healthcare marketing literature by extending the
application of the CBBE framework to a developing -country hospital context and providing
empirical evidence regarding the mediating mechanism linking branding and loyalty.
Practically, the findings provide hospita l managers and healthcare marketers with evidence -
based insights into prioritizing branding and reputation management strategies to strengthen
long-term patient retention in an increasingly competitive private healthcare market.
The remainder of this paper  is organized as follows. The Literature Review section
presents the theoretical foundations of CBBE, hospital branding, healthcare reputation, and
patient loyalty, and develops the study’s four hypotheses and conceptual framework. The
Method section describes the research design, population and sample, operational definitions,
data collection procedures, and analytical techniques. The Results section presents the
evaluation of the measurement and structural models. The Discussion section interprets the
findings in relation to previous studies, while the Conclusion section summarizes the
theoretical and practical contributions, limitations, and directions for future research.

METHOD
Research Design
This study employed a quantitative explanatory (associative) research design using a
cross-sectional survey method to examine the hypothesized relationships among hospital
branding, healthcare reputation, and patient loyalty. Data were analyzed using Parti al Least
Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS -SEM), a variance -based structural equation
modeling technique selected due to its suitability for prediction -oriented research, moderate
sample sizes, non-normal data distributions, and models involving mediation analysis, such as
the mediating role of healthcare reputation tested in H4 (Hair, 2022).
Population and Sample
The population of this study consists of patients who have received healthcare services
from private hospitals. Respondents were select ed using purposive (judgmental) sampling,
with the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18 years or older, (2) had received inpatient or
outpatient services from a private hospital within the preceding twelve months, and (3) were
willing to provide infor med consent and complete the research questionnaire. A final sample
of 150 respondents was obtained through this procedure.
This sample size satisfies established minimum sample size guidelines for PLS -SEM.
Following the widely used “10 -times rule” and the more formally derived minimum sample
size recommendations summarized by Hair et al. (2022), a model in which no construct
receives more than the two structural paths directed at healthcare reputation and patient loyalty

Data Collection Procedure
Primary data were collected using a structured, self -administered questionnaire
consisting of closed-ended items measured on a five -point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree
to 5 = str ongly agree). Questionnaire items for each construct were adapted from established
scales in the CBBE and healthcare marketing literature (Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001; Keller,
1993; Nguyen & Leblanc, 2001b)  and refined for the hospital context. Prior to full-scale
distribution, the instrument underwent a content-validity review and a small-scale pilot test to
identify and correct any ambiguous wording, after which the finalized questionnaire was
distributed to eligible respondents at the selected private hospitals.
Measurement Model Assessment Procedure
Consistent with the two -stage PLS-SEM evaluation procedure recommended by Hair
et al. (2022), the measurement (outer) model was assessed first, followed by the structural
(inner) model. Convergent validity was evaluated using indicator outer loadings and t he
Average Variance Extracted (AVE), with an AVE of at least 0.50 considered acceptable.
Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Composite Reliability (CR) and Cronbach's
alpha, with values of at least 0.70 considered adequate for confirmatory research. Discriminant
validity was assessed using the Fornell -Larcker criterion, whereby the square root of each
construct's AVE should exceed its correlations with other constructs, and the Heterotrait -
Monotrait (HTMT) ratio, with a conservative threshold of 0.90. Table 2 presents the resulting
measurement model statistics.

Structural model relationships were estimated using PLS -SEM in SmartPLS software.
The significance of path coefficients was assessed using a nonparametric bootstrapping
procedure with 5,000 subsamples, following current best -practice recommendations for PLS-
SEM inference (Hair et al., 2022). The explanatory power of the structural model was evaluated

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Respondent Profile
The final sample comprised 150 patients who ha d received inpatient or outpatient
services from private hospitals within the preceding twelve months, in accordance with the
study's purposive sampling criteria. Respondents varied in gender, age group, educational
background, and frequency of hospital visits, reflecting the heterogeneous composition of the
private hospital patient population typically served by the participating institutions.
Measurement Model Evaluation
As shown in Table 2, all three constructs  Hospital Branding, Healthcare Reputation,
and Patient Loyalty exceeded the recommended threshold for convergent validity, with AVE
values ranging from 0.65 to 0.68 (threshold ≥ 0.50). Internal consistency reliability was
likewise well established, with Composite Reliability values rang ing from 0.89 to 0.91 and
Cronbach's alpha values ranging from 0.86 to 0.88, both comfortably exceeding the
recommended minimum of 0.70 (Hair et al., 2022). Discriminant validity was further
supported, as the square root of each construct's AVE exceeded it s correlations with the other
constructs, and the HTMT ratios among all three construct pairs remained below the
conservative 0.90 threshold, indicating that Hospital Branding, Healthcare Reputation, and
Patient Loyalty represent empirically distinct const ructs. Together, these results confirm that
the measurement model possesses adequate convergent and discriminant validity, providing a
sound basis for structural model interpretation.

As shown in Table 4, all three direct structural hypotheses are supported. Hospital
branding has a significant positive effect on healthcare reputation (β = 0.72, t = 8.21, p < 0.001),
supporting H1. Healthcare reputation has a significant positive effect on patient loyalty (β =
0.65, t = 7.10, p < 0.001), supporting H2. Hospital branding also has a significant positive direct
effect on patient loyalty (β = 0.30, t = 3.50, p < 0.01), supporting H3.
Mediation Analysis (H4)
To test H4, the indirect effect of hospital branding on patient loyalty through healthcare
reputation was calculated by multiplying the two constituent path coefficients: 0.72 × 0.65 =
0.468. Combined with the direct effect of hospital branding on patient loyalty (β = 0.30), the
total effect of hospital branding on pat ient loyalty is 0.30 + 0.468 = 0.768. The Variance
Accounted For (VAF) by the indirect path is therefore 0.468 / 0.768 ≈ 60.9%.
Following the VAF classification criteria described by Hair et al. (2022), a VAF
between 20% and 80% indicates partial mediation , whereas a VAF below 20% indicates no
meaningful mediation and a VAF above 80% indicates full mediation. With a VAF of
approximately 60.9%, healthcare reputation is confirmed to partially mediate the relationship
between hospital branding and patient loyalty: hospital branding influences patient loyalty both
directly and, to a somewhat larger extent, indirectly through the reputational pathway. This
finding, together with the significant and substantial paths underlying H1 and H2, provides
support for H4.
The Effect of Hospital Branding on Healthcare Reputation (H1)
The finding that hospital branding exerts a strong, significant positive effect on
healthcare reputation (β = 0.72) is consistent with, and extends, prior evidence on the branding-
reputation lin k in healthcare. Al -Hasan (2024) similarly found that hospitals' branding and
social media marketing efforts translate into measurable gains in brand equity, a construct
closely tied to institutional reputation, while Mandagi et al. (2024) found that a fav orable
overall hospital “brand gestalt” significantly predicts patient satisfaction  a key reputational
input among patients of a private hospital in Manado, Indonesia. Mechanistically, this study's
results suggest that when hospitals communicate a clear, consistent, and credible brand identity
through visual identity, service quality signals, and communication strategy, patients interpret
these signals as evidence of institutional competence and trustworthiness, which cumulatively
builds a stronger reputati on. This is consistent with Górska -Warsewicz's (2022) systematic
review finding that brand associations, brand image, and perceived quality are among the most
consistently documented determinants of hospital brand equity across the international
literature.
The Effect of Healthcare Reputation on Patient Loyalty (H2)
The significant, substantial effect of healthcare reputation on patient loyalty (β = 0.65)
corroborates the central role of reputation in loyalty formation identified in prior healthcare

research. Amarat and Ünal (2022) found that hospital reputation exerts a stronger effect on
patient loyalty than does doctor reputation, suggesting that institutional -level reputational
perceptions carry particular weight in patients' loyalty decisions  a pattern consistent with the
present study's institutional framing of the reputation construct. Similarly, (Shie et al., 2022)
found that patient trust, a construct closely intertwined with reputation, significantly predicts
loyalty among elderly patients with chronic conditions in Taiwan, while (AlOmari & Hamid,
2022)  found that satisfaction-related, reputation-linked mechanisms mediate the relationship
between service antecedents and both loyalty and medication adherence among Syrian
healthcare pat ients. Collectively, this evidence reinforces the theoretical premise that
reputation reduces the perceived uncertainty inherent in healthcare decisions, thereby
strengthening patients' willingness to remain with, and recommend, a given hospital.
The Direct Effect of Hospital Branding on Patient Loyalty (H3)
Hospital branding was also found to exert a smaller, but still statistically significant,
direct effect on patient loyalty (β = 0.30) independent of its indirect effect through reputation.
This pattern a meaningful but comparatively modest direct effect alongside a larger indirect
effect mirrors findings from adjacent healthcare service settings. (Lin & Yin, 2022) , studying
private dental clinics in China, found that brand image and perceived value af fect outpatient
loyalty with service satisfaction acting as only a partial mediator, implying a persistent residual
direct effect similar in kind to the one observed here. (Purwianti et al., 2024)  likewise found
that multiple brand equity dimensions dir ectly and solidly influence customer loyalty in an
occupational health clinic setting. (Cham et al., 2022)   further show, in a medical tourism
context, that branding and social influences shape behavioral intention both before and after
service consumption, consistent with the view that branding's influence on loyalty operates
through multiple, complementary pathways rather than through reputation alone.
The Mediating Role of Healthcare Reputation (H4)
The mediation analysis, which yielded a Variance Accounted For (VAF) of
approximately 60.9%, confirms that healthcare reputation partially mediates the relationsh ip
between hospital branding and patient loyalty. In practical terms, this means that roughly six -
tenths of hospital branding's total effect on patient loyalty is transmitted through the
reputational pathway, while the remaining share reflects branding's direct influence on loyalty.
This result is consistent with Triastuti and Basabih's (2023) finding, in an Indonesian hospital
setting, that service quality, trust, and loyalty are interlinked in a manner that positions trust -
related, reputation-adjacent constructs as a central transmission mechanism in the process of
building patient -based hospital brand equity. Theoretically, this finding lends support to
commitment-trust theory (Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001) as applied to healthcare: reputation
appears to fu nction as the trust -building mechanism through which more abstract branding
investments are converted into concrete behavioral loyalty.
Theoretical Implications
This study extends the CBBE framework (Keller, 1993), originally developed in a
consumer-goods context and subsequently applied to hospitality and tourism (Han & Hyun,
2017; Kandampully & Hu, 2007; Lee & Back, 2008) , into a developing -country private
hospital setting, thereby addressing the empirical gap identified by Górska-Warsewicz's (2022)
systematic review, which noted a relative scarcity of hospital brand equity research outside
high-income countries. By explicitly modeling and testing healthcare reputation as a partial

Managerial Implications
For hospital management, these findings carry several practical implications. First,
because hospital branding has the strongest overall influence on patient loyalty when its effect
through reputation is taken  into account, hospitals should treat branding not merely as a
marketing communication activity but as a strategic investment that spans visual identity,
service quality standards, staff professionalism, and consistent institutional messaging. Second,
because healthcare reputation functions as the primary transmission channel for branding's
effect on loyalty, hospitals should complement branding investments with deliberate
reputation-management practices, including active monitoring and response to patient reviews
and word-of-mouth, transparent communication of quality and safety outcomes, and ongoing
investment in the technical and interpersonal competence of clinical staff. Third, hospitals
should recognize that branding also retains a direct effect on loyalty independent of reputation,
suggesting that emotionally resonant branding  for example, communicating compassion,
cultural sensitivity, and patient-centered values can build loyalty even before, or independent
of, a fully formed reputational judgment. H ospitals that integrate marketing, service -quality
management, and reputation -management functions, rather than treating them as separate
organizational silos, are therefore best positioned to convert branding investment into
sustainable patient retention and competitive advantage.

CONCLUSION
This study examined the influence of hospital branding on healthcare reputation and
patient loyalty using the Customer -Based Brand Equity (CBBE) framework (Keller, 1993),
which was tested through PLS-SEM with a sample of 150 private hospital patients. The results
demonstrated that hospital branding had a significant positive influence on healthcare
reputation (H1) and patient loyalty (H3), while healthcare reputation significantly and
positively influenced patien t loyalty (H2). Furthermore, healthcare reputation partially
mediated the relationship between hospital branding and patient loyalty, accounting for
approximately 60.9% of the total effect of branding on loyalty (H4). All four hypotheses were
supported, an d the proposed model explained a substantial proportion of the variance in
healthcare reputation (R² = 0.52) and patient loyalty (R² = 0.68).
This study had several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the
findings. First, the cross -sectional design captured relationships at a single point in time and
therefore did not allow definitive conclusions regarding causal relationships among the study
constructs. Second, the use of purposive, non-probability sampling limited the generalizability
of the findings beyond the specific private hospitals and patients included in the sample. Third,
data were collected using self -reported survey instruments, which may have been subject to
common method bias and social desirability bias. Fourth, the model examined only three core
CBBE-related constructs and did not include other potentially relevant variables, such as
patient satisfaction, perceived service quality, price fairness, or digital engagement, which
