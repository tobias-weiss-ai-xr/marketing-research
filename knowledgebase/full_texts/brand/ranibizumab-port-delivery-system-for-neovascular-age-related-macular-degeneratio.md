Abstract
Purpose To	review	the	engineering	principles,	pharmacokinetics,	clinical	evidence,	surgical	considerations,	safety	profile,
and emerging real-world implications of the ranibizumab Port Delivery System (PDS) as a sustained intraocular drug-
delivery platform for neovascular age-related macular degeneration.
Methods A systematic literature search was conducted to identify peer-reviewed publications addressing implant engineer-
ing, molecular compatibility, pharmacokinetics, phase 1–3 trials (LADDER, ARCHWAY , PORTAL), extension studies,
perioperative management, safety outcomes, health-economic analyses, and patient-reported outcomes related to the ranibi-
zumab PDS.
Results Phase	2	and	3	trials	demonstrated	that	PDS	delivering	ranibizumab	100	mg/mL	with	fixed	24-week	refill	intervals
achieved visual and anatomic outcomes noninferior to monthly intravitreal injections, with approximately 95% of patients
not	requiring	supplemental	treatment	before	scheduled	refills.	Pharmacokinetic	studies	confirmed	sustained	intraocular	drug
exposure within the therapeutic window of monthly dosing. Long-term extension data support durability through multi-year
follow-up.	The	distinct	safety	profile	features	implant-	and	conjunctival	interface	events,	including	conjunctival	erosion,	vit-
reous hemorrhage, and device-associated endophthalmitis, underscoring the importance of surgical technique and postopera-
tive surveillance. Economic analyses indicate cost parity with branded anti-VEGF agents under high injection frequencies,
while patient-reported outcomes consistently demonstrate strong preference for sustained delivery. In ARCHWAY , mean
BCV A change averaged over Weeks 36/40 was + 0.2 letters with PDS versus + 0.5 letters with monthly ranibizumab, and
98.4%	of	PDS-treated	eyes	required	no	supplemental	injections	in	the	first	24-week	interval;	across	later	intervals,	approxi-
mately	95%	avoided	supplemental	treatment	before	scheduled	refill.
Conclusions The ranibizumab PDS establishes sustained intraocular anti-VEGF delivery as a viable alternative to anti-
VEGF injections especially in patients requiring frequent dosing. Its long-term role will depend on optimization of surgical
workflows,	management	of	implant-specific	risks,	integration	of	monitoring	strategies,	and	ongoing	refinement	of	device
design to support scalable, durable retinal care.

Introduction
Intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy has become the standard
of care for neovascular age-related macular degenera -
tion (nAMD), diabetic macular edema (DME), and macu -
lar edema secondary to retinal vein occlusion (RVO) and
other macular conditions improving vision and reducing
disease progression [ 1, 2]. Anti-VEGF therapy has consis -
tently demonstrated strong short- and long-term visual and
anatomic outcomes across neovascular retinal diseases,
but	 these	 benefits	 require	 repeated	 injections	 and	 ongoing
monitoring to maintain disease control [3–10]. Vision gains
are closely tied to frequent monitoring, long-term need for
intravitreal injections, often given monthly or bi-monthly for
many years, which impose a considerable treatment burden
on patients and caregivers [4]. Real-world data demonstrate
that many patients struggle to maintain the often-intensive
injection schedules used in clinical trials, with a high rate of
early discontinuation and suboptimal long-term therapy uti-
lization documented in recent cohorts [ 11]. Beyond patient
experience, the persistent nature of anti-VEGF therapy
also drives substantial healthcare resource utilization and
economic cost, and systemic comorbidity may interact to
amplify burden [12–14].
The durability landscape now includes faricimab and
aflibercept	 8	 mg,	 both	 of	 which	 have	 the	 potential	 to
extend treatment intervals to 12–16 weeks while maintain -
ing	visual	outcomes	 noninferior	 to	aflibercept	 2	mg	in	the
TENAYA/LUCERNE and PULSAR trials [ 15, 16]. These
agents narrow the procedural-burden advantage of the PDS
because	they	are	administered	 in	the	office,	avoid	implan-
tation surgery, and maybe deployed within existing injec -
tion	 workflows.	 Nevertheless,	 they	 still	 require	 repeated
intravitreal injections and ongoing disease-activity assess -
ment, whereas the PDS provides continuous delivery with
planned	 refill-exchange	 every	 24	 weeks	 and	 eliminates
interim injections for most trial participants. Accordingly,
the likely role of PDS is increasingly selective: patients
with	 demonstrated	 high	 treatment	 need,	 difficulty	 sus-
taining injection schedules, strong preference for fewer
procedures, and acceptable conjunctival and surgical risk –
individuals with such characteristics may derive the great -
est	 incremental	 benefit.	 Direct	 comparative	 trials	 against
faricimab	 or	 aflibercept	 8	 mg	 are	 not	 currently	 available,
and	 differences	 in	 trial	 populations	 and	 retreatment	 algo-
rithms	preclude	reliable	comparisons	 of	treatment	efficacy
or burden comparisons.
The need for persistent administration of intravitreal
injections has motivated the development of sustained-
release delivery systems designed to maintain therapeutic
intraocular drug exposure with fewer interventions [ 17].
By extending dosing intervals and reducing cumulative
procedural risk, sustained-release systems aim to improve
patient adherence, alleviate caregiver and system load, and
potentially narrow the gap between real-world and trial
outcomes [18]. Their development arises directly from the
unmet need to reconcile the demands of chronic retinal

Methods
Literature search strategy
A comprehensive literature search was conducted to iden -
tify peer-reviewed publications relevant to PDS, sustained
intraocular anti-VEGF delivery, and competing long-acting
retinal therapies. Searches were performed in PubMed/
MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from data -
base inception through December 31, 2025. The search
strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH/Emtree)
and	 free-text	 terms	 related	 to:	 (1)	 disease	 areas	 (nAMD);
(2) technologies (port delivery system, ocular implant,
sustained	 release,	 long-acting	 delivery,	 refillable	 implant,
biodegradable	implant);	and	(3)	therapeutics	(ranibizumab,
anti-VEGF,	bispecific	antibodies).	A	representative	PubMed
search strategy was: (“Port Delivery System” OR “ocu -
lar implant” OR “sustained release” OR “long-acting”
OR “refillable implant” OR “intravitreal implant”) AND
(“ranibizumab” OR “anti-VEGF” OR “neovascular age-
related macular degeneration).  Searches were limited
to English-language publications. Reference lists of key
reviews and pivotal trials were hand-searched to identify
additional relevant studies. Studies were included if they:
(i) reported clinical data (phase 1–4 trials, extension studies,
or real-world evidence) on the ranibizumab Port Delivery
System;	(ii)	addressed	engineering	design,	pharmacokinet-
ics, surgical technique, or safety of implant-based ocular
drug-delivery	 systems;	 or	 (iii)	 evaluated	 patient-centered
outcomes, treatment burden, or healthcare utilization related
to chronic intravitreal therapy. Exclusion criteria included
single case reports without broader relevance, non-ophthal-
mic implant literature without retinal applicability, and con-
ference	 abstracts	 lacking	 sufficient	 methodological	 detail
(unless used to contextualize ongoing trials).
Study selection and data extraction
Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts
for	relevance.	Full	texts	were	reviewed	to	confirm	eligibil-
ity. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Priority
was given to randomized controlled trials, regulatory and
post-approval safety analyses, large observational studies,
and high-quality narrative or systematic reviews. Extracted
data included study design and population, device or thera -
peutic	 characteristics,	 efficacy	 and	 anatomic	 outcomes,
pharmacokinetic	 and	 durability	 findings,	 adverse	 events,
patient-reported outcomes, and implementation consid -
erations. Given heterogeneity across studies, a qualitative
narrative synthesis was performed organizing evidence
thematically across engineering concepts, pharmacokinet -
ics, clinical outcomes, safety, patient experience, real-world
considerations, and emerging technologies. Publicly avail -
able regulatory documents (FDA prescribing information
and	 safety	 communications;	 European	 Medicines	 Agency
statements)	 and	 clinical	 trial	 registries	 (ClinicalTrials.gov;
EU Clinical Trials Register) were reviewed to contextual -
ize clinical development and post-approval experience.
Because the objective of this manuscript was a narrative
review rather than a formal systematic review or meta-anal-
ysis, no standardized risk-of-bias scoring instrument (e.g.,
Cochrane RoB 2 or ROBINS-I) was applied. Methodologi -
cal quality nevertheless informed the narrative synthesis:
randomized controlled trials, pivotal registration studies,
long-term extension studies, pharmacokinetic analyses,
regulatory documents, and large observational cohorts were
weighted most heavily, whereas small retrospective reports,
conference abstracts, and expert-opinion sources were used
primarily to contextualize clinical implementation, evolv -
ing surgical practice, and real-world hypotheses requiring
confirmation.
Concept and design of anti-VEGF port
delivery systems
Engineering principles
The PDS represents a paradigm shift in intraocular drug
delivery by integrating sustained-release engineering with
ophthalmic clinical requirements. At its core, the PDS is
designed to provide continuous therapeutic levels of anti-
VEGF agents while minimizing the frequency of intravit -
real injections [ 22]. This sustained-delivery capability is
enabled	 by	 several	 key	 engineering	 features:	 a	 refillable
reservoir,	 passive	 diffusion	 transport	 mechanisms,	 and
advanced microfabrication using biocompatible materi -
als [29].	A	 central	 design	 element	 of	 the	 PDS	 is	 its	 refill-
able reservoir, which functions as a long-acting drug depot
implanted at the pars plana. The reservoir is preloaded with
a concentrated anti-VEGF formulation (e.g., ranibizumab)
and	can	be	periodically	refilled	in	the	clinic	via	a	minimally
invasive	 needle-in-needle	 refill-exchange	 procedure	 [22].
The	 refillable	 architecture	 allows	 the	 device	 to	 maintain

intraocular drug levels over extended durations (months)
without necessitating repeated bolus injections into the vit -

Unlike active pump systems, the PDS utilizes passive
diffusion	to	release	the	anti-VEGF	molecule	from	the	reser-
voir	into	the	vitreous.	The	driving	force	for	diffusion	is	the
concentration gradient between the drug in the reservoir and
the	 surrounding	 intraocular	 fluids,	 enabling	 a	 controlled,
steady-state	release	over	time.	The	rate	of	diffusion	is	gov-
erned by Fick’s principle, with membrane characteristics
and reservoir geometry calibrated to achieve desired release
kinetics.
The structural and functional integrity of the PDS device
depends on precision microfabrication and the use of bio -
compatible materials. The PDS is typically constructed
from medical-grade titanium chosen for its strength, corro -
sion resistance, and proven ocular compatibility. The reser-
voir is sealed with a porous membrane or controlled pore
interface	 that	 modulates	 drug	 diffusion;	 materials	 such	 as
polyethylene-based	 polymers	 are	 engineered	 to	 offer	 spe-
cific	 porosity	 and	 maintain	 consistent	 permeability	 over
time	 without	 eliciting	 significant	 inflammatory	 responses.
Microfabrication techniques (derived from semiconductor
and medical implant manufacturing) enable precise control
over dimensions, surface properties, and membrane archi -
tecture, which are critical to both device performance and
long-term stability in the intraocular environment.
Pharmacokinetics (PK)
A key PK goal of the anti-VEGF PDS is to maintain more
stable intraocular exposure over time than the peak–trough
pattern produced by repeated bolus injections [30]. Follow-
ing bolus intravitreal injection, ranibizumab demonstrates
rapid attainment of supratherapeutic intraocular concentra -
tions followed by exponential decay, with a vitreous half-
life of approximately 7–9 days and progressive decline to
trough levels prior to reinjection [ 31].	 The	 kinetic	 profile
underlies the need for frequent retreatment and contributes
to periods of subtherapeutic VEGF suppression in some
patients. In contrast, the PDS was engineered to continu -
ously maintain drug concentrations within the therapeutic
window over extended intervals [32, 33].
In the phase 2 LADDER PK analysis, ranibizumab was
quantified	in	aqueous	humor	(AH)	using	an	ELISA	with	a
lower	limit	of	quantification	(LLOQ)	of	20,000	pg/mL	(20
ng/mL),	reflecting	the	expectation	of	relatively	high	intra-
ocular levels in PDS-treated eyes and enabling longitudinal
monitoring of release over months. Across the LADDER
dataset, AH concentrations were reported to track with
systemic	 (serum)	 profiles	 in	 a	 manner	 consistent	 with	 the
implant	 providing	 continuous	 release	 (“flip-flop”	 behavior
dominated by ocular release), supporting the concept of
durable	exposure	after	implantation	and	through	refill	inter-
vals. After a single 0.5 mg intravitreal ranibizumab injec -
tion, human AH concentrations peak early and then decline
approximately mono-exponentially. In a prospective human
series (nonvitrectomized eyes) AH ranibizumab peaked on
day 1 with a measured range of 36.9–66.1 µg/mL, estimated
cmax ~ 56.1 µg/mL, and an AH half-life of ~ 7.19 days.
By using the half-life as a simple comparator, a concen -
tration	 on	 the	 order	 of	 the	 PDS	 assay	 floor	 (≥	20	 ng/mL)
corresponds to a much later post-bolus time point than
typical monthly retreatment. Under mono-exponential
decay from 56.1 µg/mL, AH levels would fall to ~ 20 ng/
mL only after roughly ~ 80 days (order-of-magnitude esti -
mate).	 This	 illustrates	 the	 qualitative	 difference	 for	 which
bolus dosing produces very high early peaks with progres -
sive	decline,	whereas	PDS	aims	for	flatter	exposure	(lower
peak-to-trough variability) over long intervals. As reported
by	Wykoff	et	 al.	 serum	 ranibizumab	 concentrations	 in	 the
100 mg/mL PDS cohort remained between the expected
peak and trough concentrations of monthly 0.5-mg injec -
tions through at least nine months [ 34]. Although systemic
exposure to ranibizumab after intravitreal injection is mini-
mal, the PDS provides continuous intraocular release that
results	in	low	but	sustained	serum	concentrations;	however,
these remain within the exposure range observed with con -
ventional monthly dosing rather than representing increased
systemic exposure.
Whether vitrectomy accelerates anti-VEGF clearance
is clinically relevant because sustained systems depend on
predictable intraocular residence time. Preclinical work in
macaques reported shorter AH half-lives in vitrectomized
vs. nonvitrectomized eyes (ranibizumab AH half-life ~ 1.4
vs. 2.3 days). However, other experimental data (also in
non-human models) suggest that overall intraocular con -
centration–time	profiles	in	vitreous,	AH,	and	retina	may	not
differ	 significantly	 over	 a	 longer	 follow-up	 window	 after
vitrectomy,	highlighting	 that	 the	 vitrectomy	 effect	 can	 be
model- and compartment-dependent (Table 1).
For clarity, the most direct evidence suggesting faster
post-vitrectomy anti-VEGF clearance derives from non-
human primate models, whereas human aqueous sampling
after ranibizumab bolus injection provides estimates for
nonvitrectomized eyes. Therefore, for vitrectomized nAMD
patients being considered for PDS implantation, current
evidence should be interpreted as mechanistic rather than
definitive	 clinical	 proof	 of	 altered	 PDS	 performance.	 In
practice, vitrectomy status may justify closer early anatomic
monitoring, but it is not by itself evidence that the implant
cannot maintain therapeutic exposure.

Molecular agents suitable for port delivery
Ranibizumab was selected as the lead molecule based on a
convergence of favorable molecular and formulation char -
acteristics. It is a 48-kDa Fab fragment with high aqueous
solubility, rapid systemic clearance, and a well-established
intravitreal	 safety	 profile.	 Importantly,	ranibizumab	 in	 the
PDS demonstrated stability at concentrations an order
of magnitude higher than those used in bolus injections,
enabling formulation at 100 mg/mL for long-term intraocu-
lar residence.
The nonclinical toxicology and biocompatibility pro -
gram described by Bantseev et al. demonstrated that contin-
uous intraocular delivery of ranibizumab from the PDS did
not introduce toxicologic liabilities beyond those observed
with intravitreal injection [ 22, 35]. Chronic implantation
studies showed no new ocular toxicity, and comprehensive
ISO-guided	testing	confirmed	that	the	implant	materials	and
ancillary devices were non-cytotoxic, non-genotoxic, non-
sensitizing, and non-irritating, supporting long-term molec-
ular compatibility with the ocular environment.
At the drug-delivery interface, Yohe et al. showed that
ranibizumab exhibits concentration-dependent, predict -
able	 passive	 diffusion	 across	 the	 porous	 titanium	 release-
control element [33]. In vitro characterization demonstrated
sustained release over at least 6–9 months, with preserved
VEGF-binding activity and reproducible kinetics across
refill	 cycles.	 Notably,	 over	 70%	 of	 the	 loaded	 drug	 was
released by 6 months, with approximately 90% of released
ranibizumab	remaining	biologically	active,	confirming	both
diffusional	 suitability	 and	 molecular	 integrity	 during	 con-
tinuous delivery.
Beyond intrinsic molecular properties, successful PDS
integration required the co-development of a customized
formulation capable of maintaining potency, purity, and
functional activity within the implant environment. Hieb
et al. systematically evaluated ranibizumab stability under
simulated long-term delivery conditions and across repeated
refill-exchange	cycles	[32]. Over 12 months of in-vitro con-
tinuous delivery, the dominant degradants were acidic vari-
ants, while overall VEGF-binding potency remained high.
Importantly, the absolute mass of degraded species released
daily	was	low	due	to	the	controlled,	diffusion-limited	release
mechanism.
While the PDS is conceptually extensible, trials under -
score that it is not a drug-agnostic container. Release
kinetics, membrane architecture, formulation composi -
tion, and stability behavior are tightly coupled to molecu -
lar size, charge, and structural complexity. As shown by
Yohe et al. even within a single molecule, release behavior
varies predictably with starting concentration indicating
that larger or structurally complex biologics would likely
require redesign of the release-control element [ 33]. Simi-
larly, the stability work of Hieb et al. demonstrates that
months-long implant residence imposes physicochemical
constraints exceeding those of conventional intravitreal
formulations [ 32]. Molecules with lower intrinsic sta -
bility, greater aggregation propensity, or more complex
multimeric architecture may not tolerate this environment
without substantial formulation and device re-engineer -
ing. From a translational standpoint, the pre-clinical work
reported by Bantseev et al. further indicates that each new
molecular payload would require a comprehensive reas -
sessment of biocompatibility, toxicology, and long-term
ocular interaction [ 35].
Even	 though	 the	 approved	 clinical	 configuration	 is
ranibizumab in a proprietary system, the available engi -
neering and PK literature implies several minimum require-
ments for any molecule to be plausibly usable in the PDS
hardware: the system depends on a large concentration gra-
dient	to	sustain	diffusive	flux.	That	generally	means	the	drug
must tolerate very high concentration without excessive vis-
cosity	 that	 would	 prevent	 reliable	 fill/refill	 through	 small-
bore needles or create handling issues. While not always
spelled out as “clogging,” any implant that controls release
via a membrane/porous element will be sensitive to par -
ticulate formation, aggregation, or precipitation, which can
alter permeability, change release kinetics, or raise safety
risks.	Larger	proteins	typically	diffuse	more	slowly	in	ocu-
lar	compartments	than	smaller	fragments;	this	affects	both

Real-world evidence
After its initial FDA approval in 2021 for nAMD, Susvimo
was adopted by retina specialists as a sustained-delivery
alternative to frequent intravitreal injections. Post-approval
observational experience including post-marketing surveil -
lance studies mandated by regulators has aimed to quantify
real-world	outcomes	outside	the	confines	of	controlled	clin-
ical trials. In October 2022, Genentech issued a voluntary
recall	of	the	Susvimo	ocular	implant	and	its	initial	fill/inser-
tion tool assembly due to reports of septum dislodgement in
some devices, which raised concerns about potential drug
leakage and device integrity. The recall temporarily paused
new	implantations,	although	ongoing	refill-exchange	proce-
dures continued for patients already implanted. Importantly,
the	recall	did	not	include	the	drug	vial	for	refill	solution	or
the	refill	needles	required	for	continuing	treatment	in	exist-
ing patients, ensuring continuity of care. Following targeted
manufacturing process improvements and component-level
design	modifications	intended	to	address	the	septum	issue,
the voluntary recall was lifted in 2024, and Susvimo was
reintroduced	to	the	market	with	updated	implant	and	refill
hardware. Furthermore, retrospective real-world reports
suggests	that	refinements	in	surgical	technique	like	careful
handling of the conjunctiva and Tenon’s capsule to avoid
tension and microtrauma appear to have reduced certain
adverse events such as vitreous hemorrhage and conjunc -
tival erosion when compared with earlier experience [ 43].
While formal post-marketing studies are ongoing, these
practice-based adaptations underline the importance of
clinician experience and technique in optimizing PDS out -
comes [44–46].
Surgical techniques and perioperative
management
Implantation procedure
Successful use of the PDS depends not only on device
design but also on surgical technique and periopera -
tive management. Because the system is a permanently
implanted,	trans-scleral	ocular	device,	implantation	differs
fundamentally from intravitreal injection and introduces
a distinct surgical learning curve. Clinical trials and post-
approval experience have shown that standardized tech -
nique, careful tissue handling, and surgeon experience are
central to minimizing complications and optimizing long-
term implant performance. PDS implantation is performed
in an operating room setting using a pars plana approach.
Although minor variations exist, the procedure is gener -
ally standardized. Implantation is performed in the supe -
rotemporal quadrant, approximately 4 mm posterior to the
limbus, after placement of an inferotemporal infusion line
and a superotemporal corneal traction suture. A 6 × 6 mm
peritomy is created with careful conjunctival and Tenon’s
capsule handling, followed by generous posterior and lat -
eral sub-Tenon dissection to create tissue laxity. A precise
3.5-mm scleral incision is fashioned parallel to the limbus,
and the exposed pars plana undergoes laser ablation to miti-
gate the risk of vitreous hemorrhage before the implant is
inserted	perpendicular	to	the	globe	and	seated	flush	against
the sclera. Complete closure of both Tenon’s capsule and
conjunctiva with scleral anchoring and mild anterior tissue
overhang at the limbus is emphasized as a critical determi -
nant of long-term conjunctival integrity [47].
Accumulated clinical trial experience has highlighted
conjunctival and Tenon’s capsule management as the central
technical	 challenge	 of	 PDS	 surgery.	Graff	 and	 colleagues
detailed that preoperative assessment of conjunctival integ-
rity is essential, particularly in patients with prior conjuncti-
val	surgery,	chronic	topical	medication	use,	or	inflammatory
ocular surface disease [48].
Intraoperatively, the authors emphasize gentle, pur -
poseful tissue handling with nontoothed forceps, com -
plete sub-Tenon dissection to eliminate residual tension,
and meticulous closure that captures both conjunctiva and
Tenon’s capsule with scleral anchoring sutures at the limbal
apex of the peritomy. These steps are intended to reduce the
incidence of conjunctival retraction and erosion, complica -
tions that were mechanistically linked to endophthalmitis
events in early clinical trials [ 48]. Following implantation,
noninvasive imaging of the implantation site can be used to
support postoperative surveillance.
Ericksen and colleagues demonstrated that anterior seg -
ment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) enables
high-resolution visualization of the conjunctiva, Tenon’s
capsule,	implant	flange,	and	underlying	sclera	[49]. In their
ARCHWAY cohort, AS-OCT facilitated longitudinal moni-
toring of conjunctival thickness and implant coverage, with
only minimal qualitative thinning observed over time and
no cases of erosion in the imaged eyes. The authors pro -
posed AS-OCT as a useful adjunct for early detection of
tissue compromise, particularly in patients with borderline

sure appears to be a part of the strategy to prevent some
of the PDS-related adverse events, including conjunctival
erosion, implant exposure, bleb formation, and secondary
infection. Early postoperative visits focus on careful inspec-

implant	flange	to	confirm:	(i)	complete	epithelialization,	(ii)
absence of wound leak or thinning, (iii) lack of conjunc -

elevation suggestive of incomplete closure. Patients are
typically instructed to avoid eye rubbing. Surgeons may
adopt a lower threshold for more frequent or early interven-

vitreoretinal surgery, given that even minor conjunctival
defects	can	evolve	into	clinically	significant	complications
in the presence of a permanent implant.

ocular complications. Awh and colleagues synthesized
management strategies for ocular adverse events observed
in LADDER and ARCHWAY , including conjunctival retrac-
tion, conjunctival erosion, vitreous hemorrhage, hypotony,
implant dislocation, and endophthalmitis [52]. Early recog-

were emphasized as critical to reducing infectious risk. Rec-

with limbal anchoring, and avoidance of suture placement
directly	 over	 the	 implant	 flange	 [52]. For recurrent con -

patch grafting techniques have been described. Ebert and
colleagues	 reported	 long-term	 outcomes	 using	 a	 modified
split-thickness corneal patch graft with a central aperture

performed	 without	 technical	 difficulty,	 illustrating	 that
reconstructive strategies can restore implant viability even
after	significant	conjunctival	complications	[53].
Recent	 engineering	 updates	 have	 further	 influenced
surgical and perioperative considerations. Pieramici and
colleagues	reported	structural	modifications	to	the	septum–

50%, resulting in simulated durability exceeding 50 years
of use without septum dislodgement [ 51]. These updates
were implemented following the 2022 voluntary recall and
underscore the dynamic interplay between device engineer-
ing, surgical technique, and long-term procedural safety.
Pharmacologic and practical considerations
From	 a	 pharmacologic	 perspective,	 refill-exchange	 serves
two purposes: restoring the concentration gradient that
drives	passive	diffusion	and	removing	aged	formulation	that
has resided in the implant over months. The use of a high
concentration ranibizumab formulation (100 mg/mL) means
that relatively small volumes can re-establish therapeutic
intraocular levels, but it also places constraints on viscosity,
needle	 gauge,	 and	 injection	 force.	 The	 refill	 solution	 is	 a
specialized, high-concentration biologic that must be stored,
prepared, and handled according to strict protocols to pre -
serve stability and sterility. Because the drug may remain
in	 the	 implant	 for	 many	 months,	 refill-exchange	 replaces
residual formulation to limit accumulation of degradation
products and to maintain consistent release kinetics. Each
refill-exchange	requires	precise	septum	puncture.	Mechani-
cal integrity of the septum over repeated cycles is critical
to prevent leakage, altered release rates, or device failure.

Safety profile and complications
The magnitude of the main device- and surgery-related
risks should be considered alongside the reduction in treat -
ment frequency. In the primary ARCHWAY analysis, ocular
adverse events of special interest occurred in 19.0% of PDS-
treated patients versus 6.0% of patients receiving monthly
ranibizumab;	endophthalmitis	occurred	in	1.6%,	conjuncti-
val erosion in 2.4%, conjunctival retraction in 2.0%, and vit-
reous haemorrhage in 5.2% of PDS-treated patients. At 96
weeks, cumulative ARCHWAY rates were approximately
1.6% for endophthalmitis, 4.0% for conjunctival erosion,
2.4% for conjunctival retraction, 6% for vitreous haemor -

the exposed pars plana and careful scleral wound construc -
tion to minimize uveal prolapse [55]. Hypotony and choroi-
dal	effusions	were	uncommon	but	clinically	relevant	early
postoperative events. These were managed with intensi -
fied	topical	steroids	and	cycloplegia,	with	surgical	revision
reserved for cases associated with wound leak or implant
malposition. Implant dislocation, though rare, required sur-
gical repositioning or explantation depending on stability
and associated tissue compromise. The authors underscored
that adherence to precise scleral incision length and perpen-
dicular insertion technique were critical to preventing this
complication.
Endophthalmitis represented the most serious adverse
outcome and was strongly associated with pre-existing
conjunctival defects. Awh et al. stressed that management
should follow established endophthalmitis protocols, includ-
ing immediate intravitreal antibiotics and early vitrectomy
when indicated [ 52]. Importantly, implant removal was
frequently required in cases associated with conjunctival
erosion	or	persistent	infection,	reflecting	the	principle	that
eradication of the nidus of infection is essential in device-
associated endophthalmitis [ 56]. This observation further
reinforced the central role of conjunctival integrity in long-
term PDS safety. Unlike intravitreal injections, where risk is
concentrated around the moment of drug delivery, implant-
based therapy introduces a chronic tissue–device interface
that remains biologically active for years. As such, the
authors proposed that PDS surveillance be conceptualized
similarly to glaucoma drainage devices or keratoprostheses,
requiring repeated evaluation of tissue health, patient educa-
tion regarding symptom recognition, and low thresholds for
surgical revision (Tables 3 and 4).

Visual and anatomical outcomes
In nAMD, the pivotal phase 3 ARCHWAY trial demonstrated
that	 the	 PDS	 with	 fixed	 refill-exchange	 every	 24	 weeks
(Q24W)	achieved	noninferior	and	equivalent	visual	acuity
outcomes compared with monthly intravitreal ranibizumab
at the primary endpoint (average BCV A change at weeks
36 and 40) [23, 25, 38, 39].	Longer	follow-up	analyses	(≈	2
years) reported sustained noninferiority with broadly com -
parable anatomic control (e.g., retinal thickness metrics)
between groups. ARCHWAY operationalizes “burden reduc-
tion” most directly through fewer treatment encounters:
PDS	Q24W	implies	two	planned	refill-exchanges	per	year,
versus ~ 12 injections/year with monthly ranibizumab. In
ARCHWAY , 98.4% of PDS-treated patients did not require
supplemental	ranibizumab	during	the	first	24-week	interval,
consistent	with	meaningful	durability	under	the	fixed	sched-
ule. Across subsequent intervals through ~ 2 years, ~ 95% of
patients similarly avoided supplemental injections in each
refill	interval,	reinforcing	sustained	reduction	in	procedure
frequency. These reductions address the widely documented
time/logistical burden of frequent anti-VEGF visits, which
is reported by patients as disruptive and anxiety-provoking
and is associated with adherence challenges in routine care.
Unlike	the	PDS,	office-based	therapies	avoid	implantation
surgery but still require repeated intravitreal injections and
ongoing monitoring. In contrast, the PDS provides continu-
ous	drug	delivery	with	scheduled	24-week	refill-exchanges
and eliminates interim injections for most patients. Its role
is therefore likely limited to patients with high treatment
burden,	 difficulty	 maintaining	 injection	 schedules,	 a	 pref-
erence for fewer procedures, and acceptable surgical risk.
Direct	comparisons	with	faricimab	or	aflibercept	8	mg	are
lacking,	 and	 differences	 in	 trial	 design	 preclude	 reliable
indirect comparisons.
Cost-effectiveness and healthcare resource
utilization
Economic implications represent a critical dimension of
implant-based therapy, particularly as the PDS shifts neo -
vascular	 AMD	 management	 from	 an	 office-based	 proce-
dural model to a surgically anchored treatment paradigm.
Using ARCHWAY clinical trial inputs and U.S. Medicare
reimbursement schedules, Al-Khersan et al. performed a
detailed cost analysis comparing PDS with monthly intra -
vitreal ranibizumab. Over the 96-week ARCHWAY horizon,
total per-patient costs were $61,988 in the facility setting
and $43,947 in the non-facility setting for PDS compared
with $56,296 and $47,928, respectively, for monthly injec -
tions. Procedural costs accounted for 34% of total PDS costs
in the facility setting, driven primarily by initial implanta -
tion surgery whereas medication costs dominated both
arms (> 65%). Adverse-event-related expenditures consti -
tuted < 1% of total modeled costs [57].
A broader payer-perspective cost analysis by Sood et al.
extended	these	findings	by	modeling	break-even	thresholds
across commonly used anti-VEGF agents. They reported
that the mean number of injections required to match the
cost	of	one	year	of	PDS	therapy	with	a	single	refill	was	10.8
for	 ranibizumab,	 9.3	 for	 aflibercept,	 and	 34.5	 for	 bevaci-
zumab [58]. One-year costs were estimated at $21,106 for
PDS	 with	 fixed	 six-month	 refills,	 compared	 with	 $22,960
for	monthly	ranibizumab,	$26,718	for	monthly	aflibercept,
and	 $4,285	 for	 monthly	 bevacizumab.	 Over	 five	 years,
PDS was projected to be more expensive than ranibizumab
or	 aflibercept	 unless	 fewer	 than	 approximately	 four	 injec-
tions	per	refill	were	required,	and	consistently	more	costly
than bevacizumab across scenarios. These analyses empha-
sized that the economic position of PDS is highly sensitive
to	 injection	 frequency,	refill	 intervals,	 and	 the	 comparator
agent selected [58].
Complementing these payer-focused models Brown et
al. performed a formal cost-utility analysis incorporating
time-tradeoff	vision	utilities,	adverse-event	disutilities,	and
long-term disease modeling [ 59]. Over a 12-year horizon
approximating mean life expectancy, cumulative quality-
adjusted	life-year	(QAL Y)	gains	were	similar	between	PDS
(1.714	QAL Ys)	and	ranibizumab	injections	(1.639	QAL Ys;
P = 0.99). Mean 12-year costs were $135,018 for PDS versus
$78,580 for injections, yielding an incremental cost-utility
ratio	of	$761,646/QAL Y ,	well	above	conventional	willing-
ness-to-pay thresholds. At one year, however, the incre -
mental	 ratio	 was	 $75,497/QAL Y ,	falling	 within	 accepted
cost-effectiveness	 ranges.	 Injection	 therapy	 maintained	 a
more	favorable	long-term	average	cost-utility	profile	unless
refill	 intervals	 could	 be	 substantially	 extended	 beyond	 six
months [ 59]. Importantly, all published PDS economic
evaluations	 are	 anchored	 primarily	 to	 monthly	 or	 fixed-
frequency	ranibizumab/aflibercept	comparators	and	do	not
evaluate contemporary treat-and-extend regimens using far-
icimab	or	aflibercept	8	mg.	If	newer	agents	achieve	stable
12- to 16-week intervals in routine care, fewer injections
and visits would reduce comparator costs and treatment bur-
den,	 potentially	 making	 the	 incremental	 cost-effectiveness
of PDS less favorable.

statements below are categorized by provenance. The cost
analyses by Al-Khersan et al., Sood et al., and Brown et
al. are formal model-based analyses using United States
payer/Medicare reimbursement assumptions rather than

Patient-centered experience: PDS vs. traditional
intravitreal injections
The clearest comparative evidence comes from a random -
ized clinical trial analysis of patient preference and treat -
ment satisfaction in nAMD patients receiving ranibizumab
via PDS vs. monthly intravitreal injections. In this study,
treatment satisfaction was high in both groups, but most
patients assigned to the PDS reported preferring the PDS
over prior intravitreal injections, supporting the idea that
reduced procedure frequency translates into meaningful
perceived	 benefit	 for	 many	 patients	 [60]. Importantly, the
clinical context for this preference is that ARCHWAY dem-
onstrated	 noninferior	 vision	 outcomes	 with	 PDS	 Q24W
compared with monthly intravitreal ranibizumab so prefer -
ence	can	be	interpreted	as	a	difference	in	experience/burden
rather	than	a	trade	for	worse	efficacy	[25, 37, 38].
Intravitreal injections require frequent, repeated vis -
its and procedures, and “burden” in real-world literature
includes time spent traveling/waiting, caregiver involve -
ment, disruption to daily activities, and emotional strain. A
time-burden (“disease burden”) analysis in nAMD found
that management imposes a substantial time burden on
patients	 and	 caregivers	 (and	 clinic	 staff).	 Patient	 survey
work similarly reports that intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy
represents a substantial burden for nAMD patients and that
longer-interval approaches are perceived as desirable. By
contrast,	in	ARCHW AY ,	PDS	Q24W	was	designed	around
twice-yearly	scheduled	refill-exchange	(rather	than	monthly
injections)	 and	 achieved	 noninferior	 efficacy,	 quantifying
a major reduction in procedure frequency versus monthly
dosing [37, 39].
General	 vision-related	 QoL	 tools	 (e.g.,	 NEI	 VFQ-25)
may	 not	 always	 show	 large	 between-group	 differences	 if
visual outcomes are similar, because they’re not designed
to be highly sensitive to visit/procedure frequency [ 59,
61].	 In	 this	 context,	 treatment-specific	 PROs	 (satisfaction,
preference, treatment convenience) are often more informa-
tive for comparing PDS vs. injections, as demonstrated by
the preference/satisfaction trial. When visual outcomes are
comparable,	broad	vision-related	QoL	instruments	may	be
relatively	 insensitive	 to	 differences	 in	 treatment	 delivery;
therefore, treatment satisfaction and preference measures
provide	 more	 direct	 insight	 into	 patient	 experience	 differ-
ences between PDS and injections.
While economic analyses provide essential systems-
level context, patient-reported outcomes address a distinct
and	equally	influential	dimension	of	therapeutic	value.	In	a
prespecified	exploratory	analysis	of	ARCHW AY ,	Chang	et
al. evaluated treatment satisfaction and patient preference
using validated instruments [ 61]. Overall Macular Disease
Treatment	 Satisfaction	 Questionnaire	 scores	 were	 high	 in
both groups at week 40 (mean 68.0 PDS vs. 66.1 injec -
tions) indicating broadly comparable satisfaction. However,
among patients assigned to PDS, 93.2% expressed prefer -
ence for PDS over prior intravitreal injections, with 73.5%
reporting a “very strong” preference. The most frequently
cited reasons were fewer treatments (80%), less discomfort
(75%), and reduced anxiety (54%). Notably, preference for
PDS persisted even among patients receiving ongoing fel -
low-eye injections, suggesting that direct experiential con -
trast	reinforced	perceived	benefit	[61].
Challenges to adoption: Workforce transition,
training, access, and monitoring
Over time delivery of intravitreal injections expanded from
tertiary retinal centers to broader outpatient networks in
many healthcare systems. In contrast, PDS implantation
inherently depends on surgical infrastructure and expertise
aligning	more	closely	with	vitreoretinal	surgical	workflows
than with routine medical retina practice. Consequently, sur-
gical retina availability and operating room capacity may
become limiting factors in the adoption of implant-based
therapy. This shift represents a meaningful departure from
the historical trajectory of anti-VEGF delivery. Whereas
injection-based care progressively decentralized, implant-
based strategies reintroduce a degree of centralization, at
least for treatment initiation. Whether existing vitreoretinal
surgical capacity can accommodate a large prevalent popu-
lation particularly in neovascular AMD remains uncertain
and will likely vary across healthcare systems.
Although	 structured	 proctoring	 and	 certification	 pro-
grams may expand the pool of trained implanters, mean -
ingful decentralization would require standardized
credentialing	 frameworks,	 defined	 procedural	 volume
thresholds and ongoing competency assessment. Given the
low tolerance for complications such as endophthalmitis,
sporadic low-volume implantation by occasional operators
may pose unacceptable safety risks. As such, early adoption
may favor center-based models of care rather than wide -
spread	diffusion	across	injection	clinics.
The current literature has primarily focused on clinical
trial	 outcomes,	safety	 characterization,	and	 device-specific
considerations, rather than adoption metrics or utilization

trends. As additional long-acting therapies that do not require
surgical implantation—such as extended-durability biolog -
ics, depot formulations, suprachoroidal approaches, and gene
therapies—advance through late-phase development or enter
clinical use, relative uptake of implant-based platforms may
be	influenced	by	comparative	invasiveness,	reversibility,	and
workflow	 demands.	These	 parallel	 innovations	underscore
the importance of situating PDS within a broader durabil -
ity landscape. Comparative assessment of sustained-deliv -
ery platforms may be facilitated by structured tabulation of
similarities	 and	 differences	 across	 technologies,	 including
delivery route, intended duration, reversibility, monitoring
requirements, and safety considerations. Such comparisons
may help clinicians contextualize PDS relative to less inva-
sive long-acting alternatives. The dependence on surgical
expertise and operating room resources raises important
equity considerations. In many regions, access to intravitreal
therapy	 itself	 remains	 constrained;	 access	 to	 vitreoretinal
surgery is often even more limited. As a result, implant-
based	sustained	delivery	may	initially	benefit	patients	in	ter-
tiary centers while having limited near-term impact in rural
or resource-constrained settings.
Potential mitigation strategies include hub-and-spoke
care models, in which implantation is centralized but follow-
up	and	refill-exchange	are	performed	at	satellite	sites.	How-
ever, this approach still requires reliable referral networks,
patient mobility, and consistent postoperative surveillance.
Thus, the global feasibility of PDS-based care will depend
not only on device performance but also on health-system
integration and workforce distribution. As treatment para -
digms shift toward extended durability, disease surveillance
becomes increasingly important. Fellow-eye conversion to
nAMD remains common over longitudinal follow-up, with
substantial incidence reported across large clinical trial
datasets. Reduced clinic visit frequency following PDS
implantation could delay detection of fellow-eye involve -
ment unless explicit surveillance strategies are incorporated.
Emerging remote monitoring technologies including
home-based OCT systems, have been proposed as adjuncts
to long-interval treatment models, enabling earlier detection
of exudation and potentially supporting both treated-eye and
fellow-eye surveillance [24]. Integration of such tools may
become increasingly relevant as durable therapies decouple
treatment delivery from traditional monthly visit schedules.
The question of bilateral PDS implantation remains
largely unanswered. Given the procedural and infectious
risk	 profile,	 bilateral	 strategies	 will	 require	 careful	 study
before they can be considered generalizable. From an
engineering perspective, future port-based platforms will
likely need to prioritize not only pharmacokinetic durabil -
ity but also procedural deployability. Reducing conjunc -
tival manipulation, simplifying insertion mechanics, and
standardizing wound construction could lower technical
barriers and broaden the pool of capable implanters. These
design	goals	may	influence	both	safety	outcomes	and	scal-
ability. The concept of combining implant placement with
other high-volume ophthalmic surgeries, such as cataract
extraction, is conceptually appealing and parallels the evo -
lution of minimally invasive glaucoma surgery. However,
current PDS implantation remains fundamentally distinct
from	anterior-segment	workflows	and	introduces	posterior-
segment–specific	 risks	 that	 are	 not	 readily	 accommodated
within routine cataract pathways.
Competing long-acting technologies
SALWEEN provides a useful contemporary comparator
because it evaluates injectable faricimab in PCV , a chal -
lenging AMD subtype common in Asian populations [ 62].
Although SALWEEN was a single-arm PCV study and is
therefore not directly comparable with ARCHWAY or POR-
TAL, it shows that treat-and-extend injectable therapy can
approach implant-like durability in selected eyes. The key
clinical	 trade-off	 is	 that	 faricimab	 avoids	 implant	 surgery
and	 device-specific	 risks	 but	 still	 requires	 ongoing	 injec-
tions and individualized interval adjustment, whereas PDS
offers	more	uniform	fixed	24-week	refill-exchange	in	most
treated nAMD eyes [62].
Several sustained-delivery strategies are emerging in par-
allel	with	refillable	implant	platforms	and	provide	important
context for evaluating the future role of port-based systems
[63]. Biodegradable intravitreal depots such as sunitinib-
based microparticle formulations aim to provide multi-
month VEGF pathway suppression after a single injection
by gradually releasing drug as the polymer matrix degrades.
Although these systems avoid permanent implants and can
be	delivered	in	an	office	setting,	durability	remains	limited
relative	to	gene-based	or	refillable	approaches,	and	inflam-
matory	 or	 particle-related	 adverse	 effects	 remain	 areas	 of
active investigation [ 26].	 Parallel	 efforts	 to	 reduce	 treat-
ment burden include sustained intravitreal depots deliver -
ing tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as axitinib and vorolanib,
designed to maintain prolonged inhibition of VEGF recep -
tor signaling after a single administration [64, 65]. Encapsu-
lated	cell	technologies	represent	a	different	paradigm,	using
implanted cellular “biofactories” to secrete therapeutic pro-
teins over extended periods [ 22, 66–70] (Table 5). While
most experience has been in neuroprotective indications,
the concept illustrates how sustained intraocular protein
production may eventually compete with device-based drug
depots. Suprachoroidal delivery approaches seek to achieve
prolonged posterior segment exposure through targeted
placement of drugs or vectors into the suprachoroidal space,
potentially combining durability with minimally invasive

administration. Finally, gene therapy programs encoding
anti-VEGF or related biologics aim to provide multi-year
expression after a single procedure and represent perhaps
the most transformative conceptual comparator. Together,
these approaches highlight a rapidly evolving landscape
in which sustained delivery may be achieved through
implants, depots, cellular systems, or gene-based strategies,
each	with	distinct	trade-offs	in	durability,	invasiveness,	and
controllability.

Clinical implementation and patient
selection
Successful clinical adoption of implant-based sustained
delivery	will	depend	not	only	on	efficacy	and	safety,	but	also
on appropriate patient selection and integration into exist -
ing care pathways. Shared decision-making is essential, as
the	 trade-off	 between	 surgical	 implantation	 and	 reduced
injection	 frequency	 varies	 widely	 among	 patients;	 some
prioritize fewer clinic visits, while others prefer to avoid
surgery even if injection burden remains high. Implementa-
tion	also	requires	workflow	adjustments	within	retina	prac-
tices, including coordination between surgical and medical
retina	services,	training	in	implantation	and	refill-exchange
procedures, and standardized postoperative monitoring.
Psychological and practical factors including tolerance for
surgery, access to specialized centers, and socioeconomic
barriers	 will	 influence	 real-world	 uptake	 and	 equity	 of
access	 across	 different	 healthcare	 systems.	 As	 with	 other
implantable ophthalmic devices, long-term surveillance and
patient education regarding symptoms of complications are
critical components of care. Future studies should elucidate
OCT-based biomarkers to better characterize retinal struc -
tural changes and treatment response in patients receiving
therapy via the port delivery system [72–77].
Regulatory and market considerations
The translation of sustained-delivery implants into clinical
practice is shaped by regulatory pathways and real-world
market dynamics. Re-introduction following manufactur -
ing	and	procedural	refinements	underscores	the	importance
of post-market surveillance and real-world data in shaping
labeling and clinical guidance (Table 1;	 Fig.	 1). Market
adoption will depend on reimbursement structures, pro -
cedural costs, and comparative durability relative to other
long-acting therapies. In healthcare systems where proce -
dural reimbursement is favorable and injection burden is
high,	sustained-delivery	implants	may	offer	economic	and
logistical	advantages;	in	other	settings,	cost	considerations
and access to surgical expertise may limit uptake. As com -
peting long-acting technologies including extended-interval
biologics and gene therapies enter clinical practice, the
relative	 positioning	 of	 refillable	 implants	 will	 continue	 to
evolve within a broader ecosystem of treatment-burden
reduction strategies.
Future directions
Future development of sustained intraocular delivery plat -
forms will likely be shaped by parallel advances in device
engineering, therapeutic durability, and biomarker-guided
care. Early post-approval experience has highlighted that
the	 long-term	 success	 of	 refillable	 implants	 depends	 not
only	on	pharmacologic	efficacy	but	also	on	the	durability	of
key structural components, including the septum interface,
conjunctival	 coverage,	 and	 refill-exchange	 workflow.	 At
the same time, the therapeutic landscape is shifting toward
longer durability across multiple platforms. Advances in
biologic engineering, including higher-concentration for -
mulations and dual-pathway antibodies are extending
treatment intervals beyond traditional monthly paradigms,
while gene therapy approaches aim to provide multi-year
expression after a single administration. These develop -
ments position ultra-long-acting injectables and gene-based
strategies as important conceptual comparators for implant-
able	 systems	 and	 are	 likely	 to	 influence	 expectations	 for
refill	intervals	and	overall	treatment	burden.	In	this	context,
future	 refillable	 implant	 designs	 may	 increasingly	 seek	 to
support maintenance intervals approaching or exceeding six
to twelve months in selected patients.
Another	emerging	priority	is	the	personalization	of	refill
timing. Rather than applying a uniform schedule, future
care models may incorporate individual disease activity
patterns, prior treatment burden, and imaging biomarkers to
determine	optimal	refill	intervals.	Integration	of	multimodal
biomarkers including structural OCT, OCT angiography,
and potentially home-based OCT monitoring may further
enable earlier detection of subclinical recurrence and sup -
port individualized treatment strategies.
Conclusion
While implant-based VEGF suppression demonstrates that
sustained	intraocular	delivery	can	be	effective	and	durable,
it also introduces new categories of risk related to chronic
tissue–device interaction and conjunctival integrity that are
largely absent from conventional injection therapy. Looking

ahead,	the	role	of	refillable	implants	will	likely	depend	on
continued reduction of device-related complications, exten-
sion	 of	 refill	 intervals	 where	 appropriate,	 and	 integration
with biomarker-driven monitoring strategies. Clinical tri -
als	 and	 early	 real-world	 experience	 confirm	 that	 continu-
ous anti-VEGF delivery can maintain visual and anatomic
outcomes comparable to monthly injections while reducing
visit frequency. However, implant-based therapy introduces
distinct surgical and device-related risks that require care -
ful patient selection, meticulous technique, and long-term
surveillance. Ultimately, sustained-delivery platforms such
as the PDS are likely to remain an important component
of	 a	 diversified	 treatment	 ecosystem	 aimed	 at	 balancing
