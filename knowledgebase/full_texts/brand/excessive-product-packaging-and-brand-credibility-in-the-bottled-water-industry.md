Abstract: This study inv estigates the influence of excessive product packaging (EPP) on consumers'
perceptions of greenwashing, green confusion, and green skepticism, and their implications for green brand
equity (GBEQ), examining brand credibility as a moderator in the Indonesia n bottled water market. A
quantitative, cross -sectional causal-comparative survey was conducted, with data collected via an online
questionnaire distributed to Indonesian consumers who recently purchased AQUA or Le Minerale, using
purposive sampling. Data were analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS -
SEM) in SmartPLS 4, with multigroup analysis assessing differences between the two brands. Results show
EPP significantly and positively affects greenwashing, green confusion, and green skepticism. Only green
skepticism significantly and negatively influences GBEQ, functioning as the sole mediator linking EPP to
GBEQ. Contrary to expectations, higher brand credibility strengthens, rather than mitigates, the adverse
effect of green confusion on GBEQ. Structural relationships did not differ significantly between AQUA and
Le Minerale.
Keywords: Excessive Product Packaging; Greenwashing; Green Skepticism; Green Brand Equity; Brand
Credibility.

(Ipsos, 2025) . Consumers are willing to spend an average of 9.7 00 per cent more for

projected to reach $2.200 trillion by 2030 (Marine Biodiversity Science Center, 2024). In
Indonesia, 42 per cent of consumers consider eco-friendly packaging a key criterion in
food product selection, the highest proportion in Asia (Fleck, 2025). These shifts have

heightened sustainability expectations have also amplified the risks of greenwashing: the
practice of overstating or misrepresenting environmental benefits (Sneideriene &
Legenzova, 2025);(Sneideriene & Legenzova, 2025) . When a gap between green claims
and actual corporate practices  is detected b y consumers, trust erodes, and green brand
equity (GBEQ) is found to suffer (Keilmann & Koch, 2026; Rahman et al., 2025).
The Indonesian bottled water industry offers a c ompelling context for examining
these dynamics. The sector comprises 7,780 products from 1,032 companies (Badan POM,
2020) and is dominated by two contrasting players. AQUA, founded in 1973, carries strong
heritage credibility as Indonesia's pioneer bottled water brand, while Le Minerale,
launched in 2015, has rapidly captured 31  per cent preference among Generation Z
consumers, surpassing AQUA's 24  per cent (Rajagukguk, 2025) . Both brands actively
promote sustainability initiatives. AQUA's #BijakBerplastik campaign uses 100  per cent
recycled plastic bottles (SehatAQUA, 2026), and Le Minerale's #JadiBaruLagi program is
claimed to collect 859 tonnes of plastic per month, with 101 per cent growth (Le Minerale,
2023). Yet both face serious credibility challenges.
The 2024 Sungai Watch Brand Audit identified Danone (AQUA's parent) as the
largest plastic polluter with 39,480 items, and Mayora Group (Le Minerale's parent)  was
ranked fifth with 30,209 items (Ayuningtyas, 2025). AQUA faced public scrutiny after a
viral video questioned its claims about its mountain water sources (Mardianti, 2025), while
Le Minerale's single -use gallon packaging drew a 50,000 -signature petition citing
contradictions with its waste -reduction me ssaging (Elhan & Helfia, 2020;
kumparanBISNIS, 2021). The contrast between these two brands, one built on heritage
and trust, and the other on rapid market disruption, makes them a n ideal pair for
comparative analysis.
A less -examined form of greenwashing is excessive product packaging (EPP)
(Pizzetti et al., 2021; Santos et al., 2024) , in which unnecessary or disproportionate
packaging is used in contradiction to a brand's environmental claims (Qayyum et al., 2023).
EPP represents a tangible, observable signal, making it particularly potent in shaping
perceptions of greenwashing. These perceptions, in turn, are theorized to generate two
distinct cognitive responses. Green skepticism, defined as doubt regarding the credibility
of environmental claims  (Sivapalan et al., 2024) , and green confusion, characterized by
uncertainty arising from inconsistent environmental information (Nguyen & Duong,
2025). Both responses are theorized to diminish GBEQ, where by brand value is derived
from perceived environmental commitment (Qayyum et al., 2023) . Brand credibility,
defined as consumer trust in a brand's reliability and expertise (Guo & Luo, 2023) , is
proposed as a moderating variable that may either buffer or amplify these negative effects,
depending on the consistency between a brand's environmental promises and its actual
packaging practices.
Despite growing scholarly attention to greenwashing, several gaps remain. First,
existing research predominantly examines greenwashing through the lens of marketing
communications (Persakis et al., 2025; Sneideriene & Legenzova, 2025), while physical
manifestations such as EPP remain underexplored (Qayyum et al., 2023) . Second, the

simultaneous psychological mechanisms of green skepticism and green confusion as
mediators between EPP and GBEQ have not been adequately examined (Qayyum et al.,
2023). Third, the moderating role of brand credibility in these relationships, particularly in
the context of physical greenwashing, has not been comprehensively investigated

international green marketing literature (Khan et al., 2022b).
These gaps are addressed by examining how EPP drives perceptio ns of
greenwashing, green confusion, and green skepticism, and how these factors subsequently
affect GBEQ, with brand credibility as a moderating variable. This study is considered
novel for three interrelated reasons. First, whereas prior research has lar gely treated
greenwashing as a communicative act embedded in advertising and marketing claims
(Persakis et al., 2025; Sneideriene & Legenzova, 2025) , EPP is examined here as a
tangible, physically observable signal that consumers encounter dir ectly at the point of
consumption; signaling theory suggests that such physical cues carry stronger diagnostic
value than verbal claims because they are harder to falsify and more directly verifiable
(Qayyum et al., 2023), extending the theoretical boundary of greenwashing research into
materially embodied signals.
Second, rather than modeling brand credibility solely as a protective resource, this
study proposes and tests the possibility that credibility functions as a double -edged
moderator: attribution theory predicts that when a highly credible brand is found to violate
its own environmental promises through EPP, consumers are more likely to attribute the
violation to in tentional deception than to situational constraint, precisely because strong
expectations of reliability had been built (Guo & Luo, 2023), implying that high credibility
may amplify rather than buffer the reputational penalty once  a contradiction is detected.
Third, by simultaneously modeling green confusion and green skepticism as parallel
mediating mechanisms and comparing these relationships across a heritage brand (AQUA)
and a challenger brand (Le Minerale) within a single emerging-market industry, this study
offers a comparative structural test absent from prior single -brand or single -mediator
designs (Qayyum et al., 2023).
These contributions extend signaling theory and attribution theory by demonstrating
that contradictory environmental signals, rather than the mere absence of green claims,
inflict disproportionately greater reputational damage, particularly for brands whose
credibility had previously been strongest. The objectives of this study are: first, to examine
the effect of EPP on greenwashing perceptions, green confusion, and green skepticism;
second, to analyze the effects of these psychological responses on GBEQ; third, to
investigate the moderating role of brand credibility; and fourth, to compare these structural
relationships across AQUA and Le Minerale through multigroup analysis.

THEORETICAL REVIEW

Signaling Theory and Attribution Theory . Two theoretical frameworks underpin
this study. Signaling theory, originally developed by Spence (1973) , posits that in
conditions of information asymmetry, observable actions serve as signals that allow
recipients to make inferences about the sender ’s underlying qualities or intentions. In the
context of green marketing, product packaging functions a tangible signal of a brand’s
environmental commitment. When packaging practices are inconsistent with stated green

claims, as in the case of EPP, these signals are interpreted as contradictory and
untrustworthy, thereby triggering negative consumer evaluations  (Guo & Luo, 2023;
Montecchi et al., 2024). Attribution theory, advanced by Kelley (1973), complements this
by explaining how consumers assign causality to corporate behavior. When an
inconsistency between green claims and actual pract ices is detected, consumers tend to
attribute the discrepancy to deliberate deception rather than unintentional error, thereby
intensifying skepticism and reducing brand equity. These theories explain the mechanism
through which excessive product packaging  (EPP) shapes consumers' perceptions of
greenwashing, green confusion, and green skepticism, as well as the subsequent influence
of these psychological responses on green brand equity (GBEQ).
Excessive Product Packaging. Excessive product packaging (EPP) i s identified as
a packaging practices that exceed functional requirements in terms of weight, dimensions,
cost, number of layers, and irrelevant decorative elements (Lu et al., 2020; Qayyum et al.,
2023). EPP is perceived as an indicator of corporate indifference toward sustainability
(Atta-Delgado et al., 2024)  and has been shown to create dissonance that intensifies
perceptions of greenwashing (Pizzetti et al., 2021; Qayyum et al., 2023)  triggers product
confusion (Raza et al., 2025) , reinforces skepticism about a brand's sustainability
commitments, and degrades GBEQ (Santos et al., 2024).
Product packaging serves as both a functional and symbolic signal that shapes
consumer perceptions and evaluations (Das et al., 2023; Granato et al., 2022; Wang, 2023).
Based on signaling theory, inconsistent signals tend to be perceived as untrustworthy or
manipulative by signal recipients  (Spence, 1973) , thereby triggering suspicion of
greenwashing. When environmental commitments are claimed by companies while EPP is
simultaneously practiced, an incongruity between communications and actual practices is
created. Empirical research confirms that the discrepancy between environmental claims
and packagin g practices is positively associated with perceptions of greenwashing and
triggers negative consumer evaluations  (Lu et al., 2020; Santos et al., 2024; Szabo &
Webster, 2021).

Greenwashing. Greenwashing is defined as the practice of making misleading
environmental claims to build an eco -friendly image without substantive sustainability
commitments. Conceptually, greenwashing is ESG communication that highlights positive
information while concealing poor environmental performance, thereby misleading all
stakeholders (Lee & Raschke, 2023).  Detrimental effects are produced at multiple levels:
for companies, reputational and l egal risks are carried, including consumer boycotts and
market value decline (Lee & Raschke, 2023; Sneideriene & Legenzova, 2025) ; for
stakeholders, it is seen as a symbolic strategy to gain legitimacy dishonestly (Talpur et al.,
2024); and at the societal level, information asymmetry is created, reducing social welfare
(Persakis et al., 2025).
Greenwashing perceptions of unreliability are generated that damage brand
reputation (Keilmann & Koch, 2026), trigger consumer confusion, erode trust, and reduce

purchase intent (Rahman et al., 2025; Torelli et al., 2020) . Contextually, greenwashing is
driven by weak regulatory enforcement (Liu et al., 2025) , market competition pressures
(Xu et al., 2025) , and the exploi tation of increased consumer awareness through
exaggerated claims (Persakis et al., 2025) . When a gap between “green talk” and “green
walk” is perceived by the consumer, the credibility of a brand’s environmental claims is
weakened, thereby reducing GBEQ (Keilmann & Koch, 2026; Pizzetti et al., 2021).

Green Confusion. Green consumer confusion is defined as a state of confusion that
arises when consumers find it difficult to assess environmental claims due to ambiguous,
exaggerated, or unrelia ble information (Persakis et al., 2025; Tarabieh, 2021) , which is
fundamentally an individual's failure to accurately understand the environmental attributes
of a product, thereby hindering decision -making that is consistent with sustainability
preferences (Keilmann & Koch, 2026). A decline in consumer trust and satisfaction is
produced by this condition, while avoidance behaviour is intensified, including reduced
purchase intent, brand avoidance, and even boycott intentions (Keilmann & Koch, 2026;
Pham & Huynh, 2025; Tarabieh, 2021).
Ambiguous and inconsistent environmental communication, including vague claims,
contradictory advertising, and excessive eco -labels, has been shown to trigger green
confusion (de Freitas Netto et al., 2020; Nguyen & Duong, 2025; Persakis et al., 2025;
Tarabieh, 2021). EPP is a signal that contradicts green claims , exacerbates information
inconsistency and increases consumers’ inability to accurately assess companies’
sustainability commitments (Qayyum et al., 2023; Raza et al., 2025) . Empirically, green
confusion has been shown to negatively affect GBEQ by decreasing trust and fostering
skeptical attitudes (Keilmann & Koch, 2026), while greenwashing intensifies green risk,
undermining green trust and encouraging brand avoidance and negative word -of-mouth
(Pham & Huynh, 2025).

Green Skepticism . Green skepticism refers to the extent to which consumers
question or distrust the environmental claims communicated by companies (Khoirina et
al., 2025; Zaid et al., 2025) , which is triggered when companies make ambiguous
environmental claims are made by companies and consumers are misled about actual
environmental practices (Zaid et al., 2025), and reflects the perception that companies do
not really care about the environment but are driven by selfish interests and manipulative
actions (Sivapalan et al., 2024).
Three main factors are identified as intensify green skepticism, namely greenwashing
practices as a direct trigger (Keilmann & Koch, 2026; Santos et al., 2024) , the perception
that sustainability strategies are based on extrinsic motives such as profit or stakeholder
pressure rather than intrinsic moral values (Keilmann & Koch, 2026; Silva et al., 2020 ),
and ambiguity and lack of transparency in corporate environmental communication (Li &

of trust and brand equity, and emergence of negative consumer responses such as negative

eco-friendly claims is doubted by consumers, trust in the brand is weakened and GBEQ is
ultimately harmed (Zaid et al., 2025) . Signaling theory further supports this pathway:

Brand Credibility . Brand credibility is refers as consumers' belief that a brand
possesses expertise (technical capabilities) and trustworthiness (honesty and commitment)
to consistently fulfill its promises without manipulation or deception (S. Lee & Kim, 2020;
Montecchi et al., 2024) . Within the framework of signaling theory, brand credibility
functions as a mechanism for reducing consumer uncertainty as well as a driver for brand
equity formation (Park et al., 2022) . In the context of green marketing, building brand
credibility is identified as a significant challenge because consumers tend to be skeptical
of environmentally friendly claims, a skepticism reinforced by rampant cases of
greenwashing and the difficulty of independently verifying environmental claims (Persakis
et al., 2025; Tarabieh, 2021).
Empirically, brand credibility has been shown to act as a bridge connecting brand
innovative superiority with purchase intention, confirming that innovation without trusted
credibility is insufficient to guarantee consumer purchasing behavior (Chen et al., 2024).
Furthermore, high brand credibility is proven to strengthen the green brand image, which
ultimately increases GBEQ (Hamdani & Yuliana, 2024; Qayyum et al., 2023), while also
helping consumers assess the reliability of environmentally friendly claims, thereby
effectively reducing doubts caused by greenwashing and green confusion (Qayyum et al.,
2023). Consumers with high brand credibility perceptions tend to give the benefit of the
doubt before interpreting environmental claims as ins incere (Persakis et al., 2025) , so
brand credibility is theorized to act as a buffer that weakens the negative effects of green
skepticism and green confusion on GBEQ (Hamdani & Yuliana, 2024; Qayyum et al.,
2023).

H7a: Brand credibility moderates the effect of green skepticism on GBEQ.

H7b: Brand credibility moderates the effect of green confusion on GBEQ.

Green Brand Equity. Brand equity refers to the added value a brand provides to a
product, shaping consum er thoughts, feelings, and actions toward it  (Keller &
Swaminathan, 2020) . In the sustainability context, this concept is extended into green
brand equity (GBEQ). GBEQ is defined by Ha et al. (2022), as assets and liabilities derived
from environmental commitments, which can add to or detract from product value for
consumers. Built on green brand image, green trust, and green satisfaction, GBEQ is found
to foster consumer loyalty, reduce perceived risk, and create sustainable competitive
advantage (Ha et al., 2022) . However, GBEQ faces considerable threats. Greenwashing,
green skepticism, and green confusion are shown to reduce brand credibility and trigger
consumer doubts (Santos et al., 2024) .  Green skepticism is established as a significant
negative predictor of green brand evaluations  (Sivapalan et al., 2024) , while green
confusion is found to reduce consumers’ ability to make informed decisions, diminishing
their willingness to engage with green brands (Nguyen & Duong, 2025).

METHODS

This study adopts a quantitative research method using a causal -comparative
framework and a cross -sectional survey design. The research object comprises two
dominant brands in Indonesia's bottled water industry, namely AQUA and Le Minerale,
while the research subjects are Indonesian consumers who have purchased or consumed
either brand within the past six months. Six constructs  are examined as the variables of
this study: excessive product packaging (EPP) as the independent variable; greenwashing,
green confusion, and green skepticism as mediating variables; green brand equity (GBEQ)
as the dependent variable; and brand credibility as the moderating variable. All constructs

EPP, therefore, is not merely a passive inconsistency but rather an active trigger of

2023). This result aligns with Wang (2023) , who confirms that EPP reduces circular

(2020), who demonstrate that EPP systematically affects green brand attachment, attitude,
and image. The tangibility of EPP makes it one of the most readily detectable forms of
greenwashing (de Freitas Netto et al., 2 020). Through attribution theory, opportunistic
motives are attributed to brands whose packaging contradicts their green claims (Keilmann
& Koch, 2026; Santos et al., 2024) . This effect is particularly salient in the Indonesian
context, where Indonesia is identified as the fifth -largest contributor to marine plastic
waste globally (Aritonang, 2023) and has the highest per capita microplastic consumption
in the world (Arif, 2024), making such contradictions highly visible and readily interpreted
as greenwashing.
However, this result is not entirely uniform across the literature. Kovač et al. (2025),
examining consumer skepticism toward eco -labels sug gest that the effectiveness of
environmentally themed packaging depends on the availability of credible evidence
supporting a firm's environmental claims. Without such substantiation, visual packaging
cues alone do not readily encourage consumers to percei ve a brand as engaging in
greenwashing. This contrast implies that EPP's power as a greenwashing signal is
conditional on the surrounding claim environment: when a brand pairs excessive
packaging with strong verbal or certified green claims, as is the case  for both AQUA and
Le Minerale, the physical –verbal contradiction becomes more salient and diagnostic,
which explains why EPP registers as a strong greenwashing signal in the present, claim -
rich context but not necessarily in claim -sparse settings. For pra ctice, this indicates that
reducing greenwashing perceptions requires addressing packaging and communication
consistently, since fixing one channel while leaving the other unchanged is unlikely to
restore consumer trust.
The Effect of EPP on Green Confusio n. H2 is supported. EPP is found to exert a
significant positive effect on green confusion. This finding is grounded in information
processing theory, which posits that inconsistencies between signals trigger cognitive
dissonance and increase interpretive ambiguity (Q. Li et al., 2025) . Font et al. (2025)
confirm that sus tainable purchasing decisions are highly susceptible to information
overload, while Granato et al. (2022) show that misalignment between graphic elements,
materials, and sustainability claims on packaging creates perceptual confusion. This result
is consistent with Wang (20 23), who emphasizes that EPP simultaneously triggers
greenwashing and green confusion. Green confusion is distinguished from green
skepticism as a qualitatively different form of cognitive ambiguity, in which consumers
remain in prolonged uncertainty befo re reaching any evaluative conclusions (Qayyum et
al., 2023; Tarabieh, 2021) . Tarabieh (2021)  verifies that green confusion negatively
impacts green trust. Pham & Huynh (2025)  find that greenwashing increases green
confusion, which in turn drives brand avoidance, a finding reinforced by Raza et al. (2025),
who demonstrate that inconsistent green marketing strategies simultaneously exacerbate
confusion and skepticism. In the Indonesian context, where environmen tal regulation
remains limited and consumers rely heavily on brand image as a substitute for formal
certification (Qayyum et al., 2023), EPP-induced confusion is further intensified.

A more conditional picture emerges elsewhere in the literature. Ding et al. (2024) ,
studying packaging complexity and green trust, find that visual complexity alone does not
uniformly predict confusion or distrust. Both nature-related and unrelated complex designs
eroded trust similarly, suggesting the confusing effect of packaging depends more on
perceived manipulative intent than on packaging volume or elaborateness. Integrating
these findings with the results of the present study suggests that EPP generates confusion
specifically  because it is interpreted as a deliberate mismatch with a brand's own
environmental claims, not simply because it is visually or physically excessive; the
mechanism is inferential (a contradiction being processed), not merely perceptual (a
cluttered package being seen). The implication for practice is that packaging consistency,
not packaging minimalism alone, is the prerequisite for reducing consumer ambiguity:
brands that fail to align their physical packaging with their stated environmental
commitments risk creating confusion that undermines trust and purchase behavior, even if
the packaging itself is well-designed.
The Effect of EPP on Green Skepticism. H3 is supported. EPP is found to exert a
significant positive effect on green skepticism. This result is explained through the
persuasion knowledge model, which holds that consumers actively build knowledge about
persuasion tactics, and when physical contradictions are detected, defensive cognitive
mechanisms are automatically activated (Rahmani, 2023). Green skepticism is established
as a stable construct that systematically doubts eco-friendly claims (Sivapalan et al., 2024)
and is formed from cumulative experiences with inauthentic claims, rendering it
particularly resistant to change (Sivapalan et al., 2024) . Greenwashing perceptions are
found to increase green skepticism and reduce purchase intentions by eroding brand trust
(Khandai et al., 2025) , while skeptical consumers are shown to attribute opportunistic
motives to others (Hoyos-Vallejo et al., 2023). Consumer skepticism is further identified
as negatively impacting purchase intentions, loyalty, and word -of-mouth across cultural
and industrial contexts (Khoirina et al., 2025). The controversy over Le Minerale's single-
use bottles, which triggered tens of thousands of petition signatures (Elhan & Helfia, 2020;
kumparanBISNIS, 2021) , illustrates the collective skepticism that EPP -induced
contradictions can generate among Indonesian consumers.
By contrast, Kovač et al. (2025)  report that packaging design cues had a limited,
non-significant influence on eco -label skepticism once credible third -party claims were
present, arguing that visual packaging elements alone cannot sustain or dispel skepticism
without substantiating evidence. This divergence suggests a boundary condition : EPP is
more likely to evoke consumer skepticism when it conflicts with a brand's previously
communicated sustainability commitments, such as the public environmental campaigns
undertaken by AQUA and Le Minerale, than when consumers assess the packaging
without considering the brand's prior claims. The theoretical contribution of this finding
therefore lies in extending signaling theory to the physical domain of packaging,
demonstrating that tangible EPP cues are as potent as communicative greenwashing in
generating skepticism, provided they are read against a brand's own stated commitments.
Practically, this means firms cannot treat packaging and communication as independent
levers; skepticism is most effectively prevented by ensuring the two remain mutual ly
reinforcing.
The Effect of Greenwashing on GBEQ. H4 is not supported. Greenwashing is not
found to exert a significant direct effect on GBEQ. This finding is explained through the
halo effect, whereby strong brand reputation provides perceptual protection that mitigates

the direct impact of greenwashing, so that the relationship between greenwashing and
GBEQ operates only indirectly through the deterioration of green brand image and green
brand trust (Ha et al., 2022) . Consumer knowledge moderation also plays a role: when
environmental knowledge is low, inconsistencies in claims are less readily identifi ed (Ha
et al., 2022). In line with cognitive dissonance theory (Rivera, 2024), contradictions tend
to be rationalized by consumers in order to maintain ingrained consumption habits.
Greenwashing is found to exert its effects on brand outcomes only when the performance
gap is very large and easily detectable (Ha et al., 2022; Santos et al., 2024), and only when
consumers are actively primed to scrutinize environmental claims (Fella & Bausa, 2024).
Santos et al. (2024) confirm that the relationship between greenwashing and GBEQ is fully
mediated by gree n brand trust and green brand image, lending further support to the
indirect mechanism observed here.
This null direct effect stands in contrast to several studies that report a significant
direct link. Amer & Abo El Ezz (2023) , studying household appliance brands, find that
greenwashing exerts a significant negative direct effect on green brand equity, brand
reputation, and brand credibility. Similarly, a recent review in the household-goods sector
reports that greenwashing directly and indirectly undermines green brand equity by
lowering brand trust and loyalty (Hong et al., 2026) . This contrast is plausibly explained
by industry and brand-maturity differences: household appliances are higher-involvement,
lower-purchase-frequency categories in  which consumers scrutinize each purchase more
deliberately, whereas bottled water is a low -involvement, high -frequency category in
which established heritage brands such as AQUA benefit from strong habitual purchase
patterns that blunt the direct penalty of greenwashing. This finding contributes to the
literature by demonstrating that greenwashing alone is insufficient to directly erode GBEQ
in low-involvement, high-frequency product categories, particularly where consumers rely
on long-established brand r eputations as cognitive shortcuts. Consistent with the higher -
involvement studies above, that the indirect pathway through brand trust and image
remains the primary channel of damage across contexts. For practice, this implies that
monitoring green brand t rust, rather than greenwashing perception alone, is the more
sensitive early-warning indicator of GBEQ erosion.
The Effect of Green Confusion on GBEQ. H5 is not supported. Green confusion
is not found to exert a significant direct effect on GBEQ. This result is understood through
the fundamental distinction between confusion and skepticism: green confusion reflects
cognitive ambiguity in which consumers have not yet reached a clear evaluative judgment
(Qayyum et al., 2023; Tarabieh, 2021) . Confusion is conceptualized as a transitional
cognitive state that precedes the formation of definitive attitudes rather than as a resolved
negative evaluation, and its impact on brand equity is therefore indirect (Chauhan & Sagar,
2021). Confused consumers tend to suspend judgment rather than immediately devalue the
brand (Qayyum et al., 2023), and brands with strong historical equity provide a substitute
quality signal that leads confused consumers to revert to the most familiar choice,
effectively buffering GBEQ from direct damage (Park et al., 2022) . Unlike skepticism,
which represents a resolved negative judgment, confusion keeps consumers in a state of
suspended evaluation, making habitual brand choice more likely than active brand
devaluation (Chauhan & Sagar, 2021; Qayyum et al., 2023; Tarabieh, 2021).
This finding departs from Qayyum et al. (2023), whose experimental study of EPP,
greenwashing, and green confusion reports that green confusion exerts a significant direct
negative effect o n green brand equity. The discrepancy is plausibly attributable to

methodological and contextual differences: Qayyum et al. (2023)  employ a controlled
within-subject experiment isolating packaging manipulation from real -world brand
history, whereas the present field study captures established, real -world brands (AQUA
and Le Minerale) whose decades of accumulated equity may absorb transitional ambiguity
that a fictitious or unfamiliar brand in an experimental design cannot. GBEQ is therefore
only eroded when a definitive negative attribution is reached by consumers, a condition
that has not yet been fulfilled in the case of pure confus ion for established brands in this
study (Qayyum et al., 2023). These results extend previous research by showing that green
confusion does not inevitably reduce green bran d equity. Instead, its impact varies
according to contextual factors, particularly the degree of brand familiarity and the strength
of existing brand equity. Consequently, managers of established brands should promptly
resolve ambiguity arising from packag ing because delaying corrective action may allow
consumer confusion to evolve into skepticism.
The Effect of Green Skepticism on GBEQ . H6 is supported. Green skepticism is
found to exert a significant negative effect on GBEQ, representing the strongest and most
theoretically consistent finding in this study. Skeptical consumers are established as less
receptive to marketing claims and more critically responsive to advertising
messages(Sivapalan et al., 2024), while skepticism is shown to act as a cognitive filter that
systematically discounts green marketing information (Sivapalan et al., 2024) . Green
skepticism is found to exert a significant negative effect on green brand attachment, which
in turn directly weakens GBEQ (Khan et al., 2022a) , and is described as a resistant prior
belief that encourages active resistance to purchasing (Sivapalan et al., 2024) . Consumer
skepticism is identified as consistently negatively impacting purchase intention and loyalty
across industrial and cultural contexts (Khoirina et al., 2025) . GBEQ is built on strong,
unique, and beneficial brand associations, and skepticism erodes all three dimensions
because environmentally friendly association s become untrustworthy in the eyes of
skeptical consumers (Keller & Swaminathan, 2020). Green skepticism is further shown to
lead to brand embarrassment, brand hate, brand avoidance, and negative word -of-mouth
(Khandai et al., 2025; Sajid et al., 2024).
Not all studies find skepticism to operate this directly, however. Zhou & Seo (2026),
examining green skepticism and green purchase intention, report that green skepticism
does not exert a significant direct effect on purchase -related outcomes, instead operating
indirectly by reducing consumers' motivation to seek further green information and
weakening anticipated guilt. The difference is best explained by outcome specificity:
purchase intention is a single transactional decision that can be swayed by situational cues
(price, convenience) even under skepticism, whereas GBEQ reflects the accumulation of
multiple brand associations over time. Skepticism has a stronger impact on GBEQ because
it challenges the strength, uniqueness, and favo rability of those associations that
collectively determine brand equity. This suggests skepticism's damage is more structural
(equity) than transactional (single purchase), reinforcing why it is the most consistent
negative predictor in this study. The pra ctical implication is that green skepticism
represents the most critical risk factor in the chain of GBEQ erosion, and firms must
prioritize credible, verifiable environmental communication to prevent its formation, since
once formed it is resistant to change (Sivapalan et al., 2024).

is not found to moderate the negative impact of green skepticism on GBEQ. The result is
explained by the distinction between green trust and general brand trust, constructs that,

have already autonomously developed among consumers, rendering general brand
credibility ineffective as a buffer (Riva et al., 2024) . Moreover, in line with expectancy

standards rather than dampening scrutiny, thereby amplifying backlash when
inconsistencies occur (Lewin & Warren, 2025; Walter et al., 2024) . This null result
contrasts with the classical branding literature. Ac cording to Qayyum et al. ( 2023) ,
consumers are less likely to be negatively influenced by perceptions of greenwashing when
a bra nd possesses strong credibility, as confidence in the firm's environmental claims

adverse perceptions. The present non -finding suggests that this classical buffer ing logic
holds for lower -intensity negative perceptions (e.g., general greenwashing doubt) but
breaks down once skepticism has crystallized into a stable, resistant trait -like belief
(Sivapalan et al., 2024) ; by that stage, credibility cues are no longer processed as new,
disconfirming information but are filtered through an already -defensive lens. From a
managerial perspective, strengthening brand credibility alone may not be enough to
preserve GBEQ after consumer skepticism has already emerged. Instead, organizations
should address potential concerns at an earlier stage before skeptical perceptions become
firmly established.
H7b supported, but in the opposite direction from that hypothesized. Brand
credibility is found to amplify rather than buffer the negative impact of green confusion
on GBEQ. Though a highly credible brand creates confusion, consumers are more likely
to take it as strategic purpose, which can lead to a more negative consumer reaction than
when a low-credibility brand causes confusion (Guo & Luo, 2023; S. Lee & Kim, 2020;
Montecchi et al., 2024; Tarabieh, 2021). This finding reveals a brand credibility paradox:
credibility elevates consumer expectations to such a degree that packaging inconsistencies
are perceived as deliberate deception rather than unintentional error, intensifying the
negative impact on GBEQ. Brand credibility thus functions as a double -edged moderator
in the context of ambiguous environmental communication, thereby providing additional
insight into signaling theory and expectancy disconfirmation theory by demonstrating that
credibility can paradoxically increase vulnerability to green confusion. This amplifyi ng
role directly contrasts with (Qayyum et al., 2023) , whose experimental study finds that
brand credibility significantly moderates and reduces the negative relationship b etween
greenwashing and green brand equity, indicating that brand credibility acts as a mitigating
factor rather than strengthening the relationship. Reconciling the two studies points to a
mediator-dependent moderation pattern: credibility appears to buff er damage from
greenwashing perception (a comparatively resolved, communication -based judgment)
while amplifying damage from green confusion (an unresolved, ambiguity -based state),
because ambiguity from a trusted source is harder to dismiss as incompetenc e and more
readily read as intent. This distinction is a genuine theoretical contribution of the present
study, since it shows that brand credibility's moderating role is not fixed but depends on
which psychological mechanism, whether it involves skepticism, confusion, or perceptions
of greenwashing.. The managerial implication is that credible, heritage brands such as
AQUA cannot rely on their reputation as a general shield; where packaging inconsistency
creates ambiguity, that same credibility becomes a l iability unless the ambiguity is
proactively resolved through clear communication.

The Mediating Role of Green Perceptual Responses on EP P and GBEQ.
Mediation analysis confirms that EPP does not exert a direct effect on GBEQ but instead
operates through an  indirect pathway fully mediated by green skepticism. EPP triggers
negative attributions toward brand sincerity (Persakis et al., 2025; C. -H. Wang, 2023) ,
which crystallizes into green skepticism (Sivapalan et al., 2024), and subsequently erodes
the quality associations and loyalty that constitute GBEQ (Keilmann & Koch, 2026; Lu et
al., 2020; Tarabieh, 2021) . The failure of greenwashing and green confusion to serve as
significant mediators is attributable to the absence of their signif icant direct effect on
GBEQ: confused consumers tend to delay rather than immediately devalue the brand
(Chauhan & Sagar, 2021; Szabo & Webster, 2021) , while greenwashing exerts greater
influence on reducing trust and purchase intention than on directly eroding brand equity
(Pham & Huynh, 2025; Santos et al., 2024). Green skepticism is therefore identified as the
most critical link in the chain of eco-friendly brand value decline (Ha et al., 2022; Persakis
et al., 2025). This finding contributes to the literature by establishing green skepticism as
the exclusive mediating pathway through which physical g reenwashing, operationalized
as EPP, influences GBEQ through one dominant mediating mechanism, rather than the
multiple indirect pathways identified in several previous studies (e.g., Qayyum et al., 2023,
where both greenwashing and confusion carried partial mediating weight), again pointing
to brand maturity as a plausible boundary condition worth testing in future research.
Cross-Brand Invariance. Multigroup analysis reveals no significant differences in
path coefficients between Le Minerale and AQUA consumers, confirming that the GBEQ
formation mechanism operates consistently across contrasting brand positions and heritage
profiles. This finding validates the universality of the theoretical framework within the
bottled water category and suggests that the identified pathways from EPP to GBEQ are
not brand-specific but reflect broader consumer psychological processes. However, partial
measurement invariance is identified, and significant differences in variance are observed
for green confusion, green skepticism, and greenwashing across the two brand groups, with
AQUA consumers exhibiting greater heterogeneity than Le Minerale consumers . This
pattern likely reflects AQUA's broader and more diverse consumer base, built over more
than five decades of market presence. In contrast, Le Minerale's more focused
environmental positioning is inferred to attract a more homogeneous sustainability -
oriented consumer segment. These findings carry implications for brand management:
even as structural relationships remain consistent, the greater within-group variance among
AQUA consumers suggests that AQUA faces a wider range of consumer perceptions to
manage, necessitating more differentiated green communication strategies than those
required by Le Minerale.

CONCLUSION

EPP is established as a dominant antecedent of greenwashing, green confusion, and
green skepticism, among Indonesian bottled water consumers, with all three hypothesized
direct effects confirmed. Among the three perceptual responses, only green skepticism is
found to significantly and negatively affect GBEQ, serving as the exclusive mediating
pathway through which EPP erodes brand equity.  Greenwashing and green confusion are
not found to directly affect GBEQ, indicating that consumer consequences are most
pronounced only when perceived deception is interpreted as intentional, the condition
represented by skepticism rather than ambiguity. A brand credibility paradox is further

identified, that, rather than buffering the negative impact of green confusion on GBEQ,
brand credibility amplifies it, as elevated expectations toward credible brands render any
packaging inconsistency more salient a nd more damaging. Structural relationships are
found to be consistent across AQUA and Le Minerale consumer groups, confirming the
universality of the theoretical framework within the bottled water category, despite partial
measurement differences observed between the two groups.
 Three theoretical contributions are offered by this study. First, signaling theory is
extended by demonstrating that contradictory environmental signals inflict greater
reputational damage than the complete absence of green claims,  thereby refining our
understanding of how signal inconsistency, rather than signal absence, drives brand equity
erosion. Second, attribution theory is advanced in the green marketing domain by
confirming that only skepticism, as a resolved and explicitly negative attribution of intent,
directly erodes GBEQ, while confusion, as an unresolved cognitive state, does not. Third,
the brand credibility literature is enriched through the identification of the credibility
paradox, in which high brand credibility am plifies vulnerability to green confusion by
elevating consumer expectations to a level where any inconsistency is interpreted as
deliberate rather than accidental. These contributions collectively demonstrate that the
mechanisms through which physical greenwashing damages green brand equity are more
nuanced and contingent than previously assumed.
 Managerially, the primacy of packaging alignment over communication volume is
underscored by these findings. Packaging contradictions are identified as the domina nt
driver of greenwashing, and communication strategies that add more environmental
information without first resolving physical inconsistencies are found to be insufficient
and potentially counterproductive. Anti -skepticism strategies centered on concrete ,
verifiable evidence and independent third -party certification are therefore recommended
over generic green messaging. Highly credible brands, particularly those with long
heritage, such as AQUA that are advised to implement stricter consistency standards
between their environmental claims and packaging practices, given that their credibility
amplifies rather than mitigates the consequences of any perceived contradiction. The
structural uniformity observed between AQUA and Le Minerale further suggests that  a
standardized green marketing framework may be applied at the product category level,
offering industry-wide guidance rather than brand-specific solutions.
 Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment and inform directions for
future research. The cross-sectional survey design precludes causal inference and does not
permit the tracking of perceptual evolution over time. Self -reported measures are
susceptible to social desirability bias, which may inflate pro -environmental response
tendencies. The focus on two brands within a single product category and cultural contexts.
Psychometrically, elevated HTMT values among green skepticism, green confusion, and
greenwashing suggest potential discriminant validity concerns, and the AVE value for
brand credibility falls below the conventional threshold, indicating a need for measurement
refinement in future work. Future research is therefore recommended to adopt
experimental designs with packaging -level manipulation to establish more definitive
causal in ferences. The inclusion of additional constructs, such as green trust, CSR
reputation, and consumer environmental knowledge, is recommended to increase
explanatory power and clarify boundary conditions. Cross -cultural and cross -category
replications are wa rranted to assess the generalizability of the framework. Longitudinal
designs are encouraged to track the temporal conversion of greenwashing into crystallized
