Abstract
A mixed method research approach has been applied in this study as explained. Data was collected through a structured
and self-administered questionnaire from a total of 295 respondents, including interviews granted to respondents, who are
majorly shoppers of brands of apparel, cosmetics and jewelry items randomly selected in Roma and its neighbourhood.
The present study contributes to the existing literature from a strategic point of view and attempts to fill existing gaps.
First, this paper provides insights on the drivers of brand preference and intention to visit, buy, or make a purchase and
repeat based on shoppers’ experiences, building on brand communication as a strategic tool.
The regression based on the least squares approach and inferential statistical methods were used to evaluate and analyze
the measurement, novel model, and hypotheses based on one of the methods of multiple regression. The findings show
that brand communication can be strategically applied and help in building a reputable brand, translate to equity, and
build preferences from the cultural lifestyles moderated by the tool of CSR, as the constructs and variables show
statistical significance at the 5% level as presented in the table of results.
Finally, a novel model was arrived at to delineate brand communication effects. In fact, brand communication is a
significant tool that can facilitate the process of building a reputable brand, enhancing consumer preferences based on
cultural lifestyles, and translating to equity, thereby leading to growth and sustainability.

Keywords:  Brand communication, preferences, brand reputation & equity, culture and lifestyles, CSR.

1.0 Introduction
Brand communication is conceived and conceptualized based on the ideology, tenets, and fact that any product or service
can be marketed by making use of key elements and differentiators from competing brands and those of competitors. Such
elements include “product quality (PQ), premium, value and quality delivered,” core messages transmitted, information
disseminated or communicated and delivered along with core messages, brand engagement, innovations with initiatives
leading to sustainability and socially responsible roles played.

PQ refers to the entirety of a product’s attributes, from marketing to design, manufacturing, and maintenance, ensuring that
said product meets customer demands (Hien et al., 2024; Islam et al., 2021). It is defined as “the sum of characteristics
indicating the ability of products to satisfy explicit and implicit expectations,” highlighting the utility and practicality of
products (Y. Kim et al., 2023). Product quality is considered a fundamental manifestation of CSR in sustainability initiatives
undertaken by companies as it directly influences customer satisfaction and loyalty (Y. Kim & Ferguson, 2019). In fact,
product quality can affect and impact the brand image, which is a very crucial element of the brand mix and communication
as it represents the first impression created by the consumers which can be long lasting. The existing literature suggests that
CSR positively impacts PQ, which, in turn, has a significant effect on brand image (BI) and customer loyalty (H. H. Kim &
Han, 2020). Consumers tend and are more likely to continue purchasing and using, developing trust in the brand and
exhibiting loyalty when they are satisfied with the quality of a product,. This loyalty extends to recommending the product to
others (Lho et al., 2019). Therefore, adhering to CSR principles is essential for organizations to provide high-quality products,
as it positively influences customer loyalty (S. Lee et al., 2020). Several studies have also highlighted the favorable and
substantial effect of CSR initiatives on PQ (C. Y. Lee et al., 2017).

Brand communication is a viable and veritable tool and means of communicating, transmitting core signals and conveying the
brand message. In addition, brand communication can be strategically embraced and shaped in a multidimensional context to
incorporate other key elements and devices or instruments and facilitators as developed in this study by embedding brand
reputation, cultural lifestyles, and CSR to arrive at a composite mix of a holistic sense and collective sum. Brand image is
defined as the perception of a brand as reflected by the associations held in consumer memory (Keller, 1993). Maintaining
brand reliability with CSR initiatives results in a positive brand impression and affects consumers’ behavior (Do et al., 2022).
The connotations related to the brand in the minds of consumers include the consumers’ feelings and thoughts about the
product, as well as the perceptions formed by the brand’s activities in the social arena. When brands are associated with social
responsibility elements, it increases brand value. These connotations affect consumers’ product-related evaluations, brand
image, and brand loyalty (Amegbe et al., 2021; Rodrigues et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2008). Positive perceptions stay in the
consumer’s mind and lead to a positive brand image for potential consumers, intending and future buyers (Lee et al., 2010).

The aim of this study is to adopt and present brand communication as a tool in shaping and building consimer preferences.

Various tools of brand communication have been applied, consisting of the extant traditional tools of brand communication
and the basic tools of “brand awareness, reputation, advertising, and sponsorship.” While word of mouth has also been applied
as a basic communication tool of engagement and conveying the brand features, the perspective of cultural lifestyles has been
inundated in this study, considering the moderating roles of CSR while building preferences from brand reputation. In fact, it
has been pointed out and recognized that brand preference is an important means to form brand loyalty (Richard et al., 2013),
in addition to building a sustainable competitive advantage in a fierce brand competition contest. Brand preference has also
been identified as a means of promoting consumer purchase intentions, positive word-of-mouth (WOM) or engagement and
communication, and a facilitator and promoter of other positive behaviours (Boubker et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2012; Batra et
al., 2012).

CSR has widely grown and assumed a multidimensional perspective, including themes like CC (corporate citizenship) and
CSM (corporate stake management), in addition to the 4 basic tenets of “economic, ethics, philanthropic & legal” entities
highlighted by Carroll (1979, 2008, 2015) in a pyramid. The four basic components of Carrol’s pyramid, consisting of
“economic, ethics, philanthropic, and legal,” were further enumerated and elaborated by Lim and Loosemore (2018), who

further elaborated on corporate philanthropy. However, CSR has since assumed a multidimensional perspective and has been
defined in several ways in a number of literature, expanding and dwelling on the basic and fundamental framework and
context of being socially responsible.

An organization’s efforts to ensure the welfare of society and stakeholders via optional operational procedures and corporate
resource donations are commonly referred to as CSR (Arghashi et al., 2021). The notion of CSR is fairly broad, based on a
wide range of endeavors, including charitable contributions, initiatives for underprivileged groups and minorities, and policies
which promote ecologically responsible production (Araœjo et al., 2023). Bianchi et al. (2019) indicate that CSR initiatives
yield modest but excellent results and gains. CSR also affects the opinions and purchasing patterns of customers. According to
Bhattacharya et al. (2020), clients’ brand views, brand equity, and financial expenditure are all positively impacted by
perceived CSR. Cowan and Guzman (2020) define CSR as the degree to which a company advantages and makes
constructive contributions to society. Although CSR and corporate morality are frequently used interchangeably, CSR is a
broader definition of a company’s societal duties, which includes moral business practices (Chikazhe et al., 2020).

In the context of this study, CSR has been considered based on a moderating role and its effects on preferences based on
connection with brand reputation associated with culture and lifestyles, as enumerated further. The aim of this paper was to
introduce and propose culture and lifestyles and point to CSR as a moderator as a novel instrument used for brand
communication and brand image as a brand preference and its influence on purchase intentions, buys, and repeat purchases,
emphatically stressing brand attraction, brand image, and cultural lifestyles and orientations, while recognizing, identifying,
and analyzing existing and previous tools unveiled earlier in the literature.

Brand equity can be described as perceived value. The literature has revealed that perceived interactivity has a positive and
significant effect on brand attitude or brand loyalty (Kuo et al., 2013; Vn Noort et al., 2012; Laroche et al., 2013). Cultural
lifestyles depict the modes and lifestyles of the people and are all-encompassing, including attributes, behavior, attitudes, and
expressions. The culture represents the people, traditions, and ways of life. As a whole or an entire sum and encompassing the
brand, it can be imbibed as part of the lifestyles of people’s lifestyles from a given cultural background or environment and
space, as revealed in this study.  Preferences can be described as a choice over another alternative or other available options
and choices that could be selected from.

Consumers often seek for preferences borne out of desires, interests, and expressions; these preferences can be influenced and
shaped by a number of factors as enumerated. The preference might reflect the behavior, perceptions, and conceptions of the
individual, transmitted having been modified and shaped by the brand, image, and reputation attached. These preferences or
alternative choices may also be confined and bound within some restricted bounds and irrational behaviours and absurdity and
unpredictable patterns that might be cognitively driven and sometimes impulsive. The brand communication tools play
significant roles in enhancing brand reputation, generating and creating effects that can be transmitted and conveyed via the
brand manifesting its image and reputation, and then further impacting the preferences, choices, and selections made by the
consumers, moderated and facilitated by the core roles of CSR as demystified in this study.

Consumers’ awareness of, interest in, and preference for a certain culture represents and transmits or depicts cultural

Consumers develop a perception that the brand is associated with a specific culture through strategies such as emphasizing

(Wiedmann et al., 2011), and marketing communication in conjunction with culture (Liu et al., 2021),

and adore, which can provide enriching benefits  (Jun et al., 2023; Park et al., 2016).

In the modern world, it is worth noting that large businesses have their own corporate identity. This corporate identity has
become the essence of these businesses and becomes a driving force for them. In order to build on this identity and
maintain a positive image in the arena of public opinion, these businesses allocate some of their resources in order to
effectively meet greater social needs. In general, the definition of CSR is not clear on how organizations should assume
responsibility for their actions toward the environment and society (Thorisdottir & Johannsdottir, 2020). As CSR is not
clearly defined, it may be interpreted differently by companies and individuals (SÆnchez-Teba et al., 2021). Simply, work
done in this context and any obligations undertaken to increase social welfare through a number of available resources is
collectively called “corporate social responsibility (CSR)” (Kotler & Lee, 2006; Sánchez-TornØ et al., 2020; Yang &
Stohl, 2020). While some researchers argue that CSR covers all the socially responsible acts an organization undertakes to
meet social, environmental, and economic needs (Chang et al., 2021; Narayanan & Singh, 2023), others claim it also
includes how businesses willingly contribute to the improvement of the environment and society by structuring their
responsibilities (Thorisdottir & Johannsdottir, 2020). Based on these definitions, it is possible to understand social
responsibility as a voluntary activity performed on behalf of society without waiting for a direct benefit in return. It must
be an obligation that is assumed by a business to take responsibility for improving society and the environment that needs
to be taken by everyone, not as a personal point of view from other individuals in society, but within the framework of an
institutional understanding.
In the definition of CSR stated by Carroll (2008), specific responsibilities that businesses are obliged to assume for
society were examined under four main headings. These are, respectively; “economic responsibility,” “legal
responsibility,” “ethical responsibility,” and “voluntary responsibility or philanthropic responsibility.” Economic
responsibility is the most important of these, and it supports the existence of the other responsibilities (Ali et al., 2020;
Suharman et al., 2022). In order to maintain their existence, businesses must fulfill these responsibilities. The second
element is legal responsibility, which requires acting in accordance with the laws (Khuong et al., 2021). The third of these
responsibilities is ethical responsibility (Hyun et al., 2021), which is not limited or defined by any regulation and is not
established for businesses. This type of responsibility is the product of a social value. The last type of social responsibility
is voluntary responsibility (Gupta & Chakradhar, 2022; Semenov & Randrianasolo, 2023). This responsibility arises from
a number of institutions that have reached a certain economic structure and made social investments without expecting
any direct result (Asemah et al., 2013; Carroll, 2008). All of these elements of CSR lead to an enterprise’s improved
financial performance (García-Madariaga & Rodríguez-Rivera, 2017) and maximize their long-term positive impact on
society (Rowea et al., 2018). The following characterization of these concepts is structured in accordance with a template,
examining and detailing a potential CSR pathway.

2.1 Literature Review

Brand communication is conceived and conceptualized based on the ideology, tenets, and fact that any product or service
can be marketed by making use of key elements and differentiators from competing brands and those of competitors’ with
key points and unique highlights or enthralling features and resonators that could laudably and effectively communicate,
thus transmitting key or core messages and signals.

Brand communication promotes connections, ties, and nexus existing between companies and customers due to the fact
that consumers buy brands that are reflected on and mostly remembered (Madhavaram et al., 2005). It is the most relevant
element and driver or tool for effectively introducing a new product or service into a market (Schultz et al., 2013). In fact,
consumers will go for brands that are dwelt on in memory and thoughts or experiences recalled and recounted from past
and previous encounters; thus, brand attraction, brand image, and cultural lifestyles are crucial factors, entities, and
significant constructs as presented in this study. Consumers align with and favour brands that resonate with their personal
values, socially responsible, transparent communication across diverse channels consistent and immersive in experiences
(Rodrigo et al., 2023).

The literature has elaborated on the multidimensional perspective of CSR (Aguascalientes & Medero Gómez, 2016;
Correa, 2007). CSR has vastly and greatly evolved since its emergence in the 1950s, as evident in the literature narratives
(LatapíAgudelo et al., 2019; Moura-Leite & Padgett, 2011), and has currently broadened and extensively widened in
scope. This justifies the fact and tenets of unveiling and presenting brand communication from the cultural lifestyles
being adopted as a tool in building a reputable brand, with preferences stressing the moderating roles of CSR. Brand was
studied from two dimensions based on the functional and affective (Martinez et al., 2014). While the functional
dimension is linked and connected to the tangible characteristics that are measurable, the affective dimension depicts
emotions psychologically involved and attached based on feelings and attitudes shown and dispositions to the company
(Kennedy, 1977). The moderating role of CSR has become pertinent as a number of studies have investigated the
relationship between CSR and brand image based on these two key dimensions of the brand (Kodua et al., 2021;
Mohammed & Rashid, 2021; Ozcan & Elci, 2020; Stivastava, 2019).
Research has unveiled and vividly shown that consumers’ attitudes and preferences toward specific cultures within a
brand determine their attitudes or behaviours and emotions toward the brand (Jian et al., 2019). In tourism research for
instance or an example, increased involvement resulted in perceived higher destination brand equity, and highly engaged
cultural tourists are more likely to enjoy the destination (Whang et al., 2016). Tourists who are more involved in the
destination culture became more satisfied with the experience and developed a stronger brand attachment or connection
and inclination (Molinillo et al., 2022). The antecedent for brands to strongly associate culture with their brand comes
from consumers being consciously aware of and enjoying the culture itself, which is denoted as brand cultural
involvement, enrichment and engagement.
Brand reputation translates to equity using CSR, as evident from the literature (Alakkas et al., 2022; Mahmood & Al-
Bashir, 2020; Wang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021). The literature further attests to this fact; in fact, CSR can impact

brand equity positively (Agyei et al., 2021; Fatma et al., 2018; Mohammed & Rashid, 2018; Park et al., 2017; Torres et
al., 2012; Wang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021).
Numerous experimental studies have demonstrated that consumers’ views of CSR impact business and product
assessments, views, and behaviors (Y. J. Kim et al., 2021). Emmanuel and Priscilla (2022) demonstrated that views and
assessments of products, as well as company reputation, are influenced by corporate competence and CSR perceptions.
Fatma et al. (2018) showed similar consequences and that the impacts of CSR on BI and buying intent are moderated by
individual traits, including perceived alignment between one’s own identity and the business’. All of these experimental
studies show that, separate client-level concepts and assessments are required in order to determine the degree, timing,
and reasons behind the impact of CSR programs on consumer behavior when considered from an holistic sense or
perspective (Mohammad Fraihat et al., 2023). CSR has also been examined in a more plausible scenario, where
competitive advantages and activities are accepted, considering the growing imortance and significance of CSR tactics
(García-Madariaga & Rodríguez-Rivera, 2017).
As consumer decision-making, choices, processes and preferences can be complex. It is expedient and crucial to look
deeply and beyond the consumer’s needs and choices or decisions. It is essential to consider what factors could determine
and influence consumer preferences. In this context, this study has considered brand communication as an essential and
vital tool that could play a lot of roles and be of huge significance in shaping and building consumer preferences, in
particular, looking at the cultural lifestyles of brand attraction from the consumer perspective, while pointing attention to
CSR as one of the tools of brand communication with the extant traditional tools.
The strategic aspect of CSR was highlighted and extensively enumerated (Hekurinien, 2018). Corporate social
responsibility (CSR) has emerged as a viable marketing tool and strategic device in positioning the brand as a major
differentiator from those of its competitors (Harjoto & Salas, 2017). The consumers show preferences for socially
responsible products and brands when making evaluations of similar products (Porter & Kramer, 2006). As a result, as
consumers are expecting more than a high-quality offer at relatively lower prices, CSR has been considered as a strategic
marketing tool; thus, CSR is affecting consumer behaviour (Kuokkaneen & Sun, 2020).
It has been stated that brand image is influenced by CSR based on cognitions and affective modes (He & Li, 2011). Domg
(2016) described the brand image as an indivisible constituent of the brand and an inseparable component, reflecting its
strength and essence. Consumers develop trust in brands that show commitment to socially responsible causes, the
environment, and welfare. Brand communication can be enhanced and moderated by CSR, as pinpointed in the context of
this study. Furthermore, the culture and lifestyles of the people and consumers could be considered quite crucial in
building preferences from the tool of CSR, as consumers favourably and passionately engage with brands that show
commitment to socially responsible roles.
Cultural involvement, an intangible state of motivation and interest in products with attractive characteristics (Howitz &
Mannell, 2005; Guo et al., 2022). It has been described is the extent to which consumers are aware of, interested in, and
prefer a certain culture (Whang et al., 2016). This study considers the consumer’s interests and preferences in connection
with cultural lifestyles, which can influence attractions, emphasizing the communication effects and the potential roles
played and building affection for the brands’ identity based on communication being linked and tied to culture to be a key
driver for interaction, the transmission of core messages and cultural involvement of the brand.
Businesses derive huge and significant benefits by implementing and practising CSR-related activities and projects. It is
vital for businesses to understand how CSR endeavors affect the consumer-brand relationship (Fatma & Khan, 2023). It
has been argued and contested that CSR has the potential to bring about diverse value-increasing effects by attracting
preferable personnel; enhancing their performance (Naqvi et al., 2021), motivation, trust (Han et al., 2020; Tian et al.,
2020), and loyalty (Islam et al., 2021); diminishing turnover (Chang et al., 2021); increasing financial efficiency (Babajee
et al., 2022); and reducing costs. Moreover, CSR enhances revenue and attracts more ethical consumers (Nicolaides,
2021); therefore, the corporations that practice CSR can gain from its demand share (Becchetti et al., 2015). Combining
these projects with an effective brand increases the brand’s value and helps improve its personality, reputation, status and
positioning. Thus, it creates an image and enhances value for the brand (Wu & Jin, 2022) by ensuring its distinct
placement in the market where the brand is located (Polonsky & Jevons, 2006; Thanh et al., 2021). Therefore, CSR

supports brand recognition (Zhang, 2014) and impacts brand performance (Araœjo, Pereira, & Santos, 2023; Cowan &
Guzman, 2020).
Brand image is defined as the perception of a brand as reflected by the associations held in consumer memory, minds or
thoughts and thinking (Keller, 1993). Sustaining, creating and maintaining brand reliability with CSR initiatives results in
a positive brand impression and affects consumers’ behavior (Do et al., 2022). The connotations related to the brand in
the minds of consumers include the consumers’ feelings and thoughts or expressions about the product, and also the
perceptions formed and created by the brand’s activities in the social arena. The brand value increases when brands are
associated with core and key elements or attributes of social responsibility. These connotations affect consumers’
product-related evaluations, brand image, and brand loyalty (Amegbe et al., 2021; Rodrigues et al., 2021; Singh et al.,
2008). Positive perceptions are registered in the consumer’s mind and lead to a positive brand image for potential
consumers (Lee et al., 2010).

Institutions and businesses engaged in volunteer activities are generally perceived as socially responsible by being able to
meet society’s expectations and preferences (Jamali & Mirshak, 2007). CSR perceptions of consumers lead to a
significant and valuable contribution to brand image (Martínez et al., 2014; Salam & Bajaba, 2022). Zhang et al. (2018)
found in their study that branding and image are the key motivators and impetus or drivers in CSR research. It is also an
effective way to strengthen the organization’s image (Kotler & Lee, 2006). For instance, Jung (2012) has concluded that
professional sports teams and sponsor companies, by connecting with fans with the help of CSR initiatives and displaying
a positive image, create a positive effect for the image of the team and sports club. Similarly, Babiak and Wolfe (2006),
Dawkins (2005), Du et al. (2010), Kotler and Lee (2006), Phan et al. (2021), and Polonsky and Jevons (2006) have shown
that CSR makes positive contributions to the brand image. Prasad and Kumar (2022) emphasize in their study that more
research into brand CSR is especially appropriate due to the change in consumer behavior and changing pattterns brought
about by the pandemic toward a more sustainable lifestyle.
As earlier mentioned and pointed out, brand image is crucial as it represents the first impression that consumers have of
an organization and its brand that can lead to long lasting memory and impression. Conversely, an enterprise’s reputation
reflects an appropriate judgment regarding its qualities (H. M. Wang et al., 2021). While BI may be created faster with
well-thought-out communication programs, business reputations usually develop gradually, as a consequence of ongoing
success supported by successful communication (C. C. Wang, 2020). Businesses aim to present a true and favorable
image to their clients. Additionally, they strive for a positive reputation among stakeholders (S. Wang et al., 2021). As a
result, corporations value their reputation and image among their clients. Clients, suppliers, sellers, vendors, collaboration
partners, banks, experts, stockholders, government regulators, advocacy groups, consumers, and staff members are the
main stakeholders that most large businesses need to be mindful of (Yang & Basile, 2019). To thrive in the fierce market
rivalry brought about by globalization and advances in technology, businesses (Nese et al., 2025) need to place greater
emphasis on their interactions with customers, with a particular focus on their reputation and BI (Zhao et al., 2021).
Therefore, a key element that affects a company’s BI is its reputation. Reputation may assist a company in finding a
competitive edge that sets it apart from its rivals, helping customers decide to purchase said company’s item or service
(H. M. Wang et al., 2021; Yang & Basile, 2019). Consumer demands and desires have a substantial influence on BI and
CR, leading to improved company attempts to meet customer expectations (Yang & Basile, 2019). On the one hand,
consumers’ opinions and perceptions of a company’s products and services are favorably affected by its reputation and BI
(Zhao et al., 2021). On the other, a company’s reputation greatly influences consumer behaviors towards it, in turn
improving the BI (Díaz et al., 2006).

The cultural significance of a brand enriches the consumer’s brand experience (Fournier, S.; Alvarez, 2019). Pleasant
experiences are essential in promoting brand equity (Dwivedi et al.,  2018). Dwivedi, Nayeem, and Murshed (2018) argue
that brand experience can increase consumers’ willingness to pay a premium. A strong self-brand connection provokes a
positive response from consumers  (Samarah et al., 2022:  Bilro & Loureiro,  2023). These positive consumer responses
(e.g., brand loyalty and willingness to pay a premium) can drive firm performance (Barrada et al., 2018).

Park, MacInnis, and Eisingerich (Park et al., 2016) proposed to increase the brand enriching benefits to cultivate customer
loyalty behavior. They found that consumers’ perceptions of self-expression lead to the development of loyalty,
awareness, and a positive association with brand equity (Salem et al., 2024). Customer-based brand equity (CBBE) refers
to the ability of consumers to differentiate between brands, as demonstrated in their brand knowledge during marketing
communications (Keller, 1993). Willingness to pay a premium is the primary aspect of brand equity. Consumer WPP can
be understood as the additional amount of money that customers are willing to pay for their favorite brand relative to
other brands  (Paris & Guzman, 2023).
CSR and CR are positively linked and correlated or associated (Le, 2023).The relationship between confidence and views
on CSR is facilitated by the concept of social exchange (J. Lee & Lee, 2018). CR may be used to describe each
stakeholder’s general perception of a business and their degree of confidence (Liu et al., 2024). Stakeholders are more
inclined to trust businesses that have good and solid CSR being a sign of excellent management, (Mahmood & Bashir,
2020). Consumers’ assessments of a company’s reputation have a positive relationship with their opinions , thoights and
perceptios or impressions of its CSR initiatives (Nguyen & Pham, 2018). Customers’ perceptions of companies’ CSR are
correlated with the degree of organizational dedication, which improves evaluations of the reputation of said companies
(Narteh & Braimah, 2020). Philanthropic endeavors are among the business tactics used to improve a company’s standing
with its top executives (Özcan & Elçi, 2020). Consumer reactions may be governed and driven by custom based on the
concept of social exchange (Pritchard & Wilson, 2018). Customers are more inclined to have faith in a business and
establish trust when they believe that such businesses act in a way to safeguard the interests of everyone involved and
participants (Ramesh et al., 2019). Companies can improve their corporate reputation by concentrating on appropriate
CSR initiatives and adopting as ways to communicate, disseminate, transmit and deliver core and essential messages
(Suki & Suki, 2019). Stakeholders assess a company’s corporate reputatio based on the signals and signs they obtain from
a company (Singh & Misra, 2021). Yang and Basile (2019)’s work centered on CSR’s charitable ideals, which, alongside
a business’ management and financial achievements, enhance people’s views and perceptions of business’ CSR of a firm
or company in play. A company’s revenues, client devotion, BI, and CR are all enhanced by CSR, representing and

The point of triangulation and convergence is core and crucial in mixed methods research (MMR).
Qualitative methods, while rich in contextual detail, were often criticized for their lack of generalizability and potential
subjectivity. Conversely, quantitative methods, though robust in measuring patterns and relationships, were seen as
insufficient for capturing the depth and complexity of human experiences. This recognition led to the emergence of MMR
to bridge the gap between these two approaches, combining their strengths to address research questions more
comprehensively (Campbell & Fiske, 1959). One of the earliest and most influential contributions to the development of
MMR was the work of Campbell and Fiske, who introduced the concept of triangulation and further elaborated and
justified by results obtained in a number of other and subsequent literature (Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Onwuegbuzie et al.,
2008; Guetterman et al., 2015; Cresswell & Plano Clark, 2018). Triangulation refers to the use of multiple methods or
data sources to validate findings, thereby enhancing the credibility and robustness of research outcomes (Greene et al.,
1989). This idea laid the groundwork for the integration of qualitative and quantitative methods, encouraging researchers
to view these approaches as complementary rather than mutually exclusive. Over time, MMR evolved into a distinct
methodology, supported by theoretical frameworks and design typologies that provided researchers with structured
guidelines for effectively combining methods (Younas et al., 2025).
