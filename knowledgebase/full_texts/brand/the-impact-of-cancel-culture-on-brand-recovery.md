ABSTRACT:
Cancel culture, the collective withdrawal of consumer support through viral social media backlash, boycott calls, negative word-of-mouth, and media
amplification, has become a serious threat to brand survival in the digital era. This study examines how the intensity of cancel culture affects brand recovery,
measured through consumer forgiveness, repurchase intention, reputation restoration, and sales and brand equity rebound. It proposes a conceptual framework in
which cancel culture intensity operates through three mediators: brand trust erosion, perceived brand authenticity, and brand image/reputation damage. The
framework further positions crisis response strategy (apology, denial, corrective action, and response speed) andconsumer and brand factors (prior brand equity,
CSR reputation, consumer-brand identification, and perceived severity) as moderators that can strengthen or weaken these relationships. Four hypotheses are
developed totest the direct andmediated paths, anda feedback loop between brand recoveryandfuture cancel culture vulnerability isalso considered. The study
isexpectedtooffermarketersevidence-basedguidanceformanagingbrandcrisesandtoextendthebrandcrisisliteraturebeyonditspredominantlyUS/UKfocus.
Keywords: Cancel culture; Brand recovery; Brand trust erosion; Perceived brand authenticity; Brand reputation damage; Crisis response
strategy;Consumerforgiveness;CSRreputation
Introduction
Socialmediahaschangedthebalanceofpowerbetweenbrandsandconsumers.Asinglecontroversialadvertisement,statement,orcorporateactioncan
nowbeshared, discussed,and condemnedbythousands of peoplewithinhours. Cancelculture, inwhichconsumers publicly withdrawsupport from a
brandthey see ashavingacted unacceptably, has emerged fromthis environmentand typically appears as viral backlash,hashtag-driven boycott calls,
negativeword-of-mouth,andamplificationbynewsmedia.
For brands, the consequences reach well beyond a short-lived dip in attention. Cancellation can weaken consumer trust, cast doubt on the brand's
perceived authenticity, and damage its image and reputation, which in turn threatens sales and long-term brand equity. Yet not every cancelled brand
fails to recover. Some regain consumer forgiveness and purchase intent, while others decline for a long time. Understanding why recovery differs
acrossbrandsisthereforeanimportantmanagerialandacademicquestion.
Existingresearchhas mainlyexamined brandtransgressions,boycotts, andapology strategies separately, andmuchofit comes fromWestern markets.
Few studies integrate cancel culture intensity, the psychological mechanisms it triggers, and the conditions under which brands recover into a single
model.Thisstudyaddresses thatgapbyproposingaconceptualframeworkoftheimpactofcancelcultureonbrandrecovery, withbrandtrusterosion,
perceived brand authenticity, and brand image/reputation damage as mediators, and with crisis response strategy and consumer and brand factors as
moderators.

ConceptualFramework
 Independentvariable:Cancelcultureintensity,reflectedinviralsocialmediabacklash,boycottcalls andhashtagactivism,negativeword-
of-mouth,andmediaamplification.
 Mediators: Three mechanisms carry the effect of cancel culture: brand trust erosion, perceived brand authenticity, and brand
image/reputationdamage.
 Dependentvariable: Brand recovery, indicated byconsumer forgiveness, repurchase intention, reputation restoration, and sales and brand
equityrebound.
 Moderator 1, crisis response strategy: Apology, denial, corrective action, and response speed shape how strongly the mediators are
triggeredandhowtheyaffectrecovery.
 Moderator2, consumer andbrand factors:Prior brandequity, CSR reputation,consumer-brand identification, andperceived severity of
thetransgressioncanstrengthenorweakentherelationships.
 H1(directeffect):Highercancelcultureintensityincreasesbrandtrusterosion,authenticitydecline,andreputationdamage.
 H2 to H4 (mediator effects): Brand trust erosion (H2) and brand image/reputation damage (H4) reduce brand recovery, while perceived
brandauthenticity(H3)improvesit.
 Feedbackloop:Thelevelofbrandrecoveryinfluencesthebrand'svulnerabilitytofuturecancelcultureepisodes.
 Overall logic: Cancel culture intensity triggers negative perceptions of trust, authenticity, and reputation, which then determine brand
recovery,withthemoderatorschangingthestrengthofthesepaths.

DataAnalysisToolsandTechniques
Completed responses were exported from Google Forms to Microsoft Excel, coded, and screened for completeness before analysis. Descriptive and
inferential statistics were computed in Excel (using built-in statistical formulas, PivotTables and LINEST-based regression) and cross-verified in
SPSS/Python-basedstatisticalroutines.Thefollowingtechniqueswereapplied:
 Descriptivestatistics–frequenciesandpercentagesfordemographicvariables;meanandstandarddeviationforeachconstruct.
 Reliabilityanalysis–Cronbach'salphatotesttheinternalconsistencyofeachmulti-itemconstruct(α≥0.70treatedasacceptable).
 Pearsoncorrelation–toexaminethestrengthanddirectionofassociationbetweenallconstructpairs.
 Chi-square test of independence – to test association between categorical/grouped variables (e.g., cancel culture intensity level, gender,
age,education,socialmediausage)andtheBrandRecoverylevel(Low/Moderate/High).
 One-way ANOVA (with Tukey HSD post-hoc tests) – to compare mean Brand Recovery scores across demographic and behavioural
groupsandtestforstatisticallysignificantdifferences,withLevene'sandWelch'stestsasassumptionchecks.
 Simple and multiple linear regression – to test H1 (effect of cancel culture intensity on each mediator) and H2–H4 (effect of the three
mediators, and subsequently crisis response strategy and consumer & brand factors, on Brand Recovery), including standardised beta
coefficients,R²,adjustedR²,F-statisticsandvarianceinflationfactors(VIF)tocheckmulticollinearity.
Thesignificancelevelforallstatisticaltestswassetat5%(p<0.05).
