Abstract
Purpose: This conceptual article explains how artificial -intelligence-based personalization
within social commerce can build durable loyalty toward local brands among Generation Z. It
addresses fragmented research that examines personalization, customer experience, electronic
trust, and loyalty in separate causal models.
Research Methodology:  This integrative conceptual review synthesized 57 peer -reviewed
journal articles published from 2000 to July 2026, while retaining earlier seminal relationship -
marketing sources. Reproducible search blocks, explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria,
backward and forward citation tracing, DOI verification, and concept coding were used to
compare definitions, analytical levels, mechanisms, and boundary conditions across six
literature streams.
Results: The synthesis defines AI -personalized social commerce as an adaptive capability that
configures recommendations, content, offers, conversational support, and social evidence
within commerce -enabled social environments. Six propositions indicate that this capability
improves digital customer experience and electronic trust; experience strengthens trust and
local brand loyalty; trust supports loyalty; and experience followed by trust forms the principal
serial pathway. Transparency, perceived data control, platform credibility, product
involvement, and local-identity salience delimit these relationships.
Limitations: This theory -synthesis review is limited to a traceable English -language journal
corpus and does not estimate effects or establish causal order. The proposed framework
therefore requires scale validation and longitudinal, experimental, and field -based testing
across social-commerce platforms and product categories.
Contribution: Recommendation accuracy alone cannot secure loyalty. Local brands must
convert algorithmic relevance into coherent encounters and credible evidence of competence,
integrity, and acceptable data conduct. Managers should combine explainable
recommendations, editable preference controls, authentic social proof, and consistent
fulfillment. The framework connects AI marketing and social commerce with customer -
experience, signaling, and commitment -trust theory, while explaining local brand loyalty as a
sequentially produced outcome rather than an immediate response to targeting.
Keywords: algorithmic personalization; social commerce; digital customer experience; electronic
trust; local brand loyalty; Generation Z

1. INTRODUCTION
Social commerce has altered the digital purchase setting by placing product discovery, peer
communication, creator content, evaluation, and transaction within the same socially connected
environment (Yadav et al., 2013; Hajli, 2015). Unlike conventional electronic commerce, social
commerce does not treat interaction as a peripheral communication layer because social
support, user-generated content, and community cues participate directly in product evaluation
and purchase formation (Busalim & Hussin, 2016; Zhang & Benyoucef, 2016; Felix et al., 2017).
The commercial value of these environments increasingly depends on algorithms that decide
which products, reviews, live streams, creators, and offers each user sees at a particular
moment (Appel et al., 2020; Davenport et al., 2020; Grewal et al., 2020). Artificial intelligence
therefore enters social commerce not as a separate application but as an allocation mechanism
that shapes the sequence and meaning of customer contact with a brand (Huang & Rust, 2021;
Tong et al., 2020; Yadav & Pavlou, 2020).
Personalization can reduce search effort and increase perceived relevance when product
recommendations correspond to current goals, prior behavior, and situational signals (Bleier &
Eisenbeiss, 2015; Tong et al., 2020; Teepapal, 2025). The same practice can trigger vulnerability
and reactance when consumers infer that a firm has collected more information than expected
or used it without adequate control and explanation (Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Aguirre et al.,
2015; Martin & Murphy, 2017; White et al., 2008). This tension is especially important in social
commerce because commercial targeting is interwoven with interpersonal content, public
reactions, and identity presentation (Appel et al., 2020; Yadav et al., 2013). A technically
accurate recommendation can therefore fail commercially when its relevance is interpreted as
surveillance rather than assistance (Puntoni et al., 2021; Aguirre et al., 2015).
Generation Z is a consequential setting for this problem because its members commonly
encounter brands through mobile, visual, and socially mediated interfaces while retaining
strong expectations of speed, autonomy, and technological fluency (Priporas et al., 2017;
Djafarova & Bowes, 2021). Evidence from social -media purchasing shows that creator
credibility, visual presentation, and platform interaction can accelerate product interest and
unplanned buying among younger consumers (Djafarova & Bowes, 2021; Godey et al., 2016).
Yet purchase activation is not equivalent to brand loyalty because loyalty requires a durable
preference that survives competing offers and repeated exposure to alternatives (Dick & Basu,
1994; Oliver, 1999; Mohamed & Ünsalan, 2025). The distinction matters for local brands, which
can possess cultural proximity and market familiarity but often compete against global brands
with greater visibility, technological resources, and perceived prestige (Schuiling & Kapferer,
2004; Özsomer, 2012).

The extant literature leaves three conceptual discontinuities (Davenport et al., 2020; Hajli ,
2015; Becker & Jaakkola, 2020). First, AI marketing research explains task automation,
prediction, and personalization but gives less attention to how these capabilities operate within
the social architecture of commerce platforms (Davenport et al., 2020; Huang & Rust, 2021;
Verma et al., 2021). Second, social -commerce studies have established the roles of social
support and trust in purchase intention, yet their dominant models rarely distinguish
algorithmic personalization from generic platform interactivity ( Hajli, 2015; Zhang &
Benyoucef, 2016). Third, customer -experience and loyalty studies show that experiences and
trust influence relational outcomes, but they do not specify whether experience precedes trust
when recommendations are generated by opaque adaptive systems (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016;
Becker & Jaakkola, 2020; Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001).
A direct personalization -to-loyalty argument is consequently underspecified because it
collapses the consumer's encounter with a recommendation, interpretation of the platform's
conduct, and relationship with the brand into one causal step (Bleier et al., 2019; Puntoni et al.,
2021; Obiegbu & Larsen, 2025). Digital customer experience captures the immediate cognitive,
affective, sensory, and behavioral response to interconnected digital touchpoints (Rose et al.,
2012; Becker & Jaakkola, 2020). Electronic trust captures the willingness to rely on a digital
seller or platform under conditions of uncertainty and perceived vulnerability (McKnight et al.,
2002; Gefen et al., 2003). Treating these states sequentially permits a more precise explanation:
personalization first alters the quality of the digital encounter, the encounter supplies evidence
for trust judgments, and trust stabilizes repeated preference for the brand ( Sirdeshmukh et al.,
2002; Lemon & Verhoef, 2016).
This article asks how AI -personalized social commerce builds Generation Z loyalty toward
local brands through digital customer experience and electronic trust, and under what
conditions this pathway weakens (MacInnis, 2011; Jaakkola, 2020). The article develops a six -
proposition framework by integrating stimulus -organism-response reasoning, signaling theory,
and commitment-trust logic (Eroglu et al., 2001; Morgan & Hunt, 1994). Its central contribution
is to conceptualize loyalty as the end of a serial conversion process in which algorithmic
relevance becomes experiential value and then relationship confidence (MacInnis, 2011;
Obiegbu & Larsen, 2025). The framework also identifies transparency, perceived data control,
platform credibility, product involvement, and local -identity salience as conditions that delimit
the proposed mechanism (Aguirre et al., 2015; Martin & Murphy, 2017; Özsomer, 2012).
The remainder of the article defines the focal constructs and theoretical architecture,
develops six propositions, explains the integrative review procedure, reports the conceptual
synthesis, and specifies implications and empirical tests (Jaakkola, 2020). This sequence follows
recommendations that conceptual research should make its problem, theoretical mechanism,
construct boundaries, and testable implications explicit rather than offering a descriptive
literature summary (MacInnis, 2011; Jaakkola, 2020).

A. LITERATURE REVIEW
Theoretical Foundation
The framework uses stimulus -organism-response reasoning to distinguish an external
digital configuration from the internal states through which it affects behavior (Eroglu et al.,
2001; Eroglu et al., 2003). In an online setting, interface cues and information architecture
function as stimuli, consumers' cognitive and affective reactions form the organism, and

social commerce is treated as the stimulus because it changes the content, sequence, timing, and
social context of brand encounters (Davenport et al., 2020; Tong et al., 2020). Digital customer
experience and e -trust are treated as distinct internal states because experience concerns the
quality of the encounter, whereas trust concerns willingness to accept vulnerability in a future
exchange (Becker & Jaakkola, 2020; McKnight et al., 2002).
Signaling theory clarifies why personalization can generate trust rather than only
convenience (Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Kim et al., 2008). Digital exchanges contain information
asymmetry because consumers cannot fully observe product quality, seller intentions, data
practices, or algorithmic criteria before acting (Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Pavlou, 2003). Consistent
recommendations, accurate product matching, transparent explanations, and credible
community evidence can signal competence and procedural reliability, whereas unexplained
targeting or manipulated social proof can signal opportunism (Aguirre et al., 2015; Kim et al.,
2008). Trust is therefore not an automatic consequence of personalization accuracy because
consumers also evaluate how the accuracy was produced (Martin & Murphy, 2017; Puntoni et
al., 2021).
Commitment-trust theory explains the transition from trust to loyalty by treating trust as a
central relational condition that supports cooperation, reduces perceived exchange risk, and
discourages short-term switching (Morgan & Hunt, 1994). Brand-trust research similarly shows
that confidence in reliability and intentions supports both attitudinal and behavioral loyalty
(Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001; Laroche et al., 2012). The framework combines these theories
without treating them as interchangeable: stimulus -organism-response specifies the process
architecture, signaling theory explains trust formation, and commitment -trust logic explains
why trust supports durable brand preference (Eroglu et al., 2001; Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Morgan &
Hunt, 1994).
AI-Personalized Social Commerce
Perceived AI -personalized social commerce is defined as a consumer’s perception that a
brand’s commerce -enabled social environment uses machine learning or related analytical
systems to adapt recommendations, content, offers, conversational assistance, and socially
embedded product cues to the individual. It is modeled at the consumer -perception level, even
when underlying technology is delivered through a platform. The definition excludes static
segmentation, generic platform usability, and recommendation accuracy considered outside a
socially embedded commerce context.
Its core dimensions are relevance, adaptivity, and social -context sensitivity. Relevance is
the perceived fit between a recommendation and the consumer’s current need, taste, or task;
adaptivity is the perceived capacity to revise content as new behavior and context become
available; and social -context sensitivity is the alignment of recommendations with credible
reviews, creator content, community practices, and interaction patterns. Governance visibility is
deliberately treated as a distinct boundary condition —rather than a fourth capability
dimension—because explanation and data control may strengthen, weaken, or reverse the
effects of personalization.
Digital Customer Experience
Digital customer experience is a customer's non-deliberate and deliberate response to a set
of digital interactions that occur before, during, and after purchase (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016;
Becker & Jaakkola, 2020; Pansari & Kumar, 2017). The construct is broader than interface
usability because it includes cognitive fluency, affective tone, sensory presentation, social
reassurance, and perceived control across touchpoints (Rose et al., 2012; Homburg et al., 2017).

Online experience becomes favorable when information is diagnostic, navigation is coherent,
content is vivid but not distracting, and the encounter is congruent with the product and brand
(Bleier et al., 2019; Klaus & Maklan, 2013). In social commerce, the experience also depends on
whether community and creator cues feel useful and authentic rather than commercially
orchestrated (Laroche et al., 2012; Yadav et al., 2013).
The framework treats digital customer experience as the first mediator because consumers
encounter personalization initially as an alteration of effort, relevance, surprise, control, and
interaction continuity (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016; Bleier et al., 2019). A consumer may value a
recommendation before deciding whether the brand or platform is trustworthy, and the same
recommendation may improve convenience while simultaneously creating concern about data
collection (Aguirre et al., 2015; Puntoni et al., 2021). Separating experience from trust therefore
avoids assuming that hedonic or utilitarian value is identical to confidence in the exchange
partner (Becker & Jaakkola, 2020; McKnight et al., 2002).
E-Trust
Brand-specific e -trust is the willingness to rely on the focal local brand in a digital
transaction despite uncertainty and vulnerability. Its recurring components are competence,
integrity, and benevolence, while perceived platform security and institutional assurance are
treated as antecedent contextual cues rather than as the trust  object itself. This distinction is
essential in social commerce: a consumer may trust a platform’s payment system yet remain
uncertain about a particular local brand’s product quality, fulfillment, or data conduct.
Brand-specific e -trust is positioned after digital customer experience because repeated
fluent and coherent brand encounters provide evidence from which consumers infer the focal
brand’s competence, integrity, and benevolence. Platform credibility can facilitate this inference
through trust transfer, but it does not replace brand -specific trust. Separating the objects
prevents a favorable platform experience from being incorrectly treated as direct evidence of
loyalty to every brand that appears on the platform.
Generation Z Local Brand Loyalty
Local brand loyalty is a deeply held preference and repeat -purchase commitment directed
toward a brand whose market meaning, origin, ownership, or cultural associations are
perceived as locally grounded (Oliver, 1999; Özsomer , 2012). Loyalty includes both relative
attitude and repeat patronage, which distinguishes it from satisfaction, purchase intention, and
episodic engagement (Dick & Basu, 1994; Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001). Local brands may
derive advantage from cultural proximity, familiarity, responsiveness to local needs, and
accumulated community relationships (Schuiling & Kapferer, 2004; Özsomer , 2012). They can
nevertheless lose digitally active consumers when global competitors provide more seamless
platforms, stronger social proof, or more precise recommendations (Steenkamp et al., 2003;
Schuiling & Kapferer, 2004).
Generation Z should not be treated as a uniform psychological category because digital
competence, income, product involvement, and privacy orientation vary within the cohort
(Priporas et al., 2017; Djafarova & Bowes, 2021). The cohort remains theoretically relevant
because mobile social media frequently organizes discovery and evaluation, making
algorithmic and social cues unusually proximal to choice (Djafarova & Bowes, 2021; Appel et
al., 2020). The framework therefore uses Generation Z as a boundary of application rather
than as an explanation by itself (Priporas et al., 2017; Mohamed & Ünsalan, 2025).

AI-Personalized Social Commerce and Digital Customer Experience
Personalization improves digital customer experience when it reduces irrelevant
information, shortens search, and presents content that fits the consumer's current task (Bleier
& Eisenbeiss, 2015; Tong et al., 2020). Adaptive recommendations can also create continuity
across browsing, messaging, live -stream, and transaction touchpoints, which supports the
journey-level coherence emphasized in customer-experience research (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016;
Homburg et al., 2017). Social -context sensitivity adds diagnostic value because reviews, creator
demonstrations, and community reactions can help consumers interpret local products with
limited prior awareness ( Hajli, 2015; Yadav et al., 2013). The effect weakens when the
recommendation is repetitive, overly narrow, or inconsistent with the consumer's self -
perception because excessive targeting can reduce exploration and produce reactance (White et
al., 2008; Puntoni et al., 2021).
P1. AI -personalized social commerce positively influences Generation Z's digital customer
experience with local brands (Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2015; Teepapal, 2025).
AI-Personalized Social Commerce and E-Trust
Personalization can build e -trust when recommendation accuracy and interaction
consistency signal that the brand understands the customer and can execute digital exchanges
competently (Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Kim et al., 2008). Social commerce can reinforce this inference
when algorithmic suggestions are accompanied by credible reviews and visible community
validation rather than unsupported brand claims (Hajli , 2015; Laroche et al., 2012). The
relationship is conditional because unexplained data collection can make the same accuracy
appear intrusive, which converts a competence signal into a vulnerability cue (Aguirre et al.,
2015; Martin & Murphy, 2017). Governance visibility and perceived control are therefore part
of the focal capability rather than external ethical additions (Puntoni et al., 2021; Huang & Rust,
2022).
P2. Perceived AI -personalized social commerce positively influences Generation Z’s brand -
specific e-trust in local brands; this effect is stronger when governance visibility, perceived
data control, and platform credibility are high.
Digital Customer Experience and E-Trust
A coherent digital experience provides repeated evidence that a seller can organize
information, fulfill promises, and respond predictably across touchpoints (Homburg et al., 2017;
Lemon & Verhoef, 2016). Low -friction navigation and useful content reduce ambiguity, while
responsive interaction and consistent social proof support benevolence and integrity judgments
(Rose et al., 2012; Sirdeshmukh  et al., 2002). Experience does not eliminate the need for
institutional assurance, but it changes trust from an abstract expectation into an inference
grounded in observed interaction (McKnight et al., 2002; Gefen et al., 2003).
P3. A favorable digital customer experience positively influences Generation Z’s brand -
specific e-trust in local brands operating through social commerce.

E-Trust and Local Brand Loyalty
Trust supports loyalty by reducing perceived exchange risk and by creating confidence that
future interactions will remain reliable (Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Sirdeshmukh et al., 2002). Brand
trust also strengthens attitudinal commitment, making consumers less likely to switch solely
because a competing brand offers a temporary incentive (Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001; Oliver,
1999). In social commerce, this stabilizing role is material because consumers repeatedly
encounter alternative sellers, reviews, creators, and price comparisons within the same session
(Yadav et al., 2013; Zhang & Benyoucef, 2016).
P5. Brand-specific e-trust positively influences Generation Z’s loyalty toward local brands in
social commerce.
Serial Mediation through Experience and Trust
The preceding arguments imply a sequence rather than two unrelated mediators (Lemon &
Verhoef, 2016; McKnight et al., 2002; Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001). AI personalization first
modifies the quality of the encounter by changing relevance, effort, continuity, and perceived
control (Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2015; Lemon & Verhoef, 2016). The encounter then supplies
evidence for competence, integrity, and benevolence judgments, which determine whether the
consumer accepts vulnerability in future exchanges (McKnight et al., 2002; Sirdeshmukh et al.,
2002). Trust subsequently converts episodic value into a stable preference for the local brand
(Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001; Oliver, 1999). This sequence explains why technically
sophisticated targeting may raise clicks without generating loyalty when the experience is
fragmented or the firm's data conduct remains doubtful (Aguirre et al., 2015; Puntoni et al.,
2021).
P6. Digital customer experience and brand -specific e -trust jointly transmit the indirect
effect of perceived AI -personalized social commerce on Generation Z local brand loyalty.
Future studies should compare this indirect -only specification with a partial -mediation
model that includes a direct path to loyalty.

METHOD
Research Design and Context
The study uses an integrative conceptual review because the research problem requires
construct clarification and mechanism building across literatures that developed with different
dependent variables and units of analysis (Torraco, 2005; Snyder, 2019). Integrative reviews
are appropriate when mature concepts remain dispersed across fields and lack a coherent
explanatory account (Torraco, 2005; Paul & Criado, 2020). The design applies Jaakkola's theory-
synthesis approach, which recombines established theories and empirical regularities to explain
a phenomenon that no single literature adequately captures (Jaakkola, 2020).
Literature Identification and Selection
The review was finalized in July 2026 and covered peer -reviewed English-language journal
articles published from 2000 onward, while retaining earlier seminal work when it defined
loyalty or relationship trust. Searches were conducted iteratively in Crossref -indexed records,

A publication was included when it (a) defined or operationalized a focal construct, (b)
explained a mechanism relevant to the framework, (c) reported peer-reviewed evidence about a
focal relationship, or (d) supplied a recognized method for conceptual synthesis. Studies were
excluded when AI was only a generic technology label; social media lacked commerce or brand
relevance; intention measures had no defensible theoretical connection to loyalty; or
bibliographic metadata could not be verified. The final analytical corpus comprised 57 articles
across six dominant streams, reported in Table 1.
Conceptual Coding and Synthesis
Each article was coded using a structured extraction matrix covering construct definition,
dimensional structure, causal mechanism, analytical level, focal object (platform, seller, or
brand), mediator or moderator role, empirical context, and stated boundary condition. The
synthesis compared competing definitions, separated overlapping constructs, mapped recurrent
causal claims, and recorded conditions under which effects changed direction or magnitude.
Four integration operations followed: differentiating AI personalization from generic targeting,
separating platform functionality from perceived consumer states, ordering experience and
trust into a serial process, and translating the resulting mechanism into directional
propositions.
The synthesis was challenged against direct algorithmic utility, price advantage, global -
brand prestige, and platform familiarity as rival explanations (MacInnis, 2011; Jaakkola, 2020).
A proposition was retained only when at least two literature streams supported its causal
mechanism and the construct boundary could be stated independently of the expected outcome
(Jabareen, 2009; MacInnis, 2011). This rule reduced circular definitions in which
personalization, experience, trust, or loyalty would be inferred from one another (Jabareen,
2009).
Quality and Traceability Procedures
Priority was given to established marketing, information -systems, retailing, and consumer-
behavior journals. Each retained source was checked against a DOI landing page or official
journal record; non -scholarly trend reports were excluded from proposition development.
Appendix A records the searchable concept blocks, eligibility rules, coding fields, and analytical
logic. The review is reproducible as an integrative theory-synthesis review, not as an exhaustive
systematic review or meta-analysis.
Ethical Considerations
The study analyzed publicly available scholarly publications and did not involve human
participants, personal data, intervention, or confidential organizational records (Snyder, 2019).
Institutional ethical approval and informed consent were therefore not required for this
conceptual review (Snyder, 2019).

Construct Architecture and Main Findings
The synthesis yields a level -consistent construct architecture: perceived AI -personalized
social commerce is an individual -level perception of a brand -platform capability; digital
customer experience is an encounter -level consumer response; brand -specific e -trust is a
relational judgment about the focal brand; and local brand loyalty is a durable brand -level
outcome reported by the consumer. This separation avoids treating platform features,
consumer states, and brand outcomes as one undifferentiated construct. Table 2 presents the
operational boundaries and dimensions derived from these literatures.
The causal map contains two entry routes into brand -specific e-trust. The direct signaling
route operates when personalization quality and credible social evidence communicate that the
focal brand is competent and procedurally reliable. The experiential route operates when
relevant and coherent encounters provide accumulated evidence for the same brand -level trust
judgment. Governance visibility, perceived data control, and platform credibility are boundary
conditions: they influence whether personalization is interpreted as helpful brand competence
or intrusive profiling, without being folded into the personalization construct itself.
The route to loyalty is also dual but temporally ordered (Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001;
Obiegbu & Larsen, 2025). Digital customer experience can influence loyalty through affect,
convenience, memory, and identity congruence, whereas e -trust stabilizes preference by
reducing anticipated relational risk (Brakus et al., 2009; Sirdeshmukh  et al., 2002). The serial
route is theoretically distinct because experience changes loyalty partly by changing willingness
to rely on the brand (McKnight et al., 2002; Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001). Table 3 summarizes
the propositions, mechanisms, and boundary conditions supported by the synthesis (Jaakkola,

B. DISCUSSION
Interpretation of the Conceptual Findings
The framework shifts the explanation of AI marketing performance from prediction quality
to conversion quality (Puntoni et al., 2021; Obiegbu  & Larsen, 2025). A recommendation can be
statistically accurate but experientially poor when it interrupts browsing, repeats narrow
assumptions, or makes the consumer feel observed (Aguirre et al., 2015; White et al., 2008). It
can also be experientially pleasant but relationally weak when the brand does not disclose data
practices or when reviews appear manipulated (Martin & Murphy, 2017; Kim et al., 2008).
Loyalty emerges only when the consumer receives value from the encounter and judges the
exchange arrangement acceptable enough to revisit (Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001; Oliver,
1999).
The serial pathway is particularly relevant to local brands because they may possess strong
cultural meaning but limited digital infrastructure (Schuiling & Kapferer, 2004; Özsomer, 2012).
Local iconness can create identification and familiarity, yet those assets do not compensate
indefinitely for fragmented navigation, irrelevant recommendations, or unreliable fulfillment
(Özsomer, 2012; Schuiling & Kapferer, 2004). Conversely, local brands need not imitate the data
intensity of global platforms when modest personalization is combined with credible
community knowledge and transparent customer control (Hajli, 2015; Martin & Murphy, 2017).
The framework therefore treats social embeddedness as diagnostic information rather than
merely a promotional reach mechanism (Yadav et al., 2013; Hajli, 2015).
Theoretical Contributions
First, the article defines AI -personalized social commerce as a multidimensional capability
rather than a synonym for recommendation accuracy (Huang & Rust, 2021; Teepapal , 2025).
Relevance and adaptivity capture algorithmic performance, social -context sensitivity captures
the commerce environment, and governance visibility captures the conditions under which
personalization remains acceptable (Huang & Rust, 2021; Yadav et al., 2013; Martin & Murphy,
2017). This definition connects AI marketing with social -commerce theory without reducing
either literature to platform technology (Huang & Rust, 2021; Yadav et al., 2013).

Second, the article distinguishes digital customer experience from e -trust and specifies
their temporal ordering (Becker & Jaakkola, 2020; McKnight et al., 2002). Customer experience
concerns what the interaction is like, whereas trust concerns whether the consumer is willing to
rely on the brand or platform under future vulnerability (Becker & Jaakkola, 2020; McKnight et
al., 2002). The distinction permits favorable -experience/low-trust and high -trust/poor-
experience configurations that disappear when trust is treated as a generic dimension of
experience (Rose et al., 2012; McKnight et al., 2002).
Third, the article reframes local brand loyalty as the outcome of an algorithmic -
experiential-relational process (Obiegbu & Larsen, 2025; Mohamed & Ünsalan, 2025). Research
on local and global brands has emphasized cultural meaning, quality, prestige, and local
iconness, while digital marketing research has emphasized targeting and engagement
(Steenkamp et al., 2003; Özsomer, 2012; Kannan & Li, 2017). The proposed model explains how
a local brand's meaning can be preserved or weakened by the design of algorithmic contact
(Özsomer, 2012; Obiegbu & Larsen, 2025).
Fourth, the framework places governance inside marketing capability (Martin & Murphy,
2017; Huang & Rust, 2022). The personalization -privacy literature shows that data collection
and trust -building strategies jointly determine effectiveness, which means governance cannot
be treated only as a compliance constraint imposed after campaign design (Aguirre et al., 2015;
Martin & Murphy, 2017). A capability that improves relevance while destroying perceived
control is strategically incomplete (Aguirre et al., 2015; Teepapal, 2025).
Managerial, Social, and Policy Implications
Local-brand managers should optimize the sequence of discovery, evaluation, and
reassurance rather than maximize personalized -message volume (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016;
Obiegbu & Larsen, 2025). Recommendation systems should expose why an item was suggested,
permit users to correct inferred preferences, and avoid using sensitive signals when their
relevance is marginal (Aguirre et al., 2015; Martin & Murphy, 2017). Social evidence should be
selected for diagnostic value and authenticity, not merely for positivity, because uniform praise
can reduce credibility (Hajli, 2015; Kim et al., 2008).
Customer-experience metrics should be separated from campaign -response metrics
(Lemon & Verhoef, 2016; Homburg et al., 2017). Click -through rate and conversion capture
immediate action, while journey coherence, perceived control, trust, and repeat preference
capture whether personalization is building a relationship (Lemon & Verhoef, 2016; Homburg
et al., 2017). Managers should examine whether a recommendation raises short -term sales at
the cost of exploration or future willingness to share information (Aguirre et al., 2015; Obiegbu
& Larsen, 2025).
For local -brand ecosystems, shared digital infrastructure can reduce capability gaps
without requiring each small firm to build an advanced model independently (Kannan & Li,
2017; Huang & Rust, 2022). Cooperatives, marketplaces, and local -business associations can
provide consent management, verified reviews, interoperable product data, and transparent
recommendation services (Hajli , 2015; Martin & Murphy, 2017). Policy should encourage
explainable personalization and accessible preference controls because these mechanisms
support both consumer autonomy and the credibility of smaller sellers (Martin & Murphy, 2017;
Puntoni et al., 2021).

relevance generated from unexpected or sensitive data can reverse the experience and trust

Agenda for Empirical Testing
An initial test should define Generation Z explicitly according to the study country’s
accepted cohort range and recruit consumers who purchased a locally grounded brand through
a social -commerce platform within a stated recall period. Local brand status should be
measured as consumer -perceived local origin, ownership, production, or cultural association
rather than assumed from seller location alone. Perceived AI -personalized social commerce can
be measured through relevance, adaptivity, and social -context sensitivity; governance visibility
should be measured separately as a moderator. Customer experience, brand -specific e -trust,
and loyalty can be adapted from validated digital and brand scales.

separately from reactions to data conduct (Aguirre et al., 2015; Martin & Murphy, 2017). Field
experiments can compare transparent explanations, editable preference profiles, and black -box

persistence and cannot be established from a one -session intention measure (Dick & Basu,
1994; Oliver, 1999).

CONCLUSION
This article explains Generation Z local brand loyalty as an indirect conversion process
rather than a direct consequence of algorithmic targeting. Perceived AI -personalized social
commerce changes relevance, continuity, and social diagnosticity; the resulting encounter
supplies evidence for brand-specific e-trust; and trust stabilizes repeated brand preference. The
six propositions integrate AI marketing, social commerce, customer experience, electronic trust,
and local-brand research in a level-consistent framework.
Better prediction is insufficient when consumers cannot understand or govern how a
prediction enters the interaction (Aguirre et al., 2015; Martin & Murphy, 2017). Local brands
should pursue personalization that is socially informed, experientially coherent, and
procedurally visible (Hajli, 2015; Lemon & Verhoef, 2016; Huang & Rust, 2022). The framework
remains limited by its conceptual design, English -language journal corpus, and absence of
empirical estimates (Snyder, 2019; Jaakkola, 2020). Future research should validate the
construct dimensions, compare mediation orders, test the identified boundary conditions, and
observe repeat behavior across product categories and social -commerce formats (Obiegbu &
Larsen, 2025; Mohamed & Ünsalan, 2025).
