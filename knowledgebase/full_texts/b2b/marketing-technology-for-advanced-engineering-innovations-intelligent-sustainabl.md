Introduction
Advanced engineering innovations (AEIs)
frequently exhibit a commercialization paradox: they
perform strongly in laboratory, prototype, or pilot settings,
yet diffuse slowly in the market, especially in business -to-
business (B2B), business-to-government (B2G), and regulated
industrial environments [2-4]. Engineering-led organizations
often prioritize technical optimization, reliability, safety, and
production readiness, while underinvesting in the market -

reduce adoption uncertainty, and coordinate stakeholder
decisions across long purchasing cycles [3 -5]. As a result, a
technically superior innovation may fail not because it lacks
engineering merit, but because the firm lacks a coherent

Abstract
Advanced engineering innovations - such as intelligent manufacturing systems, robotics -enabled production assets,
green process technologies, and digitally connected industrial products - often underperform commercially despite
technical superiority. A central reason is weak integration between engineering development and marketing technology
(martech) systems that translate complex technical value into customer adoption, ecosystem legitimacy, an d scalable
revenue. Building on the conceptual logic linking intelligent machines, information and communication technology
(ICT), sustainable marketing practices, and business performance, this paper develops a PhD -level framework for the
marketing techno logy of advanced engineering innovations. A structured conceptual synthesis methodology was
applied, integrating scholarship on intelligent machines, industrial innovation commercialization, B2B marketing,
sustainable value creation, digital transformation , and AI governance. The results propose an integrated framework in
which intelligent -machine-enabled martech capabilities - market sensing, technical -value translation, opportunity
discovery, commercialization analytics, and lifecycle orchestration - strengthen sustainable commercialization capability
and improve business performance. The framework further specifies the mediating role of sustainability evidence
orchestration and the moderating roles of explainability governance and market -regulatory turbul ence. The paper
concludes that martech in engineering-led firms should be designed as a commercialization infrastructure rather than a
promotional toolkit, requiring interoperability across engineering, sales, service, compliance, and sustainability reporting
functions. The manuscript contributes a testable proposition -based model, implementation architecture, and a multi -
stage empirical validation pathway suitable for engineering -intensive firms operating in uncertain and sustainability -
sensitive markets.

digital transformation has al tered the customer journey,
making continuous digital interaction, remote demos,
simulation artifacts, service telemetry, and lifecycle

[10-12]. Fourth, sustainability is increasingly part of industrial
purchasing logic, not only in consumer branding but in
supplier qualification, compliance, investor scrutiny, and
enterprise reporting [18-21]. Fifth, AI-enabled marketing and
decision systems require explainability and governance,
particularly where re commendations affect technical
procurement, safety, cost structures, and long -term
operations [22-24].

marketing technology be conceptualized and stru ctured to

performance of advanced engineering innovations?

Sustainable commercialization capability as a central

Explainability governance and market-regulatory turbulence

Methodology
Study Design
This study adopts a conceptual synthesis design with
a theory-building orientation, followed by a proposed
empirical validation protocol. The design is appropriate
because the central objective is not to estimate an already -
established effect size, but to construct and organize a novel
explanatory framework fo r a problem spanning multiple
literatures: intelligent machines, marketing technology,
engineering innovation commercialization, sustainability,
and business performance [1,4,5,11,13,25,26]. The study
design combines four methodological moves:
1. Conceptual b orrowing and adaptation from the
uploaded IM-ICT-sustainable marketing framework [1]
2. Integrative literature synthesis across management,
marketing, innovation, and AI governance domains [4 -
24]
3. Construct specification and proposition development for
AEI contexts [25,26]
4. Future empirical protocol design to support scale
development, hypothesis testing, and triangulation [27 -
32].

This approach aligns with theory-building traditions
that emphasize clear constructs, boundary conditions, and
falsifiable relationships before broad empirical testing [26,28].

Setting
Because the present manuscript is conceptual, no
single field site was used for primary data collection. Instead,
the analytical setting is defined as engineering-intensive firms
commercializing advanced technologies in B2B/B2G markets.
The framework is intended for organizations with the
following characteristics:
High technical complexity products/systems;
Multi-stage commercialization (prototype, pilot, deployment,
service);
Multiple decision-makers in customer organizations;
Requirement for technical documentation and evidence;
Sustainability and/or compliance relevance;
Significant post -sale service, training, or integration
requirements.
Illustrative sectors include industrial automation,
robotics, clean-tech equipment, process engineering systems,
MedTech devices, AI -enabled industrial software -hardware
systems, and advanced manufacturing tools.

Construct Development Logic
The construct development process follows a theory-
driven approach rather than purely inductive coding. It is
anchored in three principles:
1. Capability realism: Constructs must reflect
organizational actions, processes, and system
interoperability rather than abstract technology
optimism [4,5,36-38].
2. Commercialization specificity: Constructs must capture
AEI commercialization constraints (technical complexity,
risk, long cycles, cross-functional buying) [2-4,16,17].
3. Governance embeddedness: AI-assisted martech must be
explainable, auditable, and aligned with sustainability
and compliance commitments [18-24].

Accordingly, the model distinguishes:
Intelligent-machine-enabled martech capabilities (upstream
and process capabilities),
Sustainable commercialization capability (integrative
organizational capability),
Business performance (commercial and strategic outcomes),
Sustainability evidence orch estration (mediating
mechanism),
Explainability governance and market-regulatory turbulence
(boundary conditions).

Results
Overview of the Proposed Framework
The principal result of this study is an Integrated
Intelligent Martech Framework for Advanced Engineering
Innovations (IIMF-AEI). The framework extends the attached
manuscript's conceptual chain (intelligent machines -> ICT-
enabled sust ainable marketing -> performance) [1] by
relocating it into an AEI commercialization environment and
specifying AEI -relevant mechanisms. In the proposed
framework, intelligent machines are not treated merely as
production or operational tools; they are tre ated as
commercialization enablers embedded in martech
architectures. Their role is to process technical, market,
service, and sustainability data in ways that improve decision
quality, stakeholder communication, and adoption support
[1,11-14]. This shift is important because AEI adoption often
depends on evidence translation and risk reduction rather
than advertising intensity alone [2 -4,16,17]. The framework
contains six major components:

1. Intelligent-machine-enabled Martech Capabilities (five
capabilities)
IM-enabled Market Sensing
Technical-Value Translation
Opportunity Discovery and Prioritization
Commercialization Analytics
Lifecycle Orchestration

2. Martech-Engineering Integration Quality
Interoperability between engineering, marketing, sales,
service, and compliance systems.

3. Sustainability Evidence Orchestration (Mediator)
The ability to produce, curate, audit, and activate
sustainability-related evidence for stakeholder decision -
making.

4. Sustainable Commercialization Capability (Core
Organizational Capability)
The firm's ability to repeatedly commercialize AEIs in
ways that are economically viable, socially legitimate,
and operationally responsible.

5. Business Performance Outcomes
Commercial, strategic, and relationship-based outcomes.

6. Boundary Conditions
Explainability Governance
Market-Regulatory Turbulence

This structure preserves the learning capability spirit
of the attached manuscript [1] but translates it into
organizationally observable martech capabilities suitable for
engineering-led firms.

IM-Enabled Market Sensing Capability
This capability refers to the firm's ab ility to use
AI/IM-supported systems to detect and interpret signals from
markets, customer operations, competitor moves, policy
changes, procurement patterns, and service incidents. It
extends classic market orientation and sensing concepts [5 -8]
into digital and AI-mediated environments [11-14]. For AEIs,
market sensing must go beyond lead volume or campaign
click-through rates. It should identify:
Unmet operational pain points,
Adoption barriers,
Compatibility constraints,
Buyer-role-specific concerns,
Timing windows for pilot deployment,
Emerging sustainability requirements.

An engineering firm selling a smart industrial
monitoring system, for example, may use IM-enabled sensing
to identify sectors where downtime costs, re gulatory
reporting obligations, and energy optimization needs are
simultaneously increasing. Such sensing supports strategic
targeting and reduces commercialization waste [39-41].

Technical-Value Translation Capability
AEIs often fail commercially because  firms
communicate technical features rather than stakeholder value
[16,17]. Technical-value translation capability is the ability of
martech systems (supported by IM) to convert engineering
evidence into audience -specific value narratives. This
includes translation of:
Performance metrics into economic outcomes (ROI, total cost
of ownership, payback period),
Technical reliability into risk mitigation language,
Efficiency gains into sustainability and compliance
advantages,
Interoperability into implementation ease,
Lifecycle serviceability into procurement confidence.

This capability is central because different AEI
buying-center participants evaluate the same innovation
through different criteria. Engineers may focus on
specifications, operations managers  on uptime, finance on
cost/risk, and sustainability officers on reporting and
emissions implications [42-44].

Opportunity Discovery and Prioritization Capability
Drawing from the discovery logic embedded in the
attached manuscript [1], this capability refers to the use of IM
systems to identify and prioritize commercialization
opportunities across markets, segments, applications, and
partnerships. It is especially relevant for AEIs that can be
reconfigured for multiple use cases. Examples include:
discovering adjacent industries with similar process pain
points,
identifying underserved segments with lower entry barriers,
prioritizing accounts by readiness and implementation
capacity,
recommending pilot sequencing strategies.

The importance of this capability increases when
engineering innovations have platform characteristics or
service extensions [15,17,35].

Commercialization Analytics Capability
Commercialization analytics capability refers to the
ability to model, predict, and optimize the AEI
commercialization process using integrated data from
marketing, sales, engineering, service, and post -deployment
performance. Unlike generic marketing analytics, this
capability explicitly accounts for technical milestones and
customer implementation realities. It may include:
Lead qualification models incorporating technical -fit
variables,
Pilot-to-scale conversion prediction,
Proposal success analytics by buyer-role engagement pattern,
Pricing and contract scenario simulations,
Service burden forecasting for installed base expansion,
Churn/replacement risk signals.

This capability aligns with the attached paper's
emphasis on analysis and decision support [1], but it is
extended here into a full commercialization performance
layer.

Lifecycle Orchestration Capability
AEI commercialization does not end at purchase
order. Adoption, deployment, integration, training, service,
updates, and proof-of-value are all part of commercialization
success. Lifecycle orchestration capability is the ability of
martech-enabled systems to coordinate communications and
evidence flow across the pre -sale, pilot, implementation,

In AEIs, lifecycle orchestration links marketing,

Martech-Engineering Integration Quality
A major result of the framework is the identification
of Martech -Engineering Integration Quality (MEIQ) as a
distinct construct. AEI firms often possess strong technical
databases and strong customer systems, but weak
interoperability between them. This fr agmentation
undermines value translation, targeting, and performance
learning. MEIQ refers to the degree to which engineering and
commercialization systems are interoperable at the data,
process, and decision layers. High MEIQ typically includes:
Shared identifiers across products, versions, customers, and
installations;
Bidirectional data flow between engineering updates and
customer-facing systems;
Synchronized product claims and approved technical
content;
Service and incident data informing market commu nication
and segmentation;
Traceable linkage between sustainability claims and
engineering evidence.

Without MEIQ, firms risk:
Inconsistent technical claims,
Outdated marketing assets,
Overpromising in sales proposals,
Weak sustainability substantiation,
Poor feedback loops from market to engineering.

This result aligns with dynamic capability logic:
integration quality is not merely an IT hygiene factor; it is a
strategic enabler of sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring
commercialization routines [4,33,36].

Sustainability Evidence Orchestration as a Mediating
Mechanism
The attached manuscript highlights sustainable
marketing and performance benefits [1]. In AEI contexts, the
relationship between martech capability and performance is
unlikely to operate directly in all cases. A key mediating
mechanism identified here is Sustainability Evidence
Orchestration (SEO). SEO refers to the firm's ability to
systematically generate, verify, organize, and activate
sustainability-related evidence for co mmercialization
decision-making. In AEI settings, sustainability claims
increasingly affect procurement, financing, supplier
qualification, brand legitimacy, and public contracting [18 -
21]. However, claims alone are insufficient; firms need
evidence. SEO includes:
Evidence libraries (test results, certifications, lifecycle
calculations, operational benchmarks),
Claim governance (who can claim what, with what evidence),
Buyer-role customization (technical, financial, compliance -
oriented evidence views),
Traceability and version control,
Explainable AI summaries for non-technical stakeholders.

The mediating logic is straightforward: even
advanced martech capabilities do not reliably improve
performance if the firm cannot substantiate sustainability and
operational claims in a credible, audience-specific way.

lifecycle.
SCC is not equivalent to green ma rketing and not reducible

Commercialization effectiveness,
Adoption quality,

Evidence credibility,
Lifecycle continuity.

Boundary Conditions: Explainability Governance and
Market-Regulatory Turbulence
Explainability Governance (Moderator)
The attached paper explicitly notes explainability as
an important extension and links it to trust, regulatory
requirements, and responsible deployment [1]. In AEI
commercialization, this becomes even more critical because
AI-assisted recommendations may influence high -value
procurement, installation decisions, or operational risk
narratives [22 -24]. Explainability Governance refers to the
policies, processes, roles, and tools through which firms
ensure that AI -assisted martech o utputs are interpretable,
auditable, and challengeable by relevant stakeholders. Key
dimensions include:
Model documentation and versioning,
Explanation interfaces for users,
Claim traceability to data sources,
Approval workflows for AI-generated content,
Bias checks in segmentation or prioritization models,
Escalation processes for disputed recommendations.

The framework proposes that explainability
governance strengthens the positive effect of IM -enabled
martech capabilities on SCC because it improves ma nagerial
trust, customer confidence, and compliance readiness.

Market-Regulatory Turbulence (Moderator)
AEI markets often face volatility driven by policy
shifts, standards changes, procurement cycles, trade barriers,
and sustainability reporting requirem ents. Under such
turbulence, martech capabilities can become more valuable
(due to sensing and adaptation needs) but also harder to
convert into immediate performance.

Market-Regulatory Turbulence is therefore modeled as a
boundary condition that:
Increases the strategic value of market sensing and value
translation,
Raises the performance premium of lifecycle orchestration,
Amplifies the need for explainability and evidence
governance,
May weaken short-term direct performance outcomes due to
delays and uncertainty.

This reflects contingency logic in market orientation and
dynamic capability research [7,33,36].

consistency, and feedback loops, enabling stronger
commercialization routines [4,11,33,36].

P7. Sustainability evidence orchestration mediates the
relationship between IM -enabled martech capabilities and
sustainable commercialization capability.
Rationale: Capabilities improve SCC more strongly when
firms can substantiate performance and sustainability claims
with auditable evidence [18-24].

Proposition Set C: SCC and Performance
P8. Sustainable commercializa tion capability positively
influences business performance in advanced engineering
innovation firms.
Rationale: Repeated, credible, and lifecycle -aligned
commercialization improves conversion, trust, and long-term
revenue quality [1,4,5,9,11].

Proposition Set D: Boundary Conditions
P9. Explainability governance positively moderates the
relationship between IM -enabled martech  capabilities and
sustainable commercialization capability, such that the
relationship is stronger under higher explainability
governance.
Rationale: Explainability reduces resistance to AI -assisted
decisions and increases legitimacy of algorithm -supported
commercialization outputs [1,22-24].

P10. Market-regulatory turbulence moderates the
relationship between sustainable commercialization
capability and business performance, such that SCC becomes
more strategically valuable but its short -term performance
effects may vary depending on regulatory timing and
uncertainty.
Rationale: Turbulence increases the need for adaptive
commercialization while complicating immediate conversion
and scaling outcomes [7,33,36].

Measurement Considerations
A reflective or formative specification should be
determined by construct logic. For example, business
performance may be modeled formatively if multiple metrics
jointly form the construct, whereas explainability governance
may be reflective if indicators manifest an underlying
governance maturity. Pilot testing and expert review are
essential before full-scale deployment [29-31].

Illustrative Application Scenarios
To demonstrate practical relevance, the framework
can be mapped onto three AEI scenarios.

Scenario A: Robotics-Enabled Manufacturing Line Upgrade
A firm commercializing robotic quality inspection
systems has strong technical accuracy but low adoption.
Applying the framework, it improves technical -value
translation (ROI from defect reduction, line throughput
gains), builds sustainability evidence (waste reduction and
rework savings), and introduces lifecycle orchestration
(operator training, rollout dashboards, support workflows).

Scenario B: Energy-Efficient Industrial Equipment
A company offers advanced process equipment
promising energy savings. Without evidence orchestration,
sustainability claims are viewed skeptically. By integrating
engineering telemetry with martech content systems, the firm
creates explainable, customer -specific performance reports
and procurement-ready evidence packages. Expected result:
stronger trust, lower objection rates, and improved win rates
in sustainability-sensitive tenders.

Scenario C: Smart Agri-Engineering Platform
A firm selling sensor -integrated irrigation systems
operates in a turbulent policy environment. IM -enabled
market sensing tracks subsidy changes and weather risk
patterns, while opportunity prioritization identifies segments
with higher readiness. Explainability governance helps
translate AI recommendations for farmers and institutional
buyers. Expected result: improved targeting, reduced churn,
and more resilient performance under uncertainty.
These scenarios illustrate that the framework is not sector -
bound; rather, it is a genera lizable commercialization
architecture for engineering-led innovation contexts.

Discussion
Interpretation of Findings in Relation to Existing Literature
This paper's core argument is that martech should be
reconceptualized in AEI settings as a strategic
commercialization capability system. This extends three

Kowsar; Pac J Adv Eng Innov. 2026; 3 (1): 13-27
 https://scienceget.org/index.php/pjbis 23
streams of literature. First, it extends market orientation and
market-sensing theory by specifying how IM -enabled
martech capabilities can m ake sensing more technically
grounded and action-oriented in complex industrial markets
[5-8]. Traditional market orientation constructs remain
foundational, but AEI commercialization requires sensing
architectures that incorporate engineering and service
telemetry, not just customer preferences or campaign
responses. Second, it extends innovation commercialization
theory by emphasizing the digital and evidence-management
infrastructure required to move AEIs across the diffusion gap
[2-4]. Stage-gate and profiting-from-innovation logics remain
relevant [3,4], but they need augmentation in an era where
customers expect continuous digital engagement, quantified
value evidence, and implementation transparency [10 -
12,16,17]. Third, it contributes to AI in market ing and digital
transformation literature by situating AI -enabled martech in
a governance -sensitive, sustainability -linked industrial
context [11-14,22-24].
Rather than treating AI as a generic optimization
engine, the framework shows that AI value depends  on
explainability governance and interoperability with
engineering systems. This is especially important where AI
outputs shape high -stakes decisions or public sustainability
claims. The proposed mediation through sustainability
evidence orchestration is also a substantive addition.
Sustainability literature often focuses on strategic intent,
branding, or business model innovation [18 -21]. This paper
argues that in AEI commercialization, the decisive
mechanism is often the organizational ability to activat e
verifiable evidence at the right point in the customer journey.
This explains why some firms with credible engineering
performance still struggle commercially: they possess
technical evidence but lack orchestration capability.

Theoretical Implications
Martech as a Dynamic Commercialization Capability
The framework positions martech as part of a
dynamic capability system (sensing, seizing, reconfiguring)
rather than a functional marketing support domain [4,33,36].
The five capability structure (sensing, t ranslation, discovery,
analytics, orchestration) reflects a capability chain that links
environmental signals to lifecycle outcomes.

Capability Interdependence and Integration Quality
A major theoretical insight is that capability
performance is contingent on MEIQ. In many firms, martech
maturity and engineering maturity develop separately. The
framework predicts that isolated capability investments yield
weaker returns than integrated ones. This contributes to the
broader literature on digital transformat ion by emphasizing
cross-domain system coherence over tool proliferation
[11,35].

Explainability as a Commercialization Condition
Explainability is often discussed in AI ethics or
model governance terms. Here it is theorized as a
commercialization conditi on. In AEI settings, explainability
supports not only compliance and fairness but also customer
trust, internal adoption of AI recommendations, and dispute
resolution around claims and prioritization [22 -24]. This
expands the conceptual relevance of explai nability into
strategic marketing and commercialization research.

Sustainable Commercialization Capability as a Distinct
Construct
The SCC construct addresses a gap between
sustainable marketing and business performance by
introducing a meso -level capabil ity that captures
repeatability, evidence credibility, and lifecycle continuity.
This may be particularly useful for future research on
industrial sustainability positioning and procurement-driven
adoption [45].

Managerial Implications
For Engineering-Led Firms
Managers should treat martech investment as part of
commercialization system design, not campaign
modernization. Priority actions include:
Integrating engineering and customer systems,
Standardizing technical-value translation templates,
Building sustainability evidence repositories,
Implementing lifecycle orchestration workflows,
Introducing AI governance controls before scaling
automation.

For Marketing and Sales Leaders
Marketing leaders in AEI firms should collaborate
more deeply with engineering, service, and compliance
teams. Core shifts include:
Moving from feature -centric messaging to stakeholder -
specific value logic,
Using analytics to prioritize readiness rather than only lead
volume,
Designing customer journeys that include pilot  and
implementation support,
Documenting claim substantiation practices.

For Engineering and Product Teams
Engineering teams should recognize
commercialization as an extension of product performance
realization. Product data structures, documentation practices,
and telemetry design should support downstream value
translation and evidence activation. Engineering roadmaps
should also account for commercial explainability (e.g.,
interpretable dashboards, traceable metrics, version
histories).

For Sustainability and Compliance Teams
Sustainability teams should be embedded in
commercialization system design. Their role should shift
from periodic reporting support to ongoing evidence
governance, claim validation, and stakeholder -facing
substantiation. This reduc es reputational risk and
strengthens commercial credibility [46].

For Policy and Ecosystem Stakeholders
In sectors where public procurement or policy
incentives matter, ecosystem actors can accelerate AEI
adoption by standardizing evidence expectations an d
interoperability requirements. This lowers transaction costs
and reduces ambiguity in sustainability and performance
claims [47].

Relevance to Industrial Practice and Research
The framework is relevant to industrial practice
because it provides an actionable map of where
commercialization failures occur in AEI firms: not only in
sales execution but in data integration, value translation,
governance, and lifecycle coordination. I t is relevant to
research because it offers a testable, multi -level model that
can be empirically examined across industries and
geographies [48]. The framework may be especially valuable
in emerging markets and rapidly industrializing economies
where engi neering innovation is increasing but
commercialization infrastructures remain uneven. In such
settings, the architecture proposed here can help bridge the
gap between engineering competence and market
performance while supporting sustainability-aligned growth
[49-50].

Limitations
This paper has several limitations.
1. Conceptual nature: The framework is theory -building
and not empirically tested in the present manuscript.
2. Broad AEI scope: Advanced engineering innovations
vary widely across sectors; construct salience may differ
between, for example, medical devices and industrial
automation.
3. Organizational heterogeneity: Firm size, digital maturity,
and channel structure may influence implementation
feasibility.
4. Measurement complexity: Some construct s (e.g.,
explainability governance, sustainability evidence
orchestration) may require careful operationalization
and multi-respondent data.
5. Causality challenges: In empirical work, performance
effects may be lagged and confounded by
macroeconomic or regulatory events.

These limitations do not weaken the framework's
value; rather, they define the empirical agenda needed to
refine and validate it.

Conclusion
This paper developed a PhD -level conceptual
framework for the marketing technology of advanced
engineering innovations, extending the attached intelligent
machine-ICT-sustainable marketing -performance logic into
engineering-intensive B2B/B2G commercialization contexts
[1]. The study argued that AEI commercialization failures
often stem less from technical weakness and more from weak
commercialization infrastructures - particularly poor
integration between engineering systems, martech systems,
and governance mechanisms.
The main finding is an integrated framework in
which five intelligent -machine-enabled martech capabilities
(market sensing, technical -value translation, opportunity
discovery and prioritization, commercialization analytics,
and lifecycle orchestration) strengthen sustainable
commercialization capability, which in turn improves
business perf ormance. The framework further identified
martech-engineering integration quality as a key enabler,
sustainability evidence orchestration as a central mediating
mechanism, and explainability governance plus market -
regulatory turbulence as important boundary conditions.
The paper contributes theoretically by reframing
martech as a strategic commercialization architecture for
engineering-led firms and by introducing a testable
capability-based model that integrates AI, sustainability, and
commercialization pe rformance. It contributes
methodologically by outlining a mixed -method empirical
validation pathway. It contributes managerially by offering a
blueprint for redesigning commercialization systems around
evidence, interoperability, and lifecycle coordination . Future
