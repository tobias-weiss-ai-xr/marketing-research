ABSTRACT
Structural power is a decisive determinant of organisational competitiveness, yet its relationship with Industry
4.0 in sustainable B2B marketing remains underexplored. While prior research has investigated structural power
from diverse perspectives, limited attention has been given to Resource Dependence Theory (RDT) and Systems
Theory (ST) in explaining how digital drivers reshape inter-organisational dynamics. This study addresses this
gap by examining how Industry 4.0 drivers influence structural power factors in sustainability-oriented B2B
strategies. A mixed-methods design is applied. First, a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) and Thematic Analysis
(TA) are conducted to extract Industry 4.0 drivers relevant to sustainable marketing. Second, expert input from
both emerging and developed economies is utilised to model the interrelationships among structural power
factors using a Fuzzy Cognitive Map (FCM). Finally, the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal
Solution (TOPSIS) is employed to evaluate the relative impacts of the drivers. The findings highlight eight critical
Industry 4.0 drivers that enhance structural power by strengthening cooperation, trust, and network integration.
Integrating vertical and horizontal processes emerges as a key mechanism for increasing organisational auton-
omy and influence. The study advances theoretical clarity on structural power and offers practical insights for
sustainable B2B competitiveness in the digital era.
1. Introduction
Industry 4.0 is transforming business-to-business (B2B) markets by
reshaping how firms coordinate activities, share resources, and manage
interdependencies. In B2B environments—where transactions are large-
scale, relationships are long-term, and dependence between buyers and
suppliers is high—the strategic implications of digitalisation are
particularly significant. Global B2B exchanges illustrate this scale: the
B2B payments market exceeded $49 trillion in 2021 and is projected to
reach over $81 trillion by 2030, while worldwide B2B e-commerce is
expected to surpass $36 trillion by 2030 (Lee & Kim, 2024). Industry 4.0
accelerates this transformation by digitising up to 59% of B2B activities
(Szabo et al., 2020), thereby altering resource flows, collaboration
patterns, and power asymmetries within supply networks. Technologies
or drivers such as the Internet of Things (IoT) (Oluwaferanmi, 2025), Big
Data analytics (Amroni, Darmawan, & Pangilinan, 2025), Cloud
Computing (Nguyen Phu, Hoang Thi, & Tran Nguyen Bich, 2025),
Blockchain (Syed et al., 2025), Smart Services (Bonamigo & Frech,
2021), Mixed Reality (Mikail, 2024), and advanced Customer Rela-
tionship Management (CRM) systems (Alhumud & Alsulami, 2025)
restructure information visibility, real-time coordination, and trust
mechanisms—factors that collectively influence how firms build,
maintain, and exercise structural power.
Despite these developments, limited attention has been given to how
Industry 4.0 shapes structural power in B2B markets. Theorists have
conceptualised business power as stemming from two main sources:
instrumental and structural traditionally. Instrumental power refers to
direct influence over policies and decision-making, often exercised

and Valeeva (2022); James (2018)). This perspective suggests that, in
many B2B marketing contexts, structural power may carry broader and

it shapes the underlying conditions under which firms and their ex-

forms of power can vary depending on institutional settings and the

Prior research on structural power largely stems from economics,
political science, and international relations, focusing on macro-level
actors such as states, multinational organisations, or global industries
(e.g., Haug, Foot, and Baumann (2024); Malkin and He (2024); Cart-

indirect insight into organisational-level power in interfirm relation-

examined primarily through network embeddedness (Keegan, Canhoto,
Yen, & wan., 2022) or institutional pressures (Christensen, 2023),
leaving unclear how individual firms develop structural power in B2B
marketing contexts, where dependence, collaboration, and relational
value exchange are central. This presents a key research gap: if struc-
tural power is critical for shaping collaboration, coordination, and
strategic advantage, then its relationship with Industry 4.0 drivers and
sustainability must be clarified.
Industry 4.0 drivers deepen this gap by introducing new forms of
drivers’ interdependence. For instance, big data analytics and cloud
computing enhance strategic assessment and organisational capabilities
(Saraswat & Choudhari, 2025), while IoT and Blockchain reduce un-
certainty by improving transparency and traceability in supply chains
(Oriekhoe et al., 2024). Smart services and mixed reality strengthen
relational engagement and long-term collaboration (Wirtz et al., 2025).
These drivers function not merely as operational tools but as structural
enablers that can redistribute power by altering information flows,
resource control, and relationship governance in B2B networks. Without
clear theoretical guidance, however, it remains uncertain how firms can
align Industry 4.0 drivers with sustainability goals and competitive
positioning.
To address this issue, this study draws on Resource Dependence
Theory (RDT) and Systems Theory (ST) to explain how structural power
is shaped and transformed in the digital era. RDT posits that organisa-
tions depend on external entities for critical resources and seek to
manage these dependencies to maintain autonomy and influence
(Hillman, Withers, & Collins, 2009). In the context of Industry 4.0, RDT
highlights that drivers such as IoT, Cloud Computing, and Big Data an-
alytics reduce resource uncertainty and strengthen firms’ bargaining
positions by improving access to real-time information, enhancing co-
ordination, and increasing operational control. ST complements this
view by conceptualising organisations as interconnected systems that
require balance, coordination, and continuous feedback to sustain per-
formance (Bertalanffy, 1968). Industry 4.0 drivers embody systems
principles by dissolving silos, enabling vertical integration within the
firm and horizontal integration across partners, thus fostering collabo-
ration, resilience, and network stability. Together, RDT and ST illumi-
nate how structural power is shaped and transformed in the digital era,
with direct implications for B2B marketing strategy and relationship
management. Specifically, firms strengthen control over critical re-
sources, enhance systemic resilience through interconnectivity, and
align technological capabilities with sustainability and competitive
advantage. Yet despite this theoretical potential, limited research
explicitly links structural power, Industry 4.0, sustainability, and
competitive advantage in B2B contexts, creating a significant gap in
both theory and practice.
A further gap exists in integrating sustainability and competitive
advantage into the analysis of structural power. Sustainability-
—encompassing economic, social, and environmental dimensions—has
become central to long-term business strategy, while competitive
advantage ensures market survival and profitability (e.g., Dyllick and
Muff (2016); Porter (1996)). Industry 4.0 drivers are often celebrated for
enabling sustainability, whether through energy efficiency, waste
reduction, or enhanced stakeholder engagement (da Silva & Sehnem,
2025). Yet these outcomes depend on how firms harness structural
power. For instance, transparency and traceability reduce opportunism,
fostering trust and collaboration (Oriekhoe et al., 2024); smart services
and advanced CRM promote commitment and cooperation (Shainesh,
Sheth, & Jain, 2025); and digital integration enables value co-creation
(Feng, Weng, & Zhenyuan, 2025). Each of these factors links directly
to structural power. From this perspective, enhanced structural power is
not simply about dominating others—it is about balancing in-
terdependencies, fostering collaboration, and leveraging Industry 4.0
drivers to achieve sustainable competitive advantage in B2B relation-
ships. This framing is especially important in B2B markets, where re-
lationships are long-term, and value is co-created across networks. Based
on the above rationale, the study is guided by the following research
questions:
RQ1. Which Industry 4.0 drivers influence structural power in B2B
marketing based on evidence from the literature?
RQ2. How do Industry 4.0 drivers affect structural power factors in
B2B markets?
RQ3. In what ways does structural power, enhanced by Industry 4.0,
contribute to sustainability and competitive advantage in B2B contexts?
To address the gaps and research questions, this study employs a
multi-stage design. First, a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) with
Thematic Analysis (TA) identifies structural power factors and Industry
4.0 drivers relevant to B2B contexts. Second, a Fuzzy Cognitive Map
(FCM) models the causal relationships among structural power factors,
applying fuzzy reasoning to capture complex interdependencies
(Edinson & Muthuraj, 2018). Third, the Technique for Order of Prefer-
ence by Similarity to the Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) assesses the relative
impact of Industry 4.0 drivers on structural power (Abbasi Kamardi,
Amoozad Mahdiraji, Masoumi, & Jafari-Sadeghi, 2022). This method-
ological triangulation strengthens validity and ensures practical in-
sights. To further enhance credibility, experts from two distinct contexts
were consulted: Iran, representing an emerging economy, and China,
representing a developed economy. Experts from Iran provided per-
spectives shaped by resource constraints and developing infrastructure,
while Chinese experts offered insights from a more advanced, digitally
mature ecosystem. This two-panel design, while enriching in-depth
analysis in the field of study, also addresses concerns related to
expertise.
This study makes three key contributions to B2B marketing and inter-
organisational research. First, it extends the application of structural
power to the B2B marketing domain, highlighting how Industry 4.0
drivers reshape interorganisational dependencies and collaboration.
Second, it integrates RDT and ST to provide a coherent explanation of
how drivers enhance structural power, and sequentially, enable sus-
tainability and competitive advantage. Third, by drawing on perspec-
tives from both emerging and developed economies, this research offers
insights with broad international relevance. Together, these contribu-
tions advance organisational theory and offer practical implications for
managers seeking to leverage Industry 4.0 for sustainable competitive
advantage.

2. Theoretical background
2.1. Structural power
In business research, power is broadly understood as the ability of
one actor to influence the decisions, behaviours, or outcomes of others
within organisational or inter-organisational contexts (e.g., Siemie-
niako, Makkonen, and Mitręga (2023); Ragins and Sundstrom (1989)).
Among different forms of power, structural power has become increas-
ingly relevant for explaining competitive dynamics, as it relates not to
direct lobbying or coercion, but to how firms shape the rules of ex-
change, the allocation of resources, and the configuration of relation-
ships in which others must operate (e.g., Rivera-Valle and Silva (2024);
Cooiman (2024)). Recent management research highlights the relevance
of structural power in B2B contexts, where firms rely on long-term re-
lationships, shared resources, and interdependent value chains. Studies
show that structural power enables organisations to transform resources
and data into strategic insights (Usman, Moinuddin, & Khan, 2024),
reshape bargaining positions within ecosystems (Kulkov et al., 2024),
and align competitive strategies with sustainability objectives (Rivera-
Valle & Silva, 2024).
Most existing studies conceptualise structural power primarily
through physical, financial, or intangible organisational resources (See
Table A1 in the Appendix file). However, there is limited investigation
into the role of technological resources—particularly Industry 4.0
drivers —as sources of structural power. Moreover, few studies explic-
itly integrate RDT and ST to explain how technological in-
terdependencies reshape power structures in B2B networks. For
instance, Ku, Kim, and Oh (2024) demonstrated that in disaster response
networks, structural power emerges from organisations’ ability to both
provide and attract critical resources, consistent with the RDT that
power derives from control over scarce resources. Similarly, Keegan
et al. (2022) applied power dependence theory to the context of AI
adoption in B2B marketing, demonstrating how technological adoption
redistributes structural power among firms, suppliers, and tech giants.
Their findings illustrate how interdependencies shape bargaining posi-
tions, aligning with ST’s emphasis on interconnectedness and feedback
mechanisms. Additional studies, such as Cooiman (2024) and Adamik
and Sikora-fernandez (2021), indirectly support this perspective by
linking structural power to systemic financial logics and smart organi-
sations’ role in competitiveness and sustainability. These insights rein-
force the need to adopt RDT and ST to explain how structural power
arises and evolves, particularly in the context of Industry 4.0 drivers.
2.2. Industry 4.0
Industry 4.0 was introduced in 2011 at an exhibition in Hanover,
Germany (e.g., Amoozad Mahdiraji, Sharifpour Arabi, Beheshti, and
Vrontis (2023); Sharifpour, Ghaseminezhad, Hashemi-Tabatabaei, and
Amiri (2022)), and it has transformative technologies that can be used at
all levels of the production process, from product development to
completion (Amoozad Mahdiraji, Sharifpour Arabi, & Vrontis, 2024).
The aim of Industry 4.0 is to create a smart factory that enables busi-
nesses to integrate customer' direct needs into their production opera-
tions. The smart factory also uses digital and technological tools to
connect production systems across industries and institutions and to
continuously improve production (Lu, 2025). Thanks to Industry 4.0,
the potential of structural power has increased, enabling its effective
use. Furthermore, the B2B sector can use Industry 4.0 drivers to align its
innovation and service strategies. This strengthens B2B services by
providing incentives, thereby increasing the effectiveness of strategies
(Bortoluzzi, Chiarvesio, Romanello, & Tabacco, 2022). Moreover, In-
dustry 4.0 drivers can moderate the impact of power performance on
B2B marketing (Amoozad Mahdiraji, Sharifpour Arabi, Duan, & Vrontis,
2025). Since power is considered an inherent feature of inter-
organisational relationships and networks, its asymmetry with organ-
isational processes and goals weakens the organisation (Siemieniako
et al., 2023). Therefore, Industry 4.0 drivers can be used to establish a
balance of power in organisations. In addition, the development of
sustainability goals in contemporary businesses is increasing signifi-
cantly, driven by Industry 4.0 (Zopounidis & Lemonakis, 2024). Hence,
this study aims to identify the drivers in the era of Industry 4.0 that will
moderate the structural power and engender sustainability in B2B
marketing.
2.3. Sustainable marketing
Sustainability is a concept that is defined as creating and developing
the needs of the present without harming the ability of future genera-
tions to meet their needs (Thiele, 2024). Sustainability in business
prompts organisations to expand their environmental and social di-
mensions by shifting their exclusive focus from financial performance.
By doing so, organisations can develop changes that achieve the goals of
both stakeholders and shareholders simultaneously, thereby increasing
their satisfaction (Rathee & Milfeld, 2024). Furthermore, these changes
are realised through the alignment of economic, social, and environ-
mental goals, which are the basic pillars of sustainability (Tseng et al.,
2020). Economic goals are the objectives and long-term profitability of
organisations. In this way, organisations can eliminate their competitive
advantage in the long term and increase their profits (Abraham, 2024).
Fulfilling customer demands and product customisation are social goals
in sustainability (Gajdzik, Siwiec, Wolniak, & Pacana, 2024). The
environmental pillar requires organisations to utilise clean energy that is
not hazardous to produce products and to minimise environmental harm
(Niu, Dong, Niu, & Wasif Zafar, 2024). Sustainability plays a crucial role
in businesses, enabling product quality improvement and ensuring that
production aligns with consumers' expectations. This strengthens the
interaction between businesses and their customers (Gogia, Gupta, &
Rathi, 2024). Organisations can introduce innovative and creative skills
through sustainability and create new customer experiences as a result
(Meehan, 2024).
Sustainability in marketing emerged in the 2000s and led to a shift in
marketing practices to internalise social and environmental costs
(White, Cakanlar, Sethi, & Trudel, 2025). This perspective helps reduce
social and environmental constraints in marketing by offering solutions
to optimise these dimensions. Moreover, integrating sustainability into
marketing has supported the creation of long-term value and the
development of lasting relationships with customers and stakeholders
(Kemper & Ballantine, 2019). Finally, sustainable marketing was
introduced, specifically addressing environmental concerns and the
integration of sustainable development within marketing. Its core focus
is meeting customer needs, achieving organisational goals, and aligning
processes with the ecosystem. Sustainable marketing is centred around
these three pillars when planning, executing, and managing product
development, pricing, promotion, and distribution (Heath & McKechnie,
2019).
In this study, sustainable marketing refers to the integration of
environmental, social, and economic dimensions into marketing prac-
tices (Rastogi, Agarwal, & Gopal, 2024). B2B marketing refers to mar-
keting activities in inter-organisational contexts, focusing on buyer
behaviour, value creation, innovation, and relationship management
across networks (Bamberger, Reinartz, & Ulaga, 2025). The sustain-
ability of B2B marketing emerges when these two perspectives intersect,
focusing on how firms embed sustainability principles into inter-

2.4. Sustainable marketing in B2B contexts
While sustainable marketing has traditionally been discussed within
consumer markets, its application in B2B contexts presents distinct
challenges and opportunities. B2B marketing differs fundamentally from
business-to-consumer (B2C) marketing due to the nature of the buyers,
the complexity of decision-making processes, and the centrality of long-
term inter-organisational relationships (e.g., Casidy and Lie (2023);
Homburg, Stierl, and Bornemann (2013)). In this setting, sustainable
marketing cannot be reduced to appealing to end consumers’ environ-
mental or social concerns. Instead, it involves embedding sustainability
within supply chains, industrial processes, and networks of firms that
interact to co-create value.
First, sustainability in B2B marketing is closely tied to buyers’
behaviour and procurement practices (Dhanawade, 2024). Organisa-
tional buyers, unlike individual consumers, make decisions based on a
combination of economic efficiency, regulatory compliance, reputa-
tional considerations, and stakeholder pressure. For example, many in-
dustrial firms face increasing demands from regulators and global
customers to demonstrate environmental responsibility and reduce
carbon emissions across their supply chains (Rathobei, Ran¨angen, &
Lindman, 2024). Consequently, B2B sustainable marketing entails
communicating and delivering value propositions that highlight not
only cost-effectiveness but also the ability to contribute to a partner’s
own sustainability goals (Chowdhury, Balaraman, & Liu, 2023). This
differs from B2C sustainability, which often emphasises product-level
features (e.g., eco-friendly packaging). Second, sustainable B2B mar-
keting is inherently linked to innovation and inter-organisational
collaboration (Staniskiene, Stankeviciute, & Daunoriene, 2019). Firms
in B2B markets often co-develop solutions with partners, integrating
technologies and knowledge to reduce resource consumption, design
circular processes, or create renewable alternatives (Escher & Brzuste-
wicz, 2020). Such collaborative innovation reflects a networked view of
marketing, where sustainability becomes a shared agenda among firms
rather than an isolated organisational initiative. This network orienta-
tion positions B2B sustainable marketing as a key driver of structural
power, since firms that provide indispensable sustainable technologies
or practices can strengthen their influence in inter-organisational
systems.
Third, the value creation logic in B2B sustainable marketing is
broader than in consumer markets. Instead of focusing primarily on
customer satisfaction, B2B firms must demonstrate how sustainable
practices generate value for multiple stakeholders—including suppliers,
distributors, regulators, and end users (Andersen, Åberg, & Bujac,
2023). This is consistent with the triple bottom line perspective, where
sustainability is not only an ethical responsibility but also a means of
ensuring long-term competitiveness. For instance, blockchain-enabled
traceability or IoT-enabled energy efficiency can both enhance sus-
tainability and improve relational trust within B2B networks (Moosa,
Ali, Alaswad, Elmedany, & Balakrishna, 2023). Fourth, digitalisation
and Industry 4.0 drivers further amplify the role of sustainability in B2B
marketing. Unlike B2C settings, where technologies often reshape
customer experiences (Rejikumar & Asokan-Ajitha, 2022), in B2B mar-
kets, digital technologies reconfigure entire value chains (Huo, Ioniț˘a,
Onișor, & Stancu, 2024). Big data analytics enable firms to forecast
demand more accurately and minimise waste; cloud computing en-
hances collaborative resource sharing across partners; blockchain se-
cures and verifies sustainability claims in transactions; and IoT devices
optimise industrial processes to reduce environmental impact (e.g.,
Shankar and Gupta (2024); Keegan et al. (2022)). By embedding such
technologies into marketing strategies, B2B firms can achieve both
sustainability objectives and consolidate their power in inter-
organisational relationships.
Finally, sustainable marketing in B2B contexts requires a
relationship-management perspective. B2B exchange relationships are
typically long-term, trust-based, and contractually complex (Ha, 2020).
Incorporating sustainability into these relationships can act as a source
of differentiation and strengthen competitive advantage. For example,
firms that consistently demonstrate sustainable practices may be
perceived as more reliable partners, resulting in stronger relational
bonds and lower transaction costs. Conversely, firms failing to adopt
sustainability may risk exclusion from key supply chains or collaborative
networks (Fobbe & Hilletofth, 2021). Thus, B2B sustainable marketing is
not merely about promoting “green” initiatives but about safeguarding
access to markets and alliances in an era where sustainability has
become a condition of participation. In a nutshell, sustainable marketing
in B2B contexts represents a distinct and evolving field that integrates
buyers’ behaviour, inter-organisational collaboration, innovation, and
digital transformation into a cohesive framework. It underscores how
sustainability is increasingly shaping the structural power dynamics of
B2B markets, giving certain firms influence and resilience by enabling
them to align with economic, social, and environmental priorities. This
perspective is central to the present study, as it situates Industry 4.0
drivers within the disciplinary boundaries of B2B marketing rather than
treating sustainability solely as a generic or consumer-focused concern.
2.5. Competitive advantage
Competitive advantage is the ability that positions organisations
ahead of their competitors over the long term, creating value for both
stakeholders and consumers (Hart & Rodgers, 2024). According to
Porter (1996), competitive advantage can be achieved in various ways,
such as lower costs (cost leadership), offering a unique product or ser-
vice (differentiation), or a targeted strategy focused on a specific market.
This approach places an organisation in a strategic position difficult for
others to replicate. In today’s business environment—particularly in
B2B contexts—competitive advantage often involves intangible factors
such as innovation, responsiveness to social and environmental expec-
tations, and effective knowledge management (e.g., Yuen and Lam
(2024); Stan, Țîțu, and Paraschiv (2024)).
Researchers acknowledge that integrating Industry 4.0 drivers into
B2B marketing can be a powerful strategy to achieve sustainability-
based competitive advantages (e.g., Ed-Dafali, Al-Azad, Mohiuddin,
and Reza (2023); Sidek et al. (2020); Purwanti, Abadi, and Suyanto
(2019)). Drivers such as big data analytics, blockchain, and cloud
computing are transforming customer insights and boosting organisa-
tional performance. In the B2B context, these technologies support the
development of strategies that create added value—crucial for building
long-term inter-organisational relationships (Ulhaq, 2025). Therefore,
sustainable marketing emerges as a strategic approach that aligns mar-
keting activities with environmental and social goals, fostering differ-
entiation and trust among business partners. Additionally, in B2B
sustainable marketing, Industry 4.0 can enhance structural power by
improving resource control, network coordination, and responsiveness
to market changes (Nayal et al., 2023). This enables organisations to
build resilient value networks, positioning B2B sustainable marketing as
a strategic tool for leveraging technological capabilities to achieve sus-
tainable competitive advantage.

3. Conceptual framework
The convergence of Industry 4.0, structural power, and sustainability
in B2B marketing represents a significant paradigm shift in how firms
build and maintain competitive advantage. Structural power, broadly
defined as the ability of firms to shape inter-organisational dependencies
and market structures (e.g., Casidy and Lie (2023); Homburg et al.
(2013)), has historically rested on access to resources, embeddedness
within networks, and the establishment of trust. However, the digital
transformation associated with Industry 4.0 has redefined these foun-
dations by introducing new technological drivers of autonomy, coop-
eration, and strategic positioning. Digital drivers such as the IoT, big
data analytics, blockchain, cloud computing, and smart services provide
firms with advanced tools to enhance visibility across supply chains,
optimise resource flows, and improve coordination within complex
networks (e.g., Shankar and Gupta (2024); Ros´ario and Dias (2022);
Dubey et al. (2019)). These drivers not only support operational effi-
ciency but also extend the ways in which organisations exert structural
power by reducing external dependence and reshaping stakeholder
relationships.
Theoretical grounding for this perspective can be derived from
Resource Dependence Theory (RDT) (Hillman et al., 2009) and Systems
Theory (ST) (Bertalanffy, 1968). RDT explains how firms seek to mini-
mise vulnerabilities by securing greater control over resources, a process
increasingly facilitated by Industry 4.0 drivers such as big data and
cloud computing (e.g., Saraswat and Choudhari (2025); Keegan et al.
(2022)). ST, on the other hand, underscores the interconnectedness of
organisational subsystems, highlighting that digitalisation and sustain-
ability initiatives must be examined holistically rather than in isolation
(Tsiavos & Kitsios, 2022). Taken together, these perspectives illuminate
how Industry 4.0 drivers operate as both enablers of structural power
and mechanisms for achieving sustainability. Importantly, this study
situates these dynamics within the broader domain of sustainable B2B
marketing. Unlike B2C contexts, B2B marketing is characterised by
complex decision-making processes, interdependence, and long-term
relational exchanges (Homburg et al., 2013). Integrating sustainability
into B2B marketing extends these relationships by embedding environ-
mental stewardship, social equity, and economic resilience into inter-
organisational practices (e.g., White et al. (2025); Kemper and Ballan-
tine (2019)). This aligns with the triple bottom line (TBL) framework,
which emphasises that organisational success depends not only on
economic performance but also on environmental and social outcomes
(e.g., Thiele (2024); Tseng et al. (2020)). Accordingly, the rationale for
the conceptual framework lies in bridging digitalisation, power dy-
namics, and sustainability, demonstrating that Industry 4.0 drivers are
not neutral tools but active enablers of resilience, legitimacy, and long-
term value in B2B markets.
The conceptual framework (See Fig. 1) illustrates how Industry 4.0
drivers—such as the IoT, blockchain, big data, cloud computing, and
other digital technologies—reshape the dynamics of structural power in
B2B marketing. Rooted in RDT and ST, the framework provides an in-
tegrated rationale for understanding why these technologies matter and
how they contribute to competitive and sustainability outcomes. From
the perspective of RDT, Industry 4.0 drivers are critical because they
represent essential resources that firms must secure and control to
reduce their dependence on external actors and increase their auton-
omy. Access to and deployment of advanced digital resources enhance
bargaining power, resource allocation efficiency, and firms’ ability to
influence interorganisational networks. This explains why resources
matter and clarifies the role of Industry 4.0 as a source of structural
advantage in B2B contexts.
Complementing this, ST emphasises the importance of integration
and adaptation. In B2B networks, organisations do not operate in
isolation, but rather as interdependent systems, where coordination,
feedback loops, and adaptation to environmental changes are essential.
Industry 4.0 drivers enable such systemic integration by fostering in-
formation flows, strengthening cooperation, and allowing firms to adapt
to evolving sustainability and market requirements. Thus, ST provides a
rationale for adopting and embedding these resources to sustain
organisational viability and resilience. Within the framework, structural
power factors—including commitment and cooperation, resource allo-
cation, and strategic assessment—serve as mediating mechanisms. In-
dustry 4.0 drivers shape these factors by strengthening relational trust,
improving coordination, and enhancing analytical capabilities. In suc-
cession, these factors enable the achievement of strategic outcomes. The
strategic outcomes are twofold. First, firms gain a competitive advan-
tage through efficiency improvements, opportunities for differentiation,
and stronger market positioning. Industry 4.0 drivers do not inherently
generate advantage; rather, they reshape inter-organisational de-
pendencies, resource control, and coordination mechanisms. By
strengthening structural power—through improved resource allocation,
commitment, cooperation, and organisational capabilities—firms
enhance their ability to influence network structures, stabilise partner-
ships, and position themselves more favourably within B2B ecosystems.
Competitive advantage thus emerges indirectly, as firms with greater

The conceptual framework also guides the research design. The SLR

4. Methodology
The objectives of this study are twofold: (i) to identify the drivers of
Industry 4.0 that influence structural power in sustainability-oriented
B2B marketing, and (ii) to model and investigate the effects of these
drivers on structural power factors within sustainability-driven B2B
strategies. A mixed-methods approach was employed to achieve these
goals, with a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) and Thematic Analysis
(TA) (qualitative) used to extract the drivers. This approach provides
reliable results (Amoozad Mahdiraji, Sharifpour Arabi, & Vrontis,
2024); therefore, it was employed in this manuscript. Moreover, Fuzzy
Cognitive Map (FCM) models (quantitative) examine the relationships
among the drivers. This method can create a model based on expert
knowledge and environmental phenomena and determine the relation-
ships among criteria (¨Ozesmi & ¨Ozesmi, 2004). It also provides a causal
picture of facts, processes, and goals (N´apoles, Espinosa, Grau, & Van-
hoof, 2018). Hence, FCM is used to properly analyse the drivers of In-
dustry 4.0 so that managers can use its results to adopt appropriate
organisational strategies. Finally, the Technique for Order of Preference
by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) (quantitative) determines the
influence of Industry 4.0 drivers on structural power (Alghassab, 2022).
Therefore, TOPSIS is utilised to accurately evaluate the impact of In-
dustry 4.0 drivers on structural power factors. The research questions
outlined in the Introduction are structured to follow a logical progres-
sion from conceptual identification to empirical validation and finally to
sustainability-oriented implications. The first question establishes the
conceptual foundation by conducting an SLR to identify the Industry 4.0
drivers that may influence structural power in B2B marketing. Building
on this foundation, the second question empirically examines the in-
terrelationships between these drivers and structural power factors, as
well as their relative influence, using expert evaluation and multiple-
criteria decision-making (MCDM) methods (FCM and TOPSIS). The
third question then extends the analysis by exploring how the identified
drivers contribute to the broader sustainability agenda across economic,
environmental, and social dimensions. This sequential design ensures
coherence across the study: the first question identifies relevant drivers,
the second prioritises and maps their effects, and the third situates these
effects within the triple bottom line. Fig. 2 illustrates the research
framework of this study, which will be described subsequently.
The First phase. This phase focused on identifying Industry 4.0
drivers that influence structural power in sustainability-oriented B2B
marketing. In this phase, the SLR was conducted to explore the in-
tersections of structural power, Industry 4.0, and sustainability within
the B2B marketing context, thereby identifying key drivers. In this
study, a driver refers to a technology enabler—specifically, an Industry
4.0 technology or capability (e.g., blockchain, big data, smart services)
that initiates or accelerates change. These drivers influence organisa-
tional behaviour, resource access, and decision-making processes, and
are viewed as tools or mechanisms that can reshape the distribution or
exercise of structural power among firms.
Due to the limited research on the relationship between Industry 4.0
and structural power, it was not feasible to directly identify the drivers
of Industry 4.0 in terms of structural power. Consequently, an SLR was
first conducted on structural power to identify the contributing factors.
In this study, structural power factors refer to the key organisational and
relational dimensions through which firms exercise influence in inter-
organisational settings. Subsequently, an SLR was conducted on the
drivers of Industry 4.0 in sustainable marketing for two primary reasons.
Firstly, this approach enables the identification of drivers of Industry 4.0
that influence structural power. Secondly, the identified drivers will
enhance the performance of structural power and support the achieve-
ment of sustainability objectives. The SLR was, therefore, executed in
two stages.

provides clarity in the SLR with brief and efficient steps (Agrawal et al.,

enhancing the reliability of the analysis. (iv) The concepts were inter-
preted and extracted (Castleberry & Nolen, 2018) based on the authors’
and experts’ knowledge. (v) The research questions were considered
(Naeem, Ozuem, Howell, & Ranfagni, 2023). In this way, six structural

To advance the perspectives of RDT and ST, this study examines the
identified factors of structural power through these perspectives. These
perspectives are inherently complementary in their treatment of inter-
organisational power and dynamics. From the RDT perspective, power
arises from controlling critical resources and managing dependencies
(Hillman et al., 2009). Resource allocation, strategic assessment, and
organisational capabilities reflect the firm’s ability to navigate, shape,
and reduce uncertainty in its external environment. Concurrently, from
the ST perspective, structural power emerges through the organisation’s
ability to maintain coordination, adaptability, and coherence within a
broader network of actors (Gharajedaghi, 2011). Factors such as
commitment, cooperation, and the fostering of strong connections
reflect the systemic nature of interdependence, feedback loops, and the
alignment of collective goals. By integrating these two perspectives, this
study conceptualises structural power as the ability of a focal firm to
shape network dynamics and resource flows in digitally transformed
B2B ecosystems. These transformations in structural power ultimately
contribute to sustainability by improving efficiency (economic), pro-
moting transparency and trust (social), and enabling better resource use
(environmental). Therefore, the conceptual framework of this study
views Industry 4.0 drivers as enablers that mediate structural power and
sustainability.

There is no universally agreed-upon answer among decision-making
and operations research professionals regarding the ideal sample size or
number of respondents. The appropriate number of experts depends on
several factors, including the study's type and scope, the number of
criteria, the complexity of the issue, and the level of detail required in
the analysis (Jafari-Sadeghi, Amoozad Mahdiraji, Budhwar, & Vrontis,
2022). For instance, the sample size in statistical research is large (Lantz,
2013). In contrast, studies using MCDM methods often rely on a smaller
sample, as the focus is on expert judgement rather than broad statistical
generalisation (e.g., Sharifpour, Aghajani, and Safaei Ghadikolaei
(2020)). Some studies suggest that the opinions of a small group of key
managers—based on their expertise and intuition—are sufficient to
produce reliable and relevant results, especially when addressing
intangible and qualitative indicators. This is because such managers are
often responsible for critical decisions within the company, making their
insights particularly valuable (e.g., Du and Shen (2024); Palomares, Liu,
Xu, and Martínez (2012)). Consequently, since 2000, numerous re-
searchers have employed decision-making techniques in their studies,
often with small sample sizes, such as five experts (S. Khan, Khan, &
Haleem, 2019), six experts (Shamsadini, Askari Shahamabad, & Askari
Shahamabad, 2023), or ten experts (Mohammadfam, Khajevandi, Deh-
ghani, Babamiri, & Farhadian, 2022). Given that all participants are
active and certified professionals in their fields, some researchers argue
that involving two to ten experts is sufficient, as larger groups may lead
to inconsistencies and conflicting views. Others believe that engaging
three to fifteen experts provides more reliable, diverse, and compre-
hensive results. Therefore, there is no universally accepted standard for
the number of experts, and the appropriate size may vary depending on
the study's context and objectives.

Although there are other methods in this field, such as DEMATEL
(Decision-Making Trial and Evaluation Laboratory) (Thakkar & Thak-

Sureeyatanapas, 2024), this method has significant advantages. While
FCM can update conceptual values in dynamically evolving systems
(Motlagh, Jamaludin, Tang, & Khaksar, 2015), ISM and DEMATEL
cannot directly simulate them, as they focus more on structural re-
lationships (Guan, Dong, Shen, Gao, & Chen, 2022). Two-way re-
lationships are captured, where cyclical factors influence each other,
and feedback loops are modelled in FCM (Van Vliet et al., 2017).
However, ISM and DEMATEL primarily emphasise hierarchical or
directional influence. Furthermore, FCM is widely used in social, eco-
nomic, and engineering applications due to its flexibility in both quan-
titative and qualitative modelling. ISM and DEMATEL are better suited
to addressing technical problems and making hierarchical decisions.
Meanwhile, FCM uses cause-and-effect knowledge to model complex
human decision-making systems (Dolatabad, Mahdiraji, Babgohari,
Garza-Reyes, & Ai, 2022). This results in directional graphs that show
the positive and negative impacts of concepts on one another. Since the
graph in FCM combines fuzzy logic and neural networks, it can effec-
tively address complex problems and accurately predict system perfor-
mance in real-world environments (Edinson & Muthuraj, 2018). Experts'
experiences and knowledge related to the subject are latent in FCM
models, and the model's cause-and-effect relationships are formed from
them (Orang, de Lima e Silva, & Guimar˜aes, 2023). Therefore, this
model can be used for high-level managerial decisions. Moreover, the
application of fuzzy logic in cognitive mapping has enabled data
collection and processing to be based on fuzzy logic (Papageorgiou &
Stylios, 2008), thereby increasing the reliability of the results. Hence,
FCM was applied to create a quality model and reveal the impact of
Industry 4.0 drivers on structural power and sustainability.
In FCM, Wij represents the weight of the relationship between two
criteria in the cause-and-effect. Assume that ai and aj are two criteria;
Wij indicates how much criterion ai affects criterion aj. If Wij = 0, there
is no relationship between the criteria. If Wij > 0, it means that
increasing or decreasing the measure ai leads to increasing or decreasing
the value of aj; hence, there is a direct (positive) causal relationship
between the criteria. If Wij < 0, there is an inverse causal relationship
between the measures. This means that increasing the value of ai will
decrease the value of aj and vice versa (N´apoles, Rankovi´c, & Salgueiro,
2023). Expert opinions are used to determine Wij. Experts complete the

between -1 and + 1. These processes are accomplished through Eqs. 1

5. Findings
The SLR-TA was applied to identify the structural power factors and
Industry 4.0 drivers in the sustainability of B2B marketing. Accordingly,
six structural power factors and eight drivers of Industry 4.0 were
identified in Tables 2 and 3. Then, FCM was employed to analyse
structural power factors. The experts completed the FCM questionnaire.
The experts provided their opinions on the relationships among the
criteria, expressed as Triangular Fuzzy Numbers (TFNs). Eqs. 1 and 2
were then applied for defuzzification. Based on the experts' average
opinions, the interactive matrix in Fig. 5 displays the FCM's adjacency
matrix. The intensity of the colour represents the strength (weight) of
the relationships between concepts.
In the FCM analysis, “Commitment and Cooperation” was deliber-
ately frozen to simulate the system's behaviour under stable cooperative
dynamics. As one of the core structural power factors, this variable
represents the baseline trust and relational strength in inter-
organisational networks. This approach provided a controlled setting
to isolate causal effects and enhance the interpretability and credibility
of the scenario analysis. Each node in FCM has three characteristics: (i)
input degree, (ii) output degree, and (iii) centrality. The input degree is
the absolute sum of the weights influencing the node (Apostolopoulos
et al., 2024), while the output degree represents the sum of a node's
effects on other nodes (Paraskevas, Madas, & Nikolaidis, 2025). Cen-
trality is calculated by summing the input and output degrees
(Sarmiento et al., 2024). Additionally, the linear normalisation method
was employed to normalise the centrality of the structural power factors
to determine their weights (Hosseini Dolatabad et al., 2023). These
features for each structural power factor were computed using a Python
script in Jupyter, as shown in Table 5.
The results indicate a relatively balanced system, with all factors
classified as ordinary elements, meaning they both influence and are
influenced by other factors within the network. Among them, Fostering
Strong Connections exhibits the highest centrality (3.64) and weight
(0.178), highlighting its role as a key integrative mechanism through
which influence circulates across the system. Collaboration Among the
Actors (centrality = 3.52) and Commitment and Cooperation (centrality
= 3.44) also demonstrate strong systemic relevance, underscoring the
importance of relational and cooperative dimensions in shaping struc-
tural power in B2B contexts.

6. Discussion and implications
Beyond methodological integration, the primary theoretical contri-
bution of this study lies in explaining how Industry 4.0 enablers
reconfigure structural power within B2B exchange systems. By linking
RDT and ST, the findings advance B2B marketing theory by showing
how power emerges from systemic interdependencies rather than iso-
lated firm-level capabilities. This study examined how Industry 4.0
drivers influence structural power and, in turn, support sustainability-
oriented strategies in B2B marketing. Drawing on expert evaluations
treated as a single, integrated sample, the findings indicate that the
integration of horizontal and vertical processes plays a central role in
reinforcing structural power across inter-organisational relationships.
This driver consistently exerts strong influence across multiple struc-
tural power factors, underscoring the importance of coordinated pro-
cesses, aligned decision-making, and systemic integration in B2B
exchange contexts. In addition, drivers such as blockchain, upgraded
CRM systems, and smart services emerge as influential enablers by
strengthening resource coordination, enhancing relationship manage-
ment, and supporting inter-organisational collaboration. Together,
these drivers contribute to greater transparency, trust, and alignment
among exchange partners, thereby reinforcing the foundations of
structural power and supporting sustainability-oriented strategies in
B2B markets.
At the same time, the findings suggest that not all Industry 4.0
drivers contribute equally to the formation of structural power. While
data-intensive technologies such as cloud computing and big data ana-
lytics, as well as immersive tools such as mixed reality, remain impor-
tant components of digital transformation, their influence appears to be
more supportive than transformative in shaping power structures. These
drivers primarily enhance operational efficiency, analytical capacity,
and information availability, rather than directly reconfiguring in-
terdependencies or relational dynamics. Overall, the results indicate
that structural power in B2B marketing is most strongly reinforced by
Industry 4.0 drivers that promote systemic integration, coordination,
and relationship-oriented value creation, rather than by standalone
technological capabilities. This insight underscores the importance of
aligning digital investments with broader relational and sustainability
objectives in B2B markets.
These findings also provide explicit answers to the research questions
posed in this study. RQ1, which asked which Industry 4.0 drivers in-
fluence structural power in sustainable B2B marketing, was addressed
by identifying process integration as the dominant driver, com-
plemented by blockchain, upgraded CRM, cloud computing, and smart
services. RQ2, which explored how these drivers interact with structural
power factors, was answered by demonstrating that collaboration,
transparency, and systemic alignment are strengthened by these drivers,
thereby enhancing firms’ ability to manage resources and inter-firm
dependencies. Building on the insights from Iranian and Chinese ex-
perts, the analysis highlights that Industry 4.0 drivers are not merely
technical tools but strategic enablers of structural power, sustainability,
and competitive advantage. To extend these findings into a broader
theoretical contribution, we articulate several propositions that capture
the mechanisms through which Industry 4.0 drivers reshape organisa-
tional dynamics. These propositions synthesise expert perspectives with
the study’s analytical results, offering a foundation for future research
and managerial practice.

they actively reshape organisational power structures. By strengthening
both developmental capacity (efficiency, coordination, customer reach)
and autonomy (independence from government and foreign in-
stitutions), these drivers enable firms to enhance their long-term sta-
bility and resilience.

7. Implications
7.1. Theoretical Implications
This study advances the understanding of structural power in inter-
organisational contexts by demonstrating how Industry 4.0 drivers
shape the foundations of influence and dependence. By integrating RDT
and ST, the analysis explains how drivers such as IoT, Big Data, and
blockchain reshape structural power factors, particularly in areas such
as resource allocation, commitment, cooperation, and organisational
capabilities (e.g., D. H. Zhu and Westphal (2021); Kraus et al. (2021);
Nandi et al. (2021)). The findings clarify strategies to increase structural
power, showing that commitment and cooperation are critical levers
influencing other factors (e.g., Haug et al. (2024)). These relational
factors stabilise brands and enhance popularity (Daryadi, Dandono, &
Setiasih, 2023), while strong buyer–seller relationships remain central
to maintaining and extending power (Siemieniako et al., 2023). The role
of the driver in reinforcing these dynamics is also evident: IoT automates
processes (e.g., Taylor, Reilly, and Wren (2020); Lampropoulos, Siakas,
and Anastasiadis (2019)), Big Data and cloud computing improve
analytical capacity and customer satisfaction (e.g., Qi, Sun, and Hosseini
(2022); Sivarajah, Irani, Gupta, and Mahroof (2020)), and blockchain,
mixed reality, and smart services enhance interaction and customer
experience (e.g., Rokhsaritalemi, Sadeghi-Niaraki, and Choi (2020)).
Upgraded CRM systems further reinforce relationship management,
consolidating structural power in digital contexts (Gazi et al., 2024).
The research also contributes to B2B marketing theory by extending
relational perspectives into digitally mediated ecosystems. Traditional
B2B frameworks highlight the importance of long-term relationships,
trust, and cooperation as the foundations of exchange (e.g., Casidy and
Lie (2023); Homburg et al. (2013)). This study builds on that foundation
by showing how Industry 4.0 drivers embed these relational constructs
into digital infrastructures. IoT, blockchain, and advanced CRM systems
foster real-time transparency, information exchange, and collaborative
processes, strengthening cooperation and commitment in B2B networks.
By integrating technological drivers with relational factors, the findings
reframe B2B marketing not only as a social process but also as a digitally
co-constructed ecosystem, expanding theoretical insights into how firms
achieve power, resilience, and long-term stability in an interconnected
marketplace. Meanwhile, this study advances B2B marketing and digital
transformation theory by positioning competitive advantage as an in-
direct outcome of Industry 4.0 adoption mediated by structural power.
Unlike prior research that treats digital technologies as direct perfor-
mance drivers, the findings demonstrate that competitive advantage
emerges when technologies enhance firms’ control over critical re-
sources, strengthen inter-organisational dependencies, and stabilise
network positions. By integrating RDT and ST, the study explains how
Industry 4.0 reshapes the structural conditions under which firms
compete, thereby extending theoretical understanding of competitive
advantage beyond firm-internal capabilities to network-embedded
power structures.
This study deepens digital transformation theory by shifting focus
from efficiency-driven outcomes to relational and strategic conse-
quences. Much of the extant literature positions digital transformation
as a tool for operational optimisation and internal agility (e.g., Xu,
Zhang, Sun, Tang, and Li (2024); AlNuaimi, Kumar Singh, Ren, Budh-
war, and Vorobyev (2022)). In contrast, the present findings show that
digitalisation also redistributes structural power between firms, as
control over data, platforms, and digital infrastructures becomes a
critical resource. Industry 4.0 drivers emerge not merely as efficiency-
enhancing tools but as strategic mechanisms that alter in-
terdependencies, reduce external reliance, and reconfigure network
dynamics. In this sense, digital transformation is reconceptualised as
both a technological and relational shift that redefines structural power

7.2. Managerial implications
The implications of this study are both practical and managerial. On
the one hand, the findings provide actionable strategies for practitioners
seeking to leverage Industry 4.0 drivers; on the other, they offer theo-
retically grounded insights for managers to align structural power with
digital transformation initiatives. From a practical perspective, this
research provides a strategic guide on utilising Industry 4.0 drivers to
enhance structural power in B2B networks. For B2B marketing man-
agers, the results highlight the strategic importance of viewing Industry
4.0 drivers as enablers of relationship management rather than stand-
alone technologies. Investments in digital integration, data sharing, and
collaborative platforms should be evaluated for their ability to enhance
inter-organisational coordination, strengthen commitment and cooper-
ation, and reduce asymmetries in dependency with key partners. Such
an approach aligns digital transformation initiatives with core B2B
marketing objectives, including relationship stability, trust building,
and long-term value creation. The findings also suggest that managers
should prioritise Industry 4.0 drivers that support systemic alignment
across buyers, suppliers, and other network actors. By embedding digital
capabilities into relationship governance structures—such as joint
planning, transparent performance monitoring, and collaborative deci-
sion-making—firms can enhance structural power in ways that support
both sustainability goals and durable competitive advantage in B2B
markets.
Meanwhile, B2B practitioners should view Industry 4.0 drivers as
functional tools and strategic assets that strengthen relationships, build
trust, and enhance adaptability in complex B2B settings. These insights
help decision-makers design targeted actions to boost efficiency and
secure their firm’s role in sustainable supply chains. Furthermore, each
Industry 4.0 driver enhances structural power in two ways: (i) by
developing organisational power and maturity, and (ii) by increasing
autonomy while reducing reliance on government bodies. This supports
long-term stability and resilience. Table 8 summarises these insights
based on expert views and the authors' understanding. Experts warned
that failing to adopt these drivers can weaken organisational power,
create major gaps, and risk bankruptcy.
From a managerial perspective, organisations are not self-sufficient

application of Industry 4.0 drivers in B2B marketing alters the dynamics
of dependencies (Grandinetti et al., 2020), autonomy and power can be
gained and extended throughout the entire organisation in this sector.
Industry 4.0 drivers can bypass traditional businesses by analysing
customer data in real time and gaining a competitive advantage.
Furthermore, B2B marketers can create and manage their strategic re-
sources by investing in Industry 4.0 drivers. This protects them from
external disruptions or pressures and can increase their bargaining
power in competitive ecosystems.
Meanwhile, the perspective of RDT can highlight the importance of
structural power in organisations (Bolleyer & Correa, 2022). For
instance, since supplier relationships and strategic partnerships are
important in B2B marketing (Wieteska, 2016), Industry 4.0 drivers can

8. Conclusion and future recommendations
This study examined how Industry 4.0 drivers influence structural
power through the lenses of RDT and ST, thereby contributing to the
development of both perspectives and addressing a gap in the existing
literature. It offers a theoretical contribution by linking two underex-
plored areas: (i) structural power and (ii) Industry 4.0 within sustainable
B2B contexts. The findings demonstrate that Industry 4.0 drivers
contribute to sustainability and competitive advantage in B2B markets
primarily by shaping structural power. Rather than directly generating
advantage, these drivers enable firms to reshape resource dependencies,
enhance collaboration, and strengthen their position within inter-
organisational networks. By clarifying the pathway from Industry 4.0
drivers to structural power and, subsequently, to sustainability-oriented
competitive advantage, this research resolves a key gap in the literature
and provides a coherent framework for understanding competition in
digitally enabled B2B ecosystems.
RQ1 explored which Industry 4.0 drivers influence structural power
in sustainable B2B marketing. This was addressed through SLR and TA,
which identified eight drivers (IoT, Big Data, Cloud Computing, Block-
chain, Smart Services, Mixed Reality, Upgraded CRM, and Integration of
Horizontal and Vertical Processes). Expert panels confirmed their rele-
vance, and TOPSIS provided weighted rankings, with integration pro-
cesses emerging as the most critical driver. RQ2 examined how these
drivers interact with structural power factors such as commitment,
cooperation, and organisational capabilities. FCM and expert panel
validation demonstrated the causal interrelationships, revealing that
drivers such as blockchain and smart services enhance cooperation and
trust, while upgraded CRM and integration processes strengthen
resource allocation and autonomy. This clarified the pathways through
which Industry 4.0 shapes power dynamics between firms. RQ3 inves-
tigated the experts’ insights from Iran and China to understand cross-
contextual perspectives. The findings demonstrated that Industry 4.0
drivers are not isolated tools, but rather enablers of structural power and
sustainability in B2B networks, contributing to both theoretical ad-
vancements and practical competitive advantages.
This study employed qualitative and quantitative methods to analyse
the structural power drivers of Industry 4.0. However, several limita-
tions point to opportunities for future research. Data were collected from
both primary and secondary sources. Primary data were gathered using
fuzzy linguistic variables and analysed through FCM, meaning the
quantitative results are based on expert judgement and intuition.
Therefore, future research is encouraged to incorporate numerical data
from empirical studies to more robustly assess the impact of Industry 4.0
drivers on structural power and sustainability. In this study, secondary
data were obtained through document and library research. Moving
forward, researchers may also consider conducting structured or semi-
structured interviews to gain deeper insights into expert perspectives
on how Industry 4.0 shapes structural power and supports sustainable
practices.
From a methodological perspective, this study employed a mixed-
methods approach, combining qualitative and quantitative methods to
address the research questions. An SLR-TA was used to gather qualita-
tive insights, FCM to explore cause-and-effect relationships, and TOPSIS
to assess the impact of Industry 4.0 drivers on structural power. How-
ever, SLR-TA has limitations in accessing diverse and comprehensive
data sources. Future research could address this by applying Grounded
Theory or Meta-Study methods. Additionally, while FCM was effective
in mapping relationships, it does not allow for the classification of
variables as autonomous, dependent, linkage, or inde-
pendent—highlighting another area for methodological enhancement in
future studies (Sharifpour et al., 2020). By identifying the type of each
variable, a more precise understanding of the drivers of Industry 4.0 can
be achieved, enabling more effective implementation. Therefore, future
researchers are recommended to consider using the ISM method, which
classifies variables as autonomous, dependent, linkage, or independent.
This approach can offer deeper insights and enrich the analysis in this
field (Sharifpour et al., 2022). Additionally, researchers can apply the
DEMATEL method to identify cause-and-effect relationships among
variables. This approach can complement and validate the findings of
this study by enabling a comparative analysis and offering further in-
sights into the influence of Industry 4.0 drivers on structural power
(Yaftiyan, Rassaf, Borchalouei, & Ghahremani, 2023).
Regarding the scope of this study, all experts were based in either
Iran, an emerging economy, or China, a developed economy. As a result,
the findings may be more relevant to other countries. Future research is
recommended in other emerging, developing, and developed countries
to enable comparison and strengthen the foundation for broader
generalisation. Lastly, regarding the research context, this study focused
on technological resources and their impact on power effectiveness and
sustainability. Future studies could expand this by examining the role of
other resources—such as human, financial, organisational, and phys-
ical—to offer a more comprehensive understanding of structural power
dynamics in Industry 4.0 environments. Therefore, future research could
explore the role of financial resources in relation to structural power and
sustainability, uncovering new challenges and opportunities. This would
open up fresh avenues for discovery and help keep the research field
dynamic and evolving. Additionally, as this study focused primarily on
the resource-based perspective, future work should also consider the
role of organisational capabilities to provide a more holistic view of how
firms develop and sustain structural power in the Industry 4.0 era
(Amoozad Mahdiraji, Yaftiyan, Abbasi-Kamardi, Vrontis, & Gong,
2024). Hence, in the future, researchers can expand this discussion by
addressing organisational capabilities and their impact on structural
power and sustainability.
While this study has focused on Industry 4.0 drivers in shaping the
structural power of B2B marketing, future research should extend the
scope to consider the implications of Industry 5.0. Unlike Industry 4.0,
which primarily emphasises automation, digitalisation, and efficiency,
Industry 5.0 introduces a human-centric, resilient, and sustainable
perspective (e.g., Yanytska (2025); Castillo, Otero-Romero, and Alvarez-
Palau (2025)). Its vision aligns strongly with the United Nations Sus-
tainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly through prioritising
social well-being, ethical technology use, and environmental steward-
ship (Jabeen, Goli, & Kafila., 2025). For instance, integrating human
creativity and machine intelligence could foster not only economic ef-
ficiency but also more inclusive innovation processes in B2B contexts.
Similarly, Industry 5.0 is expected to reinforce corporate responsibility
by embedding sustainability into value creation, stakeholder engage-
ment, and inter-organisational collaboration. Future studies may
therefore investigate how structural power in B2B networks evolves
when human-centricity, resilience, and sustainability become central
drivers of industrial transformation. Linking Industry 5.0 to sustain-
ability goals would provide valuable insights into firms' long-term
strategic positioning and the role of marketing in navigating this para-
digm shift.
