ABSTRACT
This paper addresses the emergence of greenwashing and examines its implications for the development of green
marketing strategies. It starts from the premise that companies face vigilant environmental activism when
developing B2B marketing strategies, focusing on identifying discrepancies between intended marketing mes-
sages and actual practices, as well as an increasingly tightening regulatory environment. The core of the paper is
a systematic review of interdisciplinary research on greenwashing in the B2B context with a focus on B2B
marketing implications. In particular, this study contributes to a modern understanding of greenwashing by
providing a new framework for the typology, drivers, and consequences of greenwashing. It also contrasts
theories that help explain the greenwashing phenomenon through an interdisciplinary perspective. Finally, this
study points out the gaps in existing research on this phenomenon and offers rich future research directions.
1. Introduction
The term “greenwashing” has been popularized so much that one can
use standard dictionaries to define it. The first wave of notable work on
the topic dates to the last decades of the 20th century and the early
2000s (Greer & Bruno, 1997; Laufer, 2003; Lubbers, 2002). Moreover,
the anti-greenwashing discourse has been also informed by the
advancement of environmental protection, one of the most momentous
developments for business regulation in the post-WW2 era (Adler,
2021). It has critically examined the shift in the perception and
discourse of businesses to rhetorically embrace and communicate prin-
ciples of sustainability and care for the planet and people, which has
been often juxtaposed contradicted by against the lingering negative
environmental impacts of their operations. Initially, MNEs had been
antagonistic towards environmentalist ideals; their shift in attitudes has
been a result of the combined pressures by movements and regulators,
and the disruptive actions from within the corporate field led by
exceptionally visionary managers (Abdin, 2015).
Greenwashing originates in the gap between symbolic (Martín-de
Castro, Amores-Salvad´o, Navas-L´opez, & Balarezo-Nu˜nez, 2017),
appearance-oriented version of environmental commitments on one
hand, and substantive corporate approaches to environmental impact on
the other (Truong, Mazloomi, & Berrone, 2021). In essence, it reflects
the inability of companies to substantiate their publicly made green
claims. As firms internationalize, their environmental disclosure in-
creases, but does not necessarily lead to increase in their substantive
environmental impact (Ellim¨aki, G´omez-Bola˜nos, Hurtado-Torres, &
Arag´on-Correa, 2021). It is in this gap between symbolic rhetoric on one
side, and lack of substantive practice and impact of the other, that the
suspicions and dissatisfaction among stakeholders is born, which then
leads to greenwashing accusations (Martín-de Castro, 2021). Notably,
greenwashing is not the only type of “washing” phenomenon that poses
a challenge for companies; our analysis also captures and compares
other washing phenomena.
Greenwashing can be the outcome of different malpractices, such as
selective disclosures; empty, irrelevant or unsubstantiated claims;
referring to dubious certifications; backing up or lobbying for environ-
mentally unfriendly regulation or collaborating with companies that
have negative environmental impact (Nemes et al., 2022). Paradoxi-
cally, firms with poorer environmental track record, or those who carry
a stigma of not being committed to the environment, face less backlash
compared to those who are perceived as environmental leaders but at
some point are accused for greenwashing (Kassinis, Kay, Papagiannakis,
& Vlachos, 2022). It is by now established that markets disfavor
greenwashing (Du, 2015; Testa, Miroshnychenko, Barontini, & Frey,
2018), however, the damage done by greenwashing exceeds the impact
on financial performance and needs to be seen as protruding into
different facets of the operation of the firm and its positioning in the

greenwashing discourse, as relevant on all four levels, radically trans-
forming the totality of the environment in which businesses operate.
Such definition then informs our literature review selection.
Greenwashing encompasses not just companies' unsubstantiated
green claims, but also stakeholders' perceptions and accusations, which
can significantly impact the companies themselves, industries, and
marketing practices, regardless of actual corporate actions (Seele &
Gatti, 2017). When facing rising global environmental awareness,
companies cannot expect presumption of innocence: a company
engaging in green marketing can be held liable for greenwashing until
proven that it can substantiate its claims. Being accused for green-
washing, in this sense can be seen a boomerang effect of apparent good
intentions (Berrone, Fosfuri, & Gelabert, 2017). Sometimes, green-
washing perceptions can be a result even of non-environmental factors,
i.e. quality and experience of communication (Szabo & Webster, 2021).
Moreover, once a company is found guilty of greenwashing, it becomes
targeted disproportionately as culprit for environmental degradation or
climate change, in a way that exceeds the actual responsibility and
negative impact of its (mal)practices (Zou, Zeng, Zeng, & Shi, 2015).
Repairing trust after a greenwashing scandal is an uphill and long-
lasting task for companies, which requires a particular marketing
strategy adjustment (Guo, Zhang, Wang, Li, & Tao, 2018; Wang, Walker,
& Barabanov, 2020).
Taking into account the complexity of the issue, this paper zooms in
on the rise of the “greenwashing” paradigm and examines the conse-
quences for the development of green marketing strategies. It starts from
the premise that when developing B2B marketing strategies aimed at
people, planet and profit, companies face an increasingly complex
terrain that they have to navigate, e.g. they have to consider the rise of
vigilant environmental activism which is focused on finding discrep-
ancies between projected marketing messaging and actual practices; as
well as the increasingly tightening regulatory framework.
The paper grapples with a noisy field of greenwashing research,
analysing different bodies of work that have often developed in parallel
to each other, while at the same time left significant gaps which the
paper also identifies. It adopts an interdisciplinary perspective, which is
used as a foundation in the development in novel framework for un-
derstanding greenwashing in a B2B context. In this way, we overcome
parallelism and provide a new framework for analysing greenwashing
and formulating recommended actions for businesses and regulators.
The paper undertakes the following innovations to this end: 1) synthe-
sizing the antecedents, outcomes, and moderators of greenwashing and
distinguishing between various drivers of greenwashing, including sta-
ble external market drivers, dynamic external market drivers, and in-
ternal drivers; 2) emphasizing the under-researched role of supply chain
drivers in B2B marketing; 3) proposing a new classification of green-
washing outcomes, considering multiple levels of analysis and the eco-
nomic, environmental, or social impacts of greenwashing; 4) focusing on
the role of moderators that can influence the impact of greenwashing; 5)
exploring the possibility for moving beyond a judgmental view, e.g. by
exploring how greenwashing can be a step towards pursuing an
authentically green agenda later on.
At the core of the greenwashing predicament is the question of trust
(Guo, Tao, Li, & Wang, 2017), e.g. how can actors outside the company
know whether the green marketing rhetoric is grounded on green
practices or not. Intuitively, companies faced with such situation would
try to simply “convince” other actors in their commitment to the green
agenda that they promote in their marketing rhetoric, however this may
not be an efficient strategy; the paper suggests that first companies need
to understand the phenomenon better in order to adapt to the new
reality.
The centerpiece of the paper is a systematic review of the inter-
disciplinary research on greenwashing, which is aimed to contribute
towards taking stock of the subject area of greenwashing within and
beyond marketing and environmental management, providing insight
into the breadth, depth and limits of the knowledge produced on the
topic. In particular, this study contributes to a modern understanding of
greenwashing by providing a new framework for the typology, drivers,
and consequences of greenwashing. We believe that B2B marketing
would benefit from a systematic literature review on greenwashing for
several reasons. First, as B2B customers become more environmentally
conscious, implementing green marketing management is not only a
competitive advantage but a must for survival (Guo et al., 2018). In the
face of climate change and resource depletion, research on sustainability
in B2B marketing is crucial, although it is currently sparse and some
fundamental questions remain unanswered (Sharma, 2020). A system-
atic literature review on greenwashing can answer some of these ques-
tions by pointing to frameworks and sub-fields of sustainability research
that need more focus in the future. These include questioning assump-
tions about a B2B firm's purpose and place in society, and assuming that
only certain sustainability challenges are important to B2B markets
(Voola, Bandyopadhyay, Voola, Ray, & Carlson, 2022). Second, by
better understanding the causes of greenwashing (e.g., competitive
pressures, financial incentives) and the negative consequences (e.g.,
reputational damage, loss of trust), B2B firms can avoid such pitfalls and
build genuine sustainable relationships with their stakeholders, be they
buyers (Huang, Surface, & Zhang, 2022), suppliers (Cabanelas, Cortez,
& Charterina, 2023), or social impacts on society at large (Pfajfar,
Shoham, Małecka, & Zalaznik, 2022). Consequently, scholars could
better understand the nature of trust and commitment as two important
facets of B2B relationships (Morgan & Hunt, 1994) and further advance
relationship marketing theory. Finally, B2B firms that engage in inter-
national markets need to pay attention to compliance with regulations
that many countries have enacted to prevent false or misleading envi-
ronmental claims or, on the contrary, be rewarded for complying with
the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (Sun & Zhang, 2019;
Voola et al., 2022). In addition, B2B companies may want to avoid
greenwashing not only because of legal requirements but also because of
ethical considerations (Delmas & Burbano, 2011). Research in all these
areas is fragmented and requires consensus, which a systematic litera-
ture review can provide.
The greenwashing discussion touches upon several aspects of mar-
keting and environmental management. Greenwashing could be framed
in light of ethical marketing theories that emphasize the ethical imper-
atives that companies need to abide to when engaging in marketing
activities. More broadly, companies have ‘green’ responsibilities to their
wide arrays of stakeholders, who increasingly have expectations about
substantive environmental action and substantiated green marketing
practices. One could argue that greenwashing is the litmus test of CSR as
well (Guo, Wang, Hwang, Jin, & Zhou, 2022). Greenwashing, should it
be proven, nullifies other CSR efforts. In the context of B2B international
marketing, as it is argued below, greenwashing is central to the trust
relationship within ‘bilateral’ B2B relationships, but also within broader
constellations, ecosystems and environments. Central element to B2B
theory is the interaction between companies; however, this interaction
only takes place once and inter-company relationship is established that
are driven not only by pure economic logic, but also inherently have a
pronounced behavioral component (Hadjikhani & LaPlaca, 2013).

Sustainability positioning plays an important role in shaping B2B pref-
erences (Casidy & Yan, 2022). At the core of this is the relationship
between business actors, in which central are the elements of commit-
ment and trust (Morgan & Hunt, 1994), which is not limited to business
actors dyads, but rather to a broader set of actors in a given fields. In the
era of socially conscientious marketing, of which green marketing sub-
sets, businesses often act as activists demanding change and normative
commitment from their partners (Kapitan, Kemper, Vredenburg, & Spry,
2022). Likewise, companies that are in different relations with other
companies – be it as partners, competitors, suppliers – are exposed to the
risks of spillover effects of greenwashing accusations (Zhe, Yao, & Hu,

Business are already part of constellations, i.e. through supply
chains. Often, these are centered on focal actors (i.e. a major MNE in-
fluences the behavior of its suppliers). In this sense, the responsibility for
greenwashing is shared among all the links in the chain, with a partic-
ular share of it falling on the back of the focal MNE, which acts as a
leader (Blome, Foerstl, & Schleper, 2017). They are also part of broader
business fields, i.e. ecosystems at the industry level, where they set
certain standards and norms of conduct. Greenwashing thus can shake
up whole industries. Finally, these different business fields intersect and
conjoin in socio-economic-technological systems on the macro layer. An
illustrative example is the Volkswagen emissions scandal (so-called
‘Dieselgate’), widely considered to be a textbook case of greenwashing,
aside from having an immense impact on the company's reputation and
performance, and has had substantial spillover effects on its competitors
and suppliers, and the European and global auto-industry as a whole
(Barth, Eckert, Gatzert, & Scholz, 2022). It even had an effect on the
secondary market of used cars (Strittmatter & Lechner, 2020). Criticism
of H&M's greenwashing has been inseparable from a broader critical
discourse of the ‘fast fashion’ industry (Kaner, 2021). Oil companies and
airlines, similarly, when accused of greenwashing, bring bad reputation
to their whole industries and business networks. Related to this, is that it
had paradigmatic effect on how different stakeholders as well as ob-
servers and researchers talk about greenwashing in its aftermath (Siano,
Vollero, Conte, & Amabile, 2017).
Based on the above arguments, this study addresses the following
research questions:
RQ1: What is the extant literature's understanding of the antecedents
and consequences of greenwashing and what are the implications for
B2B marketers?
RQ2: What forms and frameworks can be used to study greenwashing
in B2B markets?
RQ3: What areas of greenwashing need further research and what
are the related research questions?
2. Methodology
2.1. Systematic literature review as a method to study greenwashing
The constantly expanding research on the topic of greenwashing
lacks a rigorous analysis of the extant literature on the subject. A sys-
tematic review can give a comprehensive picture of the current state of
the art on greenwashing, connect the seemingly disparate bodies of
work, identify gaps in the literature, and inform future research by
locating, evaluating, and synthesizing the relevant literature in a sys-
tematic manner (Paul & Criado, 2020). The results of such an under-
taking can be utilized to advance scholarly research and debates, but
also to inform and guide public policy, and help further educate and
raise awareness among different stakeholders (Paul, Lim, O'Cass, Hao, &
Bresciani, 2021).
Greenwashing has attracted the attention of academics, businesses,
and policymakers in recent years, so it is not surprising that previous
attempts have been made to map this area and find fruitful future
research directions. One approach is bibliometric analysis (e.g., Pendse,
Nerlekar, & Darda, 2023; Santos, Coelho, & Marques, 2023a), which is
limited to mapping greenwashing and provides a more descriptive
analysis of previous greenwashing research (e.g., authorship and
collaboration practices, preferred journals for publication, commonly
used keywords, etc.). A more comprehensive approach that provides a
solid conceptual foundation for future greenwashing research is a sys-
tematic literature review. Several systematic literature reviews on
greenwashing have appeared in recent years, all of which had a specific
focus and therefore had certain shortcomings. These systematic litera-
ture reviews focused on main definitions of greenwashing and its evo-
lution in a certain limited period of time (De Freitas Netto, Sobral,
Ribeiro, & Soares, 2020; Yang, Nguyen, Nguyen, Nguyen, & Cao, 2020),
characteristics and forms of greenwashing (De Freitas Netto et al., 2020;
Gatti, Seele, & Rademacher, 2019; Lyon & Montgomery, 2015; Yang
et al., 2020), the impact of greenwashing on stakeholders (Santos et al.,
2023a), greenwashing in relation to sustainability reporting and artifi-
cial intelligence (Moodaley & Telukdarie, 2023), understanding and
mitigating the causes of greenwashing by multinational companies in
host developing markets, particularly in Asia (Yang et al., 2020), prin-
ciples for detecting and reducing greenwashing (Gatti et al., 2019).
However, none of these reviews provide a comprehensive overview and
assessment of the antecedents of greenwashing, its consequences, and
moderators, while future research implications for marketing commu-
nications and/or B2B marketing in particular are very limited or non-
existent.
In conceptualizing this study we follow an emerging systematic re-
views stream within the international business and international mar-
keting literature (Christofi, Leonidou, & Vrontis, 2017; Christofi,
Pereira, Vrontis, Tarba, & Thrassou, 2021; Christofi, Vrontis, & Cado-
gan, 2021; Pereira, Hadjielias, Christofi, & Vrontis, 2023; Sinkovics &
Reuber, 2021; Vrontis et al., 2022; Vrontis & Christofi, 2021). System-
atic reviews help in understanding the breadth as well as the frontiers of
a given research area, and add nuance and context to it (Christofi,
Vrontis, & Cadogan, 2021). They provide “rigorous assessment of the
literature” based on clearly articulated research problems, and a sys-
tematic, meticulous selection and critical assessment of a set of pub-
lished studies on the topic (Christofi et al., 2017). It is aimed to
transcend the limitations of generic narrative reviews, by developing
transparent, scientific and ultimately applicable research procedures
and practices, from sampling, to reaching out to respondents and
reporting the results (Vrontis & Christofi, 2021). Such an approach is
replicable, and its outcomes characterized as more rigorous, valid and
generalizable (Christofi, Vrontis, & Cadogan, 2021). Key feature of this
is the transparency and replicability of the research process in its totality
(Vrontis & Christofi, 2021).
The review was designed to be an inclusive one, generating a sample
of articles that apply quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods, or
come in the form of conceptual or theoretical studies. In addition to
mapping and interconnecting the research area, the systematic review
serves as a foundation for us to outline a future research agenda, which
is a key element of the systemic review approach (Pereira et al., 2023).
While focused on international marketing and IB perspectives, the re-
view was open to a plurality of approaches as essentially greenwashing
calls for interdisciplinary perspective and move beyond IB's and mar-
keting's intellectual silo (Sinkovics & Reuber, 2021) in studying and
addressing it.
The review is intended to transcend several urgent issues that the
greenwashing debate is facing. For example, governments are interested
in finding out if their regulations are really successful in preventing
greenwashing. Companies are interested in finding out how technolog-
ical advances (e.g., AI, blockchain) can help prevent greenwashing
among their business partners in an international environment full of
dynamic change; in other words, how to combat greenwashing given the
rise of sustainability, the circular economy, and the regenerative econ-
omy. Finally, consumers are also interested in learning how to protect
themselves from greenwashing. In summary, the number of companies
greenwashing or thought to be greenwashing is increasing in the EU,

2.2. Article selection
We use SCOPUS and Web-of-Science (WoS) databases to search for
relevant articles in the research area. As these two databases are the
largest databases of scientific articles uniting various publishers and are
widely used for systematic literature reviews in top-tier journals (e.g.
Christofi, Vrontis, & Cadogan, 2021), we assume our choice to be
justified. Similar to previous literature reviews on greenwashing phe-
nomenon (e.g., De Freitas Netto et al., 2020; Gatti et al., 2019; Lyon &
Montgomery, 2015; Yang et al., 2020) we did not want to limit our
search by narrowing it down with additional specific keywords that
would relate for instance to B2B marketing, but rather used keyword
“greenwashing” for our search.
The search among title, abstract and keywords revealed 731 articles
in Scopus and 547 articles in WoS. In the next step we limited the search
results only to peer-reviewed articles that provide for a trusted form of
scientific communication opposed to non-peer-reviewed works. In
addition we limited results only to articles that appeared in English
language. The result of this step revealed 529 articles in Scopus and 449
articles in WoS. As we were interested in greenwashing in marketing
context, we limited in the next step search results to business and
management disciplines. Specifically, we used two separate criterions in
WoS (business, management), while in Scopus we limited results to
“business, management and accounting”. This resulted in 264 articles in
Scopus and 152 articles in WoS, which together accounts for 416 arti-
cles. We downloaded both databases in excel and removed 136 dupli-
cates in the next phase, leading to 280 articles. The next stage involved
articles abstracts screening, where we were searching for possible
connection between greenwashing and marketing. This step focused on
excluding articles that had no relevance with the reviewed topic (i.e.,
greenwashing was mentioned only as a phenomenon in a sentence
related to some other topic, while no in-depth discussion or evaluation
was provided). At this stage we eliminated additional 76 articles. We
downloaded and read through the remaining 204 articles in full-text
format. In this phase, each researcher separately reviewed and criti-
cally evaluated the selected papers according to specific quality criteria
jointly established by the authors, which were cross-checked by two
academic researchers who are also experts in the field. The aim of the
quality assessment is to assess validity and justify the selection of articles
for review (Christofi et al., 2017). We excluded articles in which
greenwashing was only briefly mentioned in the abstract or introduction
and was not part of the main model/main body of the article, or in which
no implications for greenwashing were given in the discussion section of
the article. In addition, we did not limit our search results strictly to
empirical studies, as we believe we would thereby limit also our findings
– conceptual papers may significantly contribute to theory development,
thus we believe it is imperative to include them. After reconciliation of
results between researchers and mutual agreement based on quality
criteria, 60 articles remained for the review process. As formal search
techniques of entering keywords to electronic databases may overlook
important studies (Nielsen, Asmussen, & Weatherall, 2017), we applied
a snowballing technique that is being widely used in the systematic
literature reviews lately (e.g. Christofi, Pereira, et al., 2021; Christofi,
Vrontis, & Cadogan, 2021). By a thorough review of the references of
already included articles we identified additional 8 articles to be added
to our review sample. Hence, our final review sample consists of 68
articles (see Fig. 1 for the article selection procedure).
2.3. Coding
We applied a multistep qualitative coding following best-practice-
recommendations (Gaur & Kumar, 2018) to be most suitable method
for the analysis. First, we documented basic facts about each article:
number of authors, location of the first author, authors' team composi-
tion, year of publication, publication outlet, type of the paper (empir-
ical, conceptual), core topic/context of the study, applied methodology,
theories used, industry and level of analysis. In addition, we coded also
number of citations to perform a citations analysis. We followed the
example of other systematic literature review (e.g. Christofi, Vrontis, &
Cadogan, 2021) and coded the location where the study was conducted
and later grouped countries in three categories following World Bank's
annual gross national income (GNI) per capita: developed (high in-
come), emerging (middle income) and developing (low income)
countries.
This is a framework-based systematic literature review that builds on
the established Antecedents, Decisions, and Outcomes (ADO) frame-
work (Paul & Benito, 2018). Specifically, we use an antecedents-
phenomenon-consequences logic (Christofi, Vrontis, & Cadogan, 2021;
Pisani, Kourula, Kolk, & Meijer, 2017), treating greenwashing as our
phenomenon. The antecedents category includes studies that address
the drivers, motives, or incentives for greenwashing, while the conse-
quences category includes studies that address key outcomes related to
greenwashing. We believe that the framework approach is appropriate
for examining the greenwashing field because it can help us achieve the
greatest possible breadth and depth in the review (Paul et al., 2021), and
it has been shown to be a more robust structure than other types of
systematic literature reviews (Paul & Criado, 2020). Indeed, compared
to all other types of domain-based reviews, framework-based reviews
are often more useful and impactful (Paul et al., 2021).

The descriptive analysis revealed that the phenomenon got higher
attention1 among scholars in the recent years (see Fig. 2). Journal of

the multidimensionality of the greenwashing construct as discussed

3.2. Key definitional dimensions and theories
3.2.1. Main definitions of “greenwashing” and related terms
The term ‘greenwashing’ has been associated with a number of
meanings and connotations by various authors. Its origins date back to
1986, when New York environmental activist Jay Westervelt noted that
hotels typically placed stickers in rooms to encourage the reuse of towels
for environmental reasons, when in fact they did not care about the
environment at all and their hidden agenda was to reduce the cost of
washing towels and increase profits (Romero, 2008). Following West-
ervelt's example, all such seemingly environmentally friendly actions
aimed at increasing profits were ultimately labeled as greenwashing
practices (Ahmad & Zhang, 2020). The term greenwashing originated
from the phrase “environmental whitewash,” which means “to cover up,
camouflage, or mask” (Akturan, 2018), and in its original meaning
carries a negative connotation implying corporate deception (Ahmad &
Zhang, 2020), “insincere, dubious, inflated, or misleading environ-
mental claims” (Akturan, 2018). Although greenwashing has attracted
considerable attention from various stakeholders in the field as well as
academic researchers since its emergence, there is no universally
accepted definition of the term (Torelli, Balluchi, & Lazzini, 2020),
which we also confirm through this literature review.
One of the often cited sources is Oxford dictionary, which describes
greenwashing as ‘activities by a company or an organization that are
intended to make people think that it is concerned about the environ-
ment, even if its real business actually harms the environment’ (Wedari,
Jubb, & Moradi-Motlagh, 2021). Second most often cited source are
Delmas and Burbano (2011) who understand greenwashing as the
integration of two corporate behaviors: poor environmental perfor-
mance and positive communication about environmental performance.
Third most often cited source is TerraChoice, which views greenwashing
as the act of misleading consumers regarding the environmental prac-
tices of a company or the environmental benefits of a product or service.
Besides well-established but diverse definitions some researchers pro-
vide for their own definition: co-creation of an external accusation to-
wards an organization with regard to presenting a misleading green
message (Seele & Gatti, 2017). From the marketing perspective, green-
washing is a result of over positioning an inconsistent communication
(Smith & Font, 2014). Greenwashing is “often mistakenly defined as an
intention and not caused by bounded rationality like ‘honest mistakes,’ a
lack of information, or other behavior not related to harmful objectives”
(Seele & Gatti, 2017; Nygaard & Silkoset, 2022). Ultimately, the defi-
nition of greenwashing is broad, but a common theme is misleading
communication (Lee & Raschke, 2023) and its harmful nature to envi-
ronmental progress (Glavas, Grolleau, & Mzoughi, 2023).
Recently, researchers have drawn attention to terms that are similar
to greenwashing but differ in their scope and understanding. One such
term is “brownwashing” that refers to the practice of underreporting
environmental achievements (Huang, Francoeur, & Brammer, 2022).
“Bluewashing” was derived from the color of the United Nations flag and
formulated at the 2002 World Summit to refer to greenwashing in
relation to humanitarian issues such as human rights, disaster relief, and
poverty reduction (Seele & Gatti, 2017). Cause-oriented marketing
campaigns that are not backed up by substantial action (e.g. symbolic
raising breast cancer awareness) are called out as “pinkwashing”
(Schoier & de Luca, 2017). “Redwashing” is the malpractice of claiming
“exemplary corporate citizenship” while having a negative effect on
indigenous communities (Perakslis, 2020); adherent in name only with
“woke” social movements in which identity politics plays a central role
has been known as “wokewashing” (Warren, 2022); the creation of in-
equalities and other negative developments through new forms of digital
marketing are known as “technowashing” (Perakslis, 2020). Another
such term is “machinewashing”, which is defined as a strategy used by
organizations to exhibit misleading behavior (communication and/or
action) related to ethical artificial intelligence (AI)/algorithmic systems
(Seele & Schultz, 2022). Finally, when in response to the Russian in-
vasion of Ukraine in 2022, dozens companies announced that out of
moral concerns, they will withdraw from Russia, but many of them
failed to do so, the term “moralwashing” also emerged (Carter, 2022).
3.2.2. Towards a new typology of greenwashing
One of the most comprehensive typologies of greenwashing behavior
was proposed by Lyon and Montgomery (2015), who distinguish be-
tween following eleven mechanisms of misleading behavior:
I. Organizational theory domain: (1) decoupling (disconnect between the
structures and the activities of an organization), (2) symbolic manage-
ment (disconnect between promises and actions), (3) means/end
decoupling (disconnect between the actions and the goals of an orga-
nization), II. Economics domain: (4) astroturf lobbying (covert funding of
a “front group” that can make an argument more credibly than the
funder), (5) cheap talk (verbal claim with nothing to back it up), (6)
costly state falsification (expending effort to distort the ability of others
to verify the state of the world), (7) selective disclosure (disclosing
positive information while withholding negative information), III.
Marketing domain: (8) halo effect (inability to evaluate individual attri-
butes apart from an overall impression), (9) pooling (taking the same
costly action as a more able individual in order to appear identical), (10)
implied superiority (suggests one product is better than other without
quite saying so), (11) incomplete comparisons (comparison for which
adequate referents are not provided).
Greenwashing can take many forms (Fernandes et al., 2020), and
several attempts have been made (e.g., Delmas & Burbano, 2011; Gatti
et al., 2021; Kapitan et al., 2022; Szabo & Webster, 2021) to establish a
clear typology of greenwashing forms. However, all of these typologies
consider greenwashing at the firm level in isolation from the actions of
other actors in the marketplace. However, when greenwashing is viewed
from a B2B marketing perspective, the role of suppliers and industrial
buyers can be critical to the study of greenwashing effects. When
considering the source of greenwashing, one can distinguish between
direct greenwashing (the company itself is responsible for the commu-
nication and misconduct and is therefore referred to as the internal
source of greenwashing), indirect greenwashing (the supplier is
involved in both communicating sustainability and causing environ-
mental damage and is therefore referred to as an external source of
greenwashing), and vicarious greenwashing (a company committed to
sustainability is accused of greenwashing because it buys from a supplier
that does not meet sustainability standards) (Pizzetti et al., 2021). The
answer to the question of how companies engage in operational green-
washing provides us with the following 5 types of greenwashing (Seele
& Schultz, 2022): (1) Claim greenwashing (i.e., misleading with words,
which may include (a) misleading claims (b) inaccurate claims (c)
vague/unprovable claims (d) meaningless claims (d) exaggerations); (2)
Execution greenwashing (i.e., misleading through imagery or graphics,
which refers to various forms of visual rhetoric and semiotics and in-
cludes false or misleading seals, certifications, and labels); (3) injunctive
greenwashing (i.e., corporate practices that refer to (a) complete omis-
sion of information, (b) selective disclosure, (c) incomplete compari-
sons, (d) concealment of information); (4) symbolic greenwashing/
decoupling (i.e. Misleading through symbolic actions refers to an
inconsistency or disconnect between promises about, for example, social
and environmental initiatives and actual actions), (5) covert lobbying (i.
e., involves overt or covert activities outside the marketplace aimed at
favorable laws and regulations). The combination of these two di-
mensions (i.e., types and sources of greenwashing) gives us a novel,
unique framework (see Fig. 3) for examining the multidimensional na-
ture of greenwashing. We observe rich extant research at the cross-

that can help us define and operationalize these terms. Likewise, the
practice of greenwashing via lobbying – which is complex and opaque,

greenwashing, where the obvious lack of access to information, but also
the legal and security risks, methodological challenges, and the overall
controversial makeup of the topic, are some of the reasons that have so
far prevented any in-depth inquiry.

3.3. Synthesis of key findings
This chapter provides a synthesis of the antecedents, outcomes, and
moderators of existing greenwashing research.
3.3.1. Antecedents
First, we consider the antecedents or drivers of greenwashing. The
seminal work of Delmas and Burbano (2011) serves as one of the most
comprehensive classifications of drivers of greenwashing to date.
Legitimacy theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) and signaling theory
(Connelly, Certo, Ireland, & Reutzel, 2011) are most commonly used by
researchers to explain drivers of greenwashing. On the one hand,
legitimacy theory asserts that companies engage in social responsibility
to gain corporate legitimacy (e.g., Walker & Wan, 2012). On the other
hand, signaling theory argues that companies that engage in sustain-
ability effectively signal the company's positive social and environ-
mental values to the company's stakeholders (Seele & Gatti, 2017). The
literature review in this study shows that there are more than just these
two theories that have been used to explain corporate greenwashing
behavior. Therefore, we present a novel framework of drivers of
greenwashing (see Fig. 6) that distinguishes between stable external
market drivers (environment, government, culture), dynamic external
market drivers (supply chain drivers, social media, and other stake-
holders), and internal drivers (firm, product, and individual psycho-
logical drivers). Stable external market drivers are those that are
constant over time and do not change with each disruption: For
example, cultural values or norms need a dramatic shift in society to
change; sustainability ratings and regulations need consensus at the
institutional or governmental level, etc. Dynamic external drivers, on
the other hand, are characterized by constant change, activity, or
progress: For example, competitive pressures or buyer demand change
daily. B2B marketers are particularly interested in supply chain drivers,
which have not been sufficiently explored in the existing literature.
3.3.2. Outcomes
Greenwashing is mostly associated with negative outcomes (Blome
et al., 2017), including lower green purchase intentions (Bulut et al.,
2021), reduces organizational credibility (Fernandes et al., 2020),
corporate financial performance and reputation (Leonidou & Skarmeas,
2017), limits the adoption of sustainability practices in SMEs (De Steur,
Temmerman, Gellynck, & Canavari, 2020), and overall damages con-
sumer attitudes towards green purchasing (Parguel, Benoît-Moreau, &
Larceneux, 2011). Most of the greenwashing literature has focused
almost exclusively on consumer responses, overlooking the impact of
greenwashing on other stakeholders (Gatti et al., 2021). We propose a
new classification of greenwashing outcomes (see Table 4) that ad-
dresses these shortcomings and considers multiple levels of analysis
(product, employee, firm, customer, B2B relationship, and societal) as
well as whether greenwashing impacts are economic, environmental, or
social. The analysis points to several under-researched areas in relation
to the effects of greenwashing: the employee level in relation to all three
greenwashing effects and the B2B relationship level in relation to eco-
nomic and environmental greenwashing effects. Interestingly, despite
the abundant literature on consumer responses, there is a distinct lack of
research on economic impacts. These are all fruitful areas for future
research.

3.3.3. Moderators
Few of the reviewed studies used moderators in their models. Find-
ings suggest that corporate environmental performance information
counterbalances positive effect of executional greenwashing on brand
attitudes (Parguel et al., 2015), but counterintuitively makes green
advertising leading to negative brand attitudes (Nyilasy et al., 2014).
Furthermore, environmental involvement (knowledge and concern)
positively moderates relationship between vague and false green-
washing claim and perceived greenwashing (Schmuck, Matthes, &
Naderer, 2018), while environmental sensitivity of industry moderates
relationship between 4 greenwashing levels (corporate, strategic, dark,
product) and stakeholders perceptions (Torelli et al., 2020). Green
concern negatively moderates relationship between greenwashing
perception and green purchasing intentions (Zhang et al., 2018), while
organizational stigma strengthens the negative impact of greenwashing
on market demand and reputation and status decrease negative impact
of greenwashing on expectation violation (moral insurance) and trust-
worthiness (Kassinis et al., 2022). Stringent environmental regulations
weaken the effect of greenwashing on financial performance, low media
favourability makes greenwashing effect on financial performance
positive and media visibility negatively moderates the influence of
greenwashing on financial performance (Li et al., 2022). Finally,
greenwashing was used as a moderator itself. Greenwashing concern
negatively moderates between perceived environmental knowledge/
attitude/subjective norm/environmental concern influence on purchase
intention (Rausch & Kopplin, 2021) and negatively moderates the
relationship between electronic service quality and green purchase
intention (Ahmad & Zhang, 2020). Finally, greenwashing perception
decreases the effects of environmental concern on green behavior (Bulut
et al., 2021) and positively moderates the relationship between corpo-
rate environmental performance and cumulative abnormal returns (Du,
2015).
Despite all these rich findings, the extant research does not provide
an answer what tools managers or policymakers could apply to mitigate
greenwashing (Delmas & Burbano, 2011; Parguel et al., 2015). One of
the ways suggested in the literature is for companies to engage in green
word-of-mouth (WOM), which facilitates positive communication
among various stakeholders, but customer response is largely dependent
on green consumer involvement, so not all interventions are equally
successful (Ahmad & Zhang, 2020). The business model life cycle
assessment tool can be used to validate the sustainability of a particular
business model for a particular company, helping to avoid greenwashing
and strengthen the credibility of its environmental claims (Goffetti,
B¨ockin, Baumann, Tillman, & Zobel, 2022). B2B firms can work with
consumer protection organizations such as Greenwashing Index, Ter-
raChoice, Greenpeace, and Futerra, which focus on green advertising
verification, and launch a campaign to raise awareness of environmental
communication guidelines, such as the Green Guides published by the U.
S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) (Fernandes et al., 2020). The UK
Department for Environment, Food, & Rural Affairs (DEFRA) (2011), the
Canadian Standards Association (CSA) (2008), and the Australian
Competition and Consumer Commission (2011) have issued similar
regulatory standards and practice guides to help consumers interpret
green advertising claims (Seele & Schultz, 2022).
However, previous research has shown that regulating greenwashing
does not necessarily increase the positive environmental effects of green
products, especially when the additional CSR costs are too high or the

washing to limit its negative consequences. A recent study by Glavas
et al. (2023) proposed 4 strategies on how greenwashing can be used to
promote more sustainable practices by companies: “(1) greenwashing
raises awareness and normalizes environmental friendliness; (2) a

4. Discussion
4.1. Tackling the risks and developing truly green B2B marketing
strategies
Companies must “evolve beyond reactionary tactics” (Kapitan et al.,
2022) of responding to greenwashing accusations and a posteriori
adaptation to the expectations by stakeholders. In line with B2B mar-
keting theory (Mora Cortez & Johnston, 2017), we here engage in a
discussion of established practices and new challenges looking forward.
Some greenwashing outcomes are a result of conscious decision to
manipulate/mislead or in any way engage in B2B marketing which
makes claims that cannot be substantiated. However, sometimes such
claims can be made unintentionally – this is without awareness that a
misleading message is being sent. When determining intentionality a
good rule of thumb is the size of the marketing budget especially seen in
the context of the product/service marketed, the timing of the marketing
campaigns, and ideally, relative to the budget earmarked for actually
improving the environmental impact. The oil and gas industry presents a
case in point, where companies spend very high amounts on marketing
to alleviate criticisms by stakeholders and society on their green prac-
tices, often during periods of political campaign when such issues are
widely debated (InfluenceMap, 2021). In this sense, it is hard to argue
that some of the world's largest companies, with some of the world's
largest marketing budgets, commit greenwashing unintentionally. Evi-
dence from China also suggests that companies with poorer environ-
mental performance invest more in green publicity campaigns in the
media (Cao, Faff, He, & Li, 2022).
We suggest a dual framework that consists of: a) prevention-
detection-correction model typical for quality control operations; and
b) creating conditions that facilitate and incentivize substantive green
action and its proper communication. The prevention-detection-
correction model is an established framework in various domains,
such as food safety (Rouvi`ere & Caswell, 2012) or anti-corruption. The
applicability of a framework that originates in these two fields – food
safety, and corruption, is not accidental. Food safety shares the same
biopolitical roots with environmental activism, whereas there are
proximities between anti-corruption and anti-greenwashing given that
they are both addressing transgressive behaviors and trust deficits.
Conventional approach to preventing intentional greenwashing may
seek solutions in blending voluntary business action with an institu-
tional and regulatory approach, i.e. by the formulation of industry-level
standards, blueprints and best practices that deter companies from
consciously engaging in deceptive marketing/communication (Wedari
et al., 2021). Legitimate third-party certifications can play an important
role in preventing greenwashing. These are certifications issued by in-
dependent international bodies that conduct close inspection of the
practices of companies, and often of the whole supply chain (Heras-
Saizarbitoria, Boiral, & Díaz de Junguitu, 2020). These certifications are
valid and have value across different national contexts and can be an
important resource for green global branding endeavors. All of these
measures, however, must be also strengthened by supporting relevant
laws and regulations and their implementation (Sun & Zhang, 2019).
Regarding all these measures, however, one needs to be aware that
measures to prevent transgressions, are not in themselves sufficient to
inspire or motivate substantive green action.
To prevent unintentional greenwashing practices, on the other hand,
companies must build their capacities in the form of education and
training of employees across various departments and levels of the hi-
erarchy, on both the environmental component of the operations of the
company, its products and/or services, and their impact, and the way
they should be communicated to other businesses and consumers.
Learning how to use clear, specific and accurate language is an impor-
tant building block towards developing effective substantive green
marketing strategy, and preventing unintentional greenwashing. Like-
wise, contrary to the marketer's urge to oversell, in the case of green
marketing, not overstretching one's claims is a crucial step in preventing
unintentional greenwashing. Companies have the obligation to accu-
rately represent their environmental endeavors and the relative green-
ness of their products and services. In this sense, it is their actual
commitment to the environment that creates value – and not the green
marketing campaign per se – the green marketing campaign serves as
publication tool that accurately portrays but does not embellish reality.
Education and training in this sense need to be cross-departmental – as
marketing and communication professionals need to learn about envi-
ronmental impacts, while those work on environmental issues need to
learn how to communicate the insights into the company's work in a
better way. In the process, businesses can learn from the greenwashing
education of activists (Eng et al., 2021). A related practice is to ensure
continuous review of communications and marketing materials of the
company. In sum, only strong and robust internal communication can
serve as a foundation of strong external communication.
To detect greenwashing or rather to establish whether greenwashing
accusations are grounded or not, extensive in-depth research and in-
vestigations are required, that would be rooted in sound methodology
and clearly defined concepts and measurements. The work of Nemes
et al. (2022) has been pioneering in this regard, offering an integrated
framework for addressing greenwashing, which combines different
definitions and criteria for defining greenwashing, as discussed above.
At the same time, a particular emphasis needs to be put on unintentional
greenwashing; here efforts should be made in detecting the reasons for
making claims that cannot be substantiated (e.g. errors, miscommuni-
cation, using inappropriate terminology). Successful detection then can
give way to correction, while setting the foundations for successful
prevention measures in the future.
Correction measures, should there be a clear case of intentional
greenwashing, involve various financial and legal penalties, as well as
obligations to acknowledge the damage done, and make substantial
changes in the way the company communicates its environmental
impact. There are several options to be considered here, based on in-
sights from the food industry (Rouvi`ere & Caswell, 2012). First, various
forms of repressive sanctions (from fines to prosecution to recalls to
closure) could be considered for most serious and repeated breaches of
green marketing ethics. Then they can be coupled with informative
sanctions. This should be coupled with across-the-board education and
training, and reflective discussions among employees and stakeholders.
Parallel to the corrective measures, however, companies need to look to
the future and engage in preventive behavior. Finally, there is the option
to “name and shame” greenwashers publicly. An important caveat to this
point, however, is the insight that punitive measures are not as efficient
deterrents against greenwashing as high costs of substantive green ac-
tions are (Lee et al., 2018).
At the core of this framework, however, lies a process of soul-
searching of companies, through which they need to formulate their
understanding of their actual and desired holistic environmental impact,
which is the prerequisite of effective and substantive green B2B mar-
keting. This requires a genuine stakeholder-oriented approach and
seeking the input of various relevant actors, as well as optimization of
intra-organizational practices so as to better fit the mission of the
company.

4.2. Future research roadmap
While the research agenda in international business and marketing
has been inevitably transformed by the rise of anti-greenwashing vigi-
lantism, there are a number of potential research questions that need to
be considered in future research endeavors, especially to advance the
understanding of greenwashing in a B2B marketing context (see
Table 5). Acts of greenwashing themselves can be already interpreted in
light of a transforming logic of international business (Glavas et al.,
2023), however, this notion is not exploited sufficiently in the literature.
Moreover, the literature can be moved forward by centering the agenda
on the role of the ‘beholders,’ i.e. by reflexively engaging with the
myriad of themes, tropes and narratives among critics of greenwashing,
and the way they intersect and contradict each other (Kahraman &
Kazanço˘glu, 2019). The differences among anti-greenwashing cam-
paigns should also be addressed (Gosselt, van Rompay, & Haske, 2019).
It is somewhat surprising that some theories are not on the list of
applied theories in the papers examined. For example, one would expect
marketers to talk about marketing myopia, a term coined by Theodore
Levitt in 1960 to describe a short-sighted and inward-looking approach
to marketing that focuses on the needs of the business rather than the
needs of the customer. Marketing myopia could explain greenwashing as
a form of marketing that focuses on short-term gains rather than long-
term sustainability. Similarly, the theory of strategic misrepresentation
in behavioral economics, introduced by organizational theorist Jeffrey
Pfeffer in his 1978 book Power in Organizations, could help explain
greenwashing because it states that players in a game theory may stra-
tegically misrepresent their preferences to gain an advantage over their
opponents, which can be done in a variety of ways, such as exaggerating
one's capabilities, understating one's interests, or even providing false
information. Moreover, there are studies that claim to apply green-
washing theory (Hassan et al., 2020; Kim & Lyon, 2015). However, we
believe that there is no single greenwashing theory, as there are a variety
of definitions and forms of greenwashing explained in Section 3.2.3.
Therefore, we suggest that scholars become much more specific when
making such general theoretical claims; for example, theorizing green-
washing as a form of corporate social responsibility, or as a form of
corporate environmentalism, or as a form of strategic communication,
etc.
Moreover, the intersections between anti-greenwashing discourse
and higher-order ideological constructs (Roulet & Touboul, 2015) as
well as other social justice causes remains a puzzle as well (Testa, Mir-
oshnychenko, et al., 2018), while at the same time, there is ample space
for studying analogous marketing developments such as ‘machine-
washing’ or the unsubstantiated claims of ethical use of disruptive
technologies (Seele & Schultz, 2022). Ideational diffusion perspectives
could help further our understanding of the advancement and vocali-
zation of the environmental knowledge among different stakeholders
(Majeed & Kim, 2022), including companies engaged in B2B marketing.
This, however, should include advancing the research on misperceptions
of greenwashing, i.e. wrongful accusations of greenwashing and the
motivations behind them. In fact, an unaddressed deep conceptual
problem that needs to be tackled is that stakeholders, when engaging in
vigilantism against greenwashing, and also when surveyed or inter-
viewed, talk from idealistic perspective, which often exceeds the limits
of and disregards what is doable in practice (Torelli et al., 2020).
The focus on beholders also means studying perceptions and in-
teractions and expanding the set of stakeholders studied (De Steur et al.,
2020). Marketing activities such as advertising, and their consequences,
are in themselves interesting examples, as aside from communicating
and leaving environmental impact themselves, while also affecting at-
titudes and behaviors of others beyond the marketing of the product/
service in question (Hartmann, Marcos, Castro, & Apaolaza, 2022). The
advancement in the research of perceptions and interactions in B2C
context (Bulut et al., 2021; Rahman, Park, & Chi, 2015), including the
role of non-environmental factors such as the role of websites
interactivity in forming perceptions of greenwashing (Szabo & Webster,
2021), lay forward a challenging agenda for studies carried out in a B2B
context. Such research can provide empirical insight into the signifi-
cance of greenwashing perceptions on making the decision to make a
purchase or not (Rausch & Kopplin, 2021). Here, it is important to
consider that ultimately environmental considerations are just part of
the decision calculus, so it is important to examine them in relation to
other factors (Ahmad & Zhang, 2020; Testa et al., 2020). The different
idiosyncratic reasoning processes that characterize different actors and
relationships open another avenue for further research (Schmuck et al.,
2018).
A reflexive approach can also be useful in advancing the study on the
phenomenon on greenwashing in the context of B2B marketing itself. A
worthwhile line of inquiry is to determine which companies are more
likely to greenwash under what conditions remains a challenge (Lee &
Raschke, 2023; Wu et al., 2020). The different aspects of greenwashing –
such as intentionality need to be explored (Testa, Miroshnychenko,
et al., 2018). Understanding intra-organizational communication and
organizational decision-making on environmental issues and how they
are externally communicated (Delmas & Burbano, 2011). Finally, the
literature can be advanced by developing nuances in the understanding
of the severity of greenwashing and moving beyond binary categoriza-
tion (Uyar, Karaman, & Kilic, 2020). Greenwashing should also be
studied in cross-cultural perspective, and cross-temporal, longitudinal
one, as the phenomenon itself is quite dynamic (Guo et al., 2017).
Expanding the set of drivers and motivations to include different
personal, managerial, organizational and external market and
nonmarket features (De Steur et al., 2020; Hassan et al., 2020) as well as
organizational practices (Neureiter & Matthes, 2022). Related to this is
the need to better understand the role of institutional pressures, which,
if exceeding the capacities of the firm, can potentially induce green-
washing in a desperate attempt to meet the expectations of the stake-
holders (Testa, Boiral, & Iraldo, 2018). Better understanding of the
institutional, regulatory, and policy context is important as well (Sun &
Zhang, 2019). The media often play a complicit role in greenwashing,
which is an under-researched topic (Cao et al., 2022). On the other hand,
media and in particular social media can play a role in pushing an anti-
greenwashing agenda (Fernando et al., 2014). The impact of new
technologies, in particular blockchain, can offer insights in tackling
greenwashing as well (Nygaard & Silkoset, 2022). Green e-commerce
and digital marketing channels are promising venues for research as
they offer rich data points (Topal et al., 2020).
There is ample space to explore different focal objects when it comes
to greenwashing. Most of the literature is examining greenwashing at
the level of the firm, however, product- or service-focused approach
(Ioannou et al., 2022), or in a more reflexive fashion, practice-focused
approach could provide different insights and allow more flexible
comparisons of different green marketing endeavors pursued by a same
company, or specific, product- or service-focused interfirm comparison.
This would be particularly important when done in the context of B2B
interactions in the context of global value chain and dealing with
environmental issues and environmental vigilantism (Blome et al.,
2017). Deepening our understanding of the claiming and blaming dy-
namics within global value chains is a particularly worthwhile research
direction to be advanced (Pizzetti et al., 2021). Finally, the role of power
as part of pressure from competitors or industrial buyers in global value
chains has not yet been sufficiently explored, and the claim that smaller
actors in global value chains are more susceptible to greenwashing
(Chitaka, 2021) needs further examination.
Methodological innovations, e.g. using experiments and control
groups to improve research quality, can greatly help advance the
research agenda (Gatti et al., 2021; Heras-Saizarbitoria et al., 2020).
More work can be done on conceptualizing and measuring environ-
mental actions (e.g. direct vs indirect); expanding the data universe to
include voices from within companies – dissenting or defending (Wedari
et al., 2021); as well as voices of other actors (e.g. NGOs); and smaller

5. Concluding remarks
This systematic literature review is not without limitations. First,
despite a structured approach to literature searching and many efforts to
include all relevant studies, the final list of articles may not be complete
because some relevant studies may have been neglected. However, a
thorough review of the references and additional expert consultations
addressed this limitation to some extent. Second, the list of keywords
was limited to greenwashing, while this study could be further expanded
by including similar words with a broader meaning (e.g., false market-
ing communication, false sustainability communication) in the search
criteria. Because sustainability and marketing communication are well-
researched areas, the search would result in thousands of identified ar-
ticles, most of which are not the focus of this study, so we did not take
this approach. Third, we limited our study to academic databases,
whereas the inclusion of corporate works (e.g., white papers, annual
reports) and policy makers (e.g., greenwashing regulations and stan-
dards) could shed a very different light on the greenwashing phenom-
enon. Because we focused on analysing and synthesizing previous
research, we did not provide research propositions that would link the
elements, which would be a logical next step. Despite these limitations,
our review offers several future research directions and provides prac-
titioners, policymakers, and scholars with an up-to-date overview of the
greenwashing phenomenon.
