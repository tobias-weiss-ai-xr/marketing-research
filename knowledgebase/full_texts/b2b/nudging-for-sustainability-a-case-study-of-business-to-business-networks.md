Abstract
Purpose – This study aims to explore nudging as a technique and mechanism for driving sustainability, with a particular focus on the influence of
business-to-business (B2B) network relationships and marketing strategies in the polymer manufacturing industry.
Design/methodology/approach – This research uses a qualitative single-case design with a critical case rationale to explore experiences and
perspectives of industrial managers in a polymer-based pharmaceutical packaging company. The case is selected as a critical context for examining
how sustainability-related nudges shape B2B networks and marketing, with nudging theory empirically tested and integrated into network theory
and the actors–resources–activities (ARA) model.
Findings – The findings demonstrate how managers apply nudging techniques across B2B networks to advance sustainability. Techniques such as
simplifying and framing information, changes to physical environments, default policies and social norms are embedded in the three layers of the
ARA model: activity chains, resource connections and actor ties.
Practical implications – This study provides managers with a strategic framework for incorporating nudging techniques to integrate sustainability
into B2B networks. By leveraging simplification, social norms and structural interventions, managers can guide behavioral change incrementally
while maintaining trust and autonomy across organizational boundaries.
Originality/value – This research contributes to the literature on sustainability in B2B marketing strategy by integrating nudging theory with the
ARA model through a critical single case study. The study introduces a novel framework that explains how behavioral interventions are embedded
and reinforced within network structures by integrating nudging with the constructs of interaction, adaptation and conceptualization. The integrated
framework extends nudging theory beyond consumer contexts, offering new insights into how sustainability transitions unfold through relational
and systemic mechanisms in complex B2B environments.

1. Introduction
There is a growing necessity to integrate sustainability efforts
into business-to-business (B2B) strategies (Davidavic ˇien_e et al.,
2024; Huang et al., 2022; Mariadoss et al., 2011). Emerging
regulatory initiatives, such as the EU’s Corporate Sustainability
Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD), increasingly require
organizations not only to assess and address social and
environmental risks but also to maintain active engagement
with stakeholders on sustainability-related matters (European
Commission, 2026; Dempere et al., 2024). Sustainability in
this context refers to the concept of “strong sustainability,”
which emphasizes that sustainable development depends on
transforming practices and behaviors, and not solely on
product or material innovation (Pearce et al., 1989). Such
transformation is not an individual endeavor, but a collective
responsibility that requires coordinated actions and active
involvement of all partners across business networks (United
Nations, 2015).
This paper adopts a network perspective to examine how
nudging can trigger and support strong sustainability in B2B
relationships. Recent nudging literature remains dominated by
public policy applications and consumer behavior (Moseley,
2020; Jirásek et al., 2022), with only a limited number of studies
addressing nudging in sustainability-related workplaces and

inter-organizational settings (Amiri et al., 2024). We therefore
argue for a shift in the unit of analysis from isolated, firm-level
interventions to network interactions, by theorizing nudging as
a relational mechanism that can diffuse, escalate or stall across
connected actors. By linking nudging to strong sustainability
transitions in network interactions, we explain whether nudges
work in industrial contexts and how they move and gain force
through relationship structures. B2B networks have the
potential to support strong sustainability because they
inherently rely on long-term collaboration and mutual
exchange of resources, products and services (Flygansvær et al.,
2021; Håkansson and Ford, 2002). Within these networks,
actors, resources and activities form interconnected
components that contribute to systemic complexity, where
each element depends on others to fulfill business objectives
(Håkansson and Snehota, 1995). As a result, the needs,
interests and actions of various business actors are embedded in
a dynamic and interdependent system. This interdependence
implies that even minor adjustments within the network can
trigger significant and far-reaching effects.
In light of the systemic interdependencies within B2B
networks (Håkansson and Snehota, 1995; Håkansson and
Ford, 2002), nudging is increasingly relevant as a behavioral
approach that can subtly influence decision-making without
imposing rigid structures and offers a way to guide choices
within complex systems while preserving autonomy (Lehner
et al., 2016). The concept of nudge, introduced by Thaler and
Sunstein (2008), examines how small interventions, known as
nudges, may influence individuals’ behavior and decision-
making processes without limiting their freedom of choice.
Nudges are grounded in strategically designed choice
architectures and structured to guide individuals toward certain
decisions. This approach, conceptualized as libertarian
paternalism (Thaler and Sunstein, 2003), combines the
preservation of individual autonomy with subtle guidance
toward beneficial outcomes. Hence, nudges can support
individuals and societies in making better choices that enhance
overall well-being (Thaler and Sunstein, 2008, 2021; Lehner
et al., 2016).
Given the systemic nature of B2B networks, where actors
and activities are deeply interlinked, this study argues that
integrating behavioral insights by nudging, is particularly
relevant. Nudging can shape decision-making without rigid
constraints, making it a suitable strategy for promoting
sustainability in inter-organizational contexts (Amiri et al.,
2024). From a nudging theory and a network perspective on
B2B marketing strategy, this paper conceptualizes nudges not
as one-off interventions but as distributed and gradual
processes that can move through relationship structures with
the potential to cascade across B2B networks. Nudging theory
(Lehner et al., 2016) is combined with a network perspective on
B2B marketing strategies (Håkansson and Ford, 2002; Lenney
and Easton, 2009) to investigate how interdependencies within
business networks can be influenced by nudges (Amiri et al.,
2024) to foster strong sustainability. This approach suggests a
new and innovative strategy to connect nudging to strong
sustainability transitions by framing sustainability as a long-
term, multi-dimensional shift in socio-technical systems toward
more sustainable production and consumption (Markard et al.,
2012).
The empirical context of this study is the polymer-based
pharmaceutical packaging manufacturing sector [1] and
exemplifies these challenges and underlines the evolving nature
of industrial B2B networks. Stringent quality requirements,
regulatory constraints and the need for innovation in
sustainable materials create a dynamic business environment
where marketing strategies should address sustainability and
profitability concerns (e.g. Peng et al., 2020). Sustainability in
this sector is not an add-on to existing business and industrial
marketing practices but a transformative force that reshapes
business relationships and industrial marketing (Sharma et al.,
2010).
Against this background, this study aims to explore nudging
as a technique and mechanism for driving sustainability, with a
particular focus on the influence of B2B network relationships
and marketing strategies in the polymer manufacturing
industry. By examining the strategic sustainability practices of
managers in B2B networks in the polymer-based
pharmaceutical packaging industry, the study seeks to provide
actionable insights into how behavioral interventions can
transform marketing strategies and accelerate the adoption of
sustainability throughout industrial B2B networks. The
research question (RQ) guiding the study is:
RQ1. How do managers use nudging to drive sustainability in
B2B network relationships?
This study provides a theoretical contribution by integrating
nudging theory with the actors–resources–activities (ARA)
model, demonstrating how nudging mechanisms are
embedded within network structures of interaction, adaptation
and conceptualization. It reveals how nudging, as a subtle
intervention, gains legitimacy within trusted business
relationships and triggers cascading network effects that
accelerate sustainability adoption across B2B networks. The
study offers actionable insights into how managers can use
nudging as behavioral interventions in polymer-based
pharmaceutical packaging networks to reshape B2B marketing
strategies. It demonstrates how micro-level interventions in
customer–supplier interactions translate into network-level
outcomes such as adoption, legitimacy and escalation,
reframing sustainability strategies as a distributed, networked
marketing process driven by trust, interdependence and
governance rather than isolated “best practices.”
The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews related
research and outlines nudging theory, network theory and the
ARA model, which are synthesized into an integrated
framework. Section 3 describes the qualitative single-case
design and analysis. Section 4 presents the findings, and
Section 5 discusses theoretical and managerial implications and
implications for future research.
2. Related research and theoretical foundation
2.1 Coordinating sustainability in business-to-business
networks: from industrial relationships to nudging
strategies
The manufacturing industry, a cornerstone of global economic
growth, is undergoing a radical shift as sustainability
imperatives increasingly intersect B2B strategies and business
relationships. This shift is reshaping the landscape of business

balancing the transition of a multifaceted interplay among all

initiatives across business networks. In this way, business
relationships can serve as mechanisms for coordinating
interdependent activities and mobilizing entire industries
toward sustainability transitions (Håkansson and Ford, 2002).
The conceptualization of business markets as networks of
interconnected relationships emerged prominently in the 1970s
through the groundbreaking work of the Industrial Marketing
and Purchasing (IMP) Group. This European research tradition
challenged the dominant transactional view of business markets
by demonstrating that business relationships were not discrete
events but complex, long-term processes of interaction between
buyers and sellers (Håkansson, 1982; Turnbull and Valla, 1986).
The IMP Group’s empirical studies revealed patterns of stable,
long-term relationships characterized by mutual adaptations and
complex interdependencies that could not be explained by
conventional marketing theories of the time. The research
demonstrated that firms were embedded in networks of
relationships that substantially influenced their behavior and
performance (Mattsson, 1985). Within this perspective, business
relationships serve as the fundamental mechanisms through
which organizations coordinate their interdependent activities
and achieve their objectives in business networks (Håkansson
and Snehota, 1995; Ford et al., 2011). These relationships are
complex systems that enable the coordination and integration of
resources, activities and actors across organizational boundaries
(Castañer and Oliveira, 2020).
Despite its origins in the 1980s, the network perspective on
business relations remains relevant for understanding
contemporary sustainability transformations and commitments
(Lenney and Easton, 2009), particularly complex sustainability
challenges within innovation network ecosystems, considering
both self-interests and collective interests (Munksgaard and
Medlin, 2014; Aarikka-Stenroos et al., 2017). Sustainability in
industrial business networks requires coordinated efforts across
multiple actors, making traditional firm-centric approaches
insufficient (Flygansvær et al., 2021).
Research on nudging often narrowly focuses on nudges aimed
at improving the well-being of the nudgees (Schmidt and
Engelen, 2020), such as encouraging healthier eating habits
through default menu options or modifying behaviors through
choice architecture rather than directly promoting environmental
actions (Amiri et al., 2024). Initially, this understanding of
nudging for sustainable transition dominated the research after
Thaler and Sunstein’s work in 2008. However, nudging has since
evolved to encompass broader themes of sustainability like
obesity, climate change and energy efficiency, with a growing
focus on social norms and digitalization, leading to concepts like
digital nudging and green nudges (Carpinelli, 2025). This
evolution highlights an increased societal awareness and
integration of sustainability principles into innovations and
everyday business practices (Amiri et al., 2024). In this paper, we
adopt this latter understanding of nudging as a strategy to benefit
the environment rather than individual nudgees.
2.2 A network-based theoretical model for nudging
sustainable transitions
We depart from a network perspective and emphasize how
business relationships serve as mechanisms for coordinating
interdependent activities, which provides a powerful lens for
understanding how entire industries can be mobilized toward
sustainability transitions (Håkansson and Ford, 2002;
Håkansson et al., 2009). This perspective is operationalized
through the ARA model (Håkansson and Snehota, 1995 and
integrated with theories of nudging for sustainable transition
(Lehner et al., 2016; Flygansvær et al., 2021).
2.2.1 The actors–resources–activities model
Recent developments in industrial marketing have extended
the use of the ARA model to explore how business relationships
evolve dynamically within complex and sustainability-oriented
network configurations. For instance, Kovalevskaya (2025)
applies the ARA model to examine changing interdependencies
within a buyer–supplier–supplier triad in a lean product
development project. The study demonstrates how activity
links, resource ties and actor bonds shift over time as
organizations adapt to new coordination demands, thereby
providing empirical evidence of the ARA model’s explanatory
power in capturing temporal and structural dynamics in
industrial networks. Similarly, Dominidiato, Hjelmgren and
Lind (2025) use the ARA perspective to investigate
collaborative product development in the denim industry,
showing how inter-organizational adaptations in activities,
resources and actor relationships enable a transition toward
circular economy practices. Together, these recent studies
reinforce the ARA model’s continued relevance for analyzing
both the stability and transformation of business relationships,
particularly in contemporary contexts where sustainability and
innovation are key coordination challenges.
The ARA model offers a useful framework for examining
how business relationships shape and manage inter-
organizational interdependencies (Håkansson and Snehota,
1995). It conceptualizes relationships as three connected
layers: activity chains (linked technical, administrative and
commercial activities), resource connections (how firms combine
and adapt resources through interaction) and actor ties (the
bonds, trust and shared understandings that influence how
parties perceive and act toward one another) (Lenney and
Easton, 2009; Håkansson and Ford, 2002). Because these
layers are mutually reinforcing, joint activities typically require
resource adaptations and the development of actor bonds to
enable coordination (Gadde and Håkansson, 2001). Through
this ongoing interaction, relationships develop “substance
layers” such as technical/functional, economic and social

Central to the ARA model is the recognition that business

mutual adaptations and shared understandings that enhance
coordination and make relationship-specific knowledge and

critical because it highlights how relationships accumulate
knowledge and capabilities that become valuable resources in
themselves (Håkansson et al., 2009). Relationships add another
layer of complexity within a broader network of interconnected
relationships and can be understood as coordination
mechanisms (Anderson et al., 1994). This network
embeddedness means that changes in one relationship may
create ripple effects throughout the network, affecting how
other relationships function and evolve (Halinen et al., 1999).
The ARA model also emphasizes relationships as venues for
innovation and value creation through the novel combination
of resources and activities across organizational boundaries
(Cantù et al., 2012). When organizations coordinate activities
through relationships, they create opportunities for developing
new solutions and improving efficiency in ways that would not
be possible through arm’s length market transactions (Gadde
et al., 2012). The power of business relationships as
coordination mechanisms lies in their ability to handle
complexity and uncertainty (Ford et al., 2011). Through the
development of activity links, resource ties and actor bonds,
organizations create flexible yet stable structures that can adapt
to changing circumstances while maintaining the continuity
necessary for efficient coordination. This adaptability is
particularly relevant in contexts where organizations need to
coordinate complex activities or manage significant
interdependencies, such as in sustainable business practices
(Flygansvær et al., 2021).
2.2.2 Nudging theory and sustainability transitions
Lehner et al. (2016) underscore the potential of choice
architecture to guide businesses toward more sustainable practices
by altering the way options are presented and making sustainable
choices more appealing. The authors present three dimensions of
choice architecture to understand what guides people’s decisions
and actions: regulatory, fiscal and nudges. While recognizing that
regulations and taxes related to sustainability are important in
firms’ sustainability strategies, our approach focuses solely on
nudges and not the other two dimensions.
Nudges can be divided into four different types (Lehner
et al., 2016):
1 Simplification and framing of information. This type means
that simplification makes information easier to
understand, especially for complex decisions like financial
investments. Framing involves presenting information to
activate certain values and attitudes, such as using
descriptive labels to increase sales or providing feedback
to help individuals stay on track with their goals.
2 Changes to the physical environment. This type leverages how
the physical environment affects individuals’ choices, such as
product placement on shelves influencing purchasing decisions.
3 Changes to the default policy. This type involves creating
healthier and more environmentally responsible default
policies. As an example, in a company cafeteria, the
default policy could be to serve water or a healthy juice as
the standard beverage option instead of sugary sodas. This
change encourages employees to make healthier drink
choices while still allowing them the freedom to choose
other beverages.
4 The use of social norms. This type of nudge explains
people’s behavior or makes their expectations visible. For
example, prompting students to choose fruit at lunch
increased fruit consumption from 40% to 70% in one
study (Schwartz, 2007). Similarly, informing hotel guests
that the majority reuse their towels led to higher towel
reuse rates compared to messages focused solely on
environmental protection (Goldstein et al., 2008).
2.2.3 Integrated framework: an extended actors–resources–activ-
ities-nudging framework
This paper asserts that nudging is especially relevant in B2B
network relationships for building strong sustainability, as it shapes
organizational decision-making in situations where the outcomes
of actions within interdependent relationships are not immediately
visible. While traditional approaches to business relationships often
focus on individual company initiatives or regulatory compliance,
the theoretical framework developed in this paper uses the inherent
mechanisms of business networks (Aarikka-Stenroos and Ritala,
2017) while simultaneously applying nudging techniques that
work with, rather than against, existing network dynamics
(Flygansvær et al., 2021). Building on established theories by
integrating the ARA model and nudging theory (choice
architecture), the proposed framework extends prior research by
linking network-level interdependencies to concrete nudging
mechanisms that extend our theoretical understanding of new
complex situations, as shown in Figure 1. This theoretical
framework proposes a novel approach to analyzing sustainability
transitions in B2B networks by integrating a network perspective,

3. Method
The study used a qualitative single-case design (Yin, 2009,
2018) with a critical case logic, focusing on a key industrial
manufacturer in the polymer-based pharmaceutical packaging
industry. In this sector, the company is a pioneering market
leader locally and globally, characterized by tightly coupled
customer–supplier relationships and a proactive management
approach concerning sustainability strategies. Therefore, the
case was selected as a critical context for examining how
sustainability-related nudges shape B2B networks and
marketing, using the proposed framework (Figure 1). Guided
by the RQ, the framework was applied to collected data of the
empirical case, to analyze changes in actor interactions,
resource framing and combination and activity coordination,
thereby assessing how nudging mechanisms manifest within
activity chains, resource connections and actor ties.
3.1 The case company – Nolato Cerbo
The industrial company Nolato Cerbo has been a long-
standing partner with the authors’ university for many years.
Founded in Sweden in 1938, Nolato is part of a global group
with operations across Europe, Asia and North America,
employing approximately 5,700 people. The corporate group
develops and manufactures products made from polymer
materials such as plastic, silicone, and thermoplastic elastomers
(TPE) for leading companies worldwide.
The local company, with approximately 170 employees,
specializes in polymer-based pharmaceutical packaging and
offers both customized and standard packaging solutions. Key
manufacturing techniques include injection molding and blow
molding, organized into relatively small, self-contained
production lines within a larger production facility. The
primary customer target groups are the pharmaceutical
industry and the healthcare sector.
Besides the continuous development of digital and advanced
manufacturing, quality management certifications are crucial for
supporting sustainability efforts. In 2024, Nolato earned a gold
medal recognition from EcoVadis, an award for being among the
top 5% assessed companies. This reflects the quality of the
company’s sustainability management system and commitment to
promoting transparency in the entire value chain. The company
pursues a strategic business policy aimed at reducing customers’
carbon footprints by designing products that incorporate recycled
and bio-based plastic materials. After more than 80years as a
traditional local manufacturer, primarily delivering products
shaped by supplier capacity, they have, over the past decade, shifted
toward close customer collaboration to develop sustainable, high-
complexity plastic solutions. The company’s core values emphasize
ethical, sustainable production, strong supplier–customer
relationships and a decentralized culture, captured in “the Nolato
spirit” and formalized in “Nolato’s Code of Conduct.”
3.2 Empirical data collection
The authors’ prior relationship with the company was limited to
long-term student collaboration rather than any formal, research-
driven engagement, which reduced the likelihood that pre-
existing research expectations influenced the study. Also, Nolato
had recently appointed a new CEO and quality and
environmental manager with whom the authors had no prior
relationship. This reduced the risk of familiarity or pre-
understanding shaping the data collection. The whole data
collection corpus included semi-structured interviews with global
and local managers, documentation, study visits and meetings at
the case company. The empirical focus was on the company’s
marketing strategies as a key supplier within a network of
interconnected value chains. Insights were gathered from the
management team’s experiences and perspectives, including
individual reflections from senior managers representing core
departments such as marketing (local and globally), purchasing
(local and globally), quality management and production.
During 2023–2024, the primary data collection consisted of
ten in-depth interviews with seven managers, capturing
detailed insights into the respondents’ experiences and
decision-making processes (Qu and Dumay, 2011). A semi-
structured interview guide, see Appendix 1, covered key themes
on nudging and sustainability while allowing flexibility to follow
emerging topics. The interview guide provided a framework for
exploring how sustainability perspectives are integrated into
operations, influence B2B network relationships and shape
leadership strategies in the supply chain. Interviews were
conducted in a conversational style, with the concept of
nudging negotiated between interviewer and respondent to
ensure shared understanding. This approach generated
contextual data on the company’s sustainability initiatives,

3.3 Analysis method
The thematic data analysis of the interviews followed a
systematic and iterative process (Gioia et al
., 2013; Braun and
Clarke, 2006). During the iterative coding and structuring
phases, we used and documented the analysis in Excel. The
first phase was inductive: coding transcripts and organizing
themes in iterations (Steps 1–3). The second phase was
deductive: analyzing the assembled data structure (Step 3) by
the extended ARA-nudging framework.
Each step is outlined below:
• Initial data coding of transcripts, maintaining the integrity
of first-order (respondent-centric) codes and developing a
comprehensive compendium of first-order codes
(inductive approach).
• Organizing first-order codes into second-order themes.
• Distill second-order themes into overarching aggregated
themes, and assemble them into a data structure.
• Mapping aggregated themes to theory, i.e. applying the
extended ARA-nudging framework, see Figure 1
(deductive approach).

develop a comprehensive understanding of the data. Following

4. Findings
The findings are presented according to the three layers of the
ARA model: activity chains, resource connections and actor
ties, each analyzed in relation to the four identified nudging
types. This structure clarifies how sustainability initiatives are
embedded in different dimensions of the network and how
managers apply nudging techniques to influence behaviors and
relationships across the B2B context. A summary table of
quotations linked to ARA layers and nudging types is provided

4.1 Nudging in activity chains (actors–activities)
Activity chains involve the technical, administrative and
commercial interactions between the company and its partners.
In this layer, nudging activities emerged through simplification
and framing of information, specifically in marketing strategies
and Web communication. Nudging social norms formed the
company’s internal culture.
4.1.1 Simplification and framing of information in marketing
strategies
In their marketing strategies, managers frequently emphasize
profitability and tangible outcomes to make sustainability more
accessible. Sustainability is nudged as an economic opportunity
by reducing complexity for customers and presenting the value
proposition clearly. The CMO explains:
Very few customers choose to attend our Sustainability Day unless we can
demonstrate some form of profitability or clearly show the return on
investment. [3]
Following this argumentation, the CEO uses vivid comparisons
to communicate environmental benefits by reframing abstract
CO2 data into an image that is easy to grasp, helping customers
connect sustainability with real-world outcomes. The CEO
continues:
If they would buy it [the more sustainable product], they could have driven
27 laps around the world in an average diesel car [to make the equivalent
CO2 footprint]. [1]
Furthermore, company values are framed in ways that support
daily decision-making, linking abstract sustainability goals to
practical managerial action. The operations and logistics
manager illustrates how the organizational vision provides
another anchor for simplification:
[. . .]for me, this [points to the Nolato Spirit brochure] starts to connect with
Nolato’s vision and Cerbo’s strategy and policies. As a leader, that’s when I
begin to relate to something more tangible. [6]
4.1.2 Simplification and framing of information in Web
communication
The company uses the digital Web platform for disseminating
sustainability information in the B2B network. The export sales
manager highlights:
We can launch or go out on our websites and say that we are selling this
sustainable product [bio-produced plastics], when it is so much more
expensive. But you also save, and that is very good. With a calculation
model, a CO2 spinner, we can demonstrate how much CO2 is saved. [4]
The CO2 spinner, as a nudging tool, makes sustainability
measurable and concrete, justifying higher costs and nudging
customers to adopt more sustainable options. At the same time,
managers acknowledge the difficulty of communication. This
underscores the challenge of translating technical sustainability
concepts into accessible terms, meaning simplification also
involves careful framing to maintain credibility and avoid
skepticism. The CEO adds:
We often downplay our sustainability efforts because we’re so incredibly
afraid of being accused of greenwashing, and I think finding the right
balance is a real challenge. [1]
4.1.3 Use of social norms in internal culture
Nudging social norms is illustrated by the eagerness to learn
more about sustainability and the positive response within the
company when employees recognize their progress. This
indicates that employees are receptive to sustainability when it
is framed as collective progress. Hence, social norms are
nudged internally to push employees’ motivation and drive,
and to reinforce sustainability as an organizational norm. The
quality and environmental manager reflects:
Yes, I find that I encounter surprisingly little resistance. In fact, I think
there’s a real hunger within companies to learn more. They hear these terms
and are often quite grateful when they realize, Yes, we are trying to get a
handle on this, and as long as we’re taking action, we’re on the right track.

4.2.2 Changes to the physical environment of waste reduction and
cloud solutions
Practical initiatives emphasize resource use by nudging supplier
values through the gradual integration of waste reduction
performed in small, manageable steps, i.e. supporting suppliers
in adapting to new practices. By making the process easier to
follow and emphasizing commitment to waste reduction, it
leverages the physical environment to influence behavior. The
gradual design lowers resistance and fosters supplier
engagement in sustainability. The purchase manager (PM)
describes stepwise waste reduction:
We have introduced a waste reduction project aimed at minimizing waste,
and what we’ve done is gradually integrate it into small, manageable steps.
This approach allows everyone, including our suppliers, to ease into the
process. We’ve been very clear about our commitment to eliminating or
reducing waste, and it’s essential that every action taken is with waste
reduction in mind. By being part of this step-by-step initiative, suppliers can
see real value in contributing to tangible environmental improvements. [2]
Digital infrastructure is also positioned as a physical
environment for collaboration. By embedding suppliers in a
shared platform, sustainability goals are supported structurally,
making cooperation easier. The PM further points out:
We have a cloud-based platform where we collaborate between the
companies. We include our suppliers in this[. . .] the strategic suppliers
should be included here. [2]
4.2.3 Changes to the default policy – customer expectations of a
sustainable environment
Customers can act as “nudgers,” especially large global clients
that expect renewable energy use. In this case, external
expectations shift sustainability from optional to default policy,
driving organizational change. The CMO points to:
Right now, I’m in an extensive number of meetings with our customers,
particularly large global clients, discussing sustainability. The focus is
primarily on the environmental aspect, where they expect us to use
renewable energy and take numerous other initiatives. [3]
4.3 Nudging in actor ties (actors–resources)
Actor ties involve relationships and collaborations across the
network. The findings illustrate how nudging techniques
strengthen bonds between the company and its business
partners. Nudging occurs through simplification and framing of
information in supplier relations, changes in default policies in
the “code of conduct” and social norms for customers and
leadership strategies.
4.3.1 Simplification and framing of information in supplier
relations
The company actively supports suppliers in building sustainability
capacity. Nudging environmental policy templates simplify
suppliers’ adoption of more responsible practices. The PM
explains:
In the past, we’ve done a lot of work with suppliers who lacked an
environmental policy, and I’ve said; We can probably help you with that. We
have a template for this. I’d ask them; Do you think you can implement
some changes internally? It could be a good starting point to assist them in
developing an environmental policy, which we need to have in place. [2]
4.3.2 Changes to the default policy – code of conduct
Sustainability is embedded in formal policies such as the code
of conduct. By setting sustainability expectations as standard,
the company nudges partners and employees into compliance
with sustainable practices. It sets the standard policy that
promotes responsible practices across actors. The PM states:
[. . .]we use the [code of conduct] with our new customers, for our newly
recruited employees. We also use it in our discussions with our suppliers[. . .]
in the version of sustainable purchase. [2]
4.3.3 Use of social norms for customers and leadership strategies
Differences in norms between countries also play a role in
shaping partnerships. Swedish norms around sustainability act
as a selling point, influencing international customers, as the
CMO explains:
We have a large Japanese customer[. . .] we’ve secured business because they
see that our operations in Sweden are more sustainable than, for instance,
those in the USA. [3]
The PM further emphasizes guiding suppliers:
It’s crucial to guide them in this work; it becomes important to them once
they realize it’s a priority for both us and their customers. [2]
Leadership is also framed as a form of nudging. This reflects a
philosophy of gradual, persistent influence rather than
coercion. The quality and environmental manager notes:
To me, leadership and what you call nudging are essentially the same thing.
You can’t change behavior simply by telling people they need to change.
Instead, you have to nudge them, starting gently at first. Once you’ve
established some movement, you can push a bit harder and then continue
nudging softly afterward. [7]
Finally, employee commitment itself acts as a reinforcing
nudge. Employee engagement becomes a self-sustaining social
norm that supports both internal and external credibility in
sustainability. The area sales coordinator observes:

5. Discussion and conclusion
The analysis of the empirical material, guided by the integrated
framework of nudging theory and the ARA model, reveals three
distinct constructs through which nudging operates in
industrial B2B networks: interaction, adaptation and
conceptualization. These constructs emerged from the
systematic mapping of nudging types (simplification and
framing, changes to the physical environment, default policies
and social norms) onto the three layers of the ARA model:
activity chains, resource connections and actor ties. Rather
than treating these layers as static categories, the discussion
interprets them as dynamic mechanisms through which
sustainability is enacted and reinforced. The following sections
elaborate on how each construct captures a specific mode of
behavioral influence and relational change, contributing to
long-term sustainable value creation in B2B marketing
strategies.
5.1 Interaction: nudging in activity chains
Building on the theoretical integration of nudging and network
perspectives, the findings illustrate how interaction-based
nudging operates within the activity chains dimension of the
ARA model (Håkansson and Snehota, 1995; Lenney and
Easton, 2009). This layer encompasses the technical,
administrative and commercial exchanges between actors and
is central to how sustainability-related behaviors are
coordinated across B2B networks (Ford et al., 2011; Gadde
and Håkansson, 2001).
Managers primarily use simplification and framing of
information to make sustainability comprehensible and
actionable. This aligns with Lehner et al. (2016), who
emphasize that nudging through simplification is particularly
effective in complex decision environments. In the case study,
managers translate abstract sustainability data into concrete
visualizations, such as the “27 laps around the world” analogy
and develop tools like CO 2 calculators to quantify
environmental impact. These practices reflect the importance
of choice architecture in guiding decision-making (Thaler and
Sunstein, 2008; Lehner et al., 2016). At the same time,
managers are cautious about credibility, navigating the risk of
greenwashing, a concern echoed in sustainability marketing
literature (Sharma et al., 2010; Huang et al., 2022). Internally,
nudging is reinforced through organizational framing, such as
linking sustainability to corporate values and policy documents
(e.g. Nolato Spirit brochure), which supports earlier research
on the role of internal culture in sustainability transitions
(Munksgaard and Medlin, 2014; Aarikka-Stenroos et al.,
2017).
5.2 Adaptation: nudging in resource connections
In the resource connections dimension, managers drive
sustainability by adapting and aligning resources across
organizational boundaries. This reflects the ARA model’s
emphasis on how resource ties evolve through interaction and
mutual adaptation (Håkansson and Snehota, 1995; Lenney
and Easton, 2009). By reframing material choices, modifying
physical and digital environments and responding to default
policies set by customers, managers embed sustainability
directly into the resource layer of the network (Gadde and
Håkansson, 2001; Cantù et al., 2012).
Managers use simplification and framing of information to
clarify distinctions between sustainable materials, such as mass-
balanced versus bio-based plastics. This aligns with nudging
theory, which highlights the role of cognitive framing in guiding
environmentally responsible choices (Lehner et al., 2016;
Thaler and Sunstein, 2008). The use of analogies like
comparing mass-balanced materials to green electricity
certificates, serves as a form of choice architecture that helps
customers navigate complex sustainability decisions (Schmidt
and Engelen, 2020).
Incremental changes to the physical environment, such as
stepwise waste reduction initiatives, reflect the importance of
gradual adaptation in sustainability transitions (Markard et al.,
2012; Farla et al., 2012). These design choices lower resistance
and foster supplier engagement, consistent with research on

5.3 Conceptualization: nudging in actor ties
In the actor ties dimension, managers deploy multiple nudging
strategies to foster sustainable relationships. This layer of the
ARA model emphasizes how trust, norms and shared
understandings evolve between actors and shape long-term
collaboration (Håkansson and Snehota, 1995; Lenney and
Easton, 2009). Nudging in actor ties goes beyond interaction
and adaptation, and it institutionalizes sustainability as a
relational and cultural concept embedded in the network’s social
fabric (Ford et al., 2011; Halinen et al., 1999).
One key strategy involves the simplification and framing of
information, where managers offer templates and practical
guidance to suppliers lacking environmental policies. This
reflects the role of cognitive scaffolding in nudging theory,
where simplifying complex requirements enables behavioral
change (Lehner et al., 2016; Thaler and Sunstein, 2008), hence
illustrating how resource and knowledge sharing fosters
sustainability in networked collaborations (Cantù et al., 2012;
Aarikka-Stenroos et al., 2017).
In addition, default policies are embedded in formal codes of
conduct, which guide stakeholder behavior and establish
sustainability expectations as standard. These practices
demonstrate how normative structures can be designed to
nudge actors toward responsible behavior, aligning with
insights on institutionalized sustainability in B2B networks
(Schmidt and Engelen, 2020; Sharma et al., 2010). Managers
also leverage social norms by emphasizing geographical
differences in sustainability practices, using Sweden’s
reputation to influence international customers. This approach
highlights how contextual framing and peer comparison can
shape perceptions and decisions (Goldstein et al., 2008;
Schwartz, 2007). Moreover, leadership is conceptualized as a
nudging process, where influence is exerted gradually rather
than through mandates. This leadership style reflects a
relational approach to change management in networks, where
trust and credibility are central (Flygansvær et al., 2021;
Munksgaard and Medlin, 2014). Employee commitment
further reinforces these norms, contributing to a self-sustaining
culture of sustainability.
Through these conceptual nudges, managers transform
sustainability from a compliance issue into a shared relational
norm, embedded in the everyday interactions and expectations
of the industrial network (Ford et al., 2011; Turnbull and Valla,
1986).

5.5 Practical contribution: management implications
Driving sustainable change in industrial networks requires
strategic leadership rooted in trust and gradual behavioral
influence. This study shows that nudging functions as a
relational leadership tool, where subtle cues are progressively
intensified, aligned with the principles of libertarian
paternalism (Thaler and Sunstein, 2008).
A key leadership strategy involves simplifying complex
environmental data through analogies, visual tools and
calculation models, making sustainability more understandable
and actionable while reducing the risk of greenwashing (Lehner
et al., 2016; Schmidt and Engelen, 2020). Strategic leverage
points, such as supplier codes of conduct and digital
collaboration platforms, enable structural embedding of
sustainability across the network (Cantù et al., 2012; Aarikka-
Stenroos and Ritala, 2017). These interventions shape routines
and expectations that guide actors’ behavior over time. Social
norms also play a critical role. Managers use peer comparisons,
geographic framing, and collective recognition to reinforce
sustainable practices. For example, Sweden’s sustainability
reputation is leveraged in international partnerships, and
employee-driven initiatives foster a self-reinforcing
sustainability culture (Goldstein et al., 2008; Schwartz, 2007).
A central challenge in sustainability management is the
translation of complex environmental data into actionable
insights. Managers address this by simplifying information
through analogies, visual tools and calculation models, which
facilitate understanding and decision-making among
stakeholders (Lehner et al., 2016; Mora Cortez and Johnston,
2020). These techniques also mitigate the risk of skepticism
and accusations of greenwashing, which often arise when
sustainability claims lack transparency or clarity (Schmidt and
Engelen, 2020).
Sustainability has evolved from an ethical concern to a strategic
imperative in B2B markets. This study suggests that managers
who apply nudging techniques effectively can accelerate
sustainability transitions, strengthen stakeholder relationships and
enhance competitive positioning in response to regulatory and
market shifts (Huang et al., 2022; Davidavic ˇien_e et al., 2024).
5.5.1 Study limitations and future research
This study offers in-depth insights into how nudging operates
within industrial B2B networks, but its scope is limited to a
single case in the pharmaceutical packaging industry. While the
case provides a rich empirical context, the findings may not be
directly transferable to other sectors with different regulatory
environments, network structures or sustainability pressures.
Further research is needed to examine how nudging strategies
manifest across diverse industrial contexts, particularly in
sectors with varying degrees of complexity, digital maturity and
stakeholder configurations. Comparative studies could reveal
whether certain types of nudges are more effective in specific
network contexts or cultural environments.
Longitudinal research would also be valuable to understand
how the effectiveness of nudging evolves, especially as
sustainability practices become institutionalized and expectations
shift. Such studies could capture the temporal dynamics of
behavioral change and the durability of nudging interventions.
Additionally, the ethical dimensions of nudging in B2B
contexts warrant closer examination. While nudging preserves
choice, its strategic use in business relationships raises
questions about transparency, power asymmetries and consent.
Emerging digital technologies, such as AI-driven platforms and
data analytics, may introduce new forms of nudging that
require critical scrutiny regarding their design, deployment and
impact on organizational autonomy.

1. Sustainability and strategic business development
a) How do you define sustainable development and
operations in your company?
b) How do you work strategically with sustainable
development in management?
c) Describe the core of your business, and how you
work with sustainability.
d) How do you view profitability in relation to
sustainable development?
e) What challenges do you face with sustainability and
its connection to quality?
2. Management and sustainable development
a) What is your role as a leader in the work with
sustainable development?
b) What roles do the customers and suppliers play?
c) What is the employee’s role?
d) How do you perceive yourself as a leader and formal
manager?
3. Relationship between management, employee engagement
and customer relations
a) What do engaged employees mean to you?
b) What do engaged customers mean to you?
c) How do you work to achieve engaged employees?
d) How do you work to achieve engaged customers?
4. Nudging
a) Have you implemented any nudging strategies to
promote sustainability? If yes, can you describe
them?
b) Which nudging methods do you believe are
most effective in encouraging more sustainable
behaviors?
c) How do you think nudging can contribute to
increasing the company’s economic sustainability?
d) Do you use any nudging strategies to engage
customers more in your sustainability goals?
e) How do you think customers’ behavior can be
influenced by nudging to promote more sustainable
consumption behavior?
5. Challenges
a) What are the biggest challenges you face in
implementing sustainability initiatives in B2B contexts?
b) What new opportunities do you see for sustainable
innovation and marketing in the B2B sector?
c) How do you think B2B relationships will change as a
result of increased focus on sustainability?

About the authors
Monika Hattinger has a PhD in Informatics with specialization
in work-integrated learning, at the University West, Sweden. Her
work examines how digital technologies can effectively support
industrial operators in manufacturing environments while
recognizing human expertise’s critical role in complex
production systems. Hattinger also investigates blended e-
learning approaches addressing industry knowledge needs,
evaluating how different case-based methodologies impact
practitioner learning outcomes. Her research explores the
changing roles of operators and maintenance personnel in
Industry 4.0 contexts, particularly during error recovery
processes in automated systems. Hattinger’s holistic approach to
industrial digitalization emphasizes the importance of workplace
practices and human factors alongside technological
implementations, contributing valuable insights to work-
integrated learning and human−computer interaction fields.
Malin Ulrika Sundstrom is a Professor at the School of
Business, Economics and IT at University West, Sweden,
specializing in digital transformation, consumer behavior and
marketing strategies in the evolving retail landscape. Her
research explores how digitalization reshapes marketing,
consumer experiences and business models. Her work spans
loyalty concepts, brand relationships, social media marketing
and digital transformation in marketing communications. She
has published extensively on how digital technologies
influence consumer decision-making and reshape traditional
marketing practices. Sundström contributes innovative
frameworks for retail marketing in digital environments,
exploring business opportunities in convenience-driven
markets and consumer engagement with digital marketing.
Her research also addresses sustainability in marketing and the
role of social media in B2B communications. With
publications in international marketing journals and books in
Swedish and English, Sundström bridges theoretical
marketing concepts with practical applications, providing
insights into the ongoing transformation of retail marketing
and its implications for businesses and consumer
relationships. Malin Ulrika Sundstrom is the corresponding
author and can be contacted at: malin.sundstrom@hv.se
Livia Norstrom is a Senior Lecturer in informatics at the
School of Business, Economics and IT at University West,
Sweden. Her research spans multiple domains including public
sector innovation, sustainable practices in global industries and
digital infrastructure dynamics. In the textile industry context,
Norström investigates alternatives to traditional social auditing
approaches, developing digital ecosystems that empower workers
and suppliers while addressing structural non-compliance issues.
Her work conceptualizes how digital practices can rebalance
power dynamics in global value chains and contribute to
sustainable development. Her interdisciplinary approach
combines socio-technical perspectives with ecosystem thinking
to address grand societal challenges through thoughtful digital
transformation.
