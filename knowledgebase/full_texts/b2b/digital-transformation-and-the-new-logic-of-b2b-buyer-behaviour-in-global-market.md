Modern business -to-business markets are changing under the influenc e of
factors such as digitalization, increasing demands for business transparency, and the
need to verify the reliability of partners. In these conditions, the behavior of business-
to-business buyers is increasingly transforming, as they begin to independe ntly
search for information, compare suppliers through digital channels, and make
decisions based on the data obtained. The purpose of the study is to identify the
directions of transformation of business -to-business buyer behavior in global
markets under the influence of digital technologies, compliance, cybersecurity, and
transparent business interaction. Attention is paid to the transition from a supplier -
driven model of persuasion to a model in which the buyer independently collects and
verifies evidence for the selection of a supplier. It is substantiated that the modern
business-to-business buyer is becoming more autonomous, demanding of digital
tools, and oriented towards information that can be verified. It is shown that the
ability to independently search and select a supplier, digital channels, certificates of
conformity, transparency, and cybersecurity guarantees influence decisions about
including a supplier in the list of potential partners and building trust. A matrix of
business-to-business buyer behavior changes, a decision center behavior model, a
ladder of trust signals, and a business -to-business buyer behavior transformation
cycle are proposed. The study substantiates that business -to-business marketing is
gradually moving from buyer persua sion toward evidence coordination that helps
buyers search, compare, verify and justify supplier selection. Further research should
be directed to deepening the analysis of business -to-business buyer behavior, in
particular, the impact of digital transpare ncy, compliance readiness, cybersecurity,
and verifiable evidence on trust, partner selection, and company competitiveness.
Keywords: business-to-business marketing, buyer behaviour, digital
transformation, organizational buying, institutional trust, omnichannel, self-service,
decision-making unit

usiness-to-business (B2B) marketing is
increasingly defined by behavioural change
rather than by technological modernization
alone, since digital tools matter not only
because firms adopt them, but because t hey alter how
organizational buyers search, compare, consult and
validate purchasing decisions inside their own
companies. Classical B2B theory described
organizational buying as rational, formalized,
collective and relationship based, and this view
remains analytically valuable because purchasing
decisions still involve risk, accountability and multiple
actors, yet it is no longer sufficient for explaining how
buyers behave in digital and institutionally demanding
markets [1-3].
The contemporary B2B buyer has not become less
rational; rather, buyer rationality is now enacted
through a different behavioural infrastructure in which
independent online research, peer content, supplier
portals, procurement platforms, automated comparison
tools, compliance docume ntation, cybersecurity
statements and sustainability evidence become part of
the decision process before any formal meeting with a
sales representative. As a result, the traditional
supplier-controlled path is weakened, while the buyer
gains greater contro l over the sequence, pace, and
evidence base of the purchasing decision.
The findings from leading consulting and research
companies indicate that buyer autonomy, digital self -
service and ease of interaction are becoming stable
behavioural characteristics of contemporary B2B
markets. The transformation is also institutional
because organizational buyers operate in global value
chains where partner selection is shaped by
compliance, cybersecurity, responsible business
conduct, sustainability reporting and br oader
requirements for transparent and accountable business
interaction. This transformation requires a behavioural
interpretation of digital transformation, since the
marketing significance of a technology depends on
how organizational buyers incorporate it into search,
evaluation, coordination and justification routines.

Analysis of recent publications on the problem
The literature on organizational buying began from
the recognition that B2B purchasing differs from
consumer purchasing because it is embedded in
organizational roles, formal procedures, budget
constraints and risk accountability. Webster and Wind
explained organizational buying through environmen -
tal, organizational, interpersonal and individual
variables [1], Sheth emphasized expectati ons, role
perceptions and conflict resolution within the buying
process [2], while Robinson, Faris and Wind
conceptualized purchasing as a sequence of stages and
buying situations that vary according to novelty and
complexity [3].
These early models remain  useful because they
show why B2B buying cannot be understood as the
preference of one individual, yet their behavioural
context was largely pre -digital, with high information
asymmetry, limited supplier visibility and strong
dependence on personal contact . In that setting, trust
accumulated through repeated interaction, reputation,
direct experience and the work of salespeople who
acted simultaneously as information providers,
relationship builders and uncertainty reducers.
The Industrial Marketing and Pur chasing tradition
deepened this logic by interpreting business markets as
networks of long -term relationships rather than
isolated transactions [4], while relationship marketing
positioned trust and commitment as central
mechanisms of exchange performance [5]. Anderson
and Narus added that supplier value is created through
cooperation and through the customer's evaluation of
benefits, sacrifices and relationship outcomes [6],
which makes value fundamentally interactional rather
than purely product-based.
Ukrainian scholarship has also contributed to the
conceptualization of industrial and B2B marketing as a
specific field of interorganizational exchange.
Starostina, Dligach and Kravchenko systematized
industrial marketing by combining theoretical
foundations, international experience and Ukrainian
practice [7], Dobrianska and Halaida examined
marketing of production -sector enterprises with
attention to organizational buyer behaviour and the
marketing mix in production markets [8], while
Boychuk linked the cla ssical Ukrainian tradition of
industrial marketing with the modern terminology of
business-to-business marketing [9].
Digital transformation research explains why these
classical assumptions require refinement. Vial defines
digital transformation as a proc ess in which digital
technologies generate changes in value creation and
organizational response [10], while Verhoef and
coauthors emphasize that digital transformation affects
strategy, structure, channels and customer relationships
[11]. In B2B marketing , the most immediate
behavioural consequence is a changed information
environment in which buyers gain access to supplier
websites, online demonstrations, product
documentation, peer reviews, configurators, webinars,
procurement tools and AI -supported comp arison
before direct supplier engagement.
Harvard Business Review research captures the
managerial consequences of this change. Almquist,
Cleghorn and Sherer show that B2B buyers evaluate
not only functional benefits but also ease of doing
business, confid ence, reputational effects and social
responsibility [12], Adamson argues that traditional
linear sales and marketing handoffs are increasingly
misaligned with complex B2B buying [13], and Sinha,
Shastri and Lorimer emphasize that digitally connected
buyers use multiple channels and expect coordinated
interactions across personal and digital touch -
points [14].
Identification of previously unresolved parts of the
general problem
The current transformation of B2B buyer behaviour
requires a reconsideration of how digitalization,
decision-making unit (DMU) reconfiguration and
institutional trust are conceptualized in contemporary
marketing research. These themes are often analysed
separately, although in actual buying practice they
increasingly operate as interconnected elements of the
same process, because buyers collect evidence
digitally, DMU members interpret it through
professional risk lenses and institutional trust signals
influence whether a supplier can be justified internally.
A further conceptual clari fication is needed in
relation to omnichannel behaviour. Digital B2B buying
is frequently described through the language of
channels, yet buyers experience purchasing as a
sequence of organizational tasks that must be
completed with minimum uncertainty, in cluding
problem understanding, supplier comparison, collea -
gue involvement, risk evaluation, internal defence of
the preferred option and post -purchase monitoring. A
channel becomes relevant only when it helps complete
one of these tasks, which is why this  article interprets
omnichannel behaviour as task -oriented evidence
navigation rather than media diversification.
The behavioural interpretation of B2B buying also
needs to be connected with institutional theory because
contemporary buyers increasingly mak e decisions
under conditions of auditability, transparency and
internal accountability. Procurement teams,
sustainability officers, legal departments and IT
security specialists do not merely influence
preferences; they establish approval conditions that
define acceptable supplier choice. In this sense,
institutional trust enters the decision process by
structuring attention, shaping risk perception and
limiting options that can be internally approved [15].
Formulation of research objectives (task setting)
The aim of the article is  to identify the main
directions of transformation in B2B buyer behaviour in
global markets under the influence of digital
technologies, institutional trust, compliance, cyber -
security and growing requirements for transparent
business interaction. Special attention is paid to the
shift from a model in which the supplier mainly
persuades the buyer through personal contacts and
control over information flow to a model in which the
buyer independently collects, verifies and uses

Materials and methods
The materials of the study include the works of

Presentation of main results and their justification
In the classical model, the supplier controlled much
of the information flow through sales meetings,
catalogues, trade fairs, demonstrations and acco unt
management, whereas in the digital-institutional model
buyers often reach supplier contact already informed,
partly aligned internally and equipped with comparison
criteria formed through independent digital research.
This does not make marketing less important, but
changes its position in the buying process, since
searchable technical documentation, product
information, implementation cases, certifications,
demo environments, data security statements, ESG
disclosures and procurement -ready materials bec ome
behavioural resources that help buyers verify value,
reduce uncertainty and prepare internal justification.
Recent global studies show that this tendency is
structural rather than temporary. McKinsey &
Company identifies a persistent rule of thirds in B2B
interaction, according to which organizational buyers
distribute their activity among in -person, remote and
self-service channels rather than relying on one
dominant mode of communication [16]. Gartner
reports that 61 percent of B2B buyers preferred a rep-
free buying experience in 2025 and 67 percent
expressed such a preference in 2026 [17 , 18], while
Deloitte shows that 81 percent of B2B buyers expect
more self -service and web -based tools, 69 percent
prefer digital platforms for reordering and 37 perce nt
are willing to pay a premium for suppliers that make
the buying process easier [19]. These criteria are not
external additions to marketing practice, they become
behavioural filters that determine whether a supplier
can enter the shortlist, pass interna l approval and be
justified to procurement, finance, legal, IT security and
senior management stakeholders [20-23].
Evidence coordination differs from persuasion
because it does not assume that the supplier can control
the buyer through arguments, promotional pressure or
personal relationships.

Buyer autonomy is the most visible behavioural
change, yet it should not be interpreted as an anti-sales
tendency because buyers usually want less unsolicited
selling rather than less expertise. Rep-free preferences
reflect a desire to control the pace and sequence of
engagement, to avoid irrelevant outreach and to collect
evidence without social pressure, while expert contact
remains valuable when the buyer must interpret
complexity, justify risk  or coordinate internal
stakeholders [17, 18]. Self-service behaviour is also an
efficiency behaviour because it reduces cognitive
effort, administrative delay and dependence on
repetitive communication. When buyers can reorder,
track, compare, configure or download documentation
without contacting a representative, the supplier
becomes easier to work with, and ease of doing
business becomes an economic attribute rather than a
service luxury [19]. Nonlinear omnichannel behaviour
strengthens this logic becau se buyers do not move
through a neat funnel from awareness to purchase; they
move back and forth between learning, comparison,
validation, internal discussion, expert consultation and
renewed evaluation. McKinsey's evidence that there is
no single channel customer confirms that omnichannel
should be understood as a behavioural setting where
buyers expect continuity, choice and consistency
across self -service, remote and in person
interactions [16].
In such an environment, consistency means that
product info rmation, pricing logic, service promises,
compliance evidence, sustainability claims and expert
explanations should not contradict one another.
Inconsistency becomes a trust problem because it
forces the buyer to spend additional effort reconciling
evidence, and this friction may be enough to remove a
supplier from consideration.
The decision -making unit remains central to B2B
behaviour, yet it becomes more distributed and
asynchronous in digital markets. Procurement
specialists, u sers, IT experts, finance departments,
compliance officers and senior managers may interact
with supplier evidence at different moments, through
different channels and without the supplier knowing
that evaluation has already begun. This shift means that
suppliers may lose a potential deal before the formal
sales conversation if an invisible stakeholder cannot
find role-relevant proof. A technical user may reject a
supplier because the demo is unclear, IT may hesitate
because cybersecurity documentation is i ncomplete,
finance may block the proposal because total cost
evidence is weak, and compliance may slow the
process because ownership transparency or responsible
conduct documentation is unavailable. The marketing
function therefore becomes a coordinator of  evidence
across the whole DMU. A single corporate message
cannot support all stakeholders, while an evidence
architecture can help each actor find the artefact needed
for internal interpretation and still maintain one
coherent supplier narrative. These ch anges in the

Trust remains fundamental in B2B marketing, but
the behavioural sources of trust have expanded from
interpersonal familiarity toward digital, operational
and institutional proof. Relationship continuity and
reputation continue to matter, yet buyers increasingly
ask whether supplier claims  can be demonstrated
through data, certifications, audit trails, cybersecurity
assurances, compliance systems, sustainability
disclosures and third-party verification [20-23].
Digital trust is therefore not an abstract attitude, it
is a behavioural conditi on that allows the buyer to
continue the process. When sufficient trust signals are
available, the buyer is more willing to shortlist the
supplier, request a demonstration, share data, involve
senior stakeholders or move toward contractual
commitment, wher eas missing trust signals produce
delay, additional documentation requests or exclusion.
ESG, cybersecurity and compliance work as
behavioural filters because they determine whether a
supplier can pass to the next stage of evaluation before
price and product superiority are fully assessed. This
does not mean that every B2B decision is primarily
sustainability driven, but it means that institutional
risks increasingly shape the shortlist and the internal
approval process, especially  in cross -border value
chains where buyers must justify supplier selection to
regulators, investors, customers and internal
governance bodies [20, 22, 23]. The role of trust signals
in supplier evaluation is summarized in Table 3, which

The conducted conceptual synthesis suggests that
contemporary B2B buyer behaviour is better
represented as a transformation cycle than as a linear
funnel. A funnel implies a sequential narrowing of
alternatives controlled largely by the supplier, whereas
the cycle reflects repeated evidence seeking, risk
interpretation, internal justification, expert validation
and continuous post -purchase assessment. The cycle
begins when a business problem, regulatory
requirement, disruption or innovation need triggers
search. The buyer then enters an autonomous research
phase, screens suppliers according to the availability of
credible evidence, interprets this evidence through
role-specific DMU lenses, engages human experts
when complexity remains, commits when the decision
can be justified internally and continues validating the
supplier through dashboards, service data and renewal
evidence. This logic is summarized in Table 4.
Several propositions fol low from the proposed
framework. Reliable self -service evidence can reduce
buyers’ willingness to engage with unsolicited early -
stage outreach, consistency across digital, remote and
personal channels can strengthen buyer confidence and
shortlist inclusion , evidence prepared for non -
commercial DMU members can support internal
consensus, institutional trust signals can function as
preliminary filters before product superiority is fully
evaluated, and human sales interaction creates the
greatest value after a utonomous buyer research when
the buyer needs interpretation, customization and
internal decision support. The framework also explains
why digital transformation does not automatically
improve B2B marketing performance. Digital tools
create value only when  they support real buyer tasks
and provide information that can be trusted, reused and
connected to the next step of the decision process,
which links technological design with behavioural
usefulness, evidence consistency and institutional
credibility.

Abstract

Modern business-to-business markets are undergoing substantial transformation under the influence of
digitalization, increasing transparency requirements, cybersecurity concerns, compliance expectations and the
growing need to verify the reliability of partners i n global value chains. These changes affect not only marketing
tools and communication channels, but also the behaviour of B2B buyers. Organizational buyers increasingly
conduct independent online research, compare suppliers through digital channels, use s upplier portals,
procurement platforms, technical documentation, certificates, cybersecurity statements, compliance information
and sustainability evidence before direct contact with sales representatives. As a result, the traditional supplier -
controlled m odel of persuasion is weakening, while buyers gain greater control over the pace, sequence and
evidence base of purchasing decisions.
The purpose of the study is to identify the main directions of transformation in B2B buyer behaviour in global
markets und er the influence of digital technologies, institutional trust, compliance, cybersecurity and growing
requirements for transparent business interaction. The study focuses on the shift from a model in which the supplier
mainly persuades the buyer through per sonal contact and control over information flow to a model in which the
buyer independently collects, verifies and uses evidence for supplier selection and internal approval of the
purchasing decision.
The research is based on theoretical generalization, c omparative analysis, systematization, logical
generalization and conceptual modelling. The study uses classical and contemporary academic literature on
organizational buying behaviour, industrial and B2B marketing, relationship marketing, digital transformation and
institutional trust, as well as analytical reports and managerial publications on B2B buyer behaviour, self -service,
omnichannel interaction and digital evidence in supplier evaluation.
The results show that the contemporary B2B buyer is becoming  more autonomous, digitally active and
evidence-oriented. The study substantiates that self -service tools, digital channels, documentation, certifications,
transparency, cybersecurity assurances, compliance readiness and sustainability evidence increasingl y influence
supplier shortlisting, internal approval and trust formation. The article proposes several author -developed
analytical tools: a matrix of B2B buyer behavioural shifts, a digital -institutional model of decision -making unit
behaviour, a trust-signal ladder for B2B supplier evaluation and a transformation cycle of B2B buyer behaviour.
These tools systematize how organizational buyers search, compare, verify, interpret and justify supplier choice
in digital and institutionally demanding markets.
The study concludes that contemporary B2B marketing is gradually moving from supplier -led persuasion
toward buyer-enabled evidence coordination. In this logic, marketing should not only communicate value, but also
organize reliable, accessible and role -specific evidence that helps different members of the decision -making unit
evaluate suppliers, reduce uncertainty, justify decisions internally and continuously validate supplier relationships
after purchase. Further research should deepen the conceptual and empirical analysis of how digital transparency,
compliance readiness, cybersecurity and verifiable supplier evidence influence trust formation, partner selection
and company competitiveness in international B2B markets.
