Abstract
The growing digitalization of tourism services has reshaped consumer behavior, yet many users, particularly in
developing markets, still hesitate to make online purchases due to perceived risks and low technological trust. This
study investigates the determinants of customers ’ purchase intention on online tourism platforms by integrating the
Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and Ecosystem Theory within a hybrid Fuzzy Delphi –Fuzzy Cognitive Mapping
(FCM) framework. Drawing on data from both experts and users, the research identi ﬁes 27 interrelated factors
inﬂuencing behavioral intention. Findings show that technological trust, perceived usefulness, and perceived risk
have the greatest effects, while social reassurance and usability act as key mediators. Theoretically, the study extends
TPB through dynamic trust –risk interactions modeled via FCM, advancing understanding of online decision-making.
It contributes new knowledge by linking psychological and technological perspectives, and offers practical and
managerial insights for enhancing user trust, design usability, and policy support for reliable digital tourism
ecosystems.
Introduction
Online tourism platforms such as Booking.com, Expedia, Trip.com, and regional players like Alibaba.ir and
Snapptrip have redeﬁned how consumers plan and purchase travel services by integrating ﬂights, accommodation, car
rentals, and experiences into uni ﬁed ecosystems (Aamir et al., 2025; Astanakulov et al., 2025; Panoiu and Foris,
2024). Despite online booking activity projected to surpass USD 1.3 trillion by 2025, developing markets continue to
show low digital purchase conversion, constrained by limited trust in online payment systems, incomplete digital
infrastructure, and the need for personalized reassurance (Turn šek and Radivojevi ć, 2025). Understanding purchase
intention allows businesses to allocate resources toward consumers most likely to convert, tailor personalized travel
offers, and enhance engagement (Çaylak et al., 2024; Wicaksono and Putra, 2022), making it indispensable for both
theoretical and practical advancement (Thirumaran et al., 2021). However, in developing countries where digital
payment trust and platform familiarity remain immature, the speci ﬁc psychological and technological mechanisms
that translate interest into purchase intention are still poorly understood, and no integrated model resolves this gap
(Chin et al., 2024; Shariffuddin et al., 2023).

Adoption of online tourism platforms in developing countries remains comparatively low, constrained by limited
digital literacy, payment security concerns, and a preference for in-person interaction (Jawad et al., 2022; Lama et al.,
2020). While over 70% of travelers begin booking online and roughly 60% complete at least one digital purchase
globally (Abashidze, 2024), online travel sales in Iran and the MENA region, though growing 15 –18% annually, still
account for less than half of total bookings, re ﬂecting a persistent digital trust gap (Moghadasnian, 2024). Many
Iranian businesses accordingly maintain physical branches alongside their online presence, re ﬂecting a cautious,
hybrid approach to digital transformation that balances technological advancement with consumer trust concerns
(Mirzaei et al., 2023). Addressing this requires marketing strategies that account for both online and of ﬂine touch-
points to gradually build trust (Lama et al., 2020). These challenges are magni ﬁed in tourism by the high involve-
ment, ﬁnancial commitment, and uncertainty of travel decisions, which require consumers to trust intangible services
based on limited information (Brdesee, 2024; Sembiring and Nisa, 2023); consequently, perceived risk, platform
trustworthiness, and usability are pivotal in determining whether potential customers complete a purchase, particu-
larly in markets like Iran where skepticism toward online payment persists despite growing platform accessibility
(Pratesi et al., 2021; Putrevu and Mertzanis, 2024; Zhou et al., 2022).
Even where platforms offer convenience, transparency, and integrated services, many consumers in developing
markets still hesitate to transact digitally (Elsawy, 2023; Mofokeng et al., 2024; Tiwari et al., 2024). This hesitation
reﬂects a complex interplay of trust, perceived usefulness, ease of use, and social reassurance rather than ﬁnancial or
privacy risk alone (Haque et al., 2023), and the absence of an integrated explanatory model limits both theoretical
understanding and managerial decision-making in online tourism (Damiasih, 2025; Wang, 2025).
Psychological factors shape how consumers make decisions and engage with online shopping, at times deterring
purchase completion (Shahbaz et al., 2020), and marketers apply psychological principles ethically to motivate action
(Mehreen et al., 2020). Personality and hedonic values guide online purchase intention (Lim and Kim, 2020), while
emotional intelligence helps consumers navigate shopping complexity and mediates social in ﬂuences such as word-
of-mouth; in tourism speci ﬁcally, emotional responses to destination imagery and user-generated content in ﬂuence
booking intention (Cavalcante et al., 2021). Positive attitudes, shaped by electronic word-of-mouth and brand image,
enhance purchase intention (Wicaksono and Putra, 2022), while the inability to physically examine travel services
heightens perceived risk and vulnerability (Boediman et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020). Platforms can mitigate this
through transparency, ﬂexible booking, and testimonials (Volgger et al., 2021), and reducing risk through transparent
pricing and ﬂexible cancellation policies fosters trust and purchase intention (Pasquinelli et al., 2023). Understanding
these psychological elements enables marketers to tailor strategies to consumers ’ underlying motivations ( Širola and
Sudac, 2021).
Several gaps remain in this literature. Širola and Sudac ( 2021) do not explore dynamic interactions among
psychological factors or the evolving nature of consumer behavior over time. Lim and Kim ( 2020) examine emotional
intelligence with a narrow demographic sample that limits generalizability and omits external factors such as cultural
or socio-economic differences. Zhang et al. ( 2021) focus on repeat purchase intention in peer-to-peer accommodation
but neglect the trust, risk, and usability factors driving initial bookings. Yang et al. ( 2019) and Mehreen et al. ( 2020)
offer insights limited to speci ﬁc demographic groups with uncertain broader applicability, while Handayani and
Ariﬁn( 2017) examine e-WOM and TPB factors but overlook technological and usability dimensions such as website
design and user experience. These studies typically isolate either psychological variables (e.g., trust, anxiety,
involvement) or technological/usability variables (e.g., ease of use, interface quality), failing to capture how these
drivers jointly reinforce or suppress purchase intention over time (Baydeni ̇z et al., 2024;L ăzăroiu et al., 2020;
Mehreen et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2023; Širola and Sudac, 2021). This fragmentation is especially problematic in
developing-country online tourism, where distrust in digital payment, reliance on word-of-mouth, and platform
usability act together rather than independently, with no existing model mapping these interdependencies in a
structured, predictive way (Khan and Rehman, 2024).
As the tourism industry accelerates digital transformation through AI-driven personalization, dynamic pricing, and
real-time analytics, understanding the psychological and technological antecedents of purchase intention has become
a strategic necessity (Ashok et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2024), since platforms that fail to account for user trust, usefulness,
and risk perception risk losing competitiveness (ElSayad and Mamdouh, 2024). As the primary booking channel for

To address this gap, this study investigates:
RQ1. What psychological determinants in ﬂuence purchase intention in the context of online tourism platforms?
RQ2. From the perspective of industry experts, how do these psychological determinants collectively impact
purchase intention in developing countries?
RQ3. From the perspective of consumers, how do these psychological determinants collectively in ﬂuence their
purchase intention in developing countries?
RQ4. How can these psychological determinants be conceptualized within the framework of the Theory of Planned
Behavior to provide a deeper understanding of purchase intention in online tourism platforms?
This study makes four contributions. First, it develops a comprehensive factor set spanning psychological, tech-
nological, and usability dimensions rather than treating these in isolation. Second, it integrates TPB with Fuzzy
Cognitive Mapping to model not only which factors matter but how they causally in ﬂuence one another and shape
intention. Third, it incorporates expert judgment and consumer perceptions from a developing-market context where
full digital adoption is not assumed, extending the external validity of intention models beyond mature e-commerce
settings. Fourth, it produces an interpretable decision-support structure identifying which levers, such as technolo-
gical trust and perceived usefulness, tourism platforms should prioritize to increase conversion where skepticism
toward online payment remains high.
The paper proceeds as follows. The Literature Review examines psychological and technological factors in ﬂu-
encing purchase intention in online tourism. The Methodology section details the research approach, including the
application of FCM to identify and rank key factors. The Results section presents the ranked factors and their
dynamic interactions, highlighting trust, usefulness, and behavioral intention. The Discussion interprets these ﬁndings
in relation to TPB and their implications for marketing strategy and user experience. The Conclusion synthesizes the
study’s insights, emphasizing the practical signi ﬁcance of trust and usefulness for enhancing engagement and
conversion.
Literature review
Theoretical foundations
In this research, we have considered two basic theories as theoretical lenses. The ﬁrst theory is Ecosystem Theory,
which was chosen to align with online tourism platforms, and the second theory is TBP, which was used to categorize
and summarize the psychological factors affecting the purchase intention of customers in the context of online
tourism platforms. The description of each of these two theories is described below.
Ecosystem theory as a theoretical lens
The ecosystem theory serves as a robust theoretical lens for understanding the psychological factors in ﬂuencing
consumer purchase intentions in the online tourism industry. This theory posits that individuals do not exist in
isolation but are part of a complex web of interdependent relationships and environmental in ﬂuences. The interactions
between consumers and various elements of the online tourism ecosystem, including technology, social networks,
and market dynamics, signi ﬁcantly shape their purchasing behaviors (Müller, 1997). In the context of consumer
behavior, the ecosystem theory emphasizes the importance of understanding how psychological factors, such as
motivation, perception, and social in ﬂuence, interact with external environmental factors. For instance, the interplay

between individual psychological traits and the broader ecosystem can lead to varying purchase intentions (Mada-
naguli et al., 2022). This is particularly relevant in the online tourism industry, where consumers are in ﬂuenced by
digital marketing strategies, peer reviews, and social media interactions, all of which are integral components of the
tourism ecosystem. The theory suggests that these interactions can create a feedback loop, where consumer behavior
inﬂuences the ecosystem, which in turn affects future consumer decisions (Hillebrand, 2022).
Moreover, the application of ecosystem theory in this context highlights the signi ﬁcance of resilience and
adaptability in consumer behavior. Just as ecosystems exhibit resilience to disturbances, consumers also adapt their
purchasing intentions based on their experiences and the evolving nature of the online environment. This adaptability
is crucial in the tourism sector, where rapid changes in technology and consumer preferences can signi ﬁcantly impact
purchase intentions (Madanaguli et al., 2022). Understanding these dynamics can provide valuable insights for
marketers aiming to enhance consumer engagement and drive sales in the online tourism market. Furthermore, the
integration of psychological factors within the ecosystem framework allows for a more nuanced analysis of consumer
behavior. Factors such as trust, perceived value, and emotional responses to marketing stimuli can be examined
through the lens of ecosystem interactions (Sivarajah, 2024). For example, a consumer ’s trust in an online travel
agency may be in ﬂuenced by their previous experiences, the reputation of the agency within the ecosystem, and the
perceived quality of the services offered. This interconnectedness underscores the need for a holistic approach to
understanding consumer intentions, as individual psychological factors cannot be fully understood without con-
sidering their contextual relationships within the ecosystem. In conclusion, employing ecosystem theory as a theo-
retical lens provides a comprehensive framework for analyzing the psychological factors affecting consumer purchase
intentions in the online tourism industry. By recognizing the intricate relationships between individual behaviors and
the broader ecosystem, researchers and practitioners can better understand and in ﬂuence consumer decisions in this
dynamic market.
Despite its holistic strengths, the application of Ecosystem Theory in consumer behavior research, particularly in
online tourism, remains underdeveloped (Jäckli and Meier, 2025). While prior studies have utilized the ecosystem
lens to understand macro-level interactions between stakeholders (Hillebrand, 2022), limited research has oper-
ationalized its constructs at the micro-level of consumer cognition and behavior (Wang et al., 2023). This study
contributes by bridging that gap: it interprets psychological factors (e.g., trust, perceived value, and risk) as micro-
elements nested within the broader digital tourism ecosystem. By doing so, it extends the theory ’s applicability from
organizational relationships to individual decision-making, positioning the consumer as an adaptive agent within a
dynamic socio-technical environment. This theoretical adaptation not only strengthens the conceptual foundation but
also contextualizes the psychological drivers examined later in the FCM analysis.

The theory of planned behavior
TPB was also chosen as the theoretical lens for this research on examining the factors in ﬂuencing customer purchase
intention on online tourism service platforms due to its comprehensive ability to integrate psychological, social, and
behavioral dimensions (Ajzen, 1991). The TPB, shown in Fig. 1, explains that an individual ’s behavior is guided by
three primary constructs: attitude toward the behavior, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. Attitude
reﬂects a customer’s evaluation of purchasing tourism services online, whether positive or negative. Subjective norms
encompass the in ﬂuence of social factors, such as opinions from friends, family, or online reviews, that shape the
intention to make a purchase. Perceived behavioral control relates to a customer ’s perception of the ease or dif ﬁculty
of completing the purchase, in ﬂuenced by factors like platform usability, trust in technology, and individual psy-
chological traits such as anxiety (Hagger et al., 2022). This model offers a robust framework to understand how these
factors jointly impact purchase intentions in an online tourism service context (Ajzen, 1991).
While TPB is widely used to explain behavioral intention, most prior applications treat its three constructs as linear
predictors (Juma and Douglas, 2025). This study re ﬁnes TPB by embedding it within a Fuzzy Cognitive Mapping
framework, allowing feedback and interdependence among attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral
control. The integration converts TPB from a static explanatory model into a dynamic analytical tool suitable for
complex digital behaviors such as online tourism booking (Olya et al., 2019; Park et al., 2025). Compared with TAM
or UTAUT, which emphasize technological determinants, TPB uniquely captures social and attitudinal dimensions
critical to high-involvement tourism decisions, justifying its central role in this research (Anantamongkolkul and
Kongma, 2020; Uddin, 2025).
In the context of online tourism platforms in developing markets, TPB is especially valuable because it formalizes
three constructs, attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, that are repeatedly cited in the literature
as relevant to purchase intention, yet have rarely been systematically combined with platform-level trust, usability,
and perceived risk factors in a uni ﬁed analytical model (Anugrah, 2023; Li, 2024).
The combined use of Ecosystem Theory and the Theory of Planned Behavior provides a robust foundation for this
research. Ecosystem Theory situates individual purchase decisions within a dynamic digital environment of inter-
dependent stakeholders, while TPB explains the cognitive and attitudinal mechanisms driving intention formation
(Röntynen, 2024). Together, these theories align with the study ’s research questions by enabling the exploration of
both contextual and psychological determinants of purchase intention (Gulzar et al., 2024). This dual perspective also
guides the conceptual framework and the selection of variables for Fuzzy Cognitive Mapping, allowing the model to
capture feedback loops between environmental, social, and individual-level factors (Ramos and Rodrigues, 2023).
Together, Ecosystem Theory and TPB create a dual framework linking systemic context with individual cognition,
forming the conceptual foundation for the FCM modeling that follows.
Fuzzy cognitive mapping
This study employs FCM to explore the factors in ﬂuencing customer purchase intention on online tourism service
platforms. FCM is particularly suited for modeling complex behavioral systems because it uses fuzzy causal rela-
tionships to represent the interactions among various factors (Wang et al., 2020). It creates a visual model consisting
of nodes, which represent the key variables (factors) in ﬂuencing customer purchase behavior, and directed links
between these nodes, which show the causal relationships among them (Kosko, 1986). These links can be weighted to
reﬂect the strength and nature (positive, negative, or neutral) of these causal interactions (Wang et al., 2020).
This approach directly addresses a methodological limitation in prior purchase-intention studies, which typically
rely on static, linear models (e.g., SEM) that estimate direct effects but do not capture feedback, indirect in ﬂuence, or
scenario evolution (Sadiq et al., 2022). By contrast, FCM allows us to simulate how changes in one construct (e.g.,
technological trust) propagate through perceived risk, perceived usefulness, and ultimately behavioral intention,
which is essential for understanding decision-making in high-uncertainty, low-trust online tourism environments
(Chen et al., 2025; K. Zhu et al., 2023).

As a semi-quantitative methodology, FCM models the evolution of speci ﬁc system dynamics. It is considered a
streamlined approach to mathematically represent complex systems, making it particularly suitable for case studies
where statistical data may be insuf ﬁcient, such as customer behavior in the online tourism context (Wang et al.,
2021). Furthermore, as a participatory modeling technique, FCM empowers local experts to articulate their per-
spectives, which assists the understanding of this vast sector (Bakhtavar et al., 2021).
This process allows for the exploration of “what-if” scenarios, such as examining how an increase in customer trust
might affect overall purchase intention or how perceived value may change based on improvements in service quality
(Nápoles et al., 2022). By comparing the system ’s initial state with various simulated outcomes, we can assess the
effectiveness of different strategies for enhancing customer purchase intention on online tourism platforms. FCM
enables a comprehensive analysis of the complex, interconnected factors driving consumer behavior in online
tourism. By modeling these relationships and simulating potential interventions, this method provides valuable
insights for platform developers, marketers, and policymakers aiming to improve customer engagement and con-
version rates (Bakhtavar et al., 2021).
Research background
Purchase intention
Purchase intention is often conceptualized as a form of planned behavior that is expected to result in a future
purchase. Peña-García et al. ( 2020)d e ﬁne it as the probability of a consumer making a purchase of a product or
service in the near future. From a theoretical standpoint, purchase intention has been most commonly conceptualized
within the Theory of Planned Behavior (Wang et al., 2024), which posits that behavioral intention is driven by
attitude toward the behavior, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control (Abdulghaffar and Bakr, 2024).
Complementarily, the Technology Acceptance Model extends this by introducing perceived usefulness and perceived
ease of use as critical cognitive antecedents shaping attitude and subsequent intention (Liu and Park, 2024; Yan et al.,
2024). These frameworks together suggest that both psychological and technological evaluations determine a con-
sumer’s readiness to act. Furthermore, Pradana ( 2021) emphasizes that the level of purchase intention is shaped by
consumers’ positive attitudes toward a brand, their commitment to it, and their propensity to continue purchasing
products from the brand over time. P. Zhu et al. ( 2023) have demonstrated that perceived risks, particularly in social
and psychological domains, negatively impact purchase intention, while brand credibility exerts a positive in ﬂuence.
Beyond these models, more recent integrative frameworks emphasize the mediating role of trust and the moderating
role of perceived risk in digital contexts (Al-kfairy et al., 2025; Chawla et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2023). Trust serves
as a psychological assurance that reduces uncertainty and strengthens behavioral control, while perceived risk acts as
a counterforce diminishing purchase intention (Aggarwal et al., 2025). In online tourism, where service intangibility
and ﬁnancial exposure are high, these constructs operate as key intermediaries translating consumer attitudes into
actionable intention (Miraz et al., 2025). This perspective aligns with the ﬁndings of Wicaksono and Putra ( 2022),
who also highlight that consumer attitudes, signi ﬁcantly shaped by psychological factors, play a critical role in
explaining online purchasing intentions. In the context of tourism information and communication technology (ICT),
platforms that incorporate advanced technologies, such as real-time feedback and personalized recommendations, are
increasingly inﬂuencing consumers’ purchase intention by enhancing trust and reducing perceived risks (Yang et al.,
2024). Taken together, these perspectives allow us to propose a hybrid theoretical lens that integrates TPB ’s atti-
tudinal and normative components with TAM ’s technological perceptions and the trust –risk paradigm. Under this
view, online purchase intention emerges as a cognitive –affective decision shaped by (1) attitude toward the platform,
(2) perceived usefulness and ease of use, (3) subjective norms and social in ﬂuence, (4) perceived behavioral control,
and (5) the balance between trust and perceived risk. This integrative framework underpins the present study ’s model
and justi ﬁes the selection of variables subsequently analyzed through the FCM approach (Nguyen et al., 2023).

Psychological drivers
Cai et al. ( 2022) emphasize that anxiety, particularly the fear of unpredictable outcomes and lack of control over the
booking process, plays a critical role in shaping online shopping behavior, signi ﬁcantly in ﬂuencing consumers ’
purchase intention. This anxiety can manifest as social anxieties, such as Fear of Missing Out (FOMO), which
accelerates consumer actions and increases purchase intention. In contrast, the Fear of Better Options (FOBO) and
Fear of Disappointing Alternatives (FODA) hinder decision-making, complicating online shopping behaviors
(Boediman et al., 2021). Zhuang et al. ( 2021) argue that consumer attitude, de ﬁned as a favorable or unfavorable
judgment toward online tourism services, signi ﬁcantly affects purchase intention. While positive attitudes toward
online shopping can enhance purchase intention, this effect is moderated by perceived behavioral control (PBC) and
subjective norms. Peña-García et al. ( 2020) illustrate that subjective norms, in ﬂuenced by peers such as friends and
family, shape online buying behavior by creating social pressures tied to potential rewards or punishments. These
norms can signi ﬁcantly impact purchasing decisions, although the extent of in ﬂuence may vary by service type or
platform (Chen et al., 2022). Zhuang et al. ( 2021) further identify PBC as another key factor conditioning purchase
intention, as consumers with higher perceived control over online booking processes are more likely to follow
through on their intentions. Hagger et al. ( 2022) explain that attitude, subjective norms, and PBC collectively shape
purchase intention in both traditional and online shopping contexts. Additionally, Peña-García et al. ( 2020) introduce
the concept of perceived enjoyment, or the pleasure derived from exploring travel options online, which strengthens
decision-making and increases purchase intention. Kim et al. ( 2021) highlight that perceived enjoyment enhances
customer satisfaction and loyalty, especially in personalized interactions. Turner et al. ( 2020) add that enjoyment
from co-design activities in mass customization further boosts both satisfaction and loyalty intentions.
Khan et al. ( 2019) argue that both social media and television advertising positively in ﬂuence purchase intention,
with social media having a more signi ﬁcant impact. Le and Wang ( 2020) emphasize that Location-Based Advertising
(LBA) enhances consumer attitudes by delivering relevant, location-based offers, thus providing valuable advertising
content. Pick ( 2021) further highlights that in ﬂuencer credibility and the relationships they establish with consumers
not only boost purchase intention and positive attitudes but also foster a sense of psychological ownership. The
authenticity of advertising, such as featuring unaltered models, also leads to positive attitudes and higher purchase
intention (Shoenberger et al., 2020). Khodabandeh and Lindh ( 2021) suggest that building a strong brand image
through inﬂuencer ads enhances consumer attitudes and strengthens brand relationships. Tan et al. ( 2021) argue that
the informativeness and persuasiveness of ads improve brand awareness and purchase intention, with company-
created ads outperforming those made by in ﬂuencers, thus enhancing brand identity and customer satisfaction. (Liao
et al., 2025) assert that positive attitudes toward social networking sites and social networking ads can lead to
improved brand perceptions and increased purchase intention. Lastly, Hameed et al. ( 2023) stress that the connection
and authenticity in ﬂuencers create with consumers help build positive attitudes toward both the product and the
brand, ultimately increasing the likelihood of purchase.
Bridging online tourism and smart tourism
The rapid digitalization of the tourism industry has fundamentally transformed consumer interactions with online
platforms, creating opportunities to explore how technology-mediated environments shape purchase intention
(Almakayeel, 2023). While this study examines psychological drivers, it is equally important to situate these factors
within the broader context of technological advancements in tourism. Integrating insights from the smart tourism
literature enriches the theoretical framework by linking the psychological underpinnings of consumer behavior with
the digital innovations that have rede ﬁned service delivery in this domain (Chen et al., 2022).
Smart tourism, as a technologically enhanced paradigm, focuses on leveraging ICTs to create interconnected, data-
driven ecosystems that enhance the consumer experience (Pasquinelli et al., 2023). For example, online platforms
employing big data analytics, arti ﬁcial intelligence, and real-time feedback mechanisms have demonstrated sig-
niﬁcant potential to mitigate perceived risks and improve trust, two factors critical to shaping behavioral intentions
(Chen et al., 2022). By embedding smart tourism concepts into the discussion, this study bridges the gap between

Related works
In the evolving landscape of online consumer behavior, various psychological factors play a pivotal role in shaping
purchasing decisions. Over the past decade, research has increasingly focused on understanding the cognitive,
emotional, and trust-based dimensions that in ﬂuence online purchase behavior, underscoring the complexity of these
factors. Research by Nuradina ( 2022) highlights how motivation, perception, emotions, and trust in ﬂuence buying
behavior, emphasizing that attitudes and emotions are central to decision-making. Similarly, Lim and Kim ’s( 2020)
application of the cognitive-affective-conative model shows that consumers with higher emotional intelligence (EI)
prioritize utilitarian beneﬁts over hedonic ones, leading to greater shopping satisfaction and e-loyalty. Fu et al. ( 2023)
extend this by demonstrating that customer gratitude increases psychological ownership, strengthening brand
attachment and repeat purchase intention. On the other hand, Wicaksono and Putra ( 2022) ﬁnd cognitive and conative
factors to be more signi ﬁcant in driving online purchase intention than affective components, highlighting the varying
importance of psychological dimensions across contexts.
Further complementing these ﬁndings, the paper by Zhang et al. ( 2021) employs text mining, LDA topic modeling,
sentiment analytics, and an SVM algorithm on Airbnb reviews to construct perceived value indicators and utilizes
structural equation modeling to examine how functional, hedonic, epistemic, and social relationship values impact
consumers’ repeat purchase intention. Similarly, the study by Handayani and Ari ﬁn( 2017) uses a quantitative survey
and structural equation modeling to analyze how online word of mouth and factors from the Theory of Planned
Behavior in ﬂuence purchase intention in the tourism e-marketplace. These studies indicate a strong link between
subjective perceptions, trust, and the technological interface of online platforms, but they often lack an integrated
view of evolving user behaviors in response to dynamic platform features.
The study by Benson et al. ( 2019) adds a demographic lens, indicating that younger women are more in ﬂuenced by
individual beliefs, while older women rely on cognitive factors, with trust and risk propensity also playing key roles.
Mehreen et al. ( 2020) echo this by showing how trust issues and privacy concerns speci ﬁcally affect women ’s online
shopping behaviors. Yang et al. ( 2019) examined how negative psychological factors like anxiety and social pressure
lead to emotional and comparative shopping behaviors among students in Tongren City, China, contributing to
overconsumption and debt. While these studies provide critical insights into speci ﬁc demographic groups, they do not
account for broader applicability across diverse populations and regions, leaving gaps in understanding purchase
intention in heterogeneous consumer segments.
The study by Širola and Sudac ( 2021) on 26 psychological marketing factors identi ﬁes 9 key drivers of consumer
behavior, including low prices, discounts, and a pleasant store atmosphere, while ﬁnding minimal impact from
scarcity promotions and celebrity endorsements. From a sustainability perspective, Zaremohzzabieh et al. ( 2021)
combined TPB with the Green Purchase Behavior (GPB) model to explore how consumer attitudes mediate green
purchase intention, highlighting the gap between attitudes and actual behavior toward green products. While these
studies enrich the literature with diverse foci, they emphasize individual or localized factors in isolation, often
overlooking the interplay between psychological drivers and technological usability in online environments. Here is a
comprehensive overview of all of the related studies in Table 1.
As shown in Table 1, most prior studies isolated single determinants and lacked integrative or dynamic modeling,
highlighting the need for this study ’s hybrid FCM approach. Although prior studies have explored online consumer
behavior across various settings, many remain fragmented and descriptive. Research frequently isolates single factors,
such as trust, ease of use, or social in ﬂuence, without integrating them into a coherent framework, limiting theoretical
accumulation (Benson et al., 2019; Wicaksono and Putra, 2022). Few studies have tested interactive or feedback-
based relationships among variables, leading to static explanations that fail to capture real behavioral dynamics
(Širola and Sudac, 2021). Moreover, most empirical work relies on conventional structural equation modeling,
offering strong correlation evidence but weak predictive capacity (Zaremohzzabieh et al., 2021). Finally, the tourism
sector itself remains underexplored; research has largely extrapolated from general e-commerce contexts, overlooking
service intangibility and trust-speci ﬁc risks (Lim and Kim, 2020; Nuradina, 2022). These limitations justify a
framework that combines behavioral and technological theories within a dynamic system analysis, as adopted in
this study.

Methodology
This section outlines the analytical sequence adopted to address the four research questions. The Fuzzy Delphi
technique is ﬁrst applied to reduce an initial pool of 54 candidate factors to the 27 factors retained for analysis,
addressing RQ1. Fuzzy Cognitive Mapping is then constructed and simulated separately for the expert panel and for
the consumer sample, addressing RQ2 and RQ3 respectively, through centrality analysis, iterative simulation, and
network mapping of causal relationships among the 27 factors. The resulting factors are subsequently organized
within the three constructs of the Theory of Planned Behavior: attitude toward the behavior, subjective norms, and
perceived behavioral control, addressing RQ4. Section “Identifying the main factors ” reports the Fuzzy Delphi
procedure and factor identi ﬁcation; section “Building fuzzy cognitive maps ” reports the construction and simulation
of the Fuzzy Cognitive Maps.

Building fuzzy cognitive maps
On October 2nd, 2024, an expert panel was convened online, bringing together specialists in online shopping, online
tourism, and consumer purchase intention. The panel included 10 academics, industry professionals, and consultants,
each with a deep understanding of how customers behave in digital environments, particularly when making tourism-
related purchases. The goal of this panel was to review and re ﬁne the relationships among the 27 key factors that had
been previously identi ﬁed through extensive literature analysis.
During the session, a facilitator guided the discussion, gathering the collective input of the 10 experts on each
individual cell and relationship between the factors. Rather than simply collecting individual opinions and averaging
them later, the facilitator worked to reach a consensus in real time, synthesizing the experts ’ views into a single,
agreed-upon assessment for each relationship. The experts evaluated the interactions between the factors, determining
how each in ﬂuences the others and their cumulative impact on customer purchasing decisions in online tourism
platforms. The discussion focused on understanding both direct and indirect relationships, with experts assigning a
qualitative strength and direction to each interaction, ranging from very strong negative to very strong positive. This
collaborative, consensus-driven approach was vital in forming a clear, expert-backed perspective on how these factors
function in a real-world context.
Once the experts completed their qualitative evaluations of the relationships among the factors, these were
translated into numerical values using fuzzy logic, shown in Table 3. The linguistic variables used by the experts,
such as “Very Strong Positive ” or “Weak Negative, ” were converted into fuzzy numbers, allowing for precise
mathematical representation of each relationship. This conversion facilitated the transformation of qualitative
assessments into a numerical framework, where relationships between factors could be represented along a con-
tinuous scale. The fuzzy logic approach allowed for the inclusion of uncertainty and the complexity of expert
opinions, making it ideal for modeling complex systems like customer behavior (Bakhtavar et al., 2021; Kosko,
1986). By applying these fuzzy numbers, each relationship between the 27 factors was de ﬁned in terms of its strength
and direction, ensuring that the FCM model accurately re ﬂects the nuanced dynamics at play in online tourism
purchase intention.

values of the factors are evaluated (Xu and Lu, 2020). This analysis provides insights into the key drivers of customer
purchase behavior, highlighting the most signi ﬁcant factors and their interconnections, and offering strategic
implications for enhancing customer engagement and purchase outcomes on online tourism platforms.

Discussion
The research revealed 27 key factors affecting online purchasing intentions on tourism platforms, with “Behavioral
Intention of Online Shopping, ”“ Technological Trust, ” and “Perceived Usefulness ” as the most signi ﬁcant deter-
minants. These factors play a pivotal role in shaping the initial willingness of users to engage with the platform and
make purchasing decisions. Additional critical elements encompassed “Perceived Interactivity” and “Attitude Toward
the Website, ” indicating that engaging design and favorable user experience are essential for shaping decisions.

trust and risk mitigation, aligning with the ecosystem view that individuals adapt behaviorally to external digital
conditions. However, contradictions emerge: while perceived risk was expected to suppress intention, its negative
inﬂuence diminishes when users perceive strong platform reliability, suggesting compensatory trust mechanisms.
This interaction underscores the hybrid psychological –technological nature of online tourism decisions.

Expert vs. customer ’s perspectives on online purchase intentions
Understanding the differences between expert and individual perspectives on online purchase intention is crucial for
developing a comprehensive strategy that balances theoretical rigor with practical user experience. Experts, with their
analytical and structured approach, evaluate factors based on their systemic importance and interrelationships within
the broader context of consumer behavior. In contrast, individuals provide insights grounded in their real-world
experiences, often prioritizing aspects that enhance their immediate interactions with online tourism platforms. By
comparing these perspectives, meaningful distinctions and commonalities can be identi ﬁed, shedding light on how

Experts tend to place signi ﬁcant emphasis on factors related to trust and perceived risk, viewing them as critical
barriers that must be addressed to ensure consumer con ﬁdence and long-term engagement. They highlight elements
such as “Technological Trust,”“ Perceived Financial Risk, ” and “Perceived Privacy Risk ” as foundational to shaping
purchasing behavior. Their assessments re ﬂect a preventative approach, focusing on mitigating potential threats and
ensuring that platforms adhere to robust security and usability standards. Individuals, on the other hand, are more
inclined to prioritize factors that contribute to their overall experience and satisfaction. They place greater importance
on elements such as “Perceived Ease of Use, ”“ Perceived Enjoyment, ” and “Attitude Toward the Website, ”
demonstrating a preference for platforms that offer convenience and a positive emotional connection. When inte-
grating both FCM analyses, experts ’ systemic perspective complements consumers ’ experiential perspective. Experts
emphasize structural enablers (trust, security, system quality), while consumers highlight hedonic and usability
factors (ease of use, enjoyment, design). The convergence between the two, on perceived usefulness and trust,
validates these as central constructs in the conceptual framework. The divergence, meanwhile, indicates differing time
horizons: experts assess sustainability and policy-level reliability, whereas consumers react to immediate interaction
quality. Recognizing this duality enriches the overall interpretation and re ﬂects TPB’s multi-level applicability across
contexts.
One of the key differences between expert and individual perspectives is the weight assigned to risk-related factors.
Experts tend to view “Perceived Financial Risk, ”“ Perceived Privacy Risk, ” and “Perceived Psychological Risk ” as
major determinants that in ﬂuence consumer hesitation, whereas individuals, while acknowledging these risks, are less
likely to allow them to dictate their purchasing decisions. Instead, individuals focus on engagement-enhancing factors
such as “Perceived Compatibility” and “Involvement,” which contribute to their trust and con ﬁdence in the platform
over time. This suggests that while experts emphasize precautionary measures, individuals adopt a more adaptive
approach, prioritizing their experience and familiarity with the platform.

Application of the theory of planned behavior
The Theory of Planned Behavior organizes behavior-driving factors into three core components: Attitude Toward the
Behavior, Subjective Norms, and Perceived Behavioral Control, which are essential in explaining online purchase
intention on tourism platforms. In the attitude toward the behavior category, as shown in Fig. 5, the factors re ﬂect
how positively or negatively a consumer evaluates the act of purchasing on an online tourism platform. “Attitude
toward the website ” plays a key role in shaping this evaluation, as a favorable view of the platform itself encourages
the consumer to make a purchase. Similarly, “perceived enjoyment ” enhances purchase intention by making the
shopping experience pleasurable, while “ﬂow,” which refers to the immersive experience on the platform, can
increase consumer engagement and satisfaction. “Playfulness” also contributes to positive attitudes by making the
experience fun, thereby reducing the transactional nature of online shopping. “Perceived interactivity ” is vital for
fostering favorable attitudes, as consumers who can actively engage with the platform feel more connected and
involved. “Sociability,” while also connected to social in ﬂuences, affects how welcoming and community-oriented
the platform feels, further shaping the consumer ’s attitude. Finally, “behavioral intention of online shopping, ” directly
tied to attitude, re ﬂects the consumer ’s intent to act based on their evaluation of the experience, while “perceived
compatibility” aligns the platform ’s offerings with the consumer ’s needs, increasing their likelihood of purchasing.
In the subjective norms category, the focus is on the social in ﬂuences that guide consumer behavior (Fig. 5).
“Electronic word of mouth ” is a powerful driver, as consumers often rely on reviews and recommendations from
others to validate their purchasing decisions. Social validation from peers, family, or in ﬂuencers plays a critical role in
shaping how consumers perceive a purchase. “Individual trust tendency ” also falls under subjective norms, as a
consumer’s inherent trust in technology is often in ﬂuenced by social cues and the behavior of others. If people in their
social circle or online communities trust a platform, the consumer is more likely to follow suit. “Perceived social risk”
captures the fear of negative social judgment, where consumers may hesitate to make a purchase if they feel others
will disapprove or judge their decision. “Sociability” again plays a dual role here, where social interaction on the
platform itself can in ﬂuence how a consumer perceives the behavior, reinforcing their decisions through peer
validation. Lastly, “technological trust, ” though it can also be considered a control factor, is in ﬂuenced by broader
social perceptions about the safety and reliability of the platform ’s technology, further reinforcing the role of social
expectations.
In the perceived behavioral control category, factors re ﬂect the consumer ’s sense of control or capability in
completing the purchase. “Perceived complexity” directly affects this control; if the platform is seen as too complex

platforms are more con ﬁdent and familiar with the process, making them more likely to complete purchases. Various
risk-related factors also fall under perceived behavioral control because they affect how much risk a consumer feels
they can manage. “Perceived commodity-performance risk, ” for example, captures concerns about whether the
product or service will perform as expected, which can deter purchases if consumers feel they cannot control the
outcome. “Perceived ﬁnancial risk,” including fears of fraud or unexpected costs, reduces a consumer ’s conﬁdence in
their ability to control the transaction, while “perceived privacy risk ” involves concerns about data security and the
control consumers have over their personal information. “Perceived performance risk ” reﬂects worries about the
platform’s ability to deliver satisfactory results, impacting the consumer ’s sense of control over the experience.
“Perceived psychological risk ” captures the emotional toll of a potential bad decision, which can lead to hesitation if
consumers feel they cannot adequately assess the psychological consequences of their purchase. Finally, “psycho-
logical contract violation, ” where the platform fails to meet implicit promises, lowers trust and perceived control,
leading consumers to question whether they can rely on the platform.

industry.
This study addresses the research gap identi ﬁed in the literature by exploring the dynamic interplay between
psychological and technological factors in ﬂuencing online purchase intentions on tourism platforms. Previous studies
often treated these factors in isolation or provided static analyses, overlooking their interconnectedness and evolving
nature. By employing Fuzzy Cognitive Mapping, this research bridges the gap by capturing the complex causal
relationships between 27 key factors, including Behavioral Intention, Technological Trust, and Perceived Usefulness,
while also simulating their interactions over time. This approach moves beyond merely identifying in ﬂuential factors
by offering a dynamic and integrated perspective, thus providing a comprehensive understanding of how trust,
usability, and risk perceptions collectively shape consumer decisions in the online tourism context. Through this
novel methodology, we provide actionable insights that address both theoretical gaps and practical challenges in
understanding consumer behavior.
Theoretically, this study advances TPB by introducing feedback loops between behavioral intention and its
antecedents, showing how technological trust moderates the path between perceived risk and control. It also con-
textualizes Ecosystem Theory by translating macro stakeholder dynamics into micro-level consumer adaptation.
These contributions position the model as both explanatory and predictive, extending behavioral intention research
beyond linear, static approaches. Practically, platform managers should prioritize real-time trust signals (veri ﬁed
payment gateways, transparent privacy noti ﬁcations) and simplify navigation to strengthen perceived behavioral
control. Marketing teams can leverage social proof and user-generated reviews to enhance subjective norms and
encourage adoption. Policymakers, particularly in developing tourism markets, can use these ﬁndings to design
certiﬁcation systems that reinforce digital trust, thereby supporting wider e-commerce participation.

Conclusion
Prior research has often overlooked the interconnectedness of psychological and technological drivers of online
tourism purchase behavior. This study addresses that gap by employing Fuzzy Cognitive Mapping to identify and
rank 27 key factors, capturing both their static importance and how their in ﬂuence evolves through iterative
interaction.
The FCM analysis identi ﬁes behavioral intention, perceived usefulness, and trust in technology as the three most
inﬂuential factors shaping users ’ online purchasing behavior. Behavioral intention, as the central factor, re ﬂects users’
willingness to purchase and is shaped by trust in technology and perceived usefulness: when users ﬁnd a platform
helpful and reliable, they develop stronger purchasing intentions, and reducing perceived risks ( ﬁnancial, privacy, and
performance) plays an important supporting role. Trust in technology and perceived usefulness are mutually rein-
forcing, together strengthening behavioral intention. Risk factors are in ﬂuential at the initial decision-making stage
but diminish sharply across simulation iterations, nearly reaching negligible in ﬂuence, while perceived complexity
and irritation decline similarly as users gain familiarity with the platform. In contrast, perceived usefulness and trust
in technology remain dominant throughout, indicating that once risk is mitigated, these two factors govern the
purchase stage: trust is enhanced through platform security and design, while usefulness stems from ease of use and
information practicality.
These ﬁndings align with prior research, including Lim and Kim ( 2020) and Zhang et al. ( 2021), on the centrality
of trust and usefulness, and with Fu et al. ( 2023) on customer gratitude and brand attachment. A key divergence
emerges regarding emotional factors: unlike Nuradina ( 2022) and Wicaksono and Putra ( 2022), who emphasize
emotion’s role in decision-making, this study ﬁnds that Perceived Enjoyment and Playfulness are less important than
functional aspects such as usefulness and ease of use. Similar to Mehreen et al. ( 2020), initial concerns about Trait
Anxiety and Perceived Risks decline as familiarity increases, though this study additionally highlights the growing
importance of Usefulness and Ease of Use over time, along with the underexplored roles of Technology Readiness
and Perceived Commodity-Performance Risk. The ﬁndings re ﬁne earlier models by showing that Perceived Social
Risk and Trait Anxiety are critical only in early decision stages, and that simpli ﬁed models relying solely on Ease of
Use are insuf ﬁcient given the pivotal roles of Behavioral Intention of Online Shopping and Technological Trust.
Practically, businesses can strengthen Technological Trust through robust security measures, transparent data
privacy policies, and user-friendly design, while improving Perceived Usefulness through seamless navigation,
personalized recommendations, and clear value propositions. Platforms should prioritize addressing risk factors early
in the user journey through communication strategies emphasizing security and reliability, while maintaining a long-
term focus on trust and usefulness to retain and grow the customer base. This study contributes a dynamic, actionable
model integrating FCM with psychological and technological dimensions, capturing the evolving nature of consumer
behavior and the interplay between trust, usefulness, and perceived risk.
The comparison between expert and individual perspectives further shows that while experts emphasize trust,
security, and risk mitigation as foundational to long-term consumer con ﬁdence, individuals prioritize ease of use,
enjoyment, and engagement-driven features shaping their immediate experience. Online tourism platforms should
therefore balance risk mitigation with user-centered, engaging design to foster trust, enhance usability, and drive
purchase intention.
Limitations
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to capture how
consumer behavior evolves over time; longitudinal studies are needed to explore these shifts under different market
conditions. Second, the study ’s scope is con ﬁned to online tourism, raising concerns about generalizability to other
sectors such as e-commerce or non-tourism digital services. Third, the sample ’s demographic composition may not
adequately capture variation across age, cultural background, or socio-economic status, all of which likely in ﬂuence
trust, risk perception, and purchase intention. Finally, FCM is inherently semi-quantitative and dependent on expert

Directions for future research
Future research should examine how emerging technologies, including AI-driven personalization, blockchain, and
augmented and virtual reality, affect consumer trust, usability perceptions, and emotional engagement in travel
booking systems. Longitudinal FCM analyses would allow examination of how trust, perceived risk, and purchase
intention evolve with repeated platform engagement and in response to market shifts or technological upgrades.
Beyond the 27 factors identi ﬁed here, future studies could explore consumer preferences for environmental sus-
tainability and eco-friendly tourism, as well as the in ﬂuence of global economic and political events, such as
pandemics or geopolitical instability, on risk perception. Methodologically, machine learning and predictive analy-
tics, including natural language processing of customer reviews and social media data, could uncover complex
behavioral patterns and simulate scenarios forecasting how platform design or market changes affect purchasing
behavior.
