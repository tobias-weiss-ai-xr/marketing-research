Abstract
Religious seasons play significant roles in shaping economic activities within Africa's informal
economy. This paper examines the economic effects of the 2026 Ramadan and Lenten seasons on
Lagos State's informal market, with particular attention to consumption patterns, market
behavior, employment opportunities, and income generation among informal sector operators.
Anchored on Religious Economy Theory and Social Capital Theory, the study adopts a
qualitative descriptive methodology, utilizing documentary analysis of scholarly literature,
policy documents, market reports, and relevant socioeconomic data.  Findings reveal that the
Ramadan season stimulates increased demand for food items, transportation services, clothing,
and temporary labour, thereby boosting sales and income among informal traders. Similarly, the
Lenten season influences consumer behavior through moderated spending, increased charitable
giving, and patronage of religious goods and services, resulting in resource redistribution within
communities. The study further finds that both religious seasons strengthen social networks,
create seasonal livelihood opportunities, and contribute significantly to the vibrancy of Lagos
State's informal economy. However, challenges such as price fluctuations, inflationary  pressures,
and the commercialization of religious practices were also identified.  The paper concludes that
Ramadan and Lent function not only as spiritual observances but also as important economic
drivers within Lagos State's informal sector. It recommends that policymakers incorporate
religious seasons into economic planning, strengthen market monitoring mechanisms to prevent
exploitative pricing, support informal traders through financial inclusion initiatives, and
encourage faith -based organizations to promote sustainable livelihood programs. The study
contributes to the discourse on Africa's religious economy by demonstrating the intersection
between faith practices and informal market dynamics.

Keywords: Religious Economy, Ramadan, Lent, Informal Economy, Lagos State, Faith and

Literature Review
Religious Economy: Concept and Perspectives
The concept of religious economy emerged from attempts to explain the relationship between
religion and economic activities within society. Religious economy refers to the interaction
between religious beliefs, institutions, practices, and economic behaviour. It views religious
organisations as active participants in economic life and suggests that religious activities often
influence production, consumption, distribution, and resource allocation within communities.
According to Iannaccone (1998 , p. 1467 -1470), religious economy examines how religious
actors compete, cooperate, and influence economic decisions in ways similar to other soc ial
institutions. Rather than existing outside economic systems, religion frequently shapes economic
choices, labour practices, market participation, and patterns of social welfare.
The relevance of religious economy is particularly evident in Africa where religion remains
deeply integrated into social and economic life. Religious organizations provide educational
services, healthcare, employment opportunities, charitable assistance, and community
development initiatives. In many African societies, economic decisions are often influenced by
religious values and ethical considerations. Individuals may modify consumption patterns,
investment decisions, and spending behaviours in response to religious teachings and
observances. Consequently, religious seasons such as Ramadan and Lent generate significant
economic activities that affect households, businesses, and informal markets (Ter Haar & Ellis
2006, p. 354-357). Religious economy theory further argues that religious participation produces
both spiritual and material outcomes. Faith communities create social networks that facilitate
trust, cooperation, and mutual support among members. These networks often encourage
economic transactions and provide opportunities for resource mobilization. In many developing
economies, religious institutions function as important centres of economic redistribution
through charitable giving, welfare programmes, and support for vulnerable populations. Such
activities become particularly visible during major religious observances when acts of generosity
and communal support increase significantly (Smith 2003, p. 18-21).

Fasting and Faith Practices
Fasting is a religious discipline observed in many faith traditions and generally involves
abstaining from food, drink, or certain activities for spiritual purposes. In both Christianity and
Islam, fasting is regarded as a means of spiritual purification, self -discipline, repentance, and
devotion to God. However, fasting also has important social and economic implications because
it influences consumption behaviour, household expenditure, and patterns of market demand.
Religious observances that involve fasting often create unique economic conditions that affect
producers, traders, and consumers (Essoo & Dibb 2004, p. 686 -689). Ramadan represents one of
the most significant fasting periods in Islam. During Ramadan, Muslims abstain from food and
drink during daylight hours while increasing prayer, charity, and communal engagement.

Informal Economy in Africa
The informal economy refers to economic activities that operate outside formal regulatory
frameworks but contribute significantly to employment and income generation. It includes street
vending, petty trading, transportation services, artisan work, domestic services, and numerous
small-scale enterprises. According to the International Labour Organi sation (2018, p. 20-24), the
informal sector accounts for more than 85 percent of employment in many African countries and
serves as a critical source of livelihood fo r millions of people. In Nigeria, the informal economy
plays a particularly importa nt role because of rapid urbanis ation, unemployment, and limited
opportunities within the formal sector. Lagos State hosts one of the largest informal economies
in Africa, characteris ed by diverse commercial activities ranging from food vending and
transportation to retail trading and personal services. These activities provide employment
opportunities for large segments of the population while contributing substantially to economic
growth and poverty reduction (Meagher  2013, p. 14 -17). The informal economy is highly
responsive to seasonal and social factors, including religious observances. Changes in consumer
behaviour during major religious seasons often create opportunities for traders and service
providers. Increased demand for specific products and services may result in higher sales,
temporary employment opportunities, and income growth. However, seasonal demand can also
contribute to price increases and market instability. Consequently, understanding the relationship
between religious activities and informal market dynamics is important for evaluating economic
outcomes within developing societies (Chen 2012, p. 8-10).

Ramadan and Lent possess both spiritual and socio -economic dimensions that extend beyond
personal religious devotion. These seasons influence individual behaviour, household spending,
community relationships, and market activities. During Ramadan, increased spending on food,
transportation, clothing, and charitable activities often stimulates commercial transactions within
local economies. Informal traders frequently benefit from increased consumer demand and
extended trading hours associated with religious celebrations (Osella & Osella 2009, p. 207-210).
Likewise, the Lenten season promotes charitable giving, community support, and religious
participation.  Many faith -based organis ations expand welfare programmes and humanitarian

Methodology
This study adopted a qualitative descriptive research design utilizing documentary and content
analysis methods. Qualitative research is appropriate for exploring social phenomena within their
natural contexts and for generating rich descriptions of complex relationships between religion
and economic activities. The design enabled the researcher to examine how the 2026 Ramadan
and Lenten seasons influenced economic behaviour within Lagos State's informal economy
through the analysis of existing documents, scholarly publications, government reports, market
surveys, media reports, and relevant statistical records. According to Creswell and Creswell
(2018), qualitative research provides a systematic framework for understanding social realities
through the interpretation of text ual and contextual data (pp. 41 -44). The design was considered
suitable because the study sought to investigate the economic implications of religious
observances rather than establish causal relationships through experimental procedures.
The population for this study comprised all documented economic activities associated with the

Theoretical Framework
This study is anchored on the Religious Economy Theory, Rational Choice Theory of Religion,
and Social Capital Theory. These theories provide valuable insights into understanding how
religious practices influence economic activities, market behaviour, and livelihood opportunities
within Lagos State's informal economy during the 2026 Ramadan and Lenten seasons.

Religious Economy Theory
Religious Economy Theory emerged from the works of scholars such as Rodney Stark, Roger
Finke, and Laurence Iannaccone. The theory applies economic principles to the study of religion
and views religious organi sations as participants in a religious marketplace where individuals
make choices concerning religious beliefs, practices, and commitments. According to
Iannaccone (1998, p. 1467 -1472), religion functions within a competitive environment in which
religious institutions provide spiritual and social benefits that influence the behavi our of
adherents. The theory argues that religious activities often generate economic consequences
because religious beliefs shape consumption patterns, charitable giving, labour participation, and
resource allocation. Religious Economy Theory is relevant to this study because both Ramadan
and Lent influence economic activities through fasting practices, religious gatherings, almsgiving,
and festival -related expenditures. During these religious seasons, individuals modify their
spending habits, increase participation in charitable activities, and engage in market transactions
associated with religious observances. These behaviours create economic opportunities for
traders, transport operators, food vendors, and service providers within Lagos State's informal
economy. Consequently, the theory explains how religious commitments can influence market
demand and economic outcomes.The theory further suggests that religious institutions serve as
important channels for economic redistribution and social welfare. Through mechanisms such as
zakat during Ramadan and charitable programmes during Lent, resources are transferred from
wealthier individuals to vulnerable members of society. Such activities contribute to poverty
alleviation, social support, and community development. Therefore, Religious Economy Theory
provides a useful framework for understanding the relationship between faith practices and
economic activities in Africa's informal sector.   Rational Choice Theory of Religion was
developed primarily by Rodney Stark and William Bainbridge. The theory assumes that
individuals make decisions based on perceived costs and benefits. In the religious context,
people participate in religious activities because they believe the benefits derived from such
participation outweigh the associated costs. According to Stark and Bainbridge (1987 , p. 25-30),
religious behaviour can be understood as a rational response to spiritual, social, and material
incentives. Applied to this study, Rational Choice Theory explains why individuals increase
participation in religious activities during Ramadan and Lent despite the sacrifices associated

Social Capital Theory
Social Capital Theory is associated with scholars such as Pierre Bourdieu, James Coleman, and
Robert Putnam. The theory emphasis es the value of social networks, trust, reciprocity, and
relationships in facilitating cooperation and achieving collective goals. Putnam (2000 , p. 18-24)
argues that social capital enhances social cohesion and promotes economic and community
development by encouraging collaboration among in dividuals and groups . The relevance of
Social Capital Theory to this study lies in its explanation of how religious communities create
networks that facilitate economic interactions and mutual support. During Ramadan and Lent,
individuals participate in religious gatherings, community outreach programmes, charitable
activities, and welfare initiatives.  These activities strengthen social relationships and foster trust
among community members. Such networks often provide economic benefits by facilitating
information sharing, financial support, business opportunities, and resource mobili sation. The
theory further explains how religious institutions contribute to sustainable livelihoods through
social welfare programmes and community development initiatives. During both Ramadan and

Conclusion
The study concludes that religious observances extend beyond their spiritual significance to
become important drivers of economic activities within Lagos State's informal economy. The
2026 Ramadan season significantly stimulated commercial transactions, employment
opportunities, and consumer spending, while the Lenten season promoted charitable
redistribution, community support, and socially responsible economic behaviour. The findings
affirm the propositions of Religious Economy Theory that religious practices influence economic
behaviour and market dynamics. The study further establishes that faith -based activities
contribute to livelihood sustenance, social cohesion, and economic resilience among informal
market participants. Therefore, religion should not merely be viewed as a spiritual institution but
also as a significant socio -economic force capable of influencing development outcomes within
African societies.
