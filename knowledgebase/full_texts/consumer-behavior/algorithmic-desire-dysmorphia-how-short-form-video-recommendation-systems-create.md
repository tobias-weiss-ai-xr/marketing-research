Abstract:

 Background:
Short-form video platforms increasingly personalize users’ feeds through recommendation systems that repeatedly
expose them to products, aesthetics, and consumption narratives.  This study examines whether repeated algorithmic
exposure is associated with changes in perceived product interest and uncertainty about the origins of consumer desire.

 Methods:
The manuscript analyzes aggregate descriptive survey information supplied for 200 participants in India who reported
short-form video use. Measures cover platform use, algorithmic awareness, perceived personalization, repeated exposure,
hypothetical pre - and post -exposure interest, and consumer behaviors such as saving, searching, purchasing, and later
questioning a purchase.  Results are reported descriptively because the supplied information  is aggregate rather than
respondent-level raw data.

 Results:
Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts were the most commonly reported platforms. Approximately  70% of
participants reported often or very often seeing the same product repeatedly, 62.5% reported becoming interested after
repeated exposure, and 47.5% reported searching for a product after such exposure. About 37.5% reported purchasing
primarily because of repeated exposure, 65%  reported saving/searching/wishlisting products, and 42.5% reported later
wondering why they  wanted a purchased item. In the hypothetical scenario, mean interest increased from approximately
2.2 before exposure to 5.1 after exposure on a 1–7 scale.

 Conclusions:
The descriptive pattern is consistent with the possibility that repeated personalized exposure can amplify perceived
relevance and consumer desire, while users may simultaneously recognize  that recommendation systems shape what they
see. The findings  should not be  interpreted as causal or nationally representative without respondent -level data and
inferential testing.

I. INTRODUCTION

Short-form video platforms have changed the way
consumers encounter products, lifestyles, aesthetics  and
trends. Recommendation systems continuously select content
based on users’ previous interactions, creating feeds that can
appear increasingly tailored to individual interests. This
personalization raises an important question for consumer

research: when a person develops an interest in a product after
seeing it repeatedly, to wha t extent does  that interest reflect
an existing preference and to what extent might it have been
reinforced through algorithmic exposure?

The central issue examined in this paper is not simply
whether algorithms influence purchasing behavior. It is
whether repeated recommendation  can change the
consumer’s perception of the origin of desire. A product that
initially appears irrelevant may become familiar through
repeated exposure. Familiarity  may make it seem  relevant,
and relevance may eventually be interpreted as personal taste.
The resulting preference can feel authentic even when the
consumer recognizes that an algorithm played a role in
repeatedly presenting the product.

 Research Objectives

 To examine users’ awareness of short-form video
recommendation algorithms.
 To measure perceived personalization of recommended
content.
 To examine the frequency of repeated exposure to
products, trends and lifestyles.
 To assess whether repeated exposure is associated with
increased consumer interest and search behavior.
 To examine whether users perceive algorithmically
reinforced preferences as personal preferences.
 To explore whether repeated exposure is associated with
purchase intention.

 Research Questions

 RQ1. To what extent do  short-form video users perceive
recommendations as personalized to their interests?
 RQ2. How frequently does repeated exposure to products
and lifestyles occur?
 RQ3. Is repeated exposure associated with increased
interest and consumer search behavior?
 RQ4. Do consumers experience uncertainty about whether
a preference existed before repeated algorithmic
exposure?
 RQ5. How does the reported pattern  differ across age
groups?

II. CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK

The proposed framework begins with algorithmic
personalization. Personalized recommendation increases the
probability that a  user will encounter similar products or
themes repeatedly. Repeated exposure  can increase
familiarity and attention. Familiarity  may then increase
perceived relevance, which can contribute to preference
internalization and consumer desire. Desire may
subsequently lead to searching, saving, wishlisting and
purchasing.

Proposed pathway: Algorithmic personalization → repeated
exposure → familiarity/attention → perceived relevance →
preference internalization → perceived desire → purchase
intention.

Algorithmic awareness is treated as a potential
moderating factor because users may recognize that their feed
is personalized while still experiencing recommended
products as genuinely desirable.

 Hypotheses

 H1. Greater repeated exposure is associated with higher
perceived interest in a product.
 H2. Greater repeated exposure is associated with greater
preference change.
 H3. Higher perceived personalization is associated with
stronger preference internalization.
 H4. Stronger preference internalization is associated with
higher purchase intention.
 H5. Algorithmic awareness may moderate the relationship
between repeated exposure and preference internalization.
 H6. Products with lower initial interest may show larger
preference changes following repeated exposure.

III. METHODOLOGY

 Research Design
The study  uses a quantitative, cross -sectional survey
design supplemented by  a hypothetical  repeated-exposure
scenario. The questionnaire measured demographics,
platform usage, algorithmic awareness, perceived
personalization, repeated exposure, preference -related
behavior and purchase intention.

 Sample
The supplied dataset contains 200 respondents in India.
The reported age distribution was 45 respondents  aged 18–
20, 50 aged 21–23, 40 aged 24–26, 30 aged 27–30, 20 aged
31–35 and 15 aged 36 or above. All 200 reported respondents
selected the consent option.

 Measures
Algorithmic awareness was measured through Q9–Q13
using a 5 -point agreement scale. Perceived personalization
was measured through Q14 –Q17. Repeated exposure was
assessed through Q18–Q20. Q21–Q25 measured responses
to the hypothetical scenario on  7-point scales.  Q26–Q30
assessed preference internalization and uncertainty, Q31
measured per ceived contribution of exposure, Q32 –Q34
assessed real-world consumer behavior, and Q35 provided an
optional open-ended response.

 Scenario
Participants were asked to imagine  having little  or no
initial interest in  a product category,  followed by repeated
exposure to videos about that category from different
creators. They then rated initial interest, post -exposure
interest, desire, search likelihood, purchase likelihood and the
perceived contribution of repeated exposure.

 Analysis
The current version  uses the aggregate descriptive
figures supplied by the researcher. Inferential tests cannot be
calculated without the individual response file.

V. DISCUSSION

The supplied descriptive findings are consistent with
the paper’s central proposition that repeated algorithmic
exposure can make previously unfamiliar products feel
increasingly familiar and relevant.  The hypothetical scenario
is particularly notable because reported average interest
increased from approximately 2.2 to 5.1  on a 7 -point scale.
This does not establish causation, but it provides a clear
descriptive basis for investigating preference formation under
repeated personalized exposure.

The behavioral measures show a graded pattern. More
respondents reported saving, searching or wishlisting
products than reported purchasing primarily because of
repeated exposure. This suggests that algorithmic influence,
if present, may occur across sever al stages of consumer
consideration rather than only at the point of purchase.

The reported 42.5% who had bought something  and
later wondered why they wanted it is relevant to the concept
of preference -source uncertainty. However, this measure
cannot determine whether an algorithm  caused the desire.
Post-purchase doubt can arise for many reasons, including
impulse buying, changing circumstances, price concerns or
ordinary consumer reflection.

The age-group pattern is also descriptive  rather than
inferential. Younger respondents in the supplied summaries
showed larger changes in scenario interest, while older
respondents were described as more likely to emphasize need,
price and independent reviews. Respondent -level analysis
would be required before con cluding that age moderates
algorithmic preference formation.

VI. THEORETICAL IMPLICATIONS

 Recommendation systems may be considered
environments in which preferences are not only revealed
but potentially reinforced.
 Repeated exposure may operate through familiarity and
perceived relevance before a consumer reaches a purchase
decision.
 The distinction between pre-existing preference and
perceived preference formation can help consumer
research examine subjective autonomy in personalized
media environments.
 Algorithmic awareness should be measured  rather than
assumed to eliminate influence.

VII. LIMITATIONS

 The available data are aggregate  and approximate rather
than respondent-level.
 The sample should not be described as representative of
all Indian consumers.
 The study relies on self-reported behavior and perceptions.
 The hypothetical scenario measures stated responses
rather than observed purchasing behavior.
 Descriptive figures cannot establish causality or statistical
significance.

VIII. RECOMMENDED STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
FOR THE RAW DATASET

 Calculate descriptive statistics including mean,  standard
deviation and frequency distributions.
 Test internal consistency of the multi-item scales using
Cronbach’s alpha.
 Test correlations between repeated exposure,
personalization, preference internalization and purchase
intention.
 Use a paired-samples test for individual-level initial
versus post-exposure interest.
 Use regression to test whether repeated exposure predicts
preference internalization and purchase intention.
 Test whether algorithmic awareness moderates the
relationship between repeated exposure and preference
internalization.
 Use appropriate age-group comparison tests and report
effect sizes and confidence intervals.

IX. CONCLUSION

This study examines a contemporary consumer -
behavior problem created by highly personalized short -form
video environments: the possibility that repeated
recommendations may influence not only what consumers
encounter, but how they understand their own preferences.
The supplied aggregate  results show frequent repeated
exposure, substantial interest and search behavior following
repeated exposure, and a pronounced descriptive increase in
hypothetical product interest from 2.2 to 5.1 on a 7 -point
scale.

The findings do not demonstrate that recommendation
algorithms cause consumers to want products they would
otherwise reject. Instead, they provide preliminary
descriptive evidence supporting further investigation into
how familiarity, personalization and repeated exposure may
become incorporated into consumers’ subjective sense of
taste.

Future research should use respondent-level data,
experimental or longitudinal designs, and statistical testing to
determine whether the observed relationships remain after
accounting for pre -existing interests and other consumer
characteristics.
