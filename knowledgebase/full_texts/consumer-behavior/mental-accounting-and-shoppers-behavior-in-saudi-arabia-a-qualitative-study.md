# MENTAL ACCOUNTING AND SHOPPERS’ BEHAVIOR IN SAUDI ARABIA: A QUALITATIVE STUDY

MENTAL ACCOUNTING AND SHOPPERS’ BEHAVIOR IN SAUDI ARABIA: A QUALITATIVE STUDY

Abdullah Faisal Silawi

Assistant Professor, Department of Accounting, College of Business Administration (CBA) University of

Abstract
This paper aims to explores the influence of mental accounting on the behavior of shoppers in Saudi Arabia.
The review emphasizes the role of this phenomenon in the process of purchasing durable goods in terms of
shoppers’ budget, sources of funds, and met hod of payment. Mental accounting is a concept which implies
that individuals classify their expenditures and receipts in a specific way, determining their value in terms
other than the interchangeable monetary value. This paper involves a qualitative review study which utilizes
a method of narrative and thematic analysis. The framework of this analysis was formed as a synthesis of the
existing research on mental accounting and its application to the current trends in electronic payments and
financial inclusion in Saudi Arabia. The results of the review found that several factors associated with mental
accounting were identified which influence the process of purchase decision -making in Saudi Arabia. The
relative importance of a source of funds, the budgetar y division of expenditures, the terms of a loan, and the
emphasis on the payment process itself have an impact on the shoppers’ assessment of value and justification
of expenses. This conclusion highlights the relevance of mental accounting research in ter ms of electronic
payment domination in the Saudi economy, with 85% of all retail payments made electronically in 2025. The
analysis also found the impact of substituting cash with card and delayed payments on the mental accounting
process, with a possible decrease in the effectiveness of compensatory controls. However, the analysis did
not provide sufficient data to support the claim that these factors influence all categories of consumers and
all durable goods equally strongly. Finally, the paper concludes  that mental accounting research provides a
framework for further studies on the phenomenon of mental accounting and consumer behavior in general,
with a need for further observations and experiments.

Introduction
Mental accounting is a mental process by which people classify transactions into different categories.
According to Thaler (1985, 1999), consumers often code, categorize, and evaluate transactions; consequently,
they do not treat all money as completely in terchangeable, regardless of its source or intended use. In fact, a
person’s income, which may be the same as savings, money borrowed from friends, windfall gains, or money
set aside for certain purchases is treated as very different things. This info help s the consumer determine
what is affordable, what is desirable and in which spending trade -offs are to be made.
The behavioral life -cycle hypothesis suggests that people treat different components of their wealth as not
entirely interchangeable and exercise different levels of self-control over them (Shefrin & Thaler, 1988). They
assign a budget to each component an d spend according to their preferences. Their propensities to spend
from any of the budgets are not equal, with the highest tendency to spend from current income and the lowest
from future income. People also have different inclinations to spend from curre nt assets (current income
minus desired saving) and from protected savings (the amount set aside for specific future expenditures).
The theory is especially applicable to big -ticket or durable -goods purchases, where a consumer has several
options: spending  current income, using protected savings, borrowing funds, and buying on installment.
Budgeting and financing decisions, including a choice between cash and installment payments, are affected
by mental accounting.
Firstly, it is vital to consider individuals’ characteristics. In particular, as mentioned by Muehlbacher and
Kirchler (2019), in the population, there is a significant amount of heterogeneity in terms of the proneness to
mental accounting and the different effects of previous gains and losses on forthcoming decisions. Therefore,
when studying consumer behavior, it is inc orrect to pool respondents together and assume the same
systematic decision patterns. Instead, specific attention should be paid to peculiarities of the studied group.
Secondly, the payment environment should also be taken into account since empirical stud ies highlight the
importance of payment mechanisms in determining the salience, recall, and psychological immediacy of
spending. For example, Soman (2001) revealed that the payment mechanism modulates the degree to which
past expenditures are mentally carr ied forward into present spending decisions; more specifically, with
greater reliance on rehearsals and reduced wealth, the effect of prior spending on current decisions increases.
Additionally, Prelec and Simester (2001) found a credit card effect, whereas Raghubir and Srivastava (2008)
showed that the lower the transparency of a payment instrument is, the greater is the expenditure likelihood

1.1 Objectives of the Study
The underlying aim of this study is to assess the role of mental accounting on the purchase behavior of durable
goods in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Specifically, the review will focus on consumption behaviors associated
with the allocation of personal savings, installment plans, and access to credit; the impact of budget allocation
on consumer decision -making; the differences in the spending experience resulting from the use of cash,
cards, electronic modes of payment, and alternative financing; and the i nterplay between these activities in
the context of the modern Saudi economy.

2. Research Methodology
This study employs a qualitative research design that is based on a narrative and thematic literature review.
The study is not intended to be a systematic review that covers all the existing literature in a given database.
Instead, the objective is to inte grate a broad range of the existing mental -accounting theory into the
consideration of the most relevant recent evidence on consumers’ spending, methods of payment, durable
goods, and the Saudi financial situation. The description is in line with the recom mendations for narrative
reviews, which are focused on including the most relevant evidence with an emphasis on synthesis, critical
appraisal, and explicit selection of the included studies (Green et al., 2006).
The review focuses on seminal articles dealing with the basics and intricacies of mental accounting and
consumer decision-making. The review highlights Thaler’s (1985, 1999), Shefrin and Thaler’s (1988), Hirst
et al.’s (1994), Okada’s (2001), Soman’s (2001 ), Prelec and Simester’s  (2001), Cheema and Soman’s (2006),
and Raghubir and Srivastava’s (2008) works. The later evidence includes information on individual
differences, scarcity and consumption, digital payments, mobile -payment acceptance, digital financial
inclusion, and the Saudi BNPL/fintech behaviors (Muehlbacher & Kirchler, 2019; Cheng et al., 2023; Skwara,
2023; Agarwal et al., 2024; Albarq, 2024; Ali et al., 2024; Abed & Alkadi, 2025; Bajwa et al., 2025; Smaoui ,
2025; Al -Sharafi et al., 2026; Sharaf et al., 2026). Information on the decision -making of durable -goods
consumers helps better understand the basic concepts related to mental accounting and the mechanics of
consumer purchases of higher involvement (Verma & Kapoor, 2003; Matai & Bhat, 2012).
The literature was organized based on themes, including sources of funds, budget planning, payment
methods, and Saudi contextual factors. Skwara’s (2023) study aligns with the current research since the
scoping review focuses on mental accounting, which manifests through source of funds, intended use of funds,
pricing, and payments. The current conceptual review is based on secondary data, meaning that the results
should not be over -interpreted as specific conclusions for all Saudi shoppers. Firstly, the analysis did not
estimate effect sizes. Further, the research did not control for such variables as demographics to evaluate the
effect size within a specific population. Finally, no causal conclusions can be drawn for the scope of Saudi
Arabia, although the reviewed studies might have demonstrated some causal relationships at a local level.
Thus, further evidence based on quantitative research are highly recommended.

3. Literature Review
3.1 Sources of Funds
3.1.1 Personal Savings
Personal savings are psychologically important because money accumulated for future goals could be
protected more strongly than current income. The behavioral life-cycle hypothesis proposes that households
separate wealth between different mental accounts,  which can cause different propensities to spend from
current income, current assets, and future income (Shefrin & Thaler, 1988). In a durable -goods context, it
implies that a consumer may judge the same product differently depending on whether payment req uires
using ordinary income or drawing down savings that have been mentally reserved for emergencies,
education, family needs, or long-term goals.
Individual variations in mental accounting support this point. Consumers differ in the degree of category
formation and budget monitoring (Muehlbacher & Kirchler, 2019). Therefore, personal savings should not be
seen only as a pool of available funds but a lso as a category with an associated mental tag that can increase
or reduce the decision-maker’s willingness to spend the money on a specific purchase.
3.1.2 Installment Payments
Installment arrangements can bias the context of a purchase by breaking up a large upfront price into smaller
payments. By relying on the mental -accounting theory, it is possible to hypothesize that consumers would
not evaluate the total obligation and each installment with the same weight. According to Cheema and Soman
(2006), the mental accounts can be flexible to accommodate favorable reconceptualization of the
expenditures, making it easier to spend more. For purchases of durable goods, such reconceptualization

might result in a shift in the focus from the total amount to the ease of making a periodic payment.
Such arrangements should not necessarily be viewed as inherently irrational. After all, installment purchasing
allows for better liquidity management and enables one to make durable goods purchases that would
otherwise be unaffordable. The concern with purchase on installment is the psychological accounting fallacy,
if any, that causes an individual to erroneously perceive a given monthly payment as cheaper than the total
amount due.

3.1.3 Borrowing and Credit
Borrowing creates an additional temporal separation between the consumption and payment. Thus, Hirst et
al. (1994) found that the effect of temporal contiguity on the consumer borrowing decision was reflected in
a possible preference for corresponding loan  terms that matched the expected lifespan of the purchased
goods. This means that consumers will tend to create linked mental accounts for the benefit received and the
cost that they have to repay.
Borrowed funds could therefore be considered differently from cash and from one's accumulated savings.
With respect to purchases of durable goods, credit can make spending feel less like a sacrifice at the time of
the purchase, but can also incur obligations for the future. A mental-accounting framework might explain why
one consumer would refuse to buy an item with cash but would be willing to pay financing charges on a loan
to buy the same item.

3.2 Budget Planning
One of the most straightforward examples of the application of mental accounting is budgeting. Consumers
often set budgets for themselves for various categories of spending, for example, food, entertainment,
transport, saving, and big purchases. The establishment of such limitations may help to achieve better control
of expenses, but they are usually not strict. According to Cheema and Soman (2006), ambiguity is an
important component of mental accounting that allows people to adjust the boundaries of their  mental
accounts and spend money they saved for one purpose on something they intended to buy with the money
saved for another purpose.
For durable products, this implies that the initially discretionary purchase may be justified as a necessity for
work, study, the family, or as a replacement for an existing item. Thus, the purchase falls into a category
determined by the consumer, which has an impact on the evaluation of the price.
Recent evidence further indicates that the mental accounting interacts with the type of money available.
Specifically, Cheng et al. (2023) revealed that the participants were more inclined toward the hedonic
consumption when funds were framed as the windfa ll gains, although the interaction was weaker under the
high scarcity mindset. This finding also provides further support for the general proposition that budgeting
and spending decisions depend partly on the way that funds are mentally represented.

3.3 Payment Methods
3.3.1 Cash
Soman (2001) reports that which require greater immediacy and rehearsal payment instruments bring past
expenditures to present awareness and also that which are more transparent are related to more careful
spending as opposed to less transparent forms (Rag hubir Srivastava, 2008). Also it is put forth that cash is a
key point of reference which other card and digital payments are to be compared against. At the same time it
is important to note that this review does not present that all Saudi consumers are the same in their response
to cash; rather the report is on payment transparency as a variable which should be empirically tested in the.

3.3.2 Debit and Credit Cards
One puts out money now and defers payment. Prelec and Simester  (2001) report that what shoppers put
forward to pay is greater with a credit card as compared to cash, also Soman (2001) presents that which
payments which are delayed and less noticeable reduce the association between the acts of spending. Also
Raghubir and Srivastava (2008) bring to light the role of transparency and the ease of which shoppers make
payments in which they put forth the issue of what card we use. Thus, the research reports that shoppers may
see more spending when payment is a more hidden matter, but this does not mean that all cardholders spend
more.
At the same time, card use can also enhance transaction records and facilitate expenditures auditability. Net
behavioral effects depend on the consumer’s budgetary practices, credit conditions, and account information
use, which further emphasizes the rele vance of conducting Saudi -specific empirical research rather than
relying on a general behavioral response assumption.

3.3.3 Digital Wallets and Mobile Payments
Digital wallets and mobile payments are of particular interest to the Saudi customers. Albarq  (2024) has
discovered that the perceived ease of use and trust play a critical role in shaping the attitudes towards the e -
wallet adoption in Saudi Arabia. Ali et al. (2024) have conducted a similar study on the determinants of the
mobile-payment adoption  in the Kingdom, whereas Bajwa et al. (2025) have identified the
convenience/simplicity and security as the factors which influence the trust, usage, and loyalty to the mobile-
payment apps. These studies of adoption in and of themselves do not show that in Saudi Arabia what it is
observed is a result of mobile payments which in turn is causing overspending. What is seen from larger scale
studies however is that a change in payment methods which in turn makes the payment less visible does raise
spending (Agarwal et al., 2024). In the case of Saudi Arabia which has very high digital payment use, this is
an area which may considered as a priority for future mental accounting research.

3.3.4 Alternative and Deferred Financing
Alternative financing includes merchant credit, deferred-payment arrangements, buy-now-pay-later (BNPL),
and other arrangements in which acquisition of goods or services is decoupled from settlement of the liability.
From the viewpoint of mental accounting, such transactions create opportunities for consumers to adjust the
perceived cost of the item through intertemporal budgeting. The reduced regular payment may be less
noticeable than the total amount due, especially if the former is embedded in a broader  set of expenditures
or coupled with the purchase in a seamless payment arrangement. The proposition that BNPL arrangements
and similar innovations may facilitate lower expenditure awareness is consistent with the literature on
coupled payments, budgeting,  and malleability of mental accounts (Soman, 2001; Cheema & Soman, 2006;
Raghubir & Srivastava, 2008).
In the broad scale of digital finance in Saudi Arabia deferred financing plays a special role. In that regard
Sharaf et al. (2026) report great progress in digital financial inclusion but also see social and economic
inequalities in terms of adoption. Al-Sharafi et al. (2026) which looked at a large scale of customer data over
time report security, information quality, and Sharia compliance as key issues post adoption. They found what
relates to satisfaction and loyalty rather than mental accounting itself but what they did report is that use of
BNPL in Saudi Arabia is a play of the digits of the service’s attributes and cultural finance expectations. Thus
BNPL in the Kingdom is a factor of innovation.

3.4 Saudi Consumer Context
In Saudi Arabia it is observed that great transformation which is in large part due to the growth of digital
payment methods, financial sector growth, and the increase of app -based transactions. The Central Bank of
Saudi reports that in 2025 electronic pay ments had taken up 85% of retail payments (Saudi Central Bank,
2026). Also there is a trend of consumers base their purchase decisions less on the use of physical cash.
Recent Saudi studies report that trust, convenience, perceived usefulness, security, price value, habit, and
service quality play a role in the adoption and use of fintech payment applications (Albarq, 2024; Abed &
Alkadi, 2025; Bajwa et al., 2025). It is also seen that digital financial inclusion has expanded, although it is not
the same across all socioeconomic groups (Sharaf et al., 2026). Smaoui  (2025) also reports that financial
literacy, FinTech adoption, social influence, Shariah trust, and risk attitudes are related to various aspects of
financial inclusion in Saudi Arabia. These trends influence mental accounting as the tools which people us e
to save and borrow, set a budget, and make payments shape the way in which individuals organize and watch
over their finances.
Furthermore, cultural and religious considerations may well influence financing choices for those who can
easily distinguish between acceptable and unacceptable borrowing or funding sources. The existence of such
considerations is evident within Saudi Arab ia, as reported by Smaoui  (2025), who finds Shariah trust to be
an integral part of the financial behavior. In particular, Al -Sharafi et al. (2026) find Sharia compliance to be
an essential condition for Saudi customer satisfaction and loyalty in the context of BNPL. These finding s are
indeed consistent with the contextual hypothesis; however, they do not demonstrate the strength of the direct
mental accounting link that would be present in the case of durable goods. Therefore, cultural and religious
conditions should be considered potential moderators that require empirical evaluation.

3.5 Mental Accounting and Purchase Decisions
Skwara’s (2023) systematic review provides evidence in support of the use of mental accounting to
understand purchase behaviors. The review integrates the findings of studies on source -of-funds effects,
intended use of funds, pricing, and payment mechanism s. Overall, the review provides grounds for
understanding purchasing behaviors as transactions where individuals make choices based on a
categorization of payments and sources of funds. Durable -goods research offers additional justification for
the exploration of mental accounting, as the studies have demonstrated that purchases of goods are high -
involvement decisions where consumers are concerned about replacement motives, consulting family
members, allocation of funds, product reliability, cost, brand performance, and value (Verma & Kapoor, 2003;

Matai & Bhat, 2012). Altogether, the literature offers sufficient grounds for examining purchase decisions in
terms of mental budgeting, funding sources, payment methods, and the value of the products.
Furthermore, mental accounting allows the integration of apparently independent consumer acts. Savings
decisions are characterized by protecting specific accounts, installment and borrowing decisions are
concerned with the timing and categorization of costs, budgeting is concerned with category boundaries and
payment methods are concerned with the salience and coupling of payment with consumption. Therefore a
single framework can explain why consumers may respond differently to equivalent choices.

3.6 Research Gap
The existing literature reveals three important gaps that limit understanding of how mental accounting
shapes durable-goods purchasing decisions among Saudi consumers. Three gaps are evident. First, much of
the established mental -accounting literature was developed outside Saudi Arabia. Second, recent Saudi
payment and fintech research focuses primarily on technology acceptance, trust, convenience, service quality,
financial inclusion, and BNPL satisfaction rather than on the cognitive organization of money  (Albarq, 2024;
Abed & Alkadi, 2025; Smaoui, 2025; Al-Sharafi et al., 2026). This concern accords with broader evidence that
cognitive and psychological perspectives remain insufficiently integrated into digital -payment research.
Third, although durable -goods studies show that financing, replacemen t motives, product value, and family
decision-making processes matter (Verma & Kapoor, 2003; Matai & Bhat, 2012), limited direct evidence links
these factors to mental accounting among Saudi consumers. Collectively, these gaps underscore the need for
further empirical research in the Middle East, particularly in Saudi Arabia.

4. Conclusion
The review suggests that the concept of mental accounting can be extremely useful in explaining the
psychology of shoppers who buy durable goods. It is possible that in their minds, money from different
sources (savings, income, credit, or installments) is  perceived as something separate, even though it is
essentially the same. Moreover, the overall budget and timing of a purchase also play an essential role in the
decision-making process.
The framework of analysis is of particular relevance to the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia since the retail-payment
environment is characterized by a digital -centric approach. Specifically, electronic payments comprise 85%
of retail payments in 2025, while recent  Saudi studies indicate the ongoing adoption of mobile -payment
applications and increasing digital financial inclusion (Saudi Central Bank, 2026; Albarq, 2024; Bajwa et al.,
2025; Sharaf et al., 2026).
Nevertheless, the literature does not appear to offer sufficient grounds to make strong claims regarding the
magnitude of mental -accounting effects among Saudi shoppers. The most that can be said with a degree of
confidence is that well-established mechanisms of mental accounting offer a theoretically sophisticated basis
for Saudi-specific testing. Empirical evidence is required to elucidate whether and how the mechanisms differ
across age, income, gender, education, product category, and financing method.

5. Implications of the Study
The study offers theoretical, managerial, and policy-related insights to researchers, market practitioners, and
policymakers interested in mental accounting in the Saudi context. For the academic audience, this paper
contributes a theoretical framework for  future studies that explore the connection between the source of
money, budgeting, methods of payment, and purchase decisions. At the same time, the findings of the study
that unveiled the prevalence of digital payments in the Kingdom should be regarded a s a contextual piece of
evidence. The study is helpful for retailers and financial institutions to understand how the presentation of
the prices, terms of payments, and timing affect the consumer’s budgets and spending behaviors. Literature
on card, digital, and delayed payments has demonstrated that detaching purchase from the outflow of money
may impact the spending decision (Soman, 2001; Prelec & Simester, 2001; Raghubir & Srivastava, 2008). This
conclusion aligns with the previous findings on the importance of immediacy of payments, willingness to pay,
and the role of coupled payments. The review suggests that presenting the total amount of a purchase is
essential when introducing BNPL method, and the study by Chakravarthy et al. (2021) unveiled the
significance of security, information disclosure, trust, and Shariah compliance for the consumer adoption of
BNPNL in the Kingdom. Thus, the factors should be regarded as the key influencers of the post -adoption
behavior and should be integrated into the long-term consumer strategy.
For policymakers and consumer protection agencies, the study provides justification for emphasizing the
need for budgeting and educating consumers about the dangers of all forms of debt. With more choices and
convenience in making payments, the need for co unterbalancing mechanisms to refocus consumers on their
immediate and future finances is critical. As such, it is recommended that policymakers and consumer
protection agencies encourage the use of expenditure reports, budgeting per categories, and disclos ure of
payment information to help consumers stay within their means.
6. Limitations and Future Research
This paper provides a narrative and thematic review rather than a systematic review or an empirical study.

The current research did not utilize a database search protocol that can be replicated, conducted a meta -
analysis, or used individual data from Saudi customers. Therefore, it will not make conclusions about the
prevalence and effect size of mental accounti ng in the studied population. Future research should include
preregistered experiments, survey designs, and observational studies at an individual level to operationalize
the arguments advanced in this article. Follow-up studies can be designed to explore the differences in mental
accounting between cash, debit cards, credit cards, and mobile wallets. In addition, it will be interesting to
see how spending different categories of money (savings, salary, windfall) affects customers’ psychology and
behavior. Finally, future studies should consider the impact of such accounting practices on the mediating
and moderating variables.
