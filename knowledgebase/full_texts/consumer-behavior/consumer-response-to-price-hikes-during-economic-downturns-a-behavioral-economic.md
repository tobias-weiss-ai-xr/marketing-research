Abstract
Using the behavioral economics theoretical framework, this study examines how Nigerian
consumers react to price increases during recessions. It seeks to comprehend how consumers
adjust psychologically and behaviorally to growing costs and diminishing purchasing power.
Under the direction of four research goals, the study examined how well -informed consumers
were about price changes, how they responded to inflation, how price increases affected their
purchasing power, and how demographic factors, such as income, affected consumer behavior.
400 respondents from Nigeria's main urban and semi -urban areas were given a structured
questionnaire as part of the study's quantitative design.
Descriptive and inferential statistics, such as regression, ANOVA, and correlation, were used to
analyze the data. The findings showed that 90% of consumers were aware of price changes and
that coping mechanisms such as cutting back on non -essential expenditure, switching brands,
and delaying purchases were widely used. Regression analysis verified that price hikes
significantly predict changes in consumer behavior (β = 0.680, p < 0.01) and harm purchasing
power (β = -0.340, p < 0.01). In contrast, correlation analysis demonstrated a strong positive
relationship between price hikes and consumer behavioral response (r = 0.721, p < 0.01).
According to the ANOVA results, consumer reactions are strongly influenced by income level (F
= 9.416, p < 0.05), with lower -income consumers showing greater susceptibility to inflationary
pressures.
The results demonstrate that Nigerian consumers exhibit logical but emotionally influenced
decision-making during economic hardship, consistent with Prospect theory and Bounded
Rationality theory.  The study concludes that, especially for low -income groups, inflation
reduces purchasing power and forces adaptive consumption behaviors. To stabilize prices, it
advises Nigerian officials to give consumer protection, income support, and inflation control
priority while encouraging domestic manufacturing. Future studies should investigate the
responses of digital consumption in inflationary environments and expand the investigation
longitudinally.

higher unemployment, and dwindling purchasing power. During these times, customers are
compelled to reevaluate not just what they purchase, but also how and why they do so. Price
increases are now practically a given in emerging nations like Nigeria, where unstable oil

Consumer reactions to price increases during such economic upheaval are complicated and
frequently depart from the forecasts of conventional economic theory. The premise of classical
microeconomics is that, given their financial constraints, consumers make rational choices that
maximize utility. The law of demand would predictably decrease demand if prices were raised
under this assumption.  Yet, several studies have demonstrated that, especially during times of
financial hardship, real -world customers frequently base their decisions on psychological,
emotional, and social aspects (Thaler, 2020; Ruggeri et al., 2024) . To better understand human
decision-making under un certainty, behavioral economics a branch of economics that dr aws on
insights from psychology emerged from this realization.
In terms of behavior, customers may perceive a price increase as both a personal loss and an
economic event, leading to emotional responses such as annoyance, worry, or even mistrust of
businesses and decision -makers (Babatunde, 2023). Loss aversion, in which the pain of losing
₦100 is felt more strongly than the joy of acquiring the same amount, is frequently brought
about by this sense of loss. In a similar vein, reference reliance suggests that buyers compare
current pricing with past experiences or with what they consider "fair" prices. Nigerian
consumers may view price increases for basic goods such as rice or gasoline as unjust or
exploitative, even if they result from external economic forces (Bemgba & Adabu, 2025) . These
psychological inclinations explain why, even in the absence of notable shifts in actual income,
price increases can elicit disproportionately powerful reactions, such as brand switching,
hoarding, or decreased consumption.
Because of its distinct combination of formal and informal marketplaces, wealth inequality, and
strong cultural ties to certain brands and consumption patterns, Nigeria offers an especially
intriguing setting for studying how people respond to price rises (Okafor, 2020). Negotiation is
prevalent, and pricing transparency is low in the informal market systems used by the majority of
Nigerian customers. These elements intensify behavioral influences, including anchoring, mental
accounting, and fairness judgments. For example, even when there are less expensive
alternatives, buyers may rationalize their ongoing purchase of a costly product by classifying it
as a "necessity."  Others may feel that familiar stores have betrayed their trust by raising prices
slightly, leading them to become emotionally disengaged and eventually switch brands
(Darlington & Monday, 2023).
Furthermore, in Nigeria, economic downturns are frequently accompanied by a decline in
consumer confidence, a worsening of social welfare, and increased future uncertainty. Real
incomes have been severely eroded by growing inflation, particularly for low - and middle -
income households, according to the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN, 2023). Moreover, according
to World Bank predictions from 2022, more than 40% of Nigerians are below the poverty line,
leaving them extremely susceptible to price shocks. Under such circumstances, households turn
to coping strategies such as cutting back on purchases, reducing consumption completely,
switching to informal substitutes, or buying in lesser quantities (Okoro et al., 2021) . Instead of
being solely determined by reason, these adaptive behaviors exhibit fundamental tendencies

Therefore, behavioral economics offers a better -grounded framework for understanding how

crucial given Nigeria's propensity to experience economic crises. With an emphasis on how
perceptions, cognitive biases, and socioeconomic disparities shape behavior during economic

consumer responses to price increases. To offer both academic insights and practical
implications for Nigeria's business and policy contexts, the study draws on behavioral
economics, empirical data, and context-specific observations.

Problem Statement
Consumer behavior is influenced by Nigeria's economic environment, marked by rising
unemployment, persistent inflation, and diminishing purchasing power. Although income
elasticity and substitution effects are used in traditional economic research to explain price
responsiveness, these models cannot account for the psychological and emotional factors that
influence actual decision -making (Darlington & Monday, 2023) . In addition to financial
constraints, consumers' views of justice, anxiety about future inflation, and cognitive biases all
play a role during downturns. For instance, habit persistence or loyalty bias may cause a
customer to stick with a well -known brand even if the price has increased significantly (Bemgba
& Adabu, 2025).
A significant gap in the creation of company strategies and economic policy is the lack of
comprehensive behavioral data on Nigerian consumers' reactions to price shocks. Businesses
may mistakenly perceive short-term drops in consumption as long-term losses in demand if these
behavioral subtleties are not understood, and politicians may create inefficient price -control or
subsidy policies (Okoro et al., 2021) Thus, the purpose of this study is to examine the behavioral
factors that influence how Nigerian consumers respond to price increases during recessions.

Research Objectives
The study will be guided by the following objectives:
1. To examine the patterns of consumer behavioral responses such as substitution, rationing,
brand switching, and purchase postponement to price hikes during economic downturns
in Nigeria.
2. To identify the behavioral economic factors (e.g., loss aversion, reference dependence,
and mental accounting) influencing consumer reactions to price increases.
3. To analyze how socio -economic characteristics (such as income level, employment
status, household size, and access to credit) moderate behavioral responses to price hikes.
4. To assess the policy and managerial implications of behavioral responses to price hikes
and propose behavioral interventions to mitigate adverse welfare outcomes.

Research Question
The following research questions have been developed in line with the objectives:
1. What are the dominant behavioral patterns exhibited by Nigerian consumers in response
to price hikes during economic downturns?
2. To what extent do behavioral factors such as loss aversion, reference dependence, and
perceived fairness explain variations in consumer responses?
3. How do socio -economic characteristics influence the behavioral reactions of consumers
to price increases in Nigeria?
4. What behavioral interventions and policy strategies can help mitigate the negative impact
of price hikes on consumer welfare and business sustainability?

Theoretical Framework
Prospect Theory (Ruggeri et al., 2024)  serves as the main theoretical foundation for this study.
According to prospect theory, people are more sensitive to losses than to comparable gains and
assess results in relation to a reference point.  Compared to when prices decrease, consumers
react more strongly emotionally and behaviorally when prices rise because they see this as a loss.
The Theory of Mental Accounting, which contends that consumers divide money into many
accounts and may respond differently according on which "account" the expense belongs to,
lends support to this paradigm (Thaler, 2019).
Furthermore, the Fairness Heuristic Theory Murayama (2019) will be employed to comprehend
how fairness perceptions impact consumers' purchasing decisions in reaction to price increases.
Customers frequently assess whether a price rise is justified, such as by comparing supply
shortfalls to profit maximization, in addition to the magnitude of the increase.  The psychological
and social mechanisms that influence consumer choice under financial strain are explained by
these ideas taken together.

Significance of the Study
The study is important from a theoretical, practical, and policy standpoint.  Theoretically, it adds
empirical data from a developing economy setting to the expanding body of literature on
behavioral economics. There is little knowledge of how behavioral biases function in
environments with high inflation and unregulated markets because the majority of behavioral
economics research has been focused on developed economies (Nelson et al., 2025).

This research focuses on urban and peri -urban consumers in Lagos, Abuja, and Port Harcourt,

study will include both non -essential (such as gadgets and branded consumer goods) and
essential (such as food, fuel, and healthcare) commodities. The timeline will center on the years

The study focuses only on customer behavior, not on firm -level pricing decisions. It will address
implications for price regulation and consumer welfare, but will not go into great depth about the
effects of macroeconomic policy. Triangulation using experimental and observational data will
also help offset potential biases, such as recollection errors or social desirability, which may

Operational Definitions
Price Hike:  A sustained increase in the price of a good or service beyond the consumer’s
reference point or expectation, reducing purchasing power and altering consumption behavior
(Nelson et al., 2025).
Economic Downturn: A macroeconomic phase characterized by negative or slow growth, rising
unemployment, and inflationary pressures that lower consumer confidence (World Bank, 2022).
Behavioral Response: Observable consumer actions —such as substitution , brand switching, or
rationing driven by psychological, emotional, or social factors in reaction to price changes
(Babatunde, 2023).
Loss Aversion: A behavioral tendency where consumers perceive losses (e.g., price increases)
more intensely than equivalent gains, influencing demand elasticity (Ruggeri et al., 2024).
Reference Dependence:  A cognitive bias where individuals evaluate outcomes relative to a
benchmark or past experience rather than absolute value (Thaler, 2019).
Mental Accounting:  The cognitive process by which consumers mentally categorize and
allocate their financial resources into separate spending accounts (Thaler, 2019).
Perceived Fairness:  The subjective judgment by which consumers assess whether a price
increase is justified, equitable, or exploitative (Murayama, 2019).
Consumer Welfare: The overall well-being or satisfaction consumers derive from consumption
relative to income, price stability, and perceived fairness (Okoro et al., 2021).

Theoretical Literature (Behavioral Economics Theories)
The limits of traditional economic theory, which presumes that consumers are stable, rational
agents, have been addressed by behavioral economics. In practice, people frequently behave
inconsistently due to social factors, emotional responses, and cognitive biases (Thaler, 2020;
Ruggeri, 2024). This section examines four fundamental ideas that provide an explanatory basis
for understanding how consumers respond to price increases during recessions: Prospect theory,
Mental Accounting, Fairness Heuristic theory, and Bounded Rationality. These theories are
especially pertinent to economies like Nigeria's, which are prone to inflation, as consumer
choices often reflect adaptive behavior rather than the maximization of ideal utility.

Prospect Theory
According to Ruggeri’s (2024) Prospect theory, people assess possible outcomes in relation to a
reference point rather than in absolute terms. Loss aversion, the central tenet of the theory, holds
that human emotions are more affected by losses than by comparable benefits. As a result, when
customers face price increases, they feel more psychological anguish than they would from a
similar price decrease. For instance, a 10% rise in gas prices may result in proportionally greater
discontent than the happiness brought on by a 10% drop in price (Babatunde, 2023).
Additionally, Prospect theory introduces reference reliance, proposing that consumers
benchmark against historical pricing or market norms. Even if a deviation is factually justified, it
is nevertheless viewed as a loss. Frequent shifts in reference points lead to long -lasting
annoyance and mistrust in Nigerian marketplaces, where consumers have long endured price
volatility. According to Nelson et al. (2025 ), these behavioral patterns explain why inflationary
shocks can lead to inflated consumer emotions, panic buying, or even social upheaval. Therefore,
the emotional and cognitive errors affecting how consumers react to financial adversity are well
captured by Prospect theory.

Mental Accounting Theory
According to Thaler's (2020) Mental Accounting theory, people mentally divide their expenses
into several groups, or "budgets," to organize, assess, and monitor their financial decisions.
Instead of treating all money as fungible, consumers set aside specific amounts for
entertainment, transportation, food, and rent. This mental budgeting paradigm influences how
customers re -prioritize spending as prices increase during recessions. For example, despite
having less money, many Nigerians continue to buy religious or staple meals while reducing
their spending on luxury goods or entertainment (Okoro et al., 2021).
This hypothesis also explains why Nigerian consumers tend to purchase commodities in smaller,
more economical units (such as sachet milk or oil) rather than in bulk, a practice known as
"micro-purchasing."  By taking such steps, they can avoid feeling overburdened by their finances
while remaining within their mental budgets (Okafor, 2020) . Thus, mental accounting reveals
how consumers cognitively frame their responses to inflation by bridging the gap between
psychological budgeting behavior and market realities. Furthermore, it emphasizes that
subjective perception of control and necessity rather than just objective affordability guide
buying patterns even in times of financial pressure.

Fairness Heuristic Theory
Murayana (2019 ) claims that perceptions of fairness strongly influence consumer happiness,
trust, and purchasing behavior. The Fairness Heuristic theory states that people evaluate prices
based on perceived fairness and monetary value. Customers may grudgingly tolerate price
increases if they are perceived as supported by valid arguments, such as government policy or
production costs.  On the other hand, if perceived as exploitative or opportunistic, these increases
lead to resentment, mistrust, and retaliatory measures such as boycotts or moving to unofficial
marketplaces (Bemgba & Adabu, 2025).
Given the prevalence of inadequate consumer protection and suspected corruption in pricing
systems, perceptions of fairness are crucial in Nigeria. Fairness and transparency are frequently
conflated by consumers, who perceive price increases without sufficient justification as
unethical, leading to behavioral and emotional reactions. This approach incorporates moral
judgment alongside economic reasoning. Therefore, fairness heuristic theory increases
understanding of how trust deficits and moral views influence consumer resistance to price hikes
particularly during periods of economic fragility when consumers already feel disadvantaged.

Bounded Rationality and Heuristics
The idea of bounded rationality, first proposed by Herbert Simon in 1955, acknowledges that
consumers are subject to time, information, and cognitive limitations when making decisions.
When faced with extreme economic uncertainty, people use heuristics simple rules of thumb to
make difficult decisions. To lessen cognitive load in inflationary conditions, consumers may
stick with well-known products or select the least expensive brand (Babatunde, 2023).
Social influence, cultural norms, and experience in Nigeria frequently influence these heuristics.
Consumers in informal markets, for instance, use word of mouth from the community as a quick
way to judge the fairness of prices and the quality of products. Although these heuristics make
decision-making easier, they may lead to systematic biases and suboptimal outcomes. Thus,
bounded rationality helps explain why consumers occasionally make decisions that seem
irrational, such as sticking with a pricey brand des pite lower -priced alternatives. By
acknowledging that environmental and contextual factors influence practical decision -making
just as much as intentional thinking, this theory enhances behavioral frameworks.

Empirical Studies on Consumer Response to Price Hikes
Critical evidence for comprehending how customers react to price rises in practical contexts is
provided by empirical research. These studies show cultural and contextual differences as well as
consistent behavioral trends across economies. Studies on the global and emerging economies
that provide insight into how consumers react to price changes, especially during recessions and
inflationary times, are reviewed in the subsections that follow.

International Empirical Evidence
Consumers' responses to price increases often follow behavioral principles, according to
empirical studies conducted in industrialized economies. In a clear indication of loss aversion,
Ozili (2021) found that customers react more strongly to price increases than to price decreases,
suggesting unequal sensitivity to price changes. Similarly, reference price adaptation explains
why customers stay loyal until perceived fairness thresholds are crossed, according to Adeyeye
et al., (2018).

Studies in Developing Economies
Price sensitivity is particularly pronounced in underdeveloped nations due to unstable incomes
and inadequate consumer protection laws. In their study of Kenyan households, Mugo and
Mwangi (2021) found that consumers who experience income shocks during periods of inflation
exhibit increased emotional pain and resentment driven by fairness concerns. The study
supported the fairness heuristic idea by demonstrating that perceived exploitation by retailers
exacerbates behavioral backlash.
In India, mental accounting influences spending preferences when inflation is high, according to
Aminu & Oyefesobi (2018) . While reducing discretionary spending, consumers continued to
spend on culturally significant items such as religious offerings and holidays. According to
Aladejebi (2020), consumers in South Africa rely on informal credit, peer recommendations, and
community prices as social heuristics to manage financial stress. Together, these studies show
that while the concepts of behavioral economics are applicable everywhere, they take on distinct
forms depending on the socioeconomic setting.
The social capital ingrained in purchasing decisions is another factor in developing economies.
In African contexts, households are frequently protected from inflation shocks by shared
consumption and collective purchasing (Darlington & Monday, 2023) . This connection
demonstrates that price response is a behavioral process that affects both individuals and groups,
influenced by informal economic cooperation and cultural resilience.

Key Themes from Empirical Literature
A synthesis of empirical data from around the world identifies several recurrent behavioral
themes. First, price increases regularly elicit more negative reactions than equal discounts that
result in satisfaction, which can be explained by loss aversion (Ruggeri et al., 2024) . Second,
perceptions of fairness and reference dependency serve as behavioral moderators of trust and
brand loyalty (Murayama, 2019). Third, mental accounting and constrained rationality highlight
the psychological constraints consumers face when dealing with inflationary uncertainty (Thaler,
2019).
Beyond this, recent research also suggests that behavioral reactions are mediated by socio -
demographic variables such as age, education, and digital access (Aminu & Oyefesobi, 2018) .
For example, consumers in rural areas or with lower levels of education rely on heuristics and
interpersonal trust, whereas younger, tech -savvy consumers use price comparison tools.
According to these results, behavioral economics offers a common framework for interpreting a
variety of market phenomena across different economies, revealing both context -specific and

Nigerian Contextual Evidence
Because of structural poverty, high inflation, and ongoing macroeconomic volatility, the
Nigerian consumer market offers an interesting setting for behavioral investigation. Economic
downturns over the last ten years have compelled households to make behavioral and rational
adjustments. Inflationary adaptation, market trust, and socioeconomic factors influencing
Nigerian behavior are examined in the ensuing subsections.

Inflation and Consumer Adjustment in Nigeria
According to Darlington and Monday (2023 ), during inflationary shocks, Nigerian households
frequently use coping strategies such as cutting back on consumption, switching to alternative
products, or postponing purchases. The claim made by Prospect theory that consumers view
price rises as losses is reflected in these res ponses. Similarly, Nelson et al. (2025 ) noted that as
inflation expectations rise, Nigerian consumers exhibit anticipatory behaviors, such as panic
buying or stockpiling.
The frequency of these behaviors demonstrates the psychological and emotional toll that price
volatility takes. Long -term planning is hampered by inflation, which also erodes consumer
confidence and creates a financial worry atmosphere. In response, many customers seek
unofficial coping strategies such as bulk -buying cooperatives, bartering, or communal shopping.
Adaptive rationality's attempts to maintain control in an uncertain economic environment are
reflected in these answers. The idea that behavioral responses during downturns are based on
lived experiences rather than abstract utility maximization is thus supported by Nigerian data.

Behavioral Biases and Market Trust
Perceptions of fairness and trust significantly influence behavioral biases in Nigeria. Okafor
(2020) demonstrated that customers frequently link price hikes to government or store
manipulation. Because there is less pricing transparency in informal markets, this notion is
heightened. Price adjustments that are inadequately explained or seem capricious are interpreted
by customers as moral transgressions, which cause emotional disengagement and avoidance
behavior, according to Bemgba and Adabu (2025).
Furthermore, cultural and religious beliefs are closely linked to how fairness is seen. Price
exploitation is not only an economic practice but also a moral breach in a society where moral
justice is socially reinforced. As a result, customers who believe a brand or store is dishonest
may boycott it. This pattern of behavior highlights the influence of fairness heuristics on
Nigerian consumer behavior by showing how societal norms and moral standards interact with
economic decision-making.

Socio-Economic Moderators
To moderate behavioral reactions to price increases, socioeconomic disparities are essential.
Babatunde (2023) found that whereas middle - and high -income customers exhibit greater
adaptive flexibility through brand switching and bulk buying, low-income families exhibit higher
levels of loss aversion, emotional discomfort, and fairness sensitivity. These differences show
that behavioral patterns are stratified by education levels and resource availability rather than
being uniform.

Summary of Literature Review
This paper emphasizes how behavioral economics provides a strong framework that goes beyond
traditional rational -choice theory to explain how consumers respond to price increases. The
cognitive, affective, and ethical aspects of consumer decision -making are explained by theories
such as Prospect theory, Mental Accounting, the Fairness Heuristic, and Bounded Rationality.
Consistent patterns of loss aversion, fairness sensitivity, and adaptive budgeting behavior are
seen in empirical data from a variety of economies.
These behavioral inclinations are exacerbated in Nigeria by socioeconomic inequality, low
institutional trust, and inflation. As much as economic facts influence consumer behavior,
psychological and cultural norms also play a role. However, the literature reveals a major gap:
whereas earlier research has examined Nigerian inflation and consumption, few have applied an
integrated behavioral economics lens to explain the interactions among socioeconomic
determinants, cognitive biases, and emotional reactions. By experimentally investigating
Nigerian consumers' behavioral responses to price increases during economic downturns, this
study fills a knowledge gap and advances insights into consumer welfare and behavioral policy.

Methodology
This chapter describes the methodology and research strategy used to examine how Nigerian
consumers react to pric e increases during recessions. The population and sampling strategy are
explained, the sample size of 400 respondents is justified, the questionnaire design and data -
collection methods are described, data analysis methods are outlined, and validity, reliability, and
ethical aspects are covered. The study employs a quantitative cross-sectional survey that is solely
based on a structured questionnaire in order to collect socio -demographic variables and
behavioral dimensions.

Research Design
For this study, a cross -sectional quantitative research design is used. Researchers can measure
relationships between variables and test hypotheses about predictors and moderators by using
cross-sectional surveys, which are suitable for capturing the prevalence and patterns of attitudes,
perceptions, and behaviors at a single point in time (Saunders et al., 2023) . A structured
questionnaire administered to a representative sample yields effective, generalizable empirical
evidence, given the goal of measuring behavioral constructs (loss aversion, reference
dependence, perceived fairness, mental accounting) and their association with observed
consumer responses (substitution, rationing, brand switching) (Bryman & Bell, 2019).
To guarantee measurement accuracy, enable reliable statistical testing (such as factor analysis
and multivariate regression), and generate results that managers and policymakers can use
immediately, a strictly quantitative approach is employed (Sekaran & Bougie, 2020). The study's
emphasis on short - to medium -term behavioral reactions to price increases between 2020 and
2024 is also well-suited to the cross-sectional design.

Sample Size and Sampling Technique
Sample Size
400 respondents make up the study's sample size, which is supported by both statistical and
pragmatic considerations. Recent methodological guidance suggests sample sizes of at least 300
for quantitative survey research that plans to use multivariate regression models and factor
analysis (both exploratory and confirmatory). This is especially important when constructs are
measured with multiple items and communalities are moderate to high, as it ensures stable factor
solutions, accurate parameter estimates, and sufficient statistical power (Hair et al., 2019) .
According to Hair et al. (2019), if model complexity is moderate, samples of 300 –500 are
suitable for structural equation modeling as well as robust exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and
confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).
According to Sekaran and Bougie (2020 ), a sample size of 400 offers enough statistical power
(usually ≥ 0.80) to identify medium effect sizes (Cohen's d ≈ 0.5) in multiple regression and
ANOVA tests at the traditional 5% significance level. With 400 respondents, it is practically
possible to conduct subgroup analyses across socioeconomic strata (such as income tertiles and
urban subgroups) and to split the sample if necessary (e.g., N ≈ 200 for EFA and N = 200 for
CFA), which is advised for scale validation (Hair et al., 2019) . Lastly, 400 offers excellent
external validity for the chosen metropolitan areas while still being feasible given the logistical
and financial limitations of the study.

Sampling technique
Four hundred responses were gathered using a multistage sampling technique. First, to capture
regional heterogeneity, the three metropolitan areas, Lagos, Abuja, and Port Harcourt, were
purposefully chosen. Second, to ensure coverage across a variety of neighborhoods (high -,
middle-, and low -income), a stratified sample frame is constructed within each city based on
local administrative wards or government regions. Third, using publicly accessible household
listings or street -based enumeration techniques, a systematic random sample of households is
selected within each stratum. To lessen intra -household selection bias, a KISH grid or next -
birthday approach is used to choose one eligible adult responder per family (Saunders et al.,
2023). The target allocation has been significantly modified to ensure minimum subgroup sizes
for analysis, although it is roughly proportional to the city's size (for example, Lagos ≈ 180,
Abuja ≈ 120, and Port Harcourt ≈ 100). Compared to strictly convenience sampling, our
stratified, methodical selection approach minimizes coverage bias while balancing
representativeness and feasibility (Bryman & Bell, 2019).

Instrument structure and scales
The only tool used to collect data is a standardized questionnaire administered by the
interviewer. Behavioral constructs (loss aversion, reference dependence, mental accounting,
perceived fairness), socio -demographic information (age, gender, education, employment,
household size, income bracket, and urban location), observed consumer responses (substitution,
rationing, brand switching, purchase postponement, and increased search), and control variables
(access to credit, market channel preference, and prior experience with price shocks) comprise
the five sections of the questionnaire.
Multi-item Likert scales (5 -point: 1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree) will be used to
measure behavioral components. Validated behavioral scales used in recent survey research
serve as the basis for adapting and contextualizing items for reference dependency and loss
aversion (Hardie, Johnson & Fader, 2020; Kalwani & Yim, 2019). While mental accounting
questions adhere to Thaler's conceptualization, which has been operationalized in consumer
surveys (Thaler, 2019), perceived fairness items are based on the fairness literature (Murayama,
2019). Both binary indicators (whether behavior occurred) and frequency measures (how
frequently behavior happened in the last 12 months) are examples of observed responses
(dependent variables). To ensure semantic equivalence, the questionnaire will be created in
English and, if required, translated into the main regional languages.

Measurement rigor
To enable accurate scale estimation and factor analysis, each multi -item construct will include at
least 3 items (Hair et al., 2019) . The item's phrasing is clear, pertinent to the context, and has
been tested for comprehension beforehand. Response anchors are labeled and balanced to reduce
acquiescence and central tendency bias. Sensitive questions (such as informal borrowing) will be
phrased neutrally, and guarantees of anonymity will precede the sections to reduce social
desirability bias.

Pilot Study
Forty respondents (10% of the desired sample in many methodological tr aditions; Sekaran &
Bougie, 2020) selected from a neighborhood not included in the main sample will participate in a
pilot test before the main survey. The pilot's objectives are to evaluate the multi -item scales'
preliminary reliability (Cronbach's alpha), average completion time, response distributions, and
question clarity. The outcomes of the pilot will inform modifications to items, the elimination of
unclear items, and improvements to the translation. During piloting, the lowest acceptable
criteria for internal consistency will be a Cronbach's alpha of ≥ 0.70 (Hair et al., 2019). Items
will be reworded or more items will be evaluated until sufficient reliability is attained if alpha
values drop below this cutoff.

variance (R2), path coefficients, and model goodness -of-fit indices will be presented and
interpreted.

demonstrate that the majority of Nigerians are highly alert to price changes and consciously
adjust their spending habits in response to inflationary pressures.

The study found that consumers with lower incomes are most affected by price rises, suggesting
the need for targeted intervention.  Such welfare measures can effectively reduce inequality and
maintain consumption levels during economic downturns, according to evidence from the World
Bank (2022).
To reduce Nigeria's reliance on imports, which often drive inflation through currency volatility,
officials should also encourage domestic manufacturing and value chain diversity. Promoting
domestic manufacturing of necessities, particularly food and home items, will improve price
stability and the resilience of the national economy. Local manufacturing and small enterprises
should be given incentives, including tax exemptions, low -interest loans, and infrastructure
support.

Conclusion
According to this study, Nigerian customers respond to price increases in both logical and
emotionally motivated ways, consistent with behavioral economics theories. Customers
demonstrate awareness of market changes and adopt adaptive tactics, including cutting back on
non-essential spending, switching to less expensive brands, and delaying purchases. These
coping strategies, however, are more prevalent among lower -income groups, reflecting
disparities in economic resilience.
The study confirms that inflation puts households under financial and psychological strain by
showing that price increases significantly predict changes in consumer behavior and purchasing
power. As a result, Nigeria's economic policy agenda should continue to prioritize consumer
protection and inflation management. When paired with income -supporting policies, consistent
inflation control can boost consumer confidence and advance economic stability.

Suggestions for Future Research
Although this study offers insightful information, it also creates avenues for further investigation.
A longitudinal approach might be used in future research to examine how consumer behavior
changes across several economic cycles, providing a more thorough understanding of behavioral
adaptability over time. To fully capture the emotional and cognitive components of consumer
decisions that may not be fully revealed by quantitative surveys, future research could also
employ qualitative methods such as focus groups or interviews.
Furthermore, research that compares various African economies may show how institutional and
cultural factors influence behavioral responses differently. Finally, considering the growing
significance of online platforms in Nigeria's consumer landscape, future researchers should
incorporate digital consumption behaviors, such as e -commerce responses to price volatility.
Such research would enhance our understanding of behavioral economics in emerging -market
environments.
