Abstract
Street food plays a central role in urban food systems, especially in informal and tourism-driven
settings where regulatory oversight is limited. While prior research has examined perceived food
safety and street food consumption, how these factors jointly relate to consumer behavior remains
incompletely understood. This study examines street food consumption patterns and the roles of
perceived vendor hygiene and food safety in shaping consumer behavior. The findings are
discussed in the context of existing behavioral theories related to risk perception and health-related
decision-making. A cross-sectional survey (n = 517) was conducted across two seasons in a
Mediterranean tourist city, and binary and ordinal logistic regression models were applied to
evaluate the effects of demographics, perceived hygiene, and risk perception on consumption
patterns. Street food consumption was high (78.5%) and varied seasonally. Positive perceptions of
vendor hygiene, largely based on visible cues, were associated with street food consumption,
whereas food safety concerns were associated with a lower likelihood of consumption. These
findings provide insights into consumer behavior in informal food systems and highlight the
importance of visible hygiene practices and effective food safety communication in tourism-
oriented street food environments.

1. Introduction
Street food systems play a central role in urban food environments globally, providing
accessible, affordable, and culturally embedded food options while supporting local economies.

This is particularly evident in low- and middle-income countries and in tourism-driven settings,
where informal food vendors contribute significantly to food access and urban vibrancy. However,
the informal nature of these systems often raises concerns regarding hygiene, food safety, and
regulatory oversight. Existing research has extensively documented the microbiological risks
associated with street food and the role of hygiene practices in mitigating foodborne hazards 1,2.
Parallel streams of research have examined consumer perceptions of food safety and behavioral
drivers of consumption. However, these strands of literature remain largely disconnected, with
limited integration of consumer perceptions of food safety and vendor hygiene in relation to
decision-making in informal food settings3.
In formal food settings, regulatory food safety systems and hygiene inspections help shape
consumers' perceptions of food safety when purchasing prepared foods4. However, consumers rely
less on objective hazard information and more on perceived risk when making food-related
decisions in informal environments. Previous studies have shown that objective safety indicators
alone are insufficient to explain street food consumption, as consumers rely heavily on subjective
evaluations and heuristic cues in the absence of formal certification and traceability mechanisms
5,6. Empirical evidence further indicates that perceived benefits such as taste, convenience, and
affordability often coexist with concerns about hygiene and foodborne illness, jointly shaping
purchase decisions in street food environments 7.
Previous behavioral research suggests that food-related decisions are influenced not only
by objective risk but also by subjective perceptions, previous experiences, and observable
environmental cues 8. In contexts where formal certification and regulatory signals are weak or
absent, consumers rely on observable indicators, such as cleanliness, vendor behavior, and
environmental cues, to infer safety and reduce uncertainty 9. In informal food systems such as
street food markets, these processes converge. In the absence of formal certification or regulatory
assurances, consumers often infer the safety from observable vendor indicators, including the
cleanliness of preparation areas, personal hygiene practices, and perceived professionalism. These
visual and behavioral signals function as informal proxies for institutional safety guarantees and
influence consumers' perceptions when selecting street food vendors. Previous research has shown
that familiarity, social context, and observable vendor characteristics significantly influence food
choice under uncertainty and may contribute to consumers' perceptions of food safety 8,10.

Moreover, visible hygiene indicators and food handlers’ perceived professionalism have been
shown to be associated with more favorable consumer evaluations of street food vendors11,12.
Despite this, current literature has examined perceived food safety and observable vendor
hygiene as important factors associated with consumer decision-making, but relatively few studies
have explored these concepts together in seasonal tourism-oriented street food environments.
Moreover, little is known about how these dynamics operate in tourism-oriented settings
characterized by seasonal fluctuations in vendor density, consumer demographics, and social
behavior. To address this gap, the present study examines street food consumption patterns,
consumer perceptions of vendor hygiene, and perceived food safety within a seasonal tourism-
oriented street food environment. The findings are discussed in the context of previous research
on consumer behavior and food safety perceptions. Using empirical data from a Mediterranean
tourist city, this study aims to:
1. Identify demographic and contextual predictors of street food consumption.
2. Examine consumer perceptions of vendor hygiene and food safety in relation to street food
consumption.
3. Characterize perceived food safety concerns and barriers associated with street food
consumption.
By considering findings from previous research on consumer behavior and food safety risk
perception, this study contributes to a better understanding of consumer behavior in informal and
tourism-driven food systems. The present study represents the behavioral component of a broader
investigation of street food safety in Batroun, complementing a previously published
microbiological assessment conducted during the same sampling periods.
2. Results
2.1. Participant Characteristics
Street food consumption was highly prevalent, with 78.5% of participants reporting regular
consumption. However, consumption exhibited strong seasonal dependence, with a significantly
higher frequency observed during summer. This pattern suggests that environmental exposure,
tourism intensity, and social context may contribute to street food consumption beyond individual
preferences. Consumption was primarily associated with evening and social contexts, reinforcing

2.2. Domain I – Street Food Consumption Patterns and Contextual Factors
Consumers placed high importance on vendor hygiene when selecting street food vendors,
with visible hygiene cues, such as the cleanliness of preparation areas and the use of protective
clothing, representing prominent considerations when evaluating food safety. However, more
technical hygiene practices, such as proper handwashing, were less consistently observed or
recalled. These findings suggest that consumers relied primarily on visible hygiene cues when
evaluating street food vendors. This interpretation is consistent with previous research indicating
that, in situations of uncertainty, consumers may rely on observable cues as indicators of food
safety (Table 2).
Among consumers, 8.9% reported daily consumption, 23% reported consuming street food
2-3 times per week, and 19.7% reported once per week. Seasonal trends indicated greater
consumption during summer: 32.3% of winter respondents rarely or never consumed street food
compared with only 13.1% in summer. Daily consumption was higher in summer (12.4%) than in
winter (4.4%), and 33.6% of summer consumers ate street food 2-3 times per week compared with
22.2% in winter (Table 2). Within the consumer group, dinner was the most commonly reported
meal (46.3%), followed by consumption at various times per day (36.5%). Smaller proportions
consumed street food as snacks (7.1%), lunch (7.6%), or breakfast (2.5%) (Table 3). Fast food was
the dominant category (39.7%), while more than half of participants (52.2%) reported consuming
multiple food types. Discovery of street food outlets occurred through multiple channels: 45.1%
relied on more than one method, including recommendations (20.4%), walking by (20.9%), social
media (11.3%), and online searches (2.2%). Recommendations were more influential during the
summer. Taste emerged as the primary motivator (39.4%), while affordability, convenience, and
time efficiency were also reported, though less frequently. Many participants (42.9%) cited
multiple reasons for purchasing street food (Table 3).
Binary logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with street food
consumption (Table 4, Supplementary Table S1). In the total sample, males had significantly
higher odds of consuming street food than females (OR = 2.14, p = 0.006). Participants aged 26–
35 years were more likely to consume street food than those aged 36–64 years (OR = 7.53, p =

2.3. Domain II – Consumer Perceptions of Vendor Hygiene and Food Safety
Domain II examined consumer evaluations of vendor attributes and observations of
hygiene and safety practices. Food safety concerns were commonly reported among non-
consumers and represented an important barrier to street food consumption. Hygiene concerns,
regulatory oversight gaps, and uncertainty about food-handling practices were the primary barriers.
However, more than half of non-consumers reported conditional willingness to consume street
food, indicating that risk perception is dynamic and context-dependent rather than absolute.
2.3.1. Importance of Vendor Attributes
Consumers rated taste (mean = 4.76), hygiene and safety (mean = 4.63), and customer
service (mean = 4.13) as the most important factors influencing their choice of street food vendors
(Table 5). Moderate importance was assigned to variety (mean = 3.77), price (mean = 3.47), and
location (mean = 3.41), whereas nutritional value received the lowest importance rating (mean =
3.16). Comparisons between the two sampling periods showed only minor seasonal differences.
Ratings for taste, hygiene, and safety were slightly lower during the summer sampling period,
while the perceived importance of portion size, variety, nutritional value, and location increased.
In contrast, price was considered more important during the winter sampling period.
2.3.2. Observed Hygiene and Safety Practices
Perceptions of vendor hygiene varied (Table 6). While 37.2% of consumers considered
street food safe, 42.6% remained uncertain. Most respondents observed clean preparation areas
(70.9%) and appropriate use of working clothes or gloves (77.1%). However, only half recalled
proper handwashing, and glove use and face-touch avoidance were inconsistently reported.
Seasonally, winter respondents were more likely to perceive street food as safe (41.8% vs 34.4%)
and reported better hand hygiene and face-touching practices than summer respondents.

2.3.3. Factors Associated with Street Food Consumption Frequency
Ordinal logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with street food
consumption frequency among consumers (Table 7, Supplementary Table S2). In the total model,
assigning lower importance to hygiene and safety was associated with more frequent street food
consumption (B = 1.095, OR = 2.99, p = 0.004). Participants with average or poor financial status
reported lower consumption frequency than those with good financial status (B = –0.696, OR =
0.50, p = 0.001). Male participants (B = 1.226, OR = 3.41, p < 0.001) and those aged 15–25 years
(B = 1.756, OR = 5.79, p < 0.001) were also more likely to report higher consumption frequency.
In the winter sampling period, negative food safety perception, lower importance assigned to
nutritional value and hygiene and safety, poorer financial status, having a partner, male gender,
and younger age were significantly associated with consumption frequency. During the summer
sampling period, only male gender and younger age remained significant predictors of
consumption frequency.
2.4. Domain III – Perceived Risk and Barriers Among Non-Consumers
Domain III focused on perceived risks and barriers among non-consumers (Table 8). The
findings indicate that non-consumers commonly reported food safety concerns, dietary
restrictions, and structural barriers related to street food consumption. More than half,
nevertheless, indicated situations in which they would consider purchasing street food, suggesting
that food safety perceptions are context-dependent and may vary according to individual and
situational factors.
Most non-consumers (65.8%) expressed concerns about street food, particularly during
winter (75.3%). Dietary restrictions or preferences were reported by 52.3%, again more frequently
in winter. Only 14.4% cited prior negative experiences. Despite these concerns, 54.1% of non-
consumers indicated situations in which they might consider eating street food, with greater
openness reported in summer. These findings suggest a multidimensional risk profile combining
hygiene concerns, structural uncertainty, and personal constraints.
3. Discussion
This study provides empirical insights into street food consumption patterns and consumer
perceptions of food safety in informal food environments. The findings are interpreted in the

context of previous research on consumer behavior and food safety perceptions. Unlike formal
food systems, where safety is institutionalized through certification and regulation, consumers in
informal food systems may rely on observable vendor characteristics and personal experience
when evaluating food safety.
Among the study participants, street food consumption was widespread, with nearly four
out of five participants reporting regular purchases. Consumption was higher during the summer
sampling period, likely reflecting greater vendor density, event-based food availability, and
intensified social activity, patterns consistent with those observed in other tourist destinations 13,14.
Dinner emerged as the dominant consumption occasion, reinforcing previous evidence that street
food is closely linked to evening leisure and nightlife, particularly during warmer periods when
outdoor socialization is more common 15. Taste was the strongest reported motivator of street food
choice, clearly outweighing convenience and affordability. Contrary to common assumptions,
affordability was not universally perceived as a defining feature of street food, a finding that is
consistent with reports from other tourist destinations 14,16. In high-tourism areas, elevated
operational costs, such as rent, utilities, labor, and seasonal pricing strategies, may influence
pricing structures. This economic context may partly explain why participants reporting poorer
financial status consumed street food less frequently, particularly during the winter sampling
period. Discovery pathways further highlight the embedded nature of street food within physical
and social environments. Most participants reported discovering street food outlets by walking
past them or through personal recommendations, whereas online searches and social media played
a more limited role. These findings suggest that, within this study setting, street food consumption
remained primarily experiential and place-based rather than digitally mediated. Consistent with
the regression analyses, males and younger participants reported higher street food consumption,
in agreement with previous studies 14. These associations may reflect differences in lifestyle, food
preferences, and food safety perceptions previously reported among younger males 17. Younger
consumers may also be more receptive to spontaneous food choices and social dining experiences,
particularly in tourism-oriented environments where street food forms part of the recreational
experience. The observed differences between the two sampling periods likely reflect the distinct
tourism contexts intentionally captured by the study rather than climatic seasonality alone. The
summer sampling period was characterized by a younger visitor profile, likely driven by academic
breaks, beach tourism, and favorable weather that encouraged outdoor dining, whereas the winter

sampling period coincided with the Christmas market, which attracted a larger proportion of
families and older visitors. Accordingly, the seasonal findings should be interpreted within the
context of these distinct visitor profiles and the reduced precision associated with subgroup
analyses.
Perceptions of vendor hygiene and safety emerged as important considerations when
selecting street food vendors. Consumers rated hygiene and safety among the most important
attributes influencing their choices, alongside taste and service quality. However, consumers
appeared to rely primarily on visible hygiene cues rather than on less observable food-handling
practices when evaluating vendors. Clean preparation areas and appropriate attire were frequently
observed, yet more technical practices, such as consistent handwashing or avoidance of face
touching, were less reliably recalled. This pattern is consistent with previous studies suggesting
that consumers often rely on observable hygiene indicators and experiential familiarity when
evaluating food safety in informal settings. Similar patterns have been documented across
Mediterranean and MENA contexts, where consumers often infer food safety from visual
cleanliness and habitual familiarity with vendors 18–21. This observation is consistent with previous
research indicating that consumers often rely on observable cues when making decisions under
conditions of uncertainty. Importantly, individuals who assigned lower importance to hygiene and
safety consumed street food more frequently, whereas poorer financial status was associated with
lower frequency of street food consumption. These findings are consistent with previous evidence
that perceived food safety considerations influence food-related decision-making 16. Yet, despite
reported inconsistencies in hygiene practices 14, many consumers continued to perceive vendors
as generally compliant. These findings suggest that visible hygiene cues may influence how
consumers evaluate street food vendors. Because many food safety hazards cannot be detected
visually, consumers may rely on readily observable indicators as practical proxies for food safety
when making purchasing decisions in informal food settings. Social context further influences
consumption. Unpartnered individuals were more likely to report consuming street food, possibly
reflecting time constraints and reliance on convenient meals. However, partnership status showed
a nuanced relationship with consumption frequency, suggesting that shared norms and social
regulation may shape dietary behaviors 22. These findings indicate that street food consumption is
embedded in economic and environmental factors as well as social structures.

Among the non-consumers in the study sample, perceived food safety concerns and dietary
restrictions remained important barriers to street food consumption. Hygiene concerns, particularly
during the winter sampling period, were the dominant barrier, accompanied by structural factors
such as a perceived lack of regulation and unsafe water, as well as personal constraints such as
dietary preferences and prior negative experiences. These concerns represented important self-
reported barriers to street food consumption, consistent with prior evidence highlighting the central
role of perceived food safety considerations in avoidance behavior 16. Notably, more than half of
non-consumers indicated circumstances under which they might consider consuming street food,
particularly during the summer sampling period. These findings suggest that food safety concerns
are context-dependent and may vary according to environmental, social, and individual
circumstances. Similar observations from other settings indicate that consumers often do not
perceive street food as inherently unhygienic and may consider it as safe as food in other food-
service settings 14.
Collectively, these findings suggest that street food consumption is shaped by a balance
between perceived benefits, such as taste, convenience, and accessibility, and concerns related to
food safety. Consumers also reported placing high importance on vendor hygiene and visible
hygiene practices, highlighting the relevance of observable food safety cues in informal food
settings. Together, these findings indicate that street food choices may be influenced by a
combination of perceived benefits, food safety concerns, and observable vendor characteristics.
Consumer decisions therefore appear to reflect not only evaluations of potential food safety risks
but also familiarity, cultural norms, and situational context. This may explain why street food
remained widely consumed despite uncertainty regarding its safety, as consumers appeared to
balance perceived benefits, such as taste and convenience, against their own assessment of
observable hygiene practices. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies from
Mediterranean and MENA settings, where food safety perceptions are influenced by familiarity,
observable hygiene practices, and contextual factors18–21.
The present findings should also be interpreted alongside our previous microbiological
assessment of street-vended foods collected in Batroun during the same winter and summer
sampling periods 23. That study found that 42.6% (29/68) of street food samples exceeded
microbiological safety criteria, indicating that microbiological food safety hazards were present in
the same street food environment. In contrast, the present study found that 78.5% of participants

regularly consumed street food, while 37.2% considered it safe and 42.6% remained uncertain
about its safety. Moreover, consumers primarily relied on visible hygiene indicators, such as the
cleanliness of preparation areas and the use of protective clothing, when evaluating vendors. This
discrepancy highlights the importance of integrating vendor hygiene interventions and
microbiological surveillance with consumer education and effective risk communication to
support more informed food choices. Together, these findings suggest that consumer perceptions
of food safety may not always align with the objective microbiological evidence obtained from the
same street food environment.
Policy Implications and One Health Perspective
Improving street food safety requires a multidimensional strategy that integrates regulatory
oversight, behavioral interventions, and participatory education. Vendor training in hygiene
practices, including handwashing, utensil sanitation, temperature control, and water safety, can
reduce contamination risks while improving consumers' perceptions of food safety. Embedding
such initiatives within municipal licensing frameworks and tourism programs may formalize
informal vending without undermining economic livelihoods. Public awareness campaigns
targeting high-density tourist zones and seasonal peaks can further enhance risk communication.
A One Health-oriented, risk-based framework linking microbiological monitoring with behavioral
and environmental determinants aligns with Codex Alimentarius principles and WHO guidance,
promoting cross-sector collaboration among public health authorities, municipalities, and food
safety agencies. Ultimately, strengthening street food safety should be viewed not only as a public
health imperative but as a component of social and economic resilience in Mediterranean and
MENA cities. By integrating microbial evidence, consumer behavior research, and governance
innovation, informal street food systems can evolve towards safer, inclusive, and sustainable urban
food environments. Accordingly, effective policies should combine microbiological surveillance,
vendor training, visible hygiene practices, consumer education, and risk communication strategies
that align with consumer perceptions of food safety, rather than relying solely on technical food
safety interventions.
Limitations
This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings.
First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the observed associations should

not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships. Second, participants were recruited via
convenience sampling in a single Mediterranean tourist city, which may limit the generalizability
of the findings to other geographic, cultural, or regulatory settings. Third, the study relied on self-
reported behaviors and perceptions and is therefore susceptible to recall bias and social desirability
bias. In addition, the winter and summer surveys captured two distinct tourism periods
characterized by different visitor profiles. Although the regression models adjusted for key
sociodemographic variables, residual confounding related to differences in participant
composition cannot be completely excluded. Finally, this manuscript focuses specifically on the
behavioral dimension of street food safety. The corresponding microbiological assessment of
street-vended foods collected during the same winter and summer sampling periods and from the
same study setting has been reported separately. Accordingly, the findings presented here should
be interpreted alongside the complementary microbiological evidence to provide a more
comprehensive understanding of street food safety in Batroun.
This study provides new insights into street food consumption patterns and consumer
perceptions of food safety in an informal food system within a Mediterranean tourist setting. The
findings suggest that street food purchasing decisions are shaped by a combination of perceived
benefits, food safety considerations, and observable vendor hygiene cues rather than by formal
regulatory assurance alone. The findings indicate that consumer behavior is associated with
contextual, social, and behavioral factors that extend beyond objective assessments of food safety.
When considered alongside the companion microbiological investigation conducted in the same
street food environment23, this study provides a more comprehensive understanding of street food
safety by integrating consumer behavior with objective microbiological evidence. Together, these
findings suggest that consumer perceptions of food safety may not always align with
microbiological evidence, underscoring the importance of combining vendor training,
microbiological surveillance, visible hygiene practices, and effective risk communication to
improve informed food choices. Beyond its implications for street food safety, this work
contributes to a broader One Health perspective by demonstrating the value of integrating
behavioral and microbiological evidence to better understand food safety in informal food systems.
Future research integrating behavioral, observational, and microbiological data through
longitudinal or mixed-methods approaches would further strengthen the evidence base for
designing effective food safety interventions.

4. Methods
4.1. Study Design and Setting
A cross-sectional survey was conducted in Batroun, Lebanon, a Mediterranean coastal city
characterized by seasonal tourism and active street food vending. Data collection was performed
during two peak periods (December 2023 and August 2024) to capture seasonal variability in
consumer behavior and food environments. Data were collected at high-footfall public locations
with active street food vending, including the historic souk, waterfront promenade, beach areas,
public squares, and seasonal event venues. During the winter sampling period, recruitment
primarily took place at the Batroun Christmas Market, whereas the summer sampling period
focused on beach areas, the old souk, and other popular tourist locations.
4.2. Participants
A total of 517 complete questionnaires were included in the analysis. Only complete
questionnaires were included in the analysis. Uncompleted questionnaires were excluded, resulting
in a questionnaire completion rate of 99.4%. The final sample comprised 226 participants recruited
during the winter sampling period and 291 during the summer sampling period. Participants
included adolescents (15–17 years) and adults (18–64 years), representing the dominant
demographic groups present in street food environments. A convenience sampling strategy was
used. Trained data collectors approached individuals at predefined public locations at different
times of day, on weekdays and weekends, to capture a broad range of residents and visitors.
Individuals who met the eligibility criteria and agreed to participate completed the questionnaire
on-site. The sample size exceeded the recommended thresholds for multivariable logistic
regression, ensuring sufficient statistical power to detect moderate effect sizes across predictors.
Eligible participants included adolescents aged 15-17 years and adults aged 18-64 years,
reflecting the age groups most frequently present in Batroun’s street food environments during
peak tourist seasons. For participants aged 15–17 years, written parental/legal guardian consent
and participant assent were obtained before participation in accordance with institutional review
board (IRB) approval. Individuals aged 65 years and older were excluded because they were
underrepresented among street food consumers during peak tourism periods. Data were collected
in person using the pen-and-paper (PAPI) method to ensure accessibility for respondents with
varying literacy levels. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal

research approval, the consent forms for adolescents (assent form with the parent’s consent form)
and adults, as well as the questionnaire, are provided in Supplementary Documents 1-2abc-3.
4.3. Questionnaire Design
Data were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from previously published
instruments assessing food safety perceptions and consumer behavior in Mediterranean and
MENA contexts 18–21. The questionnaire was contextualized to the Lebanese street food
environment and designed to assess distinct domains of consumer characteristics, consumption
behaviors, food safety perceptions, vendor attributes, and observed hygiene practices. As the
questionnaire was intended to capture independent variables representing different aspects of
consumer behavior rather than multiple indicators of common latent constructs, the items were
analyzed individually. Consequently, internal consistency measures and factor analysis were not
performed. The instrument was organized into three thematic domains:
1. Street food consumption patterns and contextual factors
2. Consumer Perceptions of Vendor Hygiene and Food Safety (Consumers Only)
3. Perceived risks and barriers to consumption
Sociodemographic information (age, gender, education, income, and residency status) was
collected separately at the beginning of the questionnaire. The questionnaire included Likert-type
scales, dichotomous (yes/no), and multiple-choice questions. Items assessing consumer
perceptions and the perceived importance of vendor attributes were measured using five-point
Likert scales ranging from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree" or from "not important" to "very
important," depending on the question. The instrument was pre-tested with 30 participants to
assess clarity, cultural appropriateness, and content validity. Minor wording adjustments were
made before full deployment. A summary of the questionnaire variables, response scales, coding
used for statistical analyses, and corresponding analytical methods is provided in Supplementary
Table S3. The study did not employ composite scores or latent psychometric constructs; therefore,
each questionnaire item was analyzed individually according to its predefined coding.

4.3.1. Domain I: Street Food Consumption Patterns
This domain assessed behavioral exposure and consumption context. Participants reported
frequency of street food consumption (daily, weekly, monthly, rarely/never), type of street food
purchased, time of consumption, eating companions, and primary motivations (e.g., taste,
affordability, convenience, curiosity, social influence). Consumption frequency served as the
primary behavioral outcome variable in subsequent analyses. Participants who reported “rarely or
never” consuming street food were classified as non-consumers and directed to a shorter
questionnaire focusing on perceived risk and barriers.
4.3.2. Domain II: Consumer Perceptions of Vendor Hygiene and Food Safety (Consumers
Only)
Consumer perceptions of vendor hygiene and food safety were assessed among participants
who reported consuming street food. Among consumers, the questionnaire assessed two
complementary aspects of vendor evaluation: (i) observed hygiene and safety practices and (ii) the
perceived importance of vendor attributes during purchase decisions.
Observed Vendor Hygiene and Safety Practices: Participants indicated whether they observed
specific hygiene and food-handling behaviors, including cleanliness of preparation areas,
appropriate working clothes, hair covering, hand washing, glove use, avoidance of face touching,
and separation of payment from food preparation. Participants were also asked whether they
considered street food generally safe. These items represent observable hygiene practices that
consumers may use when evaluating street food vendors.
Perceived Importance of Vendor Attributes: Participants rated the importance of hygiene and
safety, taste, price, nutritional value, variety, portion size, customer service, vendor reputation,
packaging, and location when selecting street food vendors using a five-point Likert scale. These
items captured the relative importance of vendor- and product-related attributes during purchase
decisions.

4.4. Data analysis
Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27). Descriptive statistics were
computed to summarize participants' sociodemographic characteristics, street food consumption
patterns (Domain I), consumer perceptions of vendor hygiene and food safety (Domain II), and
perceived risks and barriers among non-consumers (Domain III). Participants were classified as
consumers or non-consumers based on their self-reported street food consumption frequency.
Missing values arising from conditional questionnaire branching were treated as planned system-
missing data. Variables included in the regression models were selected a priori based on the study
objectives and previous literature. Likert-scale variables assessing vendor attributes were
dichotomized into important and average/unimportant according to the predefined coding scheme
to facilitate interpretation and ensure adequate observations within each category for regression
analyses. Prior to model fitting, multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed
using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance statistics, and no evidence of problematic
multicollinearity was identified. Model assumptions were evaluated using standard diagnostic
procedures, including assessment of the proportional odds assumption for ordinal logistic
regression. Binary logistic regression was used to identify demographic and seasonal factors
associated with consumer status (consumer vs. non-consumer). Among consumers, ordinal logistic
regression was performed to identify factors associated with street food consumption frequency.
Consumer status was coded as 1 = consumer and 0 = non-consumer. Consumption frequency was
analyzed as an ordinal outcome using the recoded variable (R_frequency), with higher categories
representing more frequent street food consumption. Sociodemographic variables were entered as
categorical predictors using indicator (dummy) coding, with the reference categories specified in
the regression tables. Likert-scale variables assessing vendor attributes were dichotomized into
important and average/unimportant according to the predefined coding scheme to facilitate
interpretation and ensure sufficient observations within each category for regression analyses. All
regression models were adjusted for age group, gender, marital status, employment status,
financial status, previous visits to Batroun, and sampling period, as appropriate. Prior to model
fitting, multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed, and no evidence of

diagnostic procedures, including assessment of the proportional odds assumption for ordinal
logistic regression. Complete regression outputs, including regression coefficients, standard errors,

diagnostics, are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Statistical significance was set at p
< 0.05.
