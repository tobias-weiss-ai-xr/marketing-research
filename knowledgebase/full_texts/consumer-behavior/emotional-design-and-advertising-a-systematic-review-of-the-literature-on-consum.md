Introduction: Emotional design integrates sensory, functional, social, and
symbolic elements to elicit affective responses that may influence consumer
perception, attitudes, and behavior. This study aimed to analyze the
relationship between emotional design, advertising, and consumer behavior
based on recent scientific literature.
Methods: A systematic literature review was conducted following the PRISMA
guidelines. Searches were performed in Scopus and Web of Science, and 66
primary studies published during the last ten years were included.
Results: The reviewed evidence shows that emotional design strategies
incorporate perceptual stimuli, user experiences, and meaning-making
processes. Color, shape, texture, packaging, and multisensory stimuli
contribute to attraction, attention, and perceived quality, while usability,
ergonomics, interactivity, and demonstrations enhance the user experience.
Storytelling, cultural identity, sustainability, and personalization contribute to
meaning construction and strengthen consumer-brand connections. The
literature also identifies positive, negative, and complex emotions, including
pleasure, trust, fear, guilt, nostalgia, attachment, and identification, which are
associated with purchase intention, satisfaction, loyalty, repurchase, and other
consumer responses.
Discussion: The findings indicate that emotional design operates across
sensory, experiential, social, and symbolic dimensions, extending its influence
beyond immediate purchase decisions toward longer-term consumer-brand
relationships. However, the methodological diversity of the reviewed studies
highlights the need for more integrated approaches combining subjective,
physiological, and digital measures to evaluate emotional and behavioral
responses.

1 Introduction
Currently, the design of products, brands, and consumer
experiences is oriented toward a human-centered perspective,
where functionality coexists with the perception, experience, and
meaning that users attribute to objects. In this context, product
choice is related not only to its physical and technical attributes
but also to the affective responses it evokes through its
appearance and the experience it provides. Given that a gap still
exists between designers’ intentions and consumers’ emotional
perceptions, understanding these affective responses has become
a relevant challenge for developing proposals more aligned with
user expectations (Lin and Wei, 2017).
Within this framework, emotional design emerges as an
approach that seeks to go beyond functionality to promote an
affective connection between the user and the product. From
this perspective, the theory of emotional processing at three
levels visceral, behavioral, and reflective explains how different
cognitive dimensions intervene in the user experience and in the
generation of meaningful emotional responses (Yao, 2024). In
addition, it has been noted that consumers value not only the
functionality and reliability of products but also affective
attributes such as texture, shape, color, and style, as these
influence the impressions and feelings objects generate.
Consequently, affective design aims to understand the emotional
relationship between consumers and products, as well as to
identify the perceptual design elements linked to these affective
qualities, although their measurement is more complex than
evaluating functionality or usability (Chan et al., 2020).
Similarly, both designers and researchers recognize that the
emotional dimension plays a decisive role in consumer choices
and purchase decisions, although accurately identifying these
emotions remains difficult due to their multidimensional nature
and the challenges users face in clearly expressing them (Vaidya
and Kalita, 2022).
In the field of commercial communication, emotion has also
become a central component. Unlike rational approaches
focused on price, quality, or objective product features,
emotional appeals rely on the affective and experiential
dimensions of consumption. Their purpose is not limited to
eliciting positive emotions such as love, humor, pride, or joy but
also includes negative emotions such as fear, guilt, or shame
when aiming to induce corrective, preventive, or avoidance
behaviors (Lee and Heere, 2018).
In this sense, emotional advertising is characterized by its
ability to capture attention, remain in consumers’ memory, and
strengthen the bond with the brand, influencing attitudes,
engagement, and purchase intention. At the same time, these
campaigns contribute to building loyalty, reinforcing values, and
associating the brand with dimensions such as self-confidence,
status, or beauty, while also encouraging deeper reflections
about the product or brand, thus supporting market
sustainability and value creation (Pol et al., 2024; Vrtana and
Krizanova, 2023). Moreover, emotionally driven marketing
highlights the symbolic, personal, and affective dimensions of
brands through creative, visual, and textual resources, and
attitudes toward advertisements can mediate the formation of
favorable brand attitudes, even before a rational evaluation of
the product (Bou Saada et al., 2022).
The literature has also shown that purchase decisions are
strongly influenced by emotional experiences triggered by
sensory and aesthetic stimuli. Factors such as color, scent,
music, and other multisensory cues can modify perceived
attractiveness, consumer attitudes, and consumption experiences,
even in digital environments, intensifying emotional responses
and encouraging purchasing behavior (Wu and Chen, 2024).
Similarly, in sectors such as fashion and personal accessories,
design elements like color, shape, and texture are strategically
used to evoke specific emotional responses. It has also been
observed that negative emotions can delay or inhibit purchases
with a strength comparable to or even greater than the ability of
positive emotions to stimulate them (Yang et al., 2025). This
reinforces the idea that emotional design constitutes an
increasingly relevant differentiation strategy, while also imposing
new demands for creativity, innovation, and the ability to
translate emotions into impactful products and experiences,
leading to the development of methods and tools associated
with emotion-centered design (Zhang, 2026).
Regarding the state of the art, previous reviews have provided
important contributions to understanding the field. Liu et al.
(2024) identified four key research themes communication,
triggers, experience, and consumer behavior and concluded that
packaging directly influences perceived value, emotional
connections with the brand, and responses such as purchase,
loyalty, and satisfaction. Vaidya and Kalita (2022), highlighted
the growing relevance of emotional design as a differentiation
strategy and organized the evidence around emotional
terminology, factors influencing consumer emotional responses
before purchase, product emotion theories, and evaluation
methods. Likewise, López et al. (2021a), focusing on emotional
design methodologies such as Kansei engineering, analyzed how
different approaches translate desired emotions into concrete
design parameters and proposed guidelines for their application
across various product types.

review, previous review studies related to emotional design and
consumer behavior were compared according to their scope,
main contributions, and remaining research gaps. Table 1

Despite these contributions, the literature remains fragmented
regarding the strategies used, the types of emotions activated, their
influence on consumer behavior, and the methods used to
measure these responses in design, advertising, and
consumption contexts. Therefore, conducting a systematic
review that integrates these components is relevant to better
understand how emotional design operates in the relationship
between products, advertising, and consumer behavior. In this
sense, the objective of this study is to analyze, based on
scientific literature, the main emotional design strategies used
and related aspects, addressing the following research questions:

1.1 Theoretical framework
Norman (2004) popularized the concept of emotional design,
understood as the ability of objects to elicit affective responses that
influence how people perceive, use, and evaluate them. According
to this author, the experience with everyday objects can be
understood through three levels of processing: visceral,
behavioral, and reflective. The visceral level is associated with
the immediate reaction generated by the appearance of an
object, particularly through attributes such as color, shape,
texture, and style, which produce rapid impressions of liking or
disliking. The behavioral level is linked to the experience of use,
functionality, and usability, such that a product that is easy to
understand, efficient, and satisfying to use tends to generate a
favorable emotional evaluation during interaction. The reflective
level, in turn, relates to the meaning the object acquires for the
user, as it may be associated with memories, identity, status,
values, or self-expression. In this way, emotional design shows
that the relationship between people and objects is determined
not only by utility but also by the product’s capacity to generate
immediate impact, satisfying experiences, and lasting
symbolic meanings.
Complementing Norman’s approach, Desmet and Hekkert
(2007) proposed that product experience emerges from the
interaction between aesthetic perception, emotional responses,
and the meanings that individuals attribute to products. From
this perspective, products are not evaluated exclusively through
their functional characteristics but also through the emotions
and experiences they generate during interaction. Similarly,
Desmet (2003) argued that product-related emotions arise from
the relationship between product characteristics and users’
concerns, expectations, and personal experiences, highlighting
that the same design element may generate different emotional
responses depending on the individual and contextual
factors involved.
Jordan (2000) proposes that design can be guided by different
strategies aimed at generating pleasure in users, organized into
four dimensions: physio-pleasure, socio-pleasure, psycho-pleasure,
and ideo-pleasure. Physio-pleasure strategies focus on sensory and
bodily stimulation through elements such as color, shape, texture,
or sound, which influence immediate user responses. Psycho-
pleasure strategies relate to the user experience, considering
aspects such as ease of use, efficiency, understanding, and a sense
of control. Socio-pleasure strategies are linked to social
interaction, belonging, and connection with others, where the
product acts as a mediator of relationships and social recognition.
Ideo-pleasure strategies address deeper dimensions related to
values, identity, and personal meaning, allowing products to
represent users’ beliefs, lifestyles, or aspirations. This framework
allows emotional design to be understood as a set of strategies
operating at different levels of the consumer experience.
From the perspective of user experience, emotional design
extends beyond usability by incorporating subjective
perceptions, affective responses, and personal meanings
developed during interaction. Hassenzahl and Tractinsky (2006)
emphasized that user experience involves both pragmatic and
hedonic dimensions, where emotional aspects influence how
individuals evaluate products and technologies. Therefore,
emotional design contributes to creating experiences that are
meaningful and engaging, strengthening the relationship
between users, products, and brands.
Consumer behavior can be understood as a broad construct
that encompasses not only purchase decisions but also various
relational and attitudinal responses toward a brand. In this
regard, Ghorbanzadeh (2020) shows that when consumers
develop a deep emotional connection with a brand, they tend to
exhibit behaviors such as purchase intention, willingness to pay
a premium price, loyalty, and positive word of mouth. This
relationship is further strengthened by variables such as brand
identification and trust, which foster a more solid and
sustainable affective bond.

From a broader marketing perspective, emotional connections
between consumers and brands are influenced by experiential and
symbolic aspects of consumption. Holbrook and Hirschman
(1982) highlighted that consumption involves not only
functional evaluation but also emotions, fantasies, meanings,
and experiences associated with products and brands. Thus,
advertising and design strategies may influence consumer
responses by connecting products with personal values,
memories, identities, and aspirations.
In the context of consumer behavior, emotions can be
understood through the hierarchical model proposed by Laros
and Steenkamp (2005), who suggest that affective responses are
organized into two general dimensions: positive affect and
negative affect. Positive affect includes emotions such as joy,
satisfaction, enthusiasm, and pleasure, associated with favorable
experiences that influence consumer evaluation and preference.
Negative affect, on the other hand, includes emotions such as
fear, guilt, shame, or sadness, which may lead to rejection,
avoidance, or alert states in response to certain stimuli. From
these broad dimensions, the authors identify a more specific level
of emotions that captures more elaborated affective responses,
such as pride, nostalgia, or attachment, which reflect more
complex emotional experiences linked to identity, memory, and
the relationship with products or brands. This approach allows
the emotional analysis of consumer behavior to be structured
from general categories toward deeper affective manifestations.
Additionally, Bagozzi et al. (1999) highlighted that emotions
play a central role in marketing decisions because they influence
consumer evaluations, intentions, and behaviors. Emotional
responses can mediate the relationship between perceptions and
actions, explaining why affective factors are relevant in processes
such as preference formation, brand attachment, and purchase
decisions. Therefore, understanding emotional design requires
integrating both the characteristics of the stimulus and the
psychological processes through which consumers interpret and
respond to products, advertisements, and brands.
2 Materials and methods
For the development of this study, a systematic literature
review was conducted following the PRISMA guidelines (Page
et al., 2021), with the aim of organizing the process of
identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion of studies in a
transparent manner. This approach enabled the structured
search, selection, and synthesis of evidence related to emotional
design and its influence on consumer behavior. The complete
process of search, screening, and study selection is available in
the Supplementary Material and in the OSF registry indicated at
the end of the study.
2.1 Eligibility criteria and information
sources
The search was conducted in the Scopus and Web of Science
databases, due to their coverage of peer-reviewed scientific
literature in areas related to design, advertising, marketing, and
consumer behavior. Search terms were applied to the title,
abstract, and keywords fields in order to retrieve studies directly
related to the topic of analysis.
Publications from the last ten years were considered, without
language restrictions, to include recent evidence and broaden the
scope of results. Records corresponding to books, errata, and
retractions were excluded, as well as documents that, after reviewing
the title, abstract, or full text, were not directly related to the
objective of the review. This delimitation allowed the refinement of
the sample, retaining only studies relevant to the analysis.
2.2 Search strategy
The search process was conducted iteratively. Initially, a query
was performed in the Scopus database using keywords aligned
with the objectives of the study. Subsequently, the retrieved
records were subjected to exploratory analysis in RStudio
(version 4.4.2), which allowed the identification of thematic
trends, relationships among terms, and co-occurrence networks.
Based on this analysis, the search strategy was refined by
incorporating more precise and relevant terms for the topic. The
filters defined in the eligibility criteria were then applied, limiting
document retrieval to those containing the key terms in the
predefined fields (see Table 2). The final search strategy was applied
uniformly across the selected databases on April 8, 2026, to ensure
consistency in the process and comparability of the results obtained.

2.4 Bias risk assessment
The risk of bias assessment was conducted through a three-
stage review process, with active participation of the authors to
ensure a double review of each study. In the first stage, titles,
abstracts, and keywords were screened. Each record was
evaluated by one author and subsequently reviewed by a second
author. In cases of disagreement, a third author acted as a
reviewer to reach consensus.
In the second stage, the selected studies were assessed using
the QuADS tool, which evaluates methodological and reporting
quality based on 13 criteria, including theoretical underpinning,
clarity of objectives, study design, sampling, data collection and
analysis, consideration of relevant stakeholders, and discussion
of limitations. This assessment was also reviewed by more than
one author to reduce individual bias and enhance consistency in
the process.
Based on the total scores obtained, studies were classified
according to their risk of bias: scores between 1 and 13 points
were considered as high risk of bias, reflecting lower
methodological quality; scores between 14 and 26 points were
classified as moderate risk of bias, indicating an intermediate
level of methodological quality; and scores between 27 and 39
points were considered as low risk of bias, representing higher
methodological quality.

studies. Of the 75 studies assessed, 45 (60.0%) presented high

2.5 Synthesis methods
The synthesis was conducted through a thematic analysis
approach combining deductive and inductive procedures. The
initial coding framework was established based on the
theoretical foundations of emotional design and the research
questions of the study. Specifically, emotional design strategies
were classified according to Jordan’s (2000) four dimensions of
pleasure: physio-pleasure, psycho-pleasure, socio-pleasure, ideo-
pleasure and cross-cutting strategies. Likewise, the emotions
identified in the literature were organized according to recurring
categories reported in previous studies, including positive
emotions, negative emotions, complex or relational emotions,
and cross-cutting emotional states.
Based on this initial framework, the information extracted
from each included study was organized in a Microsoft Excel
matrix containing authors, publication year, country, and main
findings. Subsequently, the coded information was analyzed to
identify recurring patterns, similarities, and differences among
studies. Emerging subthemes were incorporated during the
synthesis process when relevant findings did not fit completely
within the predefined categories. The final synthesis was
structured according to the research questions, addressing
emotional design strategies, emotions elicited by advertising,
their influence on consumer behavior, and methods used to
evaluate emotional responses.
3 Results
3.1 Bibliometric data
The distribution of studies by year shows a progressive
evolution in scientific production on emotional design. Between
2016 and 2017, the number of studies was limited (1 and 3
studies, respectively), followed by an increase in 2018
(8 studies), suggesting an initial rise in interest in the topic.
Between 2019 and 2020, production remained stable, with 3
studies per year, before increasing again from 2021 (8 studies)
and 2022 (9 studies), reaching its highest point in 2024 (10
studies). In the most recent years, 2023, 2025, and 2026,
production remained relatively consistent, with 7 studies in each
year, indicating consolidation of the field (see Figure 3). Overall,
these data reflect a growing academic interest in emotional
design, particularly in recent years.
The geographical distribution shows a notable concentration
in Asian countries (see Figure 4), with China as the most
represented context (26 studies), followed by Taiwan (6), South
Korea (3), India (3), and Indonesia (3). In Europe, Spain stands
out (5), while in the Americas, contributions were identified
from the United States (3), Brazil (2), and Peru (2). The high
presence of studies from China may be related to the rapid
growth of its digital and consumer markets, the development of
industries linked to e-commerce, innovation in product design,
and strong competition among brands. It should be noted that
the geographical distribution does not correspond to the total
number of included studies (n = 66), as some articles involved
authors affiliated with institutions from multiple countries.
Therefore, a single study could be represented in more than one
country, allowing the analysis to capture international
collaboration patterns.

emotional connections and foster a sense of identification with
others. This dimension highlights the role of social context in
the consumption experience.
In the case of ideo-pleasure, the grouped strategies reveal that
design also operates at a deeper level, associated with identity,
values, and symbolic meaning. Elements such as sustainability,
culture, self-expression, and status allow products to go beyond
functionality and become vehicles of personal and social
representation. From an integrated perspective, the presence of
cross-cutting strategies such as storytelling, interactivity, and
personalization indicates that emotional design operates
holistically, combining multiple dimensions to influence
perception and the consumer–product relationship.
3.3 Emotions elicited in advertising
campaigns
The emotions identified in the reviewed literature reveal that
emotional design and advertising rely on a broad spectrum of
affective responses that extend beyond the generation of
immediate positive reactions. These emotions range from
pleasure and trust to fear, guilt, nostalgia, attachment, and
identification, reflecting the multidimensional nature of
consumer emotional experiences. To facilitate their analysis, the
identified emotions were organized according to their primary
classification, subcategory, and emotional characteristics
reported in the literature, while also being interpreted through
Norman’s three levels of emotional processing (visceral,
behavioral, and reflective). Table 4 presents this classification.
The classification shows that positive emotions occupy a
prominent place in the literature, particularly those associated
with pleasure, well-being, trust, and favorable evaluation of the
product or brand. These emotions operate at different levels:
some generate immediate responses to visual or sensory stimuli,
others relate to comfort and satisfaction during the user
experience, and others are linked to recognition, prestige, or
social connection.
Negative emotions also appear as part of the identified
emotional strategies. In some cases, they are associated with
alertness, rejection, or discomfort; in others, they involve more
reflective responses such as guilt, shame, or social sanction. This
suggests that their function is not always unfavorable, as they
can be used in specific advertising contexts to attract attention,
raise awareness, or motivate particular behaviors.
Complex or relational emotions reflect a deeper dimension of
the consumer’s emotional experience. Emotions such as nostalgia,
belonging, attachment, identification, or brand passion are related
to memory, identity, and relationship building. These responses
are generally situated at a more reflective level, as they involve
personal or symbolic interpretations of the product, brand, or
advertising message.

help to understand the intensity and direction of affective
responses. Activation is expressed through emotions such as
enthusiasm, vitality, or excitement, while valence distinguishes
whether the experience is oriented toward positive, negative, or
neutral outcomes. This perspective complements the

3.5 Methods for evaluating emotional
responses and consumer behavior
According to the reviewed literature, the measurement of
emotions and consumer behavior has been addressed through
a wide range of methods. The most commonly used are self-
report techniques, particularly structured questionnair es and
surveys, often administered online and accompanied by Likert
scales or semantic differential scales (Bhutto et al., 2021; Dewi
et al., 2018; Fardi, 2021; Guo et al., 2023; Lee and Heere, 2018;
Lopes and Veiga, 2019; Ong et al., 2025; Pol et al., 2024; Wang
et al., 2025; Yen, 2018; Zhang, 2026). These instruments allow
the capture of perceptions, attitudes, satisfaction levels,
purchase intention, and self-reported emotional responses,
which explains their widespread use in marketing and
emotional design research.
At the same time, the literature highlights the use of
psychophysiological methods aimed at measuring emotional
responses more objectively. Among the most frequently used are
electroencephalography (EEG), galvanic skin response (GSR),
electromyography (EMG), eye-tracking, and facial recognition
(Guo et al., 2018; Vaidya and Kalita, 2022; Wu and Chen, 2024;
Xiao et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2026; Yang et al.,
2025). These techniques enable the recording of automatic
consumer reactions, such as attention levels, emotional arousal,
and brain activity in response to visual or advertising stimuli.
Indicators such as fixation duration, eye movements, and
changes in skin conductance provide insights into the intensity
and nature of emotional responses.
Mixed methodological approaches are also identified,
integrating qualitative and quantitative techniques such as semi-
structured interviews, focus groups, participant observation, and
ideation sessions (Jin et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021; Rocha-Vilca
et al., 2024; Tang, 2025). These strategies allow a deeper
understanding of the consumer’s subjective experience, exploring
the emotional meaning of products and their relationship with
brands. Tools such as the Kano model, customer satisfaction
scales (CSAT), and Kansei Engineering facilitate the linkage
between user emotions and design attributes (Jin et al., 2022;
Kang and Nagasawa, 2023; Li and Xue, 2023; Liu et al., 2021;
López et al., 2021a; Wang et al., 2018), helping translate affective
perceptions into concrete product features.
Recent literature shows an increasing use of data-driven and
machine learning-based techniques to evaluate emotional and
behavioral responses. These include text mining, sentiment
analysis of product reviews, the use of affective lexicons, and
artificial intelligence models such as neural networks (Chan et al.,
2020; Chang and Lee, 2018; Jin et al., 2022; Kwong et al., 2016;
Wang et al., 2018, 2024; Wang and Chin, 2017). These tools allow
the processing of large volumes of data from digital environments,
enabling the identification of emotional patterns, satisfaction
levels, and behavioral trends in real-world consumption contexts.

4 Discussion
The results of the review show that emotional design emerges
as a relevant factor in influencing consumer behavior, particularly
in purchase intention and product perception. In this regard, the
findings are consistent with those reported by Yao (2024), who
indicates that the incorporation of emotional elements in design
increases purchase intention and improves the aesthetic and
creative evaluation of products. This aligns with the results
obtained, where sensory and aesthetic strategies appear as some
of the most frequently used resources, suggesting that the visual
and emotional dimension not only captures attention but also
influences the final purchase decision.
Additionally, the results indicate that strategies based on
positive emotions, such as affection, closeness, and well-being,
are commonly used in advertising. This finding is consistent
with Masita et al. (2022), who highlight that positive emotional
content enhances brand attitude, memorability, and perceived
quality. However, the literature also shows the strategic use of
negative emotions, introducing a more complex dimension to
the analysis. In particular, the use of fear, guilt, or shame
identified in some studies confirms that these emotions can
function as persuasive mechanisms in specific contexts,
especially in health-related or personal care products (Fardi,
2021; Lee and Heere, 2018). This suggests that the effectiveness
of emotional design does not rely solely on generating positive
feelings, but also on activating responses aligned with the
objective of the message.
Regarding the identified strategies, the results reveal a strong
presence of sensory, functional, and symbolic elements. In this
sense, packaging, color, and shape play a key role in generating
expectations and emotional responses, which is consistent with
Casales-García et al. (2024), who state that these attributes directly
influence perceived quality and purchase intention. At the same
time, the use of color as an emotional element reinforces its role as
a generator of symbolic associations, as described by Correia and
Simões (2022), showing that consumer responses may vary
depending on the intensity and cultural context of the stimulus.
Another relevant aspect identified in the results is the impact
of experiential strategies, such as product demonstrations,
interactivity, and immersion. These strategies not only convey
information but also generate higher levels of emotional
activation and consumer engagement, which is consistent with
Wang et al. (2025), who show that immersive experiences
increase purchase intention by strengthening the emotional
connection with the product. This finding reinforces the idea
that emotional design extends beyond visual aspects and
encompasses the entire interaction experience.
Furthermore, the results show that complex emotions, such as
attachment, identification, and loyalty, play a significant role in
long-term consumer–brand relationships. This is consistent with
Yin and Liu (2026), who indicate that trust and brand image,
built through positive experiences, are key determinants of
consumer loyalty. In parallel, strategies related to values,
sustainability, and identity reflect a shift in consumer
preferences, particularly among younger audiences, which aligns
with Cabrera-Flores et al. (2025), who report a growing
orientation toward responsible and ecological consumption.
Regarding evaluation methods, the results reveal a diversity of
approaches, ranging from traditional techniques to neuroscientific
methods. The use of tools such as EEG, GSR, and eye-tracking, as
described by Xu et al. (2026), indicates progress toward more
objective measurements of emotional responses. This suggests a
trend toward integrating multidimensional methods to better
understand the relationship between emotion, design, and
consumer behavior.
4.1 Limitations of the study
One limitation of this study is the high methodological,
thematic, and contextual diversity of the reviewed evidence, as
the analyzed studies address emotional design in products,
advertising, packaging, fashion, digital environments, and
consumption experiences. This heterogeneity makes it difficult
to establish direct comparisons between findings. In addition,
there is a significant geographical concentration in China and
other Asian countries, so the results should be interpreted
considering possible cultural differences in how consumers
perceive and express emotions. Another limitation is related to
the variety of methods used to measure emotional responses, as
some studies rely on self-reports while others use physiological
or computational techniques, which may lead to differences
in interpretation.
4.2 Future research
Future studies could further explore comparative analyses
across regions and cultures to better understand how consumer
emotional responses vary under similar design or advertising
strategies. It would also be relevant to develop empirical studies
that integrate mixed methods, combining questionnaires,
interviews, eye-tracking, EEG, GSR, and sentiment analysis to
capture emotional and behavioral responses more
comprehensively. Additionally, further research is recommended
on the role of negative and complex emotions, such as fear,
guilt, nostalgia, or attachment, as they may significantly
influence purchase intention, loyalty, and the symbolic
relationship with brands.
An additional avenue for future research involves examining
the influence of emotional design throughout the complete
consumer decision journey, including pre-purchase processes
such as information search and product evaluation, as well as
post-purchase behaviors such as brand switching, complaint
behavior, product disposal, and consumer advocacy. These
perspectives would contribute to a more comprehensive
understanding of how emotional responses shape consumer
relationships beyond purchase intention and brand loyalty.

The review identified that emotional design strategies are
structured around sensory stimuli, user experiences, social
connections, and symbolic meanings. Among the most relevant

findings are the use of color, shape, texture, usability, interactivity,
storytelling, personalization, and sustainability. This shows that
emotional design goes beyond visual appeal, integrating
perception, functionality, experience, and values.
Regarding the emotions employed, the literature indicates a
combination of positive, negative, and complex emotions.
Positive emotions, such as pleasure, joy, satisfaction, and trust,
enhance product attractiveness and evaluation, while negative
emotions, such as fear, guilt, or shame, can function as
persuasive tools in specific contexts. Additionally, emotions such
as nostalgia, attachment, belonging, and identification reflect
deeper connections between the consumer, the product, and

These emotional elements directly influence consumer
behavior, particularly purchase intention, repurchase,
satisfaction, loyalty, positive word of mouth, willingness to pay
more, and brand perception. The literature suggests that
emotions shape how consumers evaluate, remember, and relate
to products, positioning emotional design as a strategic tool to
strengthen both immediate decisions and long-term relationships.
Regarding the evaluation of these responses, studies employ a
wide range of methods, from questionnaires, surveys, and Likert
scales to psychophysiological techniques such as EEG, GSR, eye-
tracking, and facial recognition. Computational approaches,
including text mining, sentiment analysis, and artificial
intelligence, are also identified. This methodological diversity
reflects a growing field, although there is still a need for greater
integration between subjective, physiological, and
digital measurements.
