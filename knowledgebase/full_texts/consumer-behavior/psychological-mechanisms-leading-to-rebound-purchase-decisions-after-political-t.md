Abstract
This paper has explored the psychological processes of rebound purchase behavior  after the de -
escalation of the political situations, and the target consumers were a group of consumers in Jordan.
With the help of quantitative survey method and SPSS analysis of the answers given by 370
consumers, the results are emphatic that alleviati on of political tensions can lead to rebound buying
behavior and post-boycott buying behavior. The model showed a high explanatory power as it was able
to explain 78.3 percent rebound purchasing which proves the strength of the proposed psychological
framework. Of the analyzed factors, the dissonance reduction became the strongest force, as it stressed
the importance of cognitive consistency in making consumers excuse their shift to the old brands that
it boycotted. Emotional reconciliation was also an impo rtant aspect by turning any negative emotions
related to the boycott to a positive affect when the tensions are over. Conflict resolution also
contributed to buyback through the ethical and moral closure, and the least impactful social proof
normalization demonstrated the contribution of the changed social norms to the legitimizing of the
repurchase behavior. Together the results can add to the literature of consumer behavior showing that
rebound purchasing is not only economic but it is also firmly based o n psychological and emotional
mechanisms influenced by political settings.

Introduction
In a world with a growing globalized economy, politically instigated acts (along the lines of trade,
geopolitical issues, or nationalistic actions) can generally prompt consumer boycotts of brands that
represent hostile nations or groups to consumers. Wang  et al. (2025) argued that the boycotts are driven
by either patriotism, morality issues, or social pressure and this interferes with the market forces and
doubts brand strength. But a little -investigated phenomenon occurs when the tension goes down: the s o-
called rebound purchase or buyback when previously boycotted customers not only start buying again
but manifest significantly increased interest, which results in demand booms. Such a transition of boycott
to buyback poses radically important questions r egarding the psychological processes underlying it, such
as cognitive dissonance and emotional release management, compensatory consumption, and identity
reaffirmation (Do et al., 2024).
According to Sarfraz (2025), this issue is especially severe in unsta ble areas such as the Middle East,
Asia, or Europe, whereby a particular event, such as the U.S. -China trade war or conflicts between Israel
and Palestine, will trigger an instant boycott of a certain brand such as Starbucks or Coca -Cola and the
next day t he businesses will be restored to their usual level. Al -Farsi and Hammond (2025) added that
these cycles may lose long -term brand equity, increase the volatility of the market and make international
marketing strategies difficult. The companies will spend resources in the wrong direction, or miss
opportunities to get back on track, or may even drive away customers with insensitive campaigns,
without having knowledge of the psychological motivators behind each of them: is it the feeling of guilt
relief after boycotting, or a rush of forbidden fruit recovered?
The available literature on consumer boycotts is strong, as it relies on the social identity theory (Patiro
et al., 2025) and moral disengagement theory (Bravo and Chapa, 2025), which explain the initiat ion and

Literature Review
Boycott Due to Political Tension
Boycotting is a type of protest through the organized behavior of refusing to buy, consume or
patronize certain products, services or brands due to some form of political, ethical or moral wrongdoing
(Adi et al., 2025). Boycotts are norma lly political in nature when they are fueled by geopolitical issues,
government policies, international issues, military developments, abuse of human rights, or adverse
support to controversial regimes. The purpose of these boycotts is to cause economic pr essure, create
awareness, show solidarity, or demonstrate moral disapproval, which in most cases is done via social
media campaigns, calls to action, and grassroots campaigns (Nero and Haya, 2024).
According to Citra et al. (2025), political boycotts that are based on tension are emotional and
communal. They most often appear as a reaction to a war, a territorial conflict, sanctions, or a diplomatic
crisis. An illustration is the mass protests against American brands (e.g. Coca -Cola, Starbucks,
McDonald's) in some Middle Eastern and majority -Muslim countries in times of intensification in the
Israel-Palestine conflict, or protests against Russian products and brands in Western countries during the

more enthusiastically. Knowing them is the main asset of brands that pass -through boycott recovery in

Conflict Resolution
Conflict resolution is where the outer or perceived solution of the underlying political conflict that
had caused the boycott is resolved. It can be done by diplomatic settlements, official apolo gies by the
brand or government, altering the policy or just by media statements that the problem is closed. At the
psychological level, when consumers resolve the conflict, moral justification of the continuation of the
boycott becomes weak. The eliminati on of the external stimulus enables people to go back to the brand
without the feeling that they are betraying their values hence making it easy to get back on the feet. This
resolution in most instances is a "permission slip" to consumers to start buying once again (Al -Mansoori
and Collins, 2025).

Dissonance Reduction
Dissonance reduction consists in the reduction of cognitive dissonance, which is the mental unease
that a person experiences when they are having different beliefs or behaviors (e.g., I like  this brand vs.).
Moral reasons (I am boycotting it because of moral reasons). Once the political tensions subside
consumers become more dissonant in case they remain not buying a now innocent brand. In order to
alleviate this tension, they justify their r ecurring purchase (The problem is fixed, now it is all okay) and
buy the product again. It is a self-perpetuating process where the alleviation of internal conflict makes one
become even more attached to the brand or even make a compensatory purchase in an  attempt to
establish consistency (Jedicke et al., 2025).

Emotional Reconciliation
Emotional reconciliation refers to the emotional release and healing that would be done after the
boycott season. Boycotts produce strong negative experiences anger, guilt,  moral outrage and sadness.
Once tensions go down, consumers undergo catharsis: the anger is gone, the feelings of guilt about
previous purchases go down, and positive (relief, joy, nostalgia) feelings towards the brand reappear. This
emotional re-set frequently comes in the form of forgiveness and fresh affection that drives the buyback

Social Proof Normalization
When a wider social context changes, social evidence normalization happens, which indicates that it is
time to come back to the brand that has been boycotted.  In active boycotts, non -purchase is enforced by
social norms by pressuring peers, conducting social media campaigns, and collective identity. After the
tensions decrease, influencers, peers, and the media start using again, which produces visual social
evidence that it is time to buy again. Consumers can see that other people are also going back (e.g. friends
reposting product pictures once more) and abide by the new standard not to be socially marginalized or
judged. Such a normalization is lessening the fear of backlash and leads to rapid rebound buying
(Mulyono and Rolando, 2025).

The Psychology of Boycott and Purchase decision
According to Ulfah  et al. (2025) the phenomenon of consumer boycotts and following decisions on
purchases is an exciting point of interaction between social psychology, emotion, identity, and rational
choice. Boycotts are used when people or organizations deliberately decid e to no longer purchase a brand,
a company or a country based on what they believe to be an ethical, political or moral offence, e.g., human
rights violation, environmental degradation, labor exploitation, or a geopolitical dispute. The boycotting
decision mostly is not so rooted in economics; it is entrenched in the psychological processes. On that
idea, Aji et al. (2025) stated that boycott might be rooted in the identity signaling and moral outrage.
Moral outrage is often a potent emotional reaction to injustice, which produces boycotts. Moral
foundations theory. Elshaer et al. (2025) demonstrates that infringement of care/harm, fairness/cheating
or loyalty/betrayal are highly anger -inducing. Boycotts are done by consumers to redefine a state of
moral co herence and announce their identity to others. Engaging will be a manifestation of virtue
signaling and sense of belonging: I belong to a group that would be opposed to this injustice. The social
identity theory (Patiro et al., 2025) is the reason why boyc otts are gaining traction during polarizing times -
people feel self -esteem through being a part of an in -group, and boycotting supports the us vs. them
dynamics.
Batool et al. (2025) argued that individuals might boycott as a justification and cognitive di ssonance.
After a boycott, the consumer develops cognitive dissonance in case he or she still wants to have the
product. In order to lessen the unpleasant feelings, they also indulge in post -decision rationalization: they
amplify the wrongness of the bran d, underrate its quality (It was never that good anyway), or even seek
confirmation of their mistake by their fellow believers. This reason makes boycott determination more
psychologically expensive to undo (Sen, 2024).
Anwar et al. (2025) saw motive for b oycott as a form of social pressure and conformity of norms.
Boycotts are viral and social networks enhanced by social proof and conformity help to spread. When
social media influencers, friends or online communities urge people to boycott, they feel oblig ated to do it
to avoid being ostracized. Personal convenience is less than the fear of being branded a traitor or a
hypocrite, particularly in collectivist societies or very politicized settings (Gala et al., 2024).
On the other hand, Sen (2024) argued tha t a large number of consumers begin to feel a psychological
backlash when the underlying tension is subsequently relieved (e.g. apology, change of policy or
diplomatic solution). The venting of a built -up passion, together with reduction of guilt (I have m ade my
point), emotional catharsis and the fascination of the forbidden fruit (reactance theory) tend to cause an
explosion of purchases. Others go overboard, even purchasing more than they usually do to renegotiate
commitment or to eliminate the dissonanc e. The phenomenon of buyback serves to bring out the elasticity
of consumer behavior as boycotts tend to be short -lived and tend to be more of an emotion than a shift in
preference (Kim and Kim, 2025).
On the same concept, Hammouda and Elkeerany (2024) con firmed that the length and severity of
boycotts vary according to such factors as perceived egregiousness, brand attachment, presence of
substitutes, and culture. Personal ethics are more important in individualistic society, whereas collective

From Boycott to Buyback
Powerful demonstrations of moral anger, social identity, and collective action, consumer boycotts are
usually provok ed by political conflicts, ethical scandals, or digital wrongdoing of the brand (Christy
Wahyuni Erwinata, 2024). But when the conflict behind -the-scenes subsides, with apologies, policy
reversals, diplomatic compromises or waning media coverage, a large p ortion of the boycotting
consumers start to repurchase the boycotted brand, often even more passionately than they previously
did (Isjwardhani and Rahmah, 2025). The phenomenon of rebound buying (or buyback) is a complicated
combination of psychological processes that turns the original rejection back.
On the idea, Betz (2024) argued that regarding the reactive theory and the charm of the forbidden.
This theory suggests that any form of restriction of freedom like boycotts of oneself results in
psychological tension. Once the boycott is not so socially and morally necessary, the formerly tabooed
product becomes again attractive as forbidden fruit. Consumers are overly stimulated to make purchases
as a result of the desire premise. The research on boycotts i n the U.S. China trade war revealed that the
release of this reactance led to a lot of consumers returning to purchase American brands in short periods.
Jimoh et al. (2024) confirmed that there might be a secondary theory for the rebound which is
cognitive dissonance resolution.   Boycotting usually would cause internal tension to consumers who
actually loved the brand. They exaggerate the faults of the brand (reduction of post -decision dissonance)
in order to defend the boycott. Once the boycott is over, d issonance changes and it is no longer the same
to continue to avoid a brand that has become now innocuous. The solution to this anxiety by formulating
a buyback reinstates psychological balance. Positive feelings can be enhanced by the release of the
pressure of being free to consume the product again, which makes the process of rebound.
On the same idea, Kıyak and Dora (2025) argued that emotional catharsis and alleviation of guilt.
Boycotts are associated with highly negative emotions - anger, guilt (of pr evious purchases) and moral
superiority. The consumers are the ones who get an emotional catharsis once the cause has been solved.
The discharge of accumulated guilt (I have done my part) and positive feelings of reconciliation (The
brand has changed) may cause an outburst of positive affect to the brand. This emotional re -set can be in
the form of high loyalty or even brand promotion.
From perspective of Hlavova (2025), boycotting and rebounding might be a form of reaffirmation of
identity and social authentication.  Boycotts are often identity based (I am with my group). Once the rules
of society have changed and the boycott no longer has steam, people are interested in adjusting to the
outside society. The re -buying is an act of re -establishing individual identity as fair-minded or pragmatic.
This normalization is further promoted by social proof, which is the witnessing of peers coming back and
this promotes less fear of judgement.
As for Matta et al. (2025), it can be argued that the mechanism of boycott  and rebound is a form of
habit restoration and perceived equity.  A great deal of boycotts is momentary disturbance of a customary
way of doing things. After removing the external pressure, the original brand is easily recalled by the
consumers with familiarity. Moreover, other people view the boycotting term as punishment itself, and as
a justification to feel the equity and fairness, which is necessary to receive renewed patronage.
According to ElGammal (2025), western brands have frequently been boycott ed in the Middle East
during political crises (e.g. 2023 -2024 Gaza -related boycotts of Starbucks, McDonald's, Coca -Cola), but
have usually reversed themselves quickly with diplomatic overtures or media exhaustion. Apparently, the
same requests were observe d following the recent tensions between the U.S. and China in 2018 -2021, as
consumers flocked to buy Apple and Nike again. These rebounds are not accidental; they have certain

Methods and Material
Methodological Approach
We have adopte d the quantitative methodology in order to collect primary data from a large sample
size.

Population and Sampling
Population of study consisted of Jordanian consumers during the fiscal year 2024 -2025. A convenient
sample of (500) individuals was chosen to represent study’s population. After application process, we
were able to retrieve (370) properly filled questionnaires which indicated a response rate of (74%) as
statistically accepted ratio. We have adopted the Helsinki Ethical Principles noting that the study was
non-clinical research, but we have emphasized voluntary participation, informed consent, confidentiality,
anonymity, minimal risk, and the right to withdraw at any time for participants.

Tool of Study
A questionnaire was built specifically for current study depending on previous studies including (Al -
Mansoori and Collins, 2025; Jedicke et al., 2025; Snyders, 2024; Mulyono and Rolando, 2025). The
questionnaire was built of Likert 5 -point scale and consisted of two main sections. The first too k into
perspective demographics including (gender, occupation, income, qualification), while the other section
consisted of statements related to study’s variables (Political Tension Easing and Rebound Purchase
Decisions). We have checked validity of the q uestionnaire through arbitration by scholars and academics
in the field. While Cronbach’s Alpha test (α) was used in order to check reliability and consistency of
questionnaire items. Based on table 1, it was noticed that the questionnaire was reliable as Cronbach’s

Discussion
Current study aimed to examine the psychological processes that result in rebound purchases
following the de -escalation of political tensions, through a quantitative approach that is based on a
questionnaire to be self -administered by consumers in Jordan. Total of 370 individuals responded to the
online questionnaire and SPSS was used to deal with the collected primary data. The study hypothesized
that “political tension easing might be able to cause a rebound purchase decision achieving the buyback
post bo ycott”. Results of analysis indicated a high correlation between the independent and dependent
variables. 78.3% of the variation in the dependent variable was accounted for by the independent
variables, this managed to accept the presented hypothesis.
Among the chosen sub -variables, it was noticed that all adopted variables were influential. The
highest of them was dissonance reduction scoring B= .542. Dissonance reduction is crucial in the
rebounding process, since it helps in reducing the internal tensio n experienced by the consumers after
boycotting a preferred brand. According to the cognitive dissonance theory (1957) by Festinger, boycotts
cause incongruence due to incongruent attitudes (e.g., I love this product but hate the politics). With the
tensions setting in, consumers justify their coming back by post hoc reasoning such as the problem has
been resolved or my boycott has been heard. This lowers the dissonance, which buyers will be anxious to
repurchase, usually with greater enthusiasm to compensa te excessively on the lost time. Results agreed
with Jedicke et al. (2025) who argued that with the Middle East boycotts of Western brands in 2023 -2024,
the conflict was easy to reduce, and consumers did not feel as guilty, which resulted in a swift sales
recovery of companies such as Starbucks.
The second rank was scored by emotional reconciliation with B= .229. Emotional reconciliation is the
reason behind a rebound purchase as it helps consumers overcome the negative feelings of the boycott
(anger, betrayal) by substituting it with positive feelings once the tension is removed. This process entails
a catharsis of emotions, with the end of political conflict being the outlet of frustration, which gives way to
nostalgia or a fresh love to the brand. The bra nd can be forgiven by consumers and buyback can be seen
as a form of personal reconciliation. To illustrate, following diplomatic thaws in the EU -Russia relations
following the sanctions boycotted Russian products were rebounds as consumers came to terms w ith their
feelings, linking repurchase with relief and reinstatement of normality, increasing loyalty even higher
than before the boycott. Such results agreed with Snyders (2024).
As for the third rank, it was scored by conflict resolution with B= 0.044. T he resolution of conflicts in
the context of reversing the boycott to buyback serves as a mediator of recovering the lost harmony
between the values of the consumers and the brand that is being boycotted. When political conflict
between countries eases whe ther via diplomatic negotiations, apologies or modification of policies, the
consumers feel that the conflict has solved and the ethical aspect of buying is no longer a hindrance. This
form of psychological closure enables the individual to redefine the br and in their consumption behavior
without being hypocritical. Such results was agreed on by Al -Mansoori and Collins  (2025) who noted that
when U.S.-China trade tensions were easing, the tariffs were eased, which became a sign that things were
alright and many boycotters planned to buy American tech products again as a sign that the brand was
redeemed and fitted their renewed worldview.
The least influential factor of all was social proof normalization with B= -0.093. Social proof
normalization makes it eas ier to rebound through adjusting the individual behavior with the activated
changes in group norms as political tensions deescalate. Based on the principle of social proof of Cialdini
(1984), in the case of boycotts, the consumers follow the social pressur e and act in order to evade social
ostracism. Once the tensions fizzle and society reestablish the relations (e.g., by media or influencers
telling them that it is now safe to buy), the herd mentality turns the other way round: the sight of others
returning to their purchases will give them a sense that they are safe, and no longer be afraid of being
judged. This type of normalization hastens the buyback since consumers join the crowd as a means of
reentering the social world. Mulyono and Rolando (2025) agr eed on such results adding that following
India-China border-related tensions, tensions calmed down, which resulted in normalized social proof, as
viral posts stimulated people to buyback Chinese electronic products, which saw market surges.

1. Conclusion and Recommendations
The study has a number of weaknesses that it suffers despite its contributions. First, there is the risk of
having response bias due to the use of self -reported questionnaire data whereby what respondents say
they intend to do is not necessarily what they are actually doing. Second, the cross -sectional design only
captures the consumer perceptions in one period thus incapable of tracking the development in rebound
behavior as long as the political conditions are continuously changing. Third, the study also concentrated
on the consumers in Jordan and this limits the generalization of the results to other cultural, political, or
economic contexts. Also, although the research has studied the most important psychological variables,
other important variables that  might have been included brand trust recovery strategies, media framing,
or personal political ideology.
The aforementioned limitations could be solved in approaching future research by implementing
longitudinal designs to monitor changes in consumer atti tudes and purchasing behavior before, at, and
after the political tensions are deescalating. The application of experimental or behavioral data, e.g. real
purchase records, would enhance causal inference. The role of cultural norms and political sensitivit y in
rebound purchasing in various regions could also be studied comparatively across countries. In addition,
moderating factors, including brand communication strategy, consumer nationalism, or social media
impact, could be incorporated into future resear ch to create a more acceptable perspective of post -boycott
consumption process. These extensions would enrich the theoretical perspective and offer practical advice
to companies that do business in politically sensitive markets.
