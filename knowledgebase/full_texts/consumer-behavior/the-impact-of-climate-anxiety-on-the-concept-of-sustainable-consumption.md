This chapter examines the relationship between climate anxiety and sustainable
consumption behavior within marketing and consumer behavior research. Using
document analysis and content analysis methods, empirical studies published
between 2021 and 2025 were qualitatively reviewed. The selected studies were
classified according to research design, measurement instruments, and variable roles.
Findings indicate that climate anxiety is increasingly examined as a behavioral and
mechanism-based variable in marketing literature rather than solely as a
psychological outcome. The relationship between climate anxiety and sustainable
consumption is not linear. Moderate levels of climate anxiety may enhance
environmental awareness and sustainable consumption, whereas excessive anxiety
may lead to hopelessness, avoidance, psychological exhaustion, and behavioral
inaction. Psychological resilience and self-efficacy can strengthen the transformation
of climate anxiety into constructive environmental behavior, while brand trust may
mediate this relationship. Greenwashing perceptions may weaken consumers’
willingness to translate environmental concerns into sustainable purchasing
decisions. The literature also reveals methodological limitations, including the
predominance of cross-sectional designs, self-report measures, and samples
consisting mainly of university students. Overall, sustainable consumption should be
understood as a multidimensional process shaped by psychological resources,
consumer perceptions, brand-related factors, and contextual conditions. The chapter
contributes to marketing literature by integrating behavioral perspectives and
highlights the need for longitudinal and cross-cultural research and integrated
models incorporating psychological resources and brand-related mechanisms across
diverse consumer groups.

1. Introduction
In recent years, climate change has evolved beyond being merely an
environmental issue into a multidimensional sustainability crisis encompassing
social, economic, and psychological dimensions. Rising global temperatures, sea-
level rise, the increasing frequency of extreme weather events, and biodiversity loss
demonstrate that climate change affects not only natural ecosystems but also
individuals’ lifestyles, consumption habits, and decision-making processes [1, 2].
This transformation has positioned environmental sustainability as a strategic area of
inquiry not only for policymakers and businesses but also in relation to consumers’
everyday choices.
Individuals’ attitudes and behaviors toward climate change are closely associated
with the level of environmental awareness within society. As awareness of
environmental sustainability has increased, businesses have increasingly adopted
environmentally responsible production and marketing practices, while consumers
have begun to place greater emphasis on environmental impacts in their purchasing
decisions [3]. Consequently, the marketing literature has devoted growing
attention to the behavioral consequences of consumers’ psychological responses to
environmental issues. In particular, climate anxiety has emerged as one of the most
significant psychological determinants of sustainable consumption behavior in
recent years [4, 5].
Climate anxiety is defined as a psychological construct encompassing the
cognitive, emotional, and behavioral responses individuals develop in reaction to
perceived threats associated with climate change and environmental degradation
[6]. The literature suggests that moderate levels of climate anxiety can enhance
environmental awareness and motivate individuals to engage in positive
consumption practices, such as energy conservation, recycling, the preference for
sustainable products, and environmentally friendly purchasing behaviors [7, 8].
However, when climate anxiety reaches excessive levels, it may lead to adverse
outcomes, including hopelessness, avoidance, denial, psychological exhaustion, and
behavioral disengagement, thereby weakening sustainable consumption behaviors
[5, 9]. Accordingly, the influence of climate anxiety on sustainable consumption
should not be viewed as a linear relationship; rather, it represents a multidimensional
process shaped by individuals’ psychological resources, environmental knowledge,
and the broader social context in which they operate.
Sustainable consumption refers to conscious consumption behaviors that
promote the conservation of natural resources, minimize environmental impacts,
and take into account the needs of future generations [10]. This approach extends
beyond the purchase of environmentally friendly products to encompass a holistic,
life cycle–oriented perspective that includes the production, use, and post-
consumption stages of products [11]. Therefore, explaining sustainable consumption
requires consideration not only of economic and demographic variables but also of
individuals’ perceptions of environmental risks and their underlying
psychological processes.
Although the number of studies investigating the relationship between climate
anxiety and sustainable consumption has increased rapidly in recent years, the
existing literature has largely focused on specific variables or isolated behavioral
outcomes. In particular, within the marketing literature, there remain a limited
number of systematic reviews synthesizing studies that examine climate anxiety

relationship between climate anxiety and sustainable consumption, to identify
existing research gaps, and to propose theoretical as well as practical implications for
future marketing research [2, 12–15].
2. Conceptual framework
2.1 Definition and psychosocial dimensions of climate anxiety
Climate anxiety refers to the worry, fear, and distress that individuals experience
in response to climate change and environmental degradation. This form of anxiety
encompasses not only concerns about environmental threats but also feelings of
uncertainty and fear regarding the future. It is an emotional state that heightens
individuals’ awareness of environmental problems; however, this increased
awareness often coincides with a greater psychological burden [4]. Increasingly
recognized in contemporary literature, climate anxiety is also referred to as eco-
anxiety. Growing individual concerns about global climate change may encourage
the development of environmentally conscious behaviors and sustainable lifestyles
[5]. Nevertheless, when climate anxiety becomes excessive, it may lead to adverse
psychological outcomes. High levels of anxiety have been associated with depression,
anxiety disorders, and emotional exhaustion [6, 9].
Individuals’ responses to climate anxiety manifest not only at the cognitive level
but also at the emotional level. Cognitively, climate anxiety can enhance individuals’
knowledge and awareness of climate change. This heightened awareness may
encourage people to reflect more deeply on environmental issues, gain a better
understanding of these challenges, and consequently adopt more sustainable
lifestyles. Emotionally, however, climate anxiety may evoke feelings of fear and
hopelessness. Rather than promoting environmentally responsible behavior, these
emotions may intensify the psychological burden of environmental threats, leading
to passivity, inaction, or emotional exhaustion [8].
Research in social psychology indicates that climate anxiety is not merely an
individual response but also contributes to the development of collective awareness
at the societal level. In particular, social pressures and media influence encourage
individuals to share their environmental concerns, facilitating the widespread
diffusion of climate anxiety and fostering a more collective approach to addressing

channel their anxiety into constructive action. Effective management of climate
anxiety at both the psychological and societal levels is considered essential for
promoting sustainable behaviors [18]. Addressing the psychological consequences of
climate anxiety is a critical step toward encouraging environmentally responsible
habits. Consequently, climate anxiety should be understood not only as an individual
experience but also as a matter of collective responsibility [19]. Transforming
climate anxiety into collective action can foster environmentally conscious
communities and strengthen sustainable consumption behaviors.
2.2 The concept of sustainable consumption
Sustainable consumption is defined as the conscious planning of lifestyles and
purchasing behaviors with the aim of reducing negative environmental impacts [10].
This concept encompasses not only the environmental impacts of products but also
the social, economic, and cultural dimensions of consumption patterns.
Sustainable consumption includes a wide range of behaviors, such as the efficient use
of natural resources, waste reduction, energy optimization, and the preference for
environmentally friendly alternatives [10]. These behaviors not only encourage
individuals to assume environmental responsibility but also constitute one of the
fundamental pillars of more equitable and sustainable economic development [2].
In recent years, environmental concerns have become increasingly prominent,
particularly among younger generations. A study conducted in Türkiye found that
climate anxiety positively influences sustainable consumption behaviors and
serves as an important motivational factor in the adoption of environmentally
responsible habits [14]. Similarly, research suggests that young people’s financial
decisions are influenced by climate anxiety, which shapes their sustainability
preferences and encourages environmentally conscious consumption patterns [15].
Green marketing is defined as an approach that integrates traditional marketing
strategies with the principles of environmental sustainability, exerting influence
across a broad spectrum ranging from production processes to consumer behavior
[20, 21]. By offering environmentally friendly products and sustainable alternatives,
green marketing encourages consumers to make more informed choices while
targeting individuals who are sensitive to environmental issues [11]. The growing
significance of green consumption at both the individual and societal levels has been

driven by the rapid expansion of sustainability challenges, increasing environmental
awareness, and the strengthening of ecological consumption practices [22].
Green marketing attracts environmentally conscious consumers by emphasizing
the environmentally friendly attributes of products. In this process, social media and
digital marketing play a crucial role in enhancing environmental awareness and
promoting sustainable consumption [16]. The promotion of environmentally
friendly products enables sustainable alternatives to reach broader audiences.
However, sustainable consumption requires individuals to consider not only
environmental impacts but also economic and social consequences. It has therefore
evolved into a concept closely associated with social and economic justice. In other
words, social equity and economic justice are regarded as equally important as
environmental sustainability. This perspective seeks to ensure both environmental
protection and equal opportunities for different segments of society throughout the
entire product life cycle, from production to consumption. Consequently,
sustainable consumption represents a process that not only advances environmental
sustainability but also strengthens individuals’ sense of social responsibility and
justice [23].
Within this context, it is argued that the production and supply chains of
sustainable products should comply with socially and economically fair as well as
ethical standards. Nevertheless, one of the greatest barriers to sustainable
consumption remains social and economic inequality. In many developing countries,
environmentally friendly products are often sold at premium prices, limiting access
to sustainable consumption options for low-income consumers. Therefore, the
concept of sustainable consumption should not only promote environmentally
responsible consumption habits but also evolve into a development model that
enables all segments of society to benefit equitably [10]. From this perspective,
integrating sustainable consumption with social equity and justice is directly linked
to broader societal development. Furthermore, another important factor influencing
the adoption of sustainable consumption behaviors is individuals’ environmental
concern. Climate anxiety motivates consumers to choose more environmentally
friendly products, and when effectively managed, it can facilitate the transformation
of these preferences into long-term behavioral change [17]. Accordingly, sustainable
consumption can be compatible with economic growth, and these two
phenomena can reinforce one another, contributing to a more balanced and
sustainable model of development [2].
3. Literature review
3.1 The relationship between climate concern and sustainable consumption
The influence of climate anxiety on sustainable consumption has been clearly
demonstrated in numerous studies conducted in recent years [12, 13, 24]. Growing
concern about environmental issues can encourage individuals to prefer
environmentally friendly products and adopt more sustainable lifestyles. A study
conducted among young people in Türkiye found a positive relationship between
climate change anxiety and sustainable consumption behavior [13]. The findings
indicate that as levels of climate anxiety increase, individuals’ interest in

sustainable consumption behaviors.
Another study likewise reported a positive association between climate change
anxiety and sustainable consumption behavior [24]. The findings suggest that
increased awareness of and concern about climate change enhance individuals’
environmental consciousness and strengthen their willingness to adopt sustainable
consumption behaviors. Similarly, climate anxiety has been identified as an
important motivational force that encourages environmentally responsible choices
and promotes sustainable consumption [12].
Climate anxiety also plays a significant role in shaping farmers’ attitudes toward
sustainable agricultural practices and their investment decisions within the
agricultural sector [25]. Research indicates that environmental concern increases
farmers’ willingness to invest in environmentally friendly products and sustainable
farming techniques. Furthermore, climate anxiety has been shown to encourage
the adoption of climate-adaptive technologies in agricultural enterprises while
fostering environmentally responsible consumption practices.
Comparable findings have also been reported in the international literature.
Although eco-anxiety can encourage individuals to adopt environmentally friendly
lifestyles, excessively high levels of anxiety may result in negative psychological
outcomes, including behavioral inaction and emotional exhaustion [6]. These
findings indicate that climate anxiety is not solely a positive motivational force; it
may also generate psychological effects that discourage environmentally responsible
behaviors. Excessive anxiety, in particular, has been associated with emotional
exhaustion and behavioral disengagement [5].
The Honesty-Humility, Emotionality, eXtraversion, Agreeableness,
Conscientiousness, and Openness to Experience (HEXACO) personality dimensions
have also been identified as important factors shaping the relationship between
climate anxiety and sustainable consumption [16]. While climate anxiety generally
strengthens sustainable behaviors, it may simultaneously increase the likelihood of
emotional exhaustion during the process of adopting environmentally responsible
habits. In addition, climate anxiety has been found to enhance sustainable trust and
environmentally responsible consumption behaviors [23]. These findings suggest
that climate anxiety not only influences individual behavior but also encourages
environmentally responsible collective action. Furthermore, studies focusing on
young people indicate that climate anxiety is associated with healthier
environmental choices; however, excessively high levels of anxiety may diminish its
positive influence on sustainable consumption [26].
3.2 Demographic and psychological determinants
The effects of climate anxiety on sustainable consumption are closely associated
with demographic characteristics and individual psychological attributes. The
literature has increasingly examined how climate anxiety varies across different
demographic groups and how, in conjunction with specific psychological
characteristics, it shapes sustainable consumption behaviors. Several studies
suggest that younger age groups are more sensitive to climate anxiety and that this
concern encourages environmentally responsible behaviors. Research conducted
among adolescents and young adults indicates that climate anxiety influences
spending, saving, and investment decisions, making younger individuals more

younger generations are particularly responsive to environmental concerns and
that climate anxiety has a strong influence on the adoption of environmentally
friendly habits.
Similarly, young individuals who exhibit higher levels of environmental concern
are more likely to engage in sustainable consumption behaviors and prefer
environmentally responsible brands [27]. Overall, younger consumers appear to
demonstrate higher levels of environmental awareness and a stronger sense of
environmental responsibility, making them more inclined to adopt environmentally
conscious consumption patterns.
However, climate anxiety is associated not only with demographic characteristics
such as age but also with individuals’ psychological attributes. Although climate
anxiety generally increases the tendency to engage in environmentally responsible
behaviors, the strength of this relationship varies according to factors such as self-
efficacy and environmental knowledge [7, 8]. Individuals with greater
environmental knowledge and stronger beliefs in their ability to address
environmental challenges are more likely to adopt sustainable consumption
behaviors [28]. As people become more knowledgeable about environmental issues,
they tend to make more informed consumption decisions and develop habits aimed
at reducing their environmental impact.
In addition, the literature highlights a strong relationship between psychological
resilience and environmentally responsible behavior. The ability to effectively
manage climate-related anxiety emerges as a key factor influencing sustainable
consumption. Individuals who are better able to cope with environmental concerns
are more likely to develop environmentally conscious consumption habits and
actively choose environmentally friendly products [29]. Likewise, individuals with
high levels of psychological resilience are better equipped to cope with stressors
such as climate anxiety, thereby reinforcing environmentally responsible
behaviors [30].
Climate anxiety encourages individuals to adopt environmentally responsible
behaviors [17]. However, this process is not driven solely by anxiety. Psychological
factors such as emotional intelligence and empathy also play a crucial role in
promoting sustainable behaviors. Individuals’ levels of emotional intelligence and
empathy significantly influence their responses to environmental concerns and are
important determinants of sustainable consumption behavior [31]. These
psychological resources are therefore regarded as key antecedents of
environmentally responsible consumption.
Similarly, environmental awareness strengthens consumers’ brand attitudes and
purchase intentions [32]. Environmentally conscious consumer behavior promotes
sustainable consumption and increases demand for environmentally friendly
products. Positive attitudes toward green brands represent one of the key
psychological drivers of sustainable consumption decisions [33].
In summary, the influence of climate anxiety on sustainable consumption cannot
be explained solely by demographic factors; it is also shaped by individuals’
psychological characteristics and levels of environmental knowledge. The greater
sensitivity of younger generations to environmental concerns suggests that
strengthening environmental awareness and enhancing psychological resilience can
further encourage sustainable consumption behaviors. In this regard, the effective

3.3 Negative psychological effects of climate anxiety and sustainability
Climate anxiety has emerged as an important area of research for understanding
the psychological impacts of environmental change on individuals. The literature
emphasizes that high levels of climate anxiety may lead to adverse psychological
outcomes, including emotional exhaustion, depression, anxiety disorders, and
a decline in overall quality of life [9]. These negative psychological consequences
may, in turn, undermine sustainable consumption behaviors, as excessive anxiety
can generate feelings of burnout and hopelessness that make it more difficult for
individuals to engage in environmentally responsible actions [34]. Elevated levels of
anxiety may also impair individuals’ ability to fulfill their environmental
responsibilities and contribute to passivity in response to environmental
challenges [35].
The psychological consequences of climate anxiety may reduce individuals’
willingness to choose environmentally friendly products and hinder the
development of sustainable consumption habits. Existing research indicates that
when climate anxiety reaches excessive levels, individuals’ motivation to engage
in environmentally responsible behaviors declines, thereby impeding the
adoption of sustainable consumption practices [36]. Studies on eco-anxiety have
similarly shown that individuals experiencing high levels of climate anxiety are
more likely to exhibit environmental inaction, which constitutes
a significant barrier to the development of sustainable consumption
behaviors [35].
The direct effects of excessive anxiety and emotional exhaustion on sustainable
consumption have been consistently emphasized in the literature. When climate
anxiety becomes overwhelming, individuals’ interest in environmentally friendly
products tends to decline, often resulting in environmental inaction [9]. Increasing
anxiety levels can make it more difficult for individuals to fulfill their environmental
responsibilities, fostering feelings of passivity and disengagement [34]. Excessive
anxiety may also diminish individuals’ motivation to seek solutions to environmental
problems, ultimately leading to behavioral inertia in relation to sustainable
consumption.
A study conducted in Türkiye found that climate change anxiety was
generally low among participants, with a considerable proportion reporting little
or no concern about climate change [37]. Such low levels of concern may fail to
provide sufficient motivation for individuals to prefer environmentally
friendly products or develop sustainable consumption habits. The study further
suggests that limited public awareness of climate change in Türkiye contributes
to lower levels of climate anxiety and consequently hinders sustainable
consumption behaviors. Insufficient knowledge about climate change has
likewise been identified as a significant obstacle to the adoption of
environmentally responsible consumption habits.
A study examining the relationship between climate change anxiety and
sustainable consumption behavior among university students reported a positive
association between the two constructs and highlighted the significant role of
environmental education [24]. The findings indicate that strengthening

A growing body of research also suggests that climate anxiety may function as

anxiety management strategies can encourage individuals to engage in

making it more difficult for individuals to adopt environmentally responsible

individuals from fulfilling their environmental responsibilities and limit broader

Accordingly, the literature consistently recommends the development of
psychological support mechanisms and intervention strategies aimed at helping
individuals manage environmental concerns in ways that foster sustainable
consumption. Research on eco-anxiety suggests that anxiety management
strategies represent an effective means of transforming environmental concern
into sustainable action [35]. Furthermore, strengthening psychological support
services, enhancing public awareness, and expanding environmental education
are considered essential for enabling individuals experiencing high levels of
climate anxiety to engage more effectively in environmentally responsible
behaviors [5].
4. Methodological framework and analysis
4.1 Method
This study aims to comprehensively evaluate the relationship between climate
anxiety and sustainable consumption, an increasingly important topic in the
marketing literature from a marketing perspective. The primary focus of the
research is to examine how consumers’ concerns about climate change are reflected
within the field of marketing and how these emotional responses translate into
sustainable purchasing behaviors. To achieve this objective, document analysis,
a qualitative research method, was employed. Document analysis is a data collection
technique that enables the systematic examination of written materials relevant to
the phenomenon under investigation [38].
The collected data were analyzed using content analysis. Content analysis is
defined as “a systematic, replicable, and valid research technique used to draw
valid inferences from textual data based on its content” [39]. Accordingly, the
selected studies were coded according to predetermined analytical categories,
and common patterns, similarities, and differences were comparatively
evaluated [40].
The literature review was conducted in two stages. In the first stage, studies
published between 1994 and 2025 were examined to provide a conceptual and
historical overview of the subject. This broad time frame was selected to evaluate the
historical development of green marketing, sustainable consumption, and
environmentally responsible consumer behavior. In the second stage, only empirical
studies published between 2021 and 2025 were included in the content analysis. This

consumption. Studies published before 2021 were not subjected to content analysis
but were used to support the conceptual and historical background of the
literature review, while studies whose keywords fell outside the concepts addressed
in the content analysis were excluded from the scope of the study. The remaining
studies, published between 2021 and 2025 and directly related to the marketing
perspective, were included in the content analysis.

methodological differences, and emerging research patterns across the existing

The content analysis includes only studies published between 2021 and 2025 that
met the inclusion criteria specified in the methodology section. This period was
selected because it represents the stage during which climate anxiety became
increasingly recognized as an independent research construct within the marketing
and consumer behavior literature and began to be directly associated with
sustainable consumption, green purchasing behavior, brand attitudes, and consumer
decision-making processes.

the broader literature review to establish the conceptual and theoretical foundation
of the chapter. These earlier studies were used primarily to explain the historical
development of green marketing, sustainable consumption, and environmentally
responsible consumer behavior. Accordingly, while the present content analysis
focuses exclusively on recent empirical research, earlier publications contribute to

association between climate anxiety and sustainable consumption.
4.3 Discussion
When the findings of the content analysis are considered collectively, they reveal
a clear transformation in the way climate anxiety has been conceptualized in the
literature. As discussed in the earlier sections of this chapter, early studies –
particularly those published between 2014 and 2018 – primarily examined climate
anxiety in terms of individuals’ perceptions of environmental threats, its
psychological consequences, and emotional responses, while its relationship with
consumer behavior was addressed mainly at a conceptual level. By identifying the
feelings of concern, stress, and uncertainty generated by climate change, these
studies provided an important theoretical foundation for the subsequent
development of behavior-oriented research.
In contrast, the studies included in the present content analysis (published
between 2021 and 2025) directly associate climate anxiety with sustainable
consumption, green purchasing behavior, brand trust, ethical consumption, and
financial decision-making. This trend indicates that, within the marketing literature,
climate anxiety is no longer viewed merely as a psychological outcome but has
increasingly been conceptualized as an important antecedent of consumer behavior.
Furthermore, recent studies suggest that when supported by appropriate cognitive

behaviors [49, 50].
Nevertheless, the literature also demonstrates that the influence of climate
anxiety on sustainable consumption is not consistently linear or uniformly positive.
While several studies report that climate anxiety strengthens environmentally
responsible purchasing behavior, others argue that excessive levels of anxiety may
lead to hopelessness, avoidance, psychological exhaustion, and behavioral inaction
[9, 51]. These contrasting findings suggest that climate anxiety alone does not
determine behavior; rather, its effects depend on individuals’ psychological coping
capacity, perceived self-efficacy, and confidence in their ability to contribute to
solutions.
Similarly, the studies included in the content analysis do not present a unified
perspective regarding the psychological mechanisms underlying this relationship.
Some studies identify environmental awareness and environmental identity as the
primary explanatory variables [52], whereas others highlight the greater importance
of self-efficacy, hope, and perceived personal responsibility [53, 54]. In particular,
individuals with higher levels of self-efficacy appear to be more capable of
translating climate anxiety into environmentally responsible behaviors. These
findings suggest that sustainable consumption should be explained not only by the
level of environmental concern but also by the psychological resources available to
individuals.
A similar pattern emerges in the marketing literature concerning brand trust.
Some studies indicate that trust in sustainable brands constitutes the primary
mechanism through which climate anxiety is translated into green purchasing
behavior, whereas others demonstrate that consumers’ perceptions of greenwashing
may weaken this relationship [55–57]. These findings suggest that sustainable
consumption cannot be explained solely through individual psychological processes;
organizational credibility and the effectiveness of sustainability communication also
play essential roles in shaping consumer behavior.
Another noteworthy finding of the content analysis is that the majority of the
reviewed studies were conducted among Generation Z and university students. This
concentration indicates significant gaps in the literature regarding how climate
anxiety manifests across different age groups, income levels, and cultural contexts.
Moreover, because most studies rely on cross-sectional research designs and self-
report measures, the ability to establish causal relationships among variables remains
limited. Differences in measurement instruments also represent an important
methodological limitation, making direct comparisons across studies more difficult.
Overall, the findings of the content analysis demonstrate that climate anxiety is
increasingly being examined within behavior-oriented and mechanism-based
models in the marketing literature. However, the limited number of studies that
simultaneously incorporate variables such as psychological resilience, self-efficacy,
environmental identity, consumer responsibility, organizational trust, and brand
authenticity within a single structural model highlights an important gap in the
existing literature. Future research should therefore employ integrated theoretical
frameworks that combine psychological and marketing constructs while adopting
longitudinal and cross-cultural comparative research designs. Such approaches
would contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms
through which climate anxiety influences sustainable consumption behavior.

5. Conclusion
The findings of this study demonstrate that climate anxiety has become an
increasingly important construct for explaining sustainable consumption behavior.
However, the relationship between climate anxiety and sustainable consumption is
neither linear nor unidimensional; rather, it is shaped by multiple psychological,
social, and contextual mechanisms. In this respect, the study shows that climate
anxiety should be understood not only as an individual psychological response but
also as a significant marketing construct that influences consumer decision-making
processes.
The studies included in the content analysis indicate that the influence of climate
anxiety on sustainable consumption is largely explained through mediating and
moderating mechanisms such as environmental awareness, brand trust,
environmental identity, self-efficacy, and psychological resilience. These findings
suggest that climate anxiety alone is insufficient to explain sustainable behavior and
should instead be considered in conjunction with individuals’ psychological
resources, environmental knowledge, and broader social context. Indeed, the
literature indicates that under appropriate conditions, climate anxiety can serve as
a powerful motivator for sustainable consumption; however, when it is not
effectively managed, it may become a risk factor that leads to behavioral avoidance,
hopelessness, and inaction.
A substantial proportion of the reviewed studies found that moderate levels of
climate anxiety enhance environmental awareness and strengthen sustainable
consumption tendencies. Research conducted in Türkiye, as well as in other cultural
contexts, demonstrates that climate anxiety has significant effects on sustainable
consumption awareness and environmentally responsible consumer behavior. At the
same time, the content analysis reveals that this relationship is not consistently
observed across all studies. In particular, when supportive mechanisms such as
perceived self-efficacy, psychological resilience, and brand trust are insufficient,
climate anxiety is more likely to result in avoidance, hopelessness, and behavioral
disengagement rather than sustainable action.
Another important finding of this study concerns the methodological limitations
of the existing literature. Most of the reviewed studies rely on cross-sectional
research designs and self-report measures, with samples consisting predominantly of
university students and Generation Z participants. Consequently, the
generalizability of the findings to other age groups, socioeconomic segments, and
cultural contexts remains limited. Furthermore, the use of different measurement
instruments to assess climate anxiety and sustainable consumption behavior
complicates direct comparisons across studies.
From a theoretical perspective, this chapter provides an integrated evaluation by
linking climate anxiety with a range of psychological and marketing constructs
within the sustainable consumption literature. In particular, incorporating variables
such as environmental awareness, brand trust, consumer responsibility,
psychological resilience, and self-efficacy into a unified framework may contribute
to the development of more robust theoretical models capable of explaining the
behavioral consequences of climate anxiety.
From a practical perspective, the findings suggest that businesses and
policymakers should move beyond sustainability communication strategies that
focus exclusively on environmental threats. Instead, they should develop

capacity, enhance perceptions of self-efficacy, and build consumer trust. In

the transformation of consumers’ environmental concerns into sustainable
purchasing behaviors. Likewise, environmental education and public awareness
campaigns conducted by governmental institutions and non-governmental
organizations should extend beyond risk communication to include
approaches that empower individuals to adopt solution-oriented environmental
behaviors.
Future research should employ longitudinal and experimental research designs,
expand cross-cultural comparative studies, and include a broader range of age
groups and socioeconomic segments. Furthermore, integrated studies that
simultaneously examine variables such as psychological resilience, self-efficacy,
environmental identity, brand authenticity, consumer responsibility, and
organizational trust within a single structural model would provide a more
comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms through which climate anxiety
influences sustainable consumption behavior. Research developed along these lines
is expected to make significant contributions to both the marketing literature and the
design of sustainable consumption policies.
