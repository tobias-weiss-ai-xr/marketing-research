Abstract
Grounded in dual-process theory and self-efficacy theory, this study examines the associations of
numeracy, financial arithmetic skills, and cognitive–affective mechanisms (analytical thinking, numerical self-
efficacy, and math anxiety) with conscious consumer behavior among vocational college students. Data were
collected from 150 first-year associate degree students through convenience sampling. Hierarchical multiple
regression indicated that numeracy (β = .24, p < .001), financial arithmetic skills (β = .25, p < .001), analytical
thinking (β = .29, p < .001), numerical self-efficacy (β = .23, p < .001), and math anxiety (β = −.26, p < .001)
significantly predicted conscious consumer behavior, with the full model explaining 60% of the variance (Cohen's
f² = 1.49). By integrating numerical and psychological predictors typically examined in isolation, this study
provides an integrated perspective on the cognitive and affective predictors of conscious consumer behavior and
highlights the potential educational importance of numeracy-related competencies for financially informed
decision-making among vocational college students.

Keywords: Conscious consumer behavior, financial arithmetic, math anxiety, numeracy, vocational education

1. INTRODUCTION
In everyday life, consumers increasingly encounter decisions involving complex
numerical information, including price promotions, discounts, unit prices, product quantities,
and total costs. The proliferation of digital consumption environments has further increased the
need to evaluate multiple pieces of numerical information simultaneously. However, consumers
do not always process such information systematically, particularly when their numerical skills
are limited (Kleber et al., 2025; Peters, 2012). This may increase vulnerability to misleading
pricing strategies, framing effects, and economically disadvantageous purchasing decisions.
Consumer decision-making can therefore be viewed not only as an economic act but
also as a cognitive process involving numerical interpretation and reflective evaluation.
According to dual-process theory, decision-making involves both fast, intuitive processing
(System 1) and slower, deliberate processing (System 2; Kahneman, 2011; Stanovich & West,
2000). Decisions requiring price comparisons, discount calculations, or evaluation of
alternative purchasing options are particularly likely to require deliberate analytical processing.
Consistent with this perspective, greater cognitive reflection has been associated with more
informed economic choices (Frederick, 2005).
Financial literacy is widely recognized as an important competency for informed
financial behavior and well-being (Lusardi & Messy, 2023; Lusardi & Mitchell, 2014), and
evidence from Türkiye similarly indicates its relevance to financial behavior (Aren & Zengin,
2016; Bilici & Cevik, 2023). Yet financial literacy research has predominantly emphasized
knowledge and attitudes, while giving comparatively less attention to the numerical operations
involved in everyday consumption decisions (Goyal & Kumar, 2021). Numeracy—the ability
to understand, compare, and integrate numerical information into decision-making—is
particularly relevant in this respect (Cokely et al., 2012). Numeracy influences risk
comprehension, susceptibility to framing effects, and responses to numerical information
(Peters, 2012; Reyna et al., 2009). Everyday purchasing decisions, however, also require
applied financial arithmetic skills, such as calculating discounts, comparing unit prices, and
estimating total costs.

Numerical competence alone may not ensure its effective use in real-life decisions. Math
anxiety can encourage avoidance of numerical information and reliance on simplifying
heuristics (Ashcraft, 2002), and recent evidence suggests that it can influence consumer
decisions through price-framing processes (Andersen et al., 2024). Conversely, numerical self-
efficacy—the confidence individuals have in their numerical capabilities—may increase their
willingness to engage with numerical information (Bandura, 1997; Peters & Shoots-Reinhard,
2022). Analytical thinking may likewise facilitate deliberate rather than intuitive evaluation of
numerical alternatives. Thus, financially informed consumer behavior may depend not only on
numerical competence but also on the cognitive and affective conditions under which that
competence is deployed.
Despite these advances, numerical skills and cognitive–affective mechanisms have
often been examined separately, leaving limited evidence regarding their simultaneous and
relative associations with consumer behavior within an integrated model (Goyal & Kumar,
2021). The present study addresses this gap by jointly examining general numeracy, applied
financial arithmetic skills, analytical thinking, numerical self-efficacy, and math anxiety. In
doing so, the study distinguishes between general numerical understanding and context-specific
computational competence while simultaneously considering the cognitive and affective
mechanisms that may facilitate or constrain their use.
Vocational college students provide a particularly relevant context for examining these
relationships. As students preparing for relatively rapid transitions into employment, they
simultaneously face everyday purchasing and budgeting decisions, often under constrained
financial resources. Their educational trajectories are also strongly oriented toward applied and
occupational competencies, making the translation of numerical knowledge into practical
decision-making especially relevant. Consumer decisions therefore provide an ecologically
meaningful context in which to examine whether numerical competencies are accompanied by
the cognitive and affective resources needed to use them effectively.
In the present study, conscious consumer behavior refers to deliberate, information-based,
and economically informed purchasing decisions involving such processes as price comparison,
discount interpretation, total-cost estimation, and evaluation of purchasing alternatives.
Accordingly, the purpose of this study is to examine how vocational college students’
numeracy, financial arithmetic skills, analytical thinking, numerical self-efficacy, and math
anxiety are associated with conscious consumer behavior within an integrated theoretical
framework. The study contributes to the existing literature in three respects. First, it integrates
numerical competencies and cognitive–affective mechanisms that have typically been
examined separately, allowing their relative contributions to consumer behavior to be evaluated
within the same model. Second, it distinguishes general numeracy from applied financial
arithmetic, thereby examining whether broad numerical understanding and context-specific
computational skills make distinct contributions to conscious consumer behavior. Third, it
extends this line of research to vocational college students, an educational population that
remains comparatively underrepresented in the financial literacy literature despite the practical
relevance of numerical decision-making to their transition into employment and everyday

financial life. Together, these contributions provide a more integrated, cognitive-processing-
centered perspective on financially informed consumer behavior.
1.1. Theoretical Framework: Dual-Process Theory and Self-Efficacy Theory
This study draws primarily on dual-process theory to explain conscious consumer
behavior, with self-efficacy theory incorporated as a complementary theoretical component.
Kahneman's (2011) dual-process theory proposes that individuals employ two qualitatively
distinct cognitive systems in decision-making: the intuitive System 1 and the analytic System
2 (Stanovich & West, 2000). Conscious consumer behavior requires the active engagement of
System 2—that is, systematic evaluation based on numerical information rather than intuitive
cues. In purchasing contexts, System 1 may support rapid judgments based on salient cues such
as discount labels, attractive price endings, or promotional messages. However, such cues may
not always correspond to actual economic advantage. System 2 processing is therefore required
when consumers need to calculate actual savings, compare unit prices, or evaluate the long-
term cost of installment-based purchases. In this sense, conscious consumer behavior may be
conceptualized as a consumption-related manifestation of reflective and analytical processing
rather than intuitive or heuristic-based decision-making. In this context, numeracy and financial
arithmetic skills represent the cognitive resources necessary for System 2 to function
effectively.
Within this framework, numeracy and financial arithmetic represent related but
distinguishable forms of numerical competence. Numeracy refers to the broader capacity to
understand and interpret numerical information, whereas financial arithmetic reflects the
applied ability to perform calculations in everyday financial and consumption contexts. This
distinction is important because consumers may understand numerical information in general
but still struggle with specific operations such as percentage discounts, unit-price comparisons,
or installment-cost calculations.
Bandura's (1997) self-efficacy theory explains the process by which cognitive
competencies are translated into behavior. Individuals' confidence in their competencies within
a specific domain (numerical self-efficacy) increases the likelihood that those competencies
will be expressed in actual behavior (Peters & Shoots-Reinhard, 2022). In consumption
contexts, numerical self-efficacy may determine whether individuals are willing to engage with
numerical information rather than avoid it. A student who believes that they can calculate
discounts or compare prices may be more likely to examine promotional claims critically,
whereas a student with low numerical self-efficacy may rely more heavily on intuitive cues or
external suggestions. Math anxiety operates in the opposing direction, functioning as an
affective barrier that inhibits the deployment of existing skills (Ashcraft, 2002). Math anxiety
may consume cognitive resources, increase avoidance of calculation-based tasks, and
encourage simplified decision rules in situations involving prices, percentages, or installment
options. Therefore, its role is not limited to academic mathematics; it may also influence
everyday consumer decisions that require numerical engagement. Within this framework,
numeracy and financial arithmetic skills represent individuals' numerical processing capacity,
while analytical thinking denotes the cognitive disposition that enables this capacity to be used

effectively. Numerical self-efficacy facilitates the translation of these processes into behavior,
while math anxiety constrains this mechanism.
Importantly, possessing numerical competencies does not necessarily guarantee their
effective use during consumer decision-making. Individuals may possess adequate
computational skills yet avoid engaging with numerical information due to anxiety, low
confidence, or heuristic processing tendencies. Therefore, conscious consumer behavior may
emerge not merely from cognitive competence itself, but from the interaction between
numerical capacity and motivational–affective mechanisms.
1.2. Hypotheses and Model Rationale
The research model (Fig. 1) examines the direct effects of numeracy, financial arithmetic
skills, and cognitive–affective mechanisms on conscious consumer behavior. Consumers with
higher numeracy are expected to process numerical product information more accurately,
compare alternatives more systematically, and resist misleading numerical cues more
effectively. Drawing on the experimental findings of Kleber et al. (2025) and the theoretical
framework of Cokely et al. (2012), the following hypothesis was proposed:
H1: Numeracy level will positively and significantly predict conscious consumer
behavior.
While numeracy reflects general numerical understanding, financial arithmetic skills
capture the applied calculation capacity required in everyday consumption situations. Because
conscious consumer behavior often involves discount calculations, unit-price comparisons, and
installment evaluations, financial arithmetic is expected to make an independent contribution
beyond general numeracy. Drawing on Comerford's (2025) findings regarding the independent
effect of arithmetic ability on economic judgment accuracy, the following hypothesis was
proposed:
H2: Financial arithmetic skills will positively and significantly predict conscious
consumer behavior.
Beyond numerical competencies themselves, the cognitive and affective conditions
under which individuals process numerical information may also shape consumer behavior.
Analytical thinking may increase the likelihood of deliberate information processing, numerical
self-efficacy may support engagement with numerical tasks, and math anxiety may inhibit such
engagement. Drawing on dual-process theory (Kahneman, 2011; Stanovich & West, 2000) and
self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1997; Peters & Shoots-Reinhard, 2022), the following
hypotheses were proposed:
H3a: Analytical thinking will positively and significantly predict conscious consumer
behavior.
H3b: Numerical self-efficacy will positively and significantly predict conscious
consumer behavior.

2. METHOD
2.1. Research Design
This study employed a cross-sectional and correlational quantitative research design.
The research model is presented in Fig. 1. Hierarchical multiple regression was used to examine
the incremental contribution of predictor groups to conscious consumer behavior. Predictors
were entered hierarchically in three steps: control variables were entered first (gender, age, and
perceived economic status), followed by core cognitive variables (numeracy and financial
arithmetic skills), and then cognitive–affective mechanisms (analytical thinking, numerical
self-efficacy, and math anxiety). Gender, age, and perceived economic status were included as
control variables because demographic and socioeconomic characteristics may influence
consumer decision-making tendencies.

Figure 1. Research model for the study
2.2. Participants
The study sample comprised 150 first-year associate degree students enrolled in six
different programs at a vocational college affiliated with a state university. Convenience
sampling was employed. Vocational college students constitute a theoretically relevant
population for examining numerically informed consumer behavior due to their active
engagement in daily consumption decisions under relatively constrained financial conditions.
The participants were enrolled in Office Management (n = 31, 20.7%), Accounting and
Tax (n = 30, 20.0%), Marketing (n = 28, 18.7%), Foreign Trade (n = 22, 14.7%), Logistics (n
= 20, 13.3%), and Banking and Insurance (n = 19, 12.7%). Thus, the sample included students
from multiple vocational fields rather than being concentrated in a single academic program.

171

Of the participants, 52.7% were female (n = 79), 42.7% were male (n = 64), and 4.7%
declined to specify their gender (n = 7). Regarding age, 41.3% were in the 17–19 range, 42.7%
in the 20–22 range, 11.3% in the 23–25 range, and 4.7% were 26 or older.
An a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2009) with a
medium effect size (f² = .15), 9 predictors, α = .05, and 1 − β = .80 yielded a minimum required
sample size of 122. The present sample size (N = 150) exceeds this threshold. For regression
analyses, gender was dummy coded (female = 1, male = 0); participants who responded “prefer
not to say” (n = 7) were excluded.
2.3. Measures
Numeracy Test.  Adapted from the Berlin Numeracy Test (Cokely et al., 2012) and
Schwartz et al. (1997), this test comprised 8 multiple-choice items assessing ratio, probability,
and percentage calculation. Item difficulty indices ranged from .19 to .64, and corrected item-
total correlations from .26 to .35 (KR-20 = .62). A range of .60–.70 is generally considered
acceptable for objective performance tests (Hair et al., 2019).
Financial Arithmetic Skills Test. Developed following Lusardi and Mitchell (2014) and
Comerford (2025), this test consisted of 10 objective items. Item difficulty indices ranged from
.23 to .65, and corrected item-total correlations from .19 to .39 (KR-20 = .59). Although
approaching the lower boundary, this level is generally considered acceptable for objective
performance tests with heterogeneous content (Hair et al., 2019); the possibility of measurement
error should nonetheless be considered.
Cognitive–Affective Mechanisms Scale.  An 18-item, 5-point Likert scale comprising
three subscales: analytical thinking disposition (adapted from Cacioppo & Petty, 1982; 6 items,
α = .93), numerical self-efficacy (adapted from Peters & Shoots-Reinhard, 2022; 6 items, α =
.93), and math anxiety (adapted from Hopko et al., 2003; 6 items, α = .93). Confirmatory factor
analysis yielded highly similar and satisfactory fit indices across all three subscales (χ²/df ≈
1.00, CFI ≈ .98, RMSEA ≈ .020). The near-identical fit values may be attributed to the short
scale length, unidimensional structure, and sample homogeneity; however, replication with
larger and more diverse samples is recommended to confirm the stability of these results.
Conscious Consumer Behavior Scale. A 10-item, 5-point Likert scale was developed to
assess deliberate, information-based, and economically informed purchasing tendencies in
everyday consumption contexts. Item development was guided by rational consumer behavior
theory (Simon, 1955), price perception literature (Lichtenstein et al., 1993), and the theory of
planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991). Higher scores indicate greater conscious consumer behavior.
Although the internal consistency coefficient was high (α = .97), the scale was designed to
capture multiple but theoretically interconnected aspects of conscious consumer behavior.
Exploratory factor analysis supported a unidimensional structure for the scale. The Kaiser–
Meyer–Olkin measure indicated very high sampling adequacy (KMO = .970), and Bartlett's test
of sphericity was significant, χ²(45) = 1580.33, p < .001. A single factor emerged with an
eigenvalue greater than 1 (eigenvalue = 7.73), explaining 78.0% of the total variance. Factor
loadings ranged from .76 to .95 (see Appendix A). Confirmatory factor analysis further

supported a unidimensional structure with satisfactory fit indices (χ²/df = 1.00, CFI = .99,
RMSEA = .020). The relatively high internal consistency and sampling adequacy values may
partly reflect the theoretically coherent nature of the construct and the homogeneity of the
sample.
2.4. Procedure and Ethical Considerations
Data were collected in a single session during regular class hours via an online survey,
following ethics committee approval (No: E.88656144-000-2600144612). Participants were
informed about the study purpose, gave informed consent, and anonymity was maintained
throughout. Procedural remedies recommended by Podsakoff et al. (2003) were also
implemented, including anonymity assurance, psychologically separating scale sections, and
reducing evaluation apprehension. Incomplete or carelessly completed forms were excluded
from analysis.
2.5. Data Analysis
Data were analyzed using SPSS 26. Prior to hierarchical multiple regression, the
following assumptions were verified: (a) normality (Shapiro–Wilk: W = 0.992, p = .577); (b)
linearity confirmed via residual scatterplots; (c) no heteroscedasticity observed; (d) Durbin–
Watson = 1.792 (acceptable range: 1.5–2.5); (e) VIF = 1.11–1.17 (no multicollinearity; Hair et
al., 2019); (f) Cook's Dmax = 0.075—observations examined and retained as they did not alter
model coefficients; (g) Harman single-factor test: first unrotated factor explained 28.4% of
variance (< 50% threshold). Effect size was calculated as Cohen's f² = R²/(1−R²).

4. DISCUSSION
The findings provide an integrated account of conscious consumer behavior in which
numerical competence and the cognitive–affective conditions supporting its use operate
together. Rather than suggesting that informed consumer decisions depend solely on what
students know or can calculate, the overall pattern indicates that such decisions are also
associated with students’ willingness and disposition to engage analytically with numerical
information. This interpretation is consistent with the complementary perspectives of dual-
process theory and self-efficacy theory: numerical competencies provide resources for
deliberate processing, whereas analytical thinking, self-efficacy, and anxiety may influence
whether those resources are actively employed in decision-making situations.
The role of numeracy can be understood through the demands of everyday purchasing
decisions. Comparing prices, interpreting discounts, and evaluating quantities require
consumers to move beyond salient promotional cues and deliberately process numerical
information. From a dual-process perspective, stronger numeracy may facilitate the System 2

175

processing needed to evaluate such information rather than relying primarily on intuitive
judgments (Kahneman, 2011; Stanovich & West, 2000). This interpretation is consistent with
previous evidence showing that numeracy influences how individuals interpret numerical
information and respond to framing effects (Kleber et al., 2025; Peters, 2012). At the same time,
numeracy may partly reflect broader individual differences in cognitive ability, and this
possibility should be considered when interpreting its association with consumer behavior
(Stanovich & West, 2000).
The contribution of financial arithmetic further suggests that understanding numerical
information and being able to apply calculations in context are related but distinct aspects of
financially informed decision-making. Everyday purchasing frequently requires concrete
operations such as calculating actual discounts, comparing unit prices, and determining the total
cost of installment purchases. General numerical understanding may therefore be insufficient
when consumers cannot translate that understanding into context-specific calculations. This
interpretation is consistent with evidence that arithmetic ability contributes to economic
judgment accuracy (Comerford, 2025) and with research emphasizing the importance of
numerical information throughout consumer decision processes (Santana et al., 2020). For
vocational college students, this distinction is particularly relevant because their education
emphasizes applied competencies and their transition into employment may increase the
frequency and importance of independent budgeting and consumption decisions. Financial
arithmetic can therefore be viewed not merely as an academic skill but as an applied
competency connecting classroom learning with everyday economic decisions.
The role of analytical thinking further supports the importance of deliberate engagement
with information. Conscious purchasing requires consumers to question immediately attractive
cues, compare alternatives, and assess whether an apparent economic advantage represents an
actual benefit. Students with a stronger disposition toward analytical processing may therefore
be more likely to examine promotional information critically rather than accept it at face value.
This interpretation is consistent with dual-process accounts of reflective decision-making
(Frederick, 2005; Kahneman, 2011). In the vocational college context, such reflective
processing may have particular practical relevance because students are approaching
occupational and financial independence while simultaneously encountering increasingly
complex consumer environments.
The associations involving numerical self-efficacy and math anxiety also indicate that
possessing numerical skills does not necessarily ensure their use. From a self-efficacy
perspective, students who are more confident in their numerical abilities may be more willing
to engage with calculations and comparisons instead of avoiding them (Bandura, 1997; Peters
& Shoots-Reinhard, 2022). Conversely, math anxiety may consume cognitive resources and
encourage avoidance or simplified decision strategies when numerical processing is required
(Ashcraft, 2002). Its relevance to consumer decisions is also consistent with evidence linking
math anxiety to responses to price framing (Andersen et al., 2024). These mechanisms may be
particularly consequential for vocational college students because many everyday and
occupational decisions require practical numerical engagement even when students do not
perceive themselves as operating within a formal mathematics context. Thus, difficulties

176

associated with mathematics may extend beyond academic performance and influence how
students approach financially consequential decisions in daily life.
Taken together, these findings suggest that conscious consumer behavior among
vocational college students should be understood as an applied decision-making competence
emerging from the combination of numerical capacity, analytical engagement, confidence, and
emotional readiness. This interpretation has particular educational relevance for vocational
education. Students in vocational programs are preparing for relatively direct transitions into
employment and adult financial responsibilities, while their curricula emphasize the practical
application of knowledge. Accordingly, numeracy education in this context may be most
meaningful when numerical skills are embedded in authentic financial situations—such as
comparing unit prices, evaluating discounts, calculating installment costs, and making decisions
within limited budgets—rather than taught only as decontextualized computational procedures.
At the same time, instructional practices should provide opportunities for successful numerical
problem solving and reflective evaluation so that competence development is accompanied by
stronger numerical self-efficacy and reduced avoidance of quantitative information.
The present findings therefore extend the discussion of financial literacy beyond
knowledge acquisition alone. For vocational college students, financially informed behavior
appears to involve both the capacity to process numerical information and the cognitive–
affective readiness to use that capacity. Integrating these dimensions offers a more
comprehensive educational perspective on how vocational education may prepare students not
only for occupational tasks but also for the numerical demands of everyday economic life.
4.1. Limitations
Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes
causal inference; therefore, findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.
Second, convenience sampling from a single university limits generalizability (Etikan et al.,
2016). Third, self-report measurement of cognitive–affective mechanisms introduces potential
social desirability bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Fourth, the reliability coefficients for the
numeracy (KR-20 = .62) and financial arithmetic (KR-20 = .59) tests, while acceptable for
heterogeneous performance tests (Hair et al., 2019), may have attenuated true effects. Fifth,
self-reported consumer behavior may differ from actual purchasing behavior. Replication with
more diverse and nationally representative samples is recommended. Finally, identical CFA fit
values across all three Likert scales (χ²/df = 1.00, CFI = .98, RMSEA = .020), while explainable
by short scale length and sample homogeneity (Hu & Bentler, 1999), should be interpreted
cautiously. In addition, because EFA and CFA were conducted on the same sample due to
sample size limitations, the factorial structure of the conscious consumer behavior scale should
be cross-validated in independent samples.
5. CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS
5.1. Theoretical Contributions
This study makes three theoretical contributions. First, by simultaneously addressing
numerical skills and psychological mechanisms, it reveals the multidimensional nature of

177

conscious consumer behavior. Second, by demonstrating the independent and significant
contributions of both general numeracy and domain-specific financial arithmetic skills, it
confirms that different levels of numerical competency operate through distinct behavioral
mechanisms. Third, by testing these relationships among vocational college students—a sample
underrepresented in the financial literacy literature—the study provides an empirical
contribution to the financial literacy literature, suggesting that a holistic cognitive processing-
centered perspective may complement knowledge-based approaches in understanding
financially informed consumer behavior.
5.2. Educational Implications
The findings suggest that numeracy-related competencies in vocational education
should be addressed not merely as mathematical abilities but as practical decision-making skills
relevant to students’ everyday financial lives. Accordingly, mathematics, financial literacy, and
related vocational courses may incorporate authentic consumer scenarios requiring students to
compare unit prices, calculate actual discount rates, evaluate installment versus cash-payment
options, estimate total costs, and make purchasing decisions within a predetermined budget.
Such activities would allow students to apply general numeracy and financial arithmetic skills
to situations resembling those they are likely to encounter outside the classroom.
The cognitive–affective findings also suggest that instruction should address how
students engage with numerical information, not only whether they can perform the required
calculations. Activities may therefore be structured with gradually increasing numerical
complexity, opportunities for repeated successful problem solving, and formative feedback
designed to strengthen numerical self-efficacy. To reduce avoidance associated with math
anxiety, instructors may initially use familiar everyday financial situations before progressing
to more complex numerical decisions. Reflective prompts such as asking students to explain
why one purchasing option is more advantageous than another may also encourage analytical
rather than intuitive decision-making.
5.3. Practical Implications
At the institutional level, vocational colleges may integrate short, applied numeracy and
consumer decision-making modules into existing mathematics, financial literacy, or career-
development courses rather than treating these competencies as a separate theoretical topic.
Such modules could focus on recurring financial decisions relevant to young adults, including
budgeting limited income, evaluating promotional offers, comparing unit costs, calculating
installment-related total costs, and critically assessing price-framing strategies.
Because the participants in the present study were drawn from several vocational
programs, applied activities may also be contextualized to different occupational domains. For
example, marketing-related activities may involve evaluating discounts and promotional
pricing; accounting-related activities may emphasize budgeting and cost calculations; foreign
trade and logistics activities may incorporate price, quantity, and unit-cost comparisons; and
banking and insurance activities may use scenarios involving installment costs and alternative
financial options. These examples should be regarded as context-specific instructional

178

applications derived from the overall findings rather than as evidence of differences between
academic programs, as program-level comparisons were not conducted in the present study.
Vocational colleges may also use brief diagnostic activities to identify students who
experience difficulty or avoidance when working with numerical information and provide
targeted practice or academic support. In this way, consumer education can address both
numerical competence and students’ confidence in applying that competence. At the broader
educational level, incorporating applied numeracy into vocational curricula may help connect
occupational preparation with the everyday financial decisions students are expected to manage
as they transition into employment and greater financial independence.
5.4. Future Research Directions
Future research should: (a) employ longitudinal designs to examine causal relationships;
(b) replicate findings with samples from different universities and geographic regions; (c) use
structural equation modeling to test the indirect effect of math anxiety through numerical self-
efficacy; (d) evaluate the effects of numeracy education through experimental intervention
studies; (e) use behavioral measures based on actual purchase data rather than self-report scales;
and (f) adopt mixed-method designs incorporating qualitative data. More broadly, the findings
support the view that numeracy education may play an important role not only in academic
achievement, but also in preparing students for informed participation in everyday economic
life.
