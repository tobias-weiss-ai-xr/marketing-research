Abstract
The rapid growth of electronic devices has resulted in a global surge of electronic
waste (e-waste), posing critical challenges for environmental sustainability and
public health due to toxic emissions, unsafe handling, and informal recycling
practices. Effective e-waste management is crucial for resource efficiency, advancing
sustainability transitions, supporting the circular economy (CE) practices, reducing
health hazards for consumers and waste workers and achieving the United Nations
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Consumer behavioral drivers play an
important role in shaping safe and sustainable e-waste management outcomes. This
study presents a systematic literature review of 133 peer-reviewed articles (2009–
2025) using the Theory–Context–Characteristics–Methodology (TCCM) framework,
following the PRISMA protocol. The analysis integrates thematic coding and TCCM-
based synthesis to provide a multi-dimensional understanding of consumer behavior
in e-waste management. The findings indicate a strong dominance of intention-
focused recycling studies grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). Key
behavioral drivers such as intention, attitude, subjective norms, awareness, and
perceived behavioral control are widely studied and are associated with participation
in recycling and disposal practices. However, the persistence of the intention–
behavior gap suggests that structural constraints, including limited infrastructure,
convenience issues, and privacy concerns, may influence behavioral outcomes. The
literature is predominantly concentrated in Asia and relies heavily on quantitative
methods particularly Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), with limited cross-cultural
and longitudinal research. The review also highlights that while consumer behavior
is increasingly linked to circular economy practices and SDG targets, explicit
integration with public health considerations remains underexplored. By triangulating
thematic and TCCM findings, the study identifies key theoretical, contextual, and
methodological gaps and proposes a future research agenda that emphasizes
interdisciplinary approaches, digital dimensions, and system-level interventions.

 1 Introduction
Rapid technological developments, in conjunction with the rising consumer desire for
advanced technology products, has resulted in a surge in e-waste quantity [ 1–3]. Fur-
thermore, shorter useful life of electronics items [ 4, 5], the rapid shift in consumer pref -
erences, the quick decline in selling prices and the rapid growth of the economy have all
contributed to the frequent replacement of older electronic items with newer models
that have shorter lifespans [6, 7].
The United Nations (UN) Global e-waste monitor 2024 reports that the e-waste gen -
eration rate is rapidly increasing annually [ 8]. Year 2022 recorded the generation of 62
million metric tons (Mt) of e-waste and further e-waste generation is projected to reach
74.7 Mt by the year 2030 [ 9]. According to the annual report of Central Pollution Con -
trol Board (CPCB), 1.61 Mt of electronic waste was generated in India in the financial
year 2021–2022, of which 0.53 Mt was collected and processed.
It is believed that the term “e-waste” first appeared in the 1990s. Although the exact
origin of the term is difficult to determine, the idea came about in response to the grow -
ing amount of electronic items that were being thrown away and the problems that their
disposal was posing to the environment [10].
e-waste requires proper handling since it contains both hazardous and useful materi -
als in it [11–13]. Among the harmful elements present in the e-waste includes cadmium,
arsenic,  lead,  mercury,  polyvinyl chloride, and several brominated flame-retardant
classes [14–16]. Sustainability and circular economy principles emphasize resource effi -
ciency, waste minimization, and the reintegration of materials into productive cycles
[17].
Consumers produce e-waste and then decide where it ends up [ 18, 19], making their
behavior central to efficient e-waste management [ 20]. Decisions to store, donate, resell,
or recycle directly shape material flows and determine whether devices are reintegrated
into the circular economy or contribute to unsustainable waste streams but it won't be
able to attain their maximum potential in terms of effectiveness and efficiency without
enough public awareness, behavior and public involvement [21–25].
Although several studies examine consumer behavior in e-waste management, exist -
ing reviews often emphasize isolated aspects such as recycling or remanufacturing
intentions without explicitly linking them to sustainability, circular economy, or SDG
frameworks. To fill this gap, this study adopts the TCCM framework for systematically
synthesizing articles to assess theoretical applications, contextual dynamics, behavioral
characteristics, and methodological approaches.
This study offers several novel contributions. First, it integrates thematic analysis with
the Theory–Context–Characteristics–Methodology (TCCM) framework, enabling a
multi-dimensional synthesis of consumer behavior in e-waste management. Second,
unlike prior reviews that focus primarily on recycling behavior, this study connects con -
sumer behavioral drivers with public health perspectives, circular economy principles
strategies and public health perspectives can support the development of more
effective and sustainable e-waste management systems.

2 Theoretical background
2.1 Definition of e-waste
  • e-waste or WEEE, as defined by the Basel Convention of 2002, is any electrical or
electronic device that is determined to be a waste, including all of the equipment's
component parts, sub-assemblies, and consumables at the time the equipment was
declared obsolete [26].
  • In accordance with the United Nations Environment Protection (UNEP) e-waste
management manual (2007), end-of-life products, also known as e-waste, are
electrically powered devices that require electromagnetic fields or currents to
function but are either non-functional or obsolete.
  • e-waste (Management) Rules, 2022, Government of India defines e-waste as electrical
and electronic equipment waste, including solar photovoltaic modules, panels, or
cells, either in its entirety or in parts or components that consumers discard as waste
or that manufacturers, refurbishers, or repairers reject from their processes [27].
Summarizing the above definitions, e-waste means abandoned electronics items or gad -
gets including its parts etc. that are no longer wanted or have reached their end of life,
and which are generated at any stage of their life cycle.

2.2 Circular economy and sustainable development goals
The concept of the circular economy (CE) has emerged as a transformative framework
for addressing resource scarcity, environmental degradation, and waste generation. CE
emphasizes closed-loop systems that promote reuse, recycling, remanufacturing, and
material recovery.
In the context of e-waste, circular economy principles aim to extend product lifecy -
cles, reduce material leakage, and enhance resource efficiency. Consumer behavior plays
a pivotal role in enabling these circular flows, particularly through decisions related to
reuse, repair, resale, and recycling of electronic products.
The management of e-waste is closely aligned with several United Nations Sustainable
Development Goals (SDGs). Most notably:
  • SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production)
  • SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities)
  • SDG 13 (Climate Action)
Additionally, it also indirectly supports SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) by reduc -
ing exposure to hazardous substances, and SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastruc -
ture) through the development of sustainable recycling technologies.
However, achieving these goals depends significantly on consumer participation. Even
the most advanced recycling infrastructure may remain underutilized if consumers do
not engage in responsible disposal practices. This highlights the importance of under -
standing behavioral drivers within the circular economy framework.
Despite the strong conceptual alignment between CE and SDGs, existing literature
suggests that the integration of behavioral insights with these frameworks remains
limited. This gap underscores the need for integrative approaches, such as the TCCM
framework adopted in this study.
2.3 Public health perspective
E-waste poses significant environmental and public health risks due to the presence of
hazardous materials such as lead, mercury, cadmium, and brominated flame retardants.
Informal recycling practices, particularly prevalent in developing countries, expose
workers and communities to toxic substances, leading to severe health implications
including respiratory disorders, neurological damage, and long-term ecological degra -
dation. Exposure to e-waste toxins has been associated with a range of adverse health
outcomes, neurological damage due to lead and mercury exposure, respiratory disorders
caused by toxic fumes, long-term risks such as cancer and endocrine disruption. Chil -
dren and pregnant women are particularly vulnerable to these health impacts. Moreover,
environmental contamination caused by improper e-waste disposal may lead to bioac -
cumulation of toxins in food chains, further amplifying health risks.
From a behavioral perspective, consumer decisions regarding storage, disposal, and
recycling of electronic products directly influence these public health outcomes. For
instance, prolonged storage of unused devices may delay entry into formal recycling
systems, while improper disposal may increase environmental exposure. Therefore, safe
and sustainable e-waste management is not only an environmental necessity but also a
public health imperative.

3 Research methodology
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) based
protocol [28] was used in this study to ensure methodological transparency and reliabil -
ity in the screening, identification, and inclusion of relevant studies followed by thematic
analysis using standard software i.e. ATLAS.ti along with MS Excel to evolve various
themes in the reviewed literature. To provide a structured and comprehensive analysis,
the TCCM framework was then applied to organize and interpret the findings from the
selected literature.
3.1 Search strategy and reproducibility protocol
The literature search was carried out on 11 October 2025 using two major academic
databases: Scopus and Web of Science Core Collection.
The search strategy was designed to capture studies related to consumer behavior in
e-waste management while maintaining sufficient breadth and relevance. Boolean oper -
ators (“ AND” , “OR”) and multiple keyword combinations were used to retrieve studies
associated with e-waste, recycling behavior, disposal behavior, awareness, intention, and
consumer-related constructs. The search queries applied in the databases are presented
below:

4. Full-text eligibility assessment to ensure conceptual and methodological relevance.

3.3 Quality appraisal of studies
To enhance the methodological rigor and credibility of this systematic review, a struc -
tured quality appraisal was conducted for all included studies. This step ensures that
the synthesis is not only comprehensive but also critically evaluates the reliability and
robustness of the evidence base. Quality appraisal of the included studies was conducted
using three criteria:
(1) Research Design Quality (cross-sectional vs. longitudinal vs. experimental designs,
Clarity of research objectives and hypotheses): Studies employing longitudinal or
experimental designs were considered stronger due to their ability to better capture
causality and temporal relationships, whereas cross-sectional studies were considered
moderate due to inherent limitations in causal inference.
(2) Measurement Validity (use of validated constructs/scales): Studies that employed
well-validated instruments and reported reliability/validity metrics were rated
higher, as they reduce measurement error and improve internal consistency.
(3) Analytical Rigor (use of advanced statistical or qualitative methods): Studies using
advanced techniques with proper validation procedures were considered high
quality, while those relying solely on descriptive statistics or basic regression were
rated lower.

matic analysis of 121 empirical studies, and recurring patterns that influence consumer

framework to all 133 selected studies, providing a detailed examination of theoretical
foundations, contextual variations, key characteristics, identifying key behavioral drivers

4.4.1 Context
Context describes the circumstances that influence the research environment. The con -
textual analysis examines the geographical, sectoral and participant distribution in the
literature, shedding light on the regions, types of e-waste and behavioral aspects that
have actively contributed to the understanding of consumer behavior in e-waste man -
agement. While at the participant level, studies provide valuable perspectives on deci -
sion-making processes.
4.4.1.1 Geographical distribution of literature The geographical analysis indicates that
China has published the highest number of articles which accounts for around 21% of all
the papers, which may be associated with its significant role in global e-waste generation.
India follows China with 17% of all the papers, reflecting high population and tech-savvy
consumers. The other countries that follow them are Indonesia, Pakistan, and UAE, high-
lighting regional variations in research focus and e-waste management priorities.
Continent wise classification indicates Asia has a higher concentration of studies (71%
of publications), which may reflect higher e-waste generation and growing policy atten -
tion in the region, followed by Europe at 16%, largely from developing nations. Research
in developed regions like America and Australia is limited. Also, there is a notable
absence of cross-national or comparative studies that examine how consumer behavior
varies across different cultural, economic, and regulatory contexts.
4.4.1.2 Sectoral distribution of literature Research on e-waste focuses mainly on mobile
phones (35%) and IT products (15%), with consumer electronics like TVs and refrigera -
tors covering 45%. Consumer studies further indicate that storage behavior for consumer
appliances differs from that of mobile phones. While both categories share some behav -
ioral commonalities, their behavioral dynamics can be different, and this aspect remains
underexplored in depth. Small household appliances and other categories are even less
studied, highlighting substantial research gaps and opportunities for future inquiry in
understanding diverse consumer e-waste behaviors.
Research highlights consumer behaviors; recycling, disposal, and storing as central to
e-waste management. Recycling is influenced by awareness, infrastructure, and incen -
tives, while disposal often involves reselling or donating, and storing is driven by data
security or future utility. Consumers often store devices such as mobile phones and lap -
tops at home due to data security concerns, or perceived future utility. While recycling
has been most frequently studied, storing and disposal behaviors require deeper investi -
gation, especially in the context of India, where informal channels dominate.
4.4.1.3 Participant distribution of literature A key contextual factor in e-waste research
is participant type. Most studies focus on end-users with 60–65% targeting household
consumers and 20–25% focusing on younger consumers due to high IT usage and early
technology adoption. Very few studies focus on corporate employees or institution con -
sumers. In India, research is concentrated in metropolitan cities, where income and
device penetration are high, while rural and semi-urban areas remain underrepresented

ing insights are presented to encourage transformative thinking and action.

(ii) Theoretical Concentration and Limited Conceptual Diversity
E-waste research is heavily dominated by the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), often
extended with additional variables. The continued dominance of TPB may be attributed
to its simplicity, predictive capability, and widespread acceptance in behavioral research.
However, this reliance may also limit the exploration of alternative dimensions such as
emotional, moral, and contextual influences. The tendency to extend TPB rather than
adopt new frameworks suggests a path-dependency in research design, where familiar
models are preferred over conceptual innovation.
Future research may benefit from integrating diverse theoretical perspectives, includ -
ing moral norm-based models, behavioral reasoning approaches, and digital behavior
frameworks. Expanding theoretical diversity could provide a more nuanced understand -
ing of consumer decision-making and better capture emerging influences such as tech -
nology adoption and data privacy concerns.
(iii) Contextual Concentration and Limited Global Representation
Research on consumer behavior in e-waste management is predominantly concentrated
in Asia, particularly in countries such as China and India and studies focus on segments
like mobile phones, IT equipment’s e-waste. This concentration may be associated with
higher levels of e-waste generation, rapid technological adoption, and increasing policy
attention in these regions. However, it may also reflect research accessibility and funding
patterns. The limited representation of regions such as Africa, Latin America, and small-
island economies suggests that global behavioral patterns are not fully captured.
Future research should expand geographical coverage and include cross-cultural and
comparative studies. This would improve the generalizability of findings and support the
development of globally relevant e-waste management strategies. Understanding behav -
ioral differences across socio-economic and cultural contexts is essential for designing
inclusive and effective policies. Studies should expand the focus beyond mobile phones

new recycling opportunities.
(iv) Methodological Imbalance and Limited Contextual Depth
E-waste research is characterized by a strong dominance of quantitative, cross-sectional
survey-based studies. This pattern may be influenced by the relative ease of data collec -
tion and analysis using structured questionnaires and statistical tools such as SEM and
regression. However, such approaches may not fully capture the contextual and socio-
cultural factors influencing behavior. The limited use of qualitative and mixed-method
approaches suggests a gap in understanding the deeper motivations, perceptions, and
lived experiences of consumers.
There is a need to adopt more diverse methodological approaches, including quali -
tative, longitudinal, and mixed-method designs. These approaches may provide richer
insights into behavioral dynamics and support the development of context-sensitive
interventions. Greater methodological diversity could also strengthen the empirical
foundation of policy recommendations.
(v) Fragmentation Across Dimensions
The review indicates that studies often examine theory, context, characteristics, and
methodology in isolation rather than as an integrated framework. This fragmentation
may arise from disciplinary silos and the tendency to focus on specific aspects of con -
sumer behavior without considering their interdependencies. For example, behavioral
drivers are often studied independently of contextual constraints or methodological lim-
itations, leading to partial insights.
Adopting integrative frameworks such as TCCM more holistically could enhance the
explanatory power of research, for e.g., combining context-specific characteristics with
novel theories and mixed methods) can yield more actionable knowledge. For example,
linking digital literacy (characteristic) with innovation diffusion theory (theory) using
field experiments (methodology) in rural India (context) could generate unique, practi -
cal insights.
(vi) Digitalization as an enabler of sustainable systems
Emerging factors such as digital literacy, data security, and technological trust are rel -
atively underexplored in the literature. Traditional behavioral models may not fully
account for the role of digital systems and data-related concerns in shaping consumer
decisions. As electronic devices increasingly store personal and sensitive informa -
tion, privacy concerns may influence disposal behavior, leading to storage or informal
handling.
Future research should incorporate digital and technological dimensions into behav -
ioral models. Addressing privacy concerns through secure data destruction mecha -
nisms and transparent recycling systems may enhance consumer trust and participation,
thereby supporting both circular economy goals and public health protection. Such sys -
tems can advance SDG 9 (industry, innovation, and infrastructure) while embedding CE
principles of traceability and extended producer responsibility. Industries need to col -
laborate with technology providers to implement transparent, user-friendly platforms to
strengthen consumer participation.

(vii) Strengthening Policy—Consumer Synergy
Success of regulations such as Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) depends on con -
sumer cooperation and alignment with local behavioral patterns. Currently, many con -
sumers remain unaware of their responsibilities or lack trust in recycling systems. To
support SDG 11 (sustainable cities and communities), e-waste policies should empha -
size inclusivity, transparency, and community-driven approaches. Co-designed policies
where consumers, industries, recyclers, and policymakers collaboratively define roles
and incentives can enhance trust and accessibility of recycling systems. Such collabora -
tive governance can transform cities into hubs of circular innovation, strengthen local
collection and recycling infrastructure, and ensure that urban communities actively con-
tribute to sustainable e-waste management pathways aligned with circular economy and
SDG targets.
(viii) Limited Integration with Public Health Outcomes
While environmental impacts are widely discussed, the explicit linkage between con -
sumer behavior and public health outcomes remains limited. This gap may be due to the
disciplinary focus of existing studies, which often prioritize environmental or economic
perspectives over health-related implications. Additionally, the indirect nature of the
relationship between consumer behavior and health outcomes may make it less visible in
behavioral studies.
Integrating public health perspectives into consumer behavior research could provide
a more comprehensive understanding of the consequences of e-waste practices. Future
studies should explore how behavioral interventions can reduce exposure to hazardous
substances and contribute to improved health outcomes, particularly in regions where
informal recycling is prevalent.
The above insights collectively call for a holistic ecosystem approach involving
researchers, recyclers, manufacturers/ producers and policymakers. By aligning these
stakeholders, e-waste management can progress toward sustainable outcomes, support -
ing both circular economy goals and the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals
(SDGs).
5.1 Public health and policy implications
The findings of this review suggest that consumer behavior in e-waste management
should not be viewed solely from an environmental or waste management perspective,
but also as an important public health and societal issue. Consumer decisions regard -
ing the storage, disposal, resale, donation, repair, and recycling of electronic products
directly influence whether e-waste enters formal recycling systems or informal process -
ing channels. These pathways have important implications for environmental contami -
nation, occupational safety, and community exposure to hazardous substances such as
lead, mercury, cadmium, and brominated flame retardants.
The review demonstrates that behavioral drivers including awareness, attitude, sub -
jective norms, perceived behavioral control, infrastructure accessibility, incentives, and
privacy concerns significantly shape consumer participation in responsible e-waste
management practices. Consequently, improving consumer behavior may contribute
not only to sustainability and circular economy objectives but also to the reduction of

environmental and public health risks associated with unsafe disposal and informal
recycling.
One of the major practical implications emerging from this review is the need for
large-scale awareness and educational interventions. Public awareness campaigns focus -
ing on safe disposal practices, environmental health risks, and formal recycling chan -
nels can help improve consumer understanding and encourage responsible behavior.
Schools, universities, workplaces, and community organizations may play a significant
role in promoting environmental literacy and long-term behavioral change through edu-
cational initiatives and sustainability-oriented programs.
The findings also highlight the importance of strengthening formal recycling infra -
structure and improving accessibility to authorized collection systems. In many regions,
consumers face barriers such as inconvenience, lack of collection centers, and uncer -
tainty regarding appropriate disposal methods. Expanding collection facilities, reverse
logistics systems, and community-level take-back programs may encourage greater par -
ticipation in formal recycling systems and reduce reliance on informal recycling prac -
tices associated with unsafe handling and occupational health hazards.
From a policy perspective, municipalities and public health authorities may integrate
e-waste management initiatives within broader environmental health and urban sus -
tainability programs. Local governments can organize periodic collection drives, estab -
lish neighborhood-level drop-off centers, and promote community-based recycling
campaigns to increase public participation. Such interventions may help reduce illegal
dumping, open burning, and unsafe dismantling activities that contribute to environ -
mental pollution and health risks.
Privacy concerns and fear of data misuse emerged as important barriers affecting dis -
posal behavior, particularly for mobile phones, laptops, and other IT devices. Consum -
ers frequently store obsolete devices at home due to concerns regarding personal data
security. This finding indicates the practical need for secure recycling channels, certi -
fied data destruction services, and awareness regarding safe digital disposal practices.
Strengthening digital literacy and consumer trust in formal recycling systems may sig -
nificantly improve participation in authorized e-waste management channels.
The review further emphasizes the importance of policy instruments such as Extended
Producer Responsibility (EPR), financial incentives, exchange programs, and regulatory
enforcement mechanisms. Producers and manufacturers can support sustainable behav-
ior by developing convenient take-back systems, eco-design strategies, and consumer
awareness initiatives. Behavioral nudges and incentive-based interventions may further
encourage participation in responsible recycling practices.
Another important implication concerns the protection of informal recycling workers
and vulnerable communities exposed to hazardous e-waste processing activities. In sev -
eral developing regions, informal recycling operations continue to function without ade-
quate occupational safety measures, exposing workers and nearby populations to toxic
emissions and contaminated environments. Policymakers should therefore strengthen
monitoring systems, worker safety regulations, training programs, and safer recycling
technologies to minimize occupational and environmental health risks.
Overall, the findings suggest that sustainable and responsible e-waste management
requires a multidisciplinary and collaborative approach integrating behavioral science,
environmental governance, public health systems, educational institutions, industries,

5.3 Study limitations
This review study has certain limitations. First, the studies indexed in the Scopus and
Web of Science databases and written in English were considered, potentially over -
looking relevant research available in other databases or languages. Second, the inclu -
sion criteria were restricted to peer-reviewed articles published between 2009 and
2025, excluding relevant information from book chapters and conference proceedings
that might also be useful. Third, the review was limited to a set of keywords, which may
not capture all related publications in the field. Finally, this study applied the PRISMA
framework to ensure transparency in the review process. However, alternative frame -
works such as SALSA (Search, Appraisal, Synthesis, and Analysis) and SPAR-4-SLR
(Scientific Procedures and Rationales for Systematic Literature Reviews) could also be
adopted in future studies to broaden methodological perspectives. The study did not
employ advanced bibliometric techniques such as bibliographic coupling, co-authorship
analysis, or citation mapping, nor meta-analysis, which could enhance understanding
of research networks, scholarly influence, and produce more significant and credible
results in future studies.
6 Conclusion
This review study addresses four key research questions, providing comprehensive
understanding of consumer behavior in e-waste management and linking it to broader
sustainability transitions, circular economy imperatives, SDG targets and public health.
The findings collectively suggest that consumer behavior plays a pivotal role in shap -
ing e-waste management outcomes and may influence both environmental sustain -
ability and public health, particularly in settings where informal recycling practices are
prevalent.
