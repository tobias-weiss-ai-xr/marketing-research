Abstract
While the circular economy is increasingly recognized as a key element of sustainable
development, the utilization of digital platforms in promoting circular consumption re -
mains an under-researched topic. This article discusses how consumer-oriented platforms
balance economic and environmental goals, and how platform logic can support the cir -
cular economy transition by facilitating changes in consumer behavior. The study draws
on a multiple-case analysis of seven Business-to-Consumer platforms in Japan’s food
services sector. While the platforms share a societal purpose-driven design, they differ
in profit orientations and revenue models. Our findings indicate that platform providers
avoid overt sustainability messaging, instead highlighting economic or health benefits to
attract consumers. To engage supply-side users, they emphasize instrumental benefits such
as increased visibility, surplus revenue, and cost savings. The study illustrates how plat -
forms reduce barriers to circular consumer behavior, but their impact depends on reaching
a sufficient user base to leverage network effects. In addition, the study highlights how
cultural factors, such as Japan’s emphasis on social harmony, significantly shape adoption
and scalability of circular economy platforms. This study contributes to circular economy
discourse by deepening understanding of the interaction between platform logic and circu -
lar consumption. It offers contextual insights into consumer platforms’ potential to support
the circular economy transition.

approach of the linear economy, the socio-cultural dimensions of consumer behaviour are
often overlooked in CE literature [ 1–3]. Circular consumption is particularly relevant in
resource intense fields such as the food sector. While major sustainability challenges are

In parallel, another fundamental transformation has reshaped the current economic sys -
tem since the turn of the century: the rise of the platform economy. This new economic para-
digm and organizational model refers to the increasing dominance of multi-sided platforms
(MSPs) which connect user groups and shape the terms in which they interact with one
another [9]. The core of this transformation is rooted in consumption, particularly in activi-
ties facilitated by global MSPs, such as Amazon, Alibaba, Uber, and Airbnb [ 10]. There is
an interesting intersection of circular economy and platform economy, the latter referred to
here as platform logic. The former is primarily seen as an aspirational expression of mate -
rial processes, while the latter represents a radical business model transformation [ 11–13].
Despite the dramatic impact platform logic has had on the economy and society, there is
limited insight into its role in CE transition and its effects on CE practices in business and
consumer behaviour [3, 13].
The possibilities of platforms to enhance CE are particularly compelling in the food
industry, as platforms have been noted for their ability to bridge circularity gaps in the
food supply chain [4, 7]. Research has primarily focused on food-sharing platforms, which
facilitate the redistribution of edible surplus to reduce food waste. These include digital
marketplaces through which businesses sell leftover food at a discount, such as ResQ Club
from Finland [4, 7, 13, 14]; platforms enabling consumers to give surplus food to peers, for
example UK-based OLIO [ 7, 13–18]; and platforms connecting businesses with charities
to support the donation of surplus food, such as FoodCloud from Ireland [ 7, 14, 18, 19].
However, the potential of MSPs to support sustainable consumption extends beyond sur -
plus redistribution. Platforms can, for example, help users to identify businesses that adhere
to specific environmentally or ethically oriented practices. Although such platforms have
received little attention in the CE context, they are widely used to support other forms of
sustainable or ethical consumption practices in the food sector. Examples include promoting
locally produced food by connecting consumers with local producers [20], or mapping and
reviewing plant-based food outlets [21].
In addition to the dominant focus on food-sharing platforms, another limitation of cur -
rent CE platform research in the food sector is its heavy skew toward the Western—and
particularly European— contexts. For example, Michelini et al. [ 18] identified 52 digital
food-sharing initiatives, 48 of which were based in Europe or USA. As Zhao et al. [ 19]
point out, research conducted in diverse cultural settings is needed to provide a more robust
understanding of this phenomenon.
This study explores the intersection of circular and platform economies, enhancing
understanding of the socio-cultural dimensions of platform-mediated circular business and
consumer practices. It addresses the following research question: How do aspirations for
profits and circularity blend in the rationale and functioning of consumer-oriented CE plat-
forms, and what role does platform logic play in fostering circular consumption within food
sector? To address this question, the study adopts a multiple-case study approach examin -

Japan presents a particularly interesting institutional context among developed econo -

food sharing [23] and waste minimization, exemplified by the concept of mottainai (もった
いない or 勿体無い in Japanese), which expresses the regret over waste and appreciation
of existing goods [24–26]. On the other hand, high risk avoidance, strict quality standards,
and a strong emphasis on aesthetics contribute to unsustainable food consumption behav -
iors [23, 26, 27]. Finally, as discussed above, research on CE-oriented platforms in the food
sector has so far been concentrated primarily in European contexts [ 18, 19], making Japan
an especially compelling setting for this study. While for-profit food-sharing platforms in
Japan have appeared in recent literature [23], alternative platform-based approaches remain
underexplored.
Theoretical Framework
Socio-Cultural Factors Shaping CE Transition in Consumption and Business
Korhonen and others [ 28, p. 547] define CE as “ a sustainable development initiative with
the objective of reducing the societal production-consumption systems’ linear material and
energy throughput flows by applying materials cycles, renewable and cascade-type energy
flows to the linear system.” It transcends conventional recycling, as it advances high-value
material cycles and systemic collaboration among producers, consumers, and other stake -
holders to support sustainable development in all sectors of society [ 28]. Despite this sys -
temic nature of CE, the discussion often focuses on technological solutions such as product
design and industrial symbiosis. This focus tends to sideline how social and cultural dimen-
sions effect CE transitions at individual, organizational, and societal levels [1]. Particularly,
consumers’ circular practices are often viewed in isolation, with emphasis on individual
preferences rather than addressing the social nature of consumer behaviour [3].
In studies of socio-cultural factors, national-level cultural dimensions, particularly those
identified by Hofstede [ 29–32] — including individualism–collectivism, power distance,
uncertainty avoidance, achievement orientation, long-term vs. short-term orientation, and
indulgence–restraint — have been widely used in research on consumer behavior [ 30, 31],
corporate culture [32, 33], and entrepreneurial attitudes [34, 35]. While the dimensions have
been found to correlate with national environmental sustainability [ 36], their explanatory
power at the organizational level should be treated with caution. For example, in their com-
parative study, Lee and Herold [32, p. 1] concluded that Hofstede’s framework is “partially
applicable in explaining corporate sustainability practices.” Nonetheless, these depictions
can offer a practical conceptual starting point to the discussion on sustainable practices and
attitudes toward CE in consumption, business, and entrepreneurship.

In consumer behaviour research, individualism versus collectivism is considered a par -
ticularly influential factor [ 30, 34]. It is theorized that in highly individualistic cultures,
conformity to social norms may be seen as restricting personal autonomy, whereas in rela -
tively collectivistic cultures like Japan, individuals may experience autonomy by aligning
with internalized cultural norms [ 37]. Purchasing decisions in such contexts are argued to
rely more on implicit communication and socially shared connotations, so-called descrip -
tive norms, rather than on explicit media or peer reviews [30, 38]. In Japan, businesses have
been found to adopt a “masked” approach to CE by avoiding overt sustainability messaging
to better align with dominant consumption patterns and the linear system [23, 34]. Rovanto
and Finne [34] associate this tendency with consumers’ preference to maintain social inte -
gration and avoid public disruption, typical for collectivist cultures. Regarding food sec -
tor, Yamabe-Ledoux et al. [23] noticed that Japanese consumers’ motivations to use digital
food-sharing services primarily include convenience, affordability, and access to tasty or
novel food, whereas sustainability considerations play a secondary role. In contrast, a study
from Germany highlighted that user’s motivations centered more strongly on social and
ecological values, including activism against the linear system [ 39]. Although the studies
are not directly comparable due to different methodological approaches, the findings have
been suggested to indicate differences in attitudes regarding the established social norms
[23].
Japanese cultural characteristics have also been studied in relation to corporate sustain -
ability and entrepreneurs’ attitudes towards CE [ 32, 34]. Entrepreneurial motivations for
implementing CE practices have been explained through business-dominant logic, includ-
ing motivations such as reducing production costs using second-hand materials or seek -
ing economic benefits from sustainability-oriented branding, or ecologically dominant
logic, reflecting aspirations to address environmental challenges and contribute to sys -
temic circularity [ 34]. Oe and Yamaoka [ 40] found that Sanpo-yoshi, meaning “good in
three directions” and referring to benefits for the seller, the buyer, and society, constitutes
a key non-economic motive underlying Japanese entrepreneurs’ engagement in sustainable
business practices. Sanpo-yoshi mentality has been suggested to support new CE business
opportunities, for example through collaborative reuse-recycling arrangements [ 12]. How-
ever, it has been noted that Sanpo-yoshi ( 三方よし) emphasizes responsibility toward the
local community rather than society or the environment at large, making it a narrower con-
cept than, for example, corporate social responsibility [41, 42]. Such strong sense of loyalty
and shared responsibility toward the in-group is often regarded as characteristic feature of
Japanese business culture, typically associated with collectivism [32, 33, 43].
Moreover, it has been argued that collectivistic values and a focus on social harmony,
combined with Japan’s relatively high power-distance, hierarchical organizational struc -
tures, and a strong achievement-focus, can increase reluctance to propose unconventional
solutions and resistance to organizational change [ 33, 34, 43]. Such resistance has been
linked to weakened corporate responsiveness to environmental issues [ 32] and suggested
to hinder entrepreneurs’ ecologically dominant approach [ 34]. On the other hand, Japan’s
cultural long-term orientation has been found conducive to the strengthening of corporate
sustainability [32]. It has also been associated with the concept of mottainai, contributing to
the ecologically dominant logic towards CE [34].

Platform Business Models
The rise of platforms has given impetus to novel business models, demonstrating the power
of platforms as socio-technical loci to generate value [ 9]. Digital platforms are generally
characterized by modular architecture and at least two distinct user groups, with platform
owners responsible for connecting users and orchestrating resource flows within the plat -
form ecosystem [ 44, 45]1. Such platforms are often referred to as multi-sided platforms
(MSPs). User groups typically include supply-side users —such as producers, retailers, ser-
vice providers, content creators, or application developers— and demand-side users, such
as buyers, viewers, or service and product end-users [ 45, 46]. Additional sides, such as
advertisers or couriers, may also be involved [ 47]. All user groups engage with the plat -
form through platform-specific investments, including financial contributions, opportunity
costs, or time [ 48]. In essence, the MSP business model is defined by its ability to reduce
interaction barriers, lower transaction costs, and enable new value-adding arrangements by
facilitating the exchange of goods, services, or information [47, 49].
The effectiveness of MSPs is based on leveraging both direct network effects, i.e. bene -
fits increasing with more users in the same group, and indirect network effects, which refers
to benefits increasing with more users in the other user groups [ 45, 46, 48, 50]. This inter-
dependence drives user growth on different market sides, creating a reinforcing cycle that
enables rapid scaling. However, obtaining network effects requires simultaneously reaching
a critical mass of users on both sides of the market, which is one of the core challenges of the
MSP logic [50]. This is one of the core challenges faced by platform owners [45]. It makes
audience building, i.e. the attraction of users simultaneously and in right proportions to all
sides of the market, one of the critical tasks of platform owners [49].
The operation logic of MSPs is commonly classified according to two independent fac -
tors: their profit orientation and market structure [51, 52]. Profit orientation refers to whether
a platform operates on for-profit or non-profit basis, reflecting its primary goals and revenue
model, whereas market structure means the composition of the platform’s user groups and
related transactions, the most common categories being peer-to-peer (P2P), business-to-
consumer (B2C), and business-to-business (B2B) [51]. In addition, a third important factor
can be identified: organizational type. This refers to the owner and operator of the platform
and is typically linked to profit orientation. For-profit platforms are generally run by com -
panies, while non-profit initiatives are usually led by social enterprises, non-governmental
organizations (NGOs), or other non-profit organizations (NPOs) [7]. Recently, publicly pro-
vided MSPs have also gained traction among non-profit platforms [53].

“a system that 1) has a societal purpose-driven design and 2) allows a network of actors to
retain resource and product value 3) by utilizing circular resource strategies in the business
processes to meet the societal CE purpose. ” Building on Bocken et al. [ 54], Konietzco et

of CE: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle and Recover [56], with ‘narrowing’ corresponding to reduc-
ing, ‘slowing’ to reusing, and ‘closing’ to both recycling and recovering. In addition, CE
platforms serve a social function by connecting and educating consumers and helping to
mainstream circular consumption [3].
Ellen MacArthur Foundation [ 57] recognises sharing —understood broadly as sharing
assets, second-hand markets, and prolonging product lifetimes— as one of the key actions
in circular business strategies. In particular, in the context of MSPs, circularity is often asso-
ciated with sharing and discussed in relation to the sharing economy. The sharing economy
is often narrowly defined as P2P exchanges that enables multiple users access to goods and
resources temporarily rather than through ownership [58]. Broader definitions also encom-
pass P2P second-hand markets, such as the Japanese online marketplace Mercari [ 12, 59],
and may include other market structures, such as B2C, as well [51]. While sharing can slow
resource loops through more efficient use of resources, the sharing economy has also been
criticized for its negative environmental externalities, including rebound effects fostering
over-consumption [55, 58]. Therefore, although some CE platforms operate within the shar-
ing economy, not all sharing economy platforms contribute to CE goals. In the food sector,
platforms facilitating the sale or donation of surplus food are often referred to as food-shar-
ing platforms [e.g. 14–19], but unlike sharing practices based on temporary access, these
platforms primarily capture value at the end of the product life cycle by preventing disposal,
i.e., they contribute to closing resource loops.
CE platforms typically vary in profit orientations , reflecting different approaches to
balancing sustainability goals and economic objectives [ 7, 15, 52]. Prior research high -
lights that different profit logics shape the kinds of CE possibilities platforms enable. While
for-profit platforms dominate the platform economy at large and thus play a key role in
implementing CE objectives, non-profit CE platforms have been argued to hold significant
potential as well, because they are likely to prioritise environmental and social aims over
commercial ones [ 52]. This variation is evident in the food service industry, where non-
profit platforms are notably active [see e.g. 7, 14–16, 18]. Examples often featured in litera-
ture include for-profit platforms like ResQ Club [4, 7, 13, 14], Matsmart [7, 14], and Spoiler
Alert [7, 18]; and non-profit platforms such as Foodsharing.de [15, 18, 39], and FoodCloud
[7, 14, 18, 19].
In terms of organizational type, while CE platforms are typically owned and operated
by private companies and different types of NPOs [ 7], public sector organizations as plat -
form owners have started to emerge [ 60]. Although still relatively uncommon, platforms
provided by public sector can offer certain advantages in the CE context, as they often serve
as central actors in CE ecosystems [61], have the capacity to absorb high initial costs [ 11],
and can leverage extensive pre-existing networks and partnerships in CE initiatives [53].
CE platforms operate under varying market structures, most notably P2P, B2C [13, 15,
52], and B2B [ 13, 53, 55], but also others such as B2NGO [ 7]. In the food services sec -
tor, there are many P2P-based CE platforms [ 14–17, 39]. However, given the much larger

Data and Methods
This research is based on a multiple-case study strategy, which enables cross-case analysis
and deeper view into nuances among cases within similar mission or organizational context.
It discusses the varieties among cases that create added value in terms of nuances and gen -
eralizability when compared with single case study [ 62]. A combination of desk research,
the utilization of professional networks, and the snowball sampling technique were used to
identify case platforms that meet the selection criteria.
Case Selection Criteria
Case selection was guided by six main criteria that provided analytically meaningful lens
for exploring CE-oriented consumer platforms. First, we limited our selection to B2C plat -
forms, given their high transaction volumes and corresponding potential to shape consumer
behaviour. Second, given the social nature of circular consumption and the importance of
culturally specific factors in shaping CE transitions [ 1, 3], we chose to focus on a single
national context. Japan was selected for its distinctive cultural and institutional characteris-
tics, that offer valuable insights into CE platforms.
Third, we focused on platforms operating within the food services sector, due to its criti-
cal relevance for advancing CE. Fourth, to capture the diversity of CE platforms within
the food sector in terms of profit orientation and organizational type [ 7], we specifically
searched for cases that varied along these dimensions.
Fifth, we aimed to include platforms employing a variety of circularity strategies to
reflect the breadth of CE interventions within the sector. Thus, rather than limiting the anal-
ysis to food-sharing platforms, which dominate prior research [see e.g. 4, 7, 16, 17], we
purposefully sought cases that represent different circularity strategies, reflecting our view
of the CE transition as a system-level change, including reduce waste intensity (narrowing),
extend product lifetime through reuse (slowing), and preventing disposal in the end of prod-
uct life (closing) (see Table 1). Finally, to ensure that the cases were sufficiently established
for studying their development, we limited the selection to platforms that have been operat-
ing for at least two years.

Data Collection and Analysis
One of the researchers conducted in-depth semi-structured interviews with key informants
from each case platform. In total, eight informants were interviewed, including one joint
interview of two key informants. The interviewees were founders or operators of their
respective platforms, ensuring comprehensive knowledge of the platform’s operations, stra-
tegic decisions, and circular economy initiatives. Interviews were conducted in Japanese,
transcribed and subsequently translated into English.
Semi-structured interview style combines open-ended and theory-driven questions to
capture both participants’ experiences and insights aligned with existing research [63]. The
interview protocol included both platform-specific questions as well as broader questions
on interviewees’ personal views on Japan as the CE-oriented consumer platform market (see
interview outline in Appendix 1). This method was well-suited for data collection of this
study, as it provided enough structure to address key dimensions of the research question,
while allowing interviewees to introduce insightful views of and novel perspectives on the
topic [63].
In addition to the interviews, we reviewed supplementary materials such as blog posts,
website content, social media posts, press interviews, and other publicly available com -
munications. These sources were used to contextualise and triangulate our findings, thereby
supporting and enriching the analysis. To maintain anonymity supplementary sources are
reported generically in the Appendix 2.
The data was analysed using reflexive thematic analysis. Braun and Clarke [ 64]
outline six phases of this method: (1) data familiarization, (2) systematic coding, (3)
generating initial themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) refining and naming themes, and (6)
reporting. While this method accommodates both inductive and deductive approaches,
this study leaned towards the latter, as existing theoretical and dominant conceptual
models within the discipline informed the later stages of the analysis. Coding followed
a data-driven, inductive process in line with the reflexive thematic analysis method
[64]. However, during the theme refinement and naming, as well as reporting phases,
relevant theoretical concepts were applied to interpret and contextualize the findings,
enabling meaningful engagement with the research literature. References to selected
concepts and prior research are thus integrated into the reporting of the results in the
following section.

Findings
Balancing Profits and Circularity
Nearly all interviewees described elements aligning with the ecologically dominant logic, as
defined by Rovanto and Finne [34]. This applied to all non-profit platforms (D, E, F, G) and
to two of the for-profit platforms (A, B). Informants emphasized this logic especially when
they described their motivation for founding the platform. Typically, it was a concern over a
specific environmental problem, or a feeling of regret over waste. In a media interview, the
founder of platform A named mottainai ethic as the key inspiration for the platform (media
article, see Appendix 2).
Originally I didn’t want to set up some kind of consumer business or make money
with it, I just thought that there was this problem and I wanted to use my creativity to
solve this. (D)
I saw farmers discarding a lot of fruit and vegetables, which I felt was a waste. (…)
And at that time I thought that if I made a place for people, I could monetize it later.
So at first we did it anyway, even though it was deficit. (B)
In some cases, interviewees also presented platforms’ educational efforts —such as raising
awareness among businesses and communities about environmental issues through events
and communications— as evidence of their ecologically dominant logic (A, B, C, D, G).
In the public communications, the advocacy and educational content appeared most promi-
nent in the blogs, particularly those of platforms E and D, and, to lesser extent in social
media posts, mainly from platforms E and F (see Appendix 2). However, these efforts also
often simultaneously served as platform marketing and, in some cases, as a revenue stream
through speaker fees, workshops, or events organized for third parties such as educational
institutions or businesses (see Table 1).
Only one interviewee (C) openly expressed a more business-oriented logic, explicitly
framing the platform as a commercial MSP and emphasizing market opportunity as the
primary motivation.
So they [consumers] started thinking about more about sustainability and everything.
And I saw it from the business side and so we decided to just build a product to help
accelerate this change to help promote sustainable brands and shops in Japan […] I
mean what we’re working on, it’s basically a commerce platform. So we introduce
consumers to businesses and they do transactions. (C)
At times interviewees also referred to motives that highlighted collective benefits but were
not specifically sustainability related. This often included contributing to local community,
for example establishing connections and trust between people in the community (C, D, E)
or revitalizing local economy by supporting local businesses (B, C, E, G).

Benefits for the Supply-Side Users
Although the platforms themselves varied in profit orientation, all had to consider how to
attract for-profit businesses as supply-side users to the platform. The value that the platforms
offered to supply-side users depended on the platform’s circularity strategy (see Table 1).
For example, platforms aiming to reduce food waste by offering a marketplace for surplus
food (A, B) enabled supply-side users to generate additional revenue. In platforms encour -
aging businesses to sell products without disposable containers (E, F, G) interviewees also
noted potential cost savings. As a representative of platform E pointed out in panel discus -
sion, zero-waste practices can lower expenses related to waste management (event report,
see Appendix 2).
Moreover, platforms that helped consumers find businesses committed to sustainable
practices provided visibility and directed customers to these stores (C, D, E, F, G). This visi-
bility was also considered beneficial for the brand image. For example, platform D describes
its supply-side users as “the coolest” businesses that “share your values” on its website (see
Appendix 2). The emphasis on promoting supply-side users was particularly evident in the
platforms’ on social media content: in platforms C, D, E and G posts introducing and pro -
moting supply-side users was the most common type of content. An exception in this type
of platforms was F, as promotion of participating businesses played a much smaller role in
its social media presence. This aligns with the interviewee’s concern, that “some businesses
just see it as free advertising rather than a community initiative” (F).
Notably, although nearly all interviewees —both for-profit and non-profit platform own-
ers and operators — themselves highlighted ecologically dominant logic, they assumed that
the supply-side users started using the platform primarily for business motive instead of
ecological reasons. As one of the interviewees (F) notes, “ there are those who do it for the
sake of making money”. This suggests that supply-side users, particularly cafés, restaurants,
and stores, were not perceived as fully committed to sustainability.
companies are talking about the SDGs, but there are still very few that are making
fundamental changes (E).
Platform Logic Accelerating CE
A key feature of platform logic is connecting user groups that might not otherwise find
each other [44, 48, 49]. This was central to the circularity strategies of all case platforms,
which aimed to advance the CE by linking consumers with enterprises offering sustain -
able products or practices, or by enabling businesses with surplus goods to find buyers and
reduce food waste (see Table 1). To maximize the impact on consumption patterns, many
interviewees emphasized their focus on reaching consumers who did not have existing link-
ages to sustainable businesses and were unfamiliar with sustainable practices (B, C, E, G).
I think there are a lot of conscious people who are users, but I wanted to target those
who are not conscious and have not thought about it at all. I hope it will spread to more
and more and encourage them to think about it. (B)

Another key feature of platform logic is reducing barriers and costs to interaction and trans-

Cultural Factors Effecting Consumer CE Platforms
It has been suggested that in the Japanese context, where maintaining social harmony and
avoiding disruption are highly valued, behaviours that visibly deviate from prevailing
norms —such as overtly sustainable actions— may encounter subtle social barriers [30, 34].
Although the interviewees acknowledged that attitudes towards sustainability are gradually
changing, they noted that there is still certain hesitance or even a stigma around sustainable
lifestyles, and it might require courage to stand out from the norm (A, C, E).
we saw a lower-than-expected rate of sharing from user to user and one of the assump-
tions we have is that it’s related to Japanese culture. Like if you are a sustainable
person or a vegan person, you’re kind of rare, right? And if you share this activity
with a regular friend who’s not interested in this yet, then it’s also strange. So there’s
this barrier to sharing something new or different. But in the US or in other countries,
maybe it’s easier like “hey, look, there’s this cool app with discounts in sustainable
places”. Yeah, it’s easier to say that in Japan, because then people might think “oh,
you’re weird or something”. (C)
Early adopters of CE platforms were often described as people with high environmental
awareness (A, B, E, G), using expressions that at times implied social distance and othering,
such as ishiki takai-kei (意識高い系) —a colloquial label for “highly conscious” individu-
als, often used to imply pretentiousness [ 65]. Such expressions underscore the perception
that overt sustainability practices may still be viewed as unusual or even slightly off-putting
in mainstream consumer culture in Japan.
While such social barriers may make it difficult for CE platforms to build an audience,
platforms can also help mitigate them by fostering a sense of community and shared experi-
ence. As one interviewee explained:
It takes a lot of courage for people to bring their own containers and go shopping, but
if you’re with people who have done it before, you can try it, so we’ve done events
where everyone gets together and goes shopping. (E)
Platform E provided businesses with stickers indicating that customers can use their own
containers was also considered helpful, as it meant that customers ‘don’t need to ask’ (sur -
vey summary, see Appendix 2).
Some interviewees noted that it can be more beneficial to highlight the economic or
health benefits for the users instead of the sustainability aspect (A, D, E, F).
If you say “for the environment” or “for society”, people tend to think you are being
hypocritical. But if you can explain how, it is useful for me, it is easier to work on it
without being seen as hypocritical. In a country where this kind of activity has a nega-
tive image, the stakes are high. (E)
Platforms’ social media content matched this strategy. While feeds included occasional edu-
cational or advocacy posts—typically tied to global awareness days or local events— they
were far less frequent than content promoting the products (A, B, D), supply-side users

Another culturally specific challenge raised by the interviewees was Japan’s high emphasis
on hygiene, often associated with social norms around risk avoidance, strict quality stan -
dards, and attention to aesthetics in food [ 26, 27]. This concern was especially noted by
interviewees from platforms that help consumers find businesses offering food or water
in their own containers (E, F, G). As a result, hygiene concerns were considered to pose a
significant barrier for CE platforms in Japan’s food service sector. Interviewees noted that
this emphasis can also manifest as suspicion toward anything perceived as sub-standard,

Discussion
Platform Logic and Circularity Strategies
Platform logic includes two basic elements that have a potential to enhance the CE. First,
MSPs are designed to connect user groups  and reduce transaction and interaction costs
between them [45, 48, 49], thereby lowering barriers to circular consumption. For example,
case platforms’ map features helped consumers to locate stores with CE practices, thereby
effectively reducing the “friction” involved in making sustainable choices. Second, MSPs

1 3
Page 15 of 23   350
Circular Economy and Sustainability           (2026) 6:350
hold the potential to leverage network effects —the more users join on each side, the more
valuable the platform becomes for all [46, 50]. This can build cultural momentum that sup-
ports the mainstreaming of circular consumption [3]. Several interviewees described aspira-
tions to “move the needle” toward CE mainstreaming, underlining this potential.
To harness the platform logic to enhance CE, platforms can adopt different circularity
strategies: narrowing, slowing, and closing resource loops [ 55]. Platforms A and B con -
tributed to closing by enabling the redistribution of surplus and non-standard food items,
thereby ensuring their optimal use in the end of resource loop, instead of disposal. Platforms
C to G focused on narrowing and slowing resource loops by encouraging sustainable busi -
ness and consumption practices, such as zero waste or using refillable water bottles and cus-
tomers’ own containers (see Table 1). These strategies help reduce resource input and extend
the lifespan of materials in use. Whereas prior research on CE platforms in the food sector
has predominantly examined food-sharing platforms focused on closing the loops [e.g. 4 , 7,
14–18], the selected cases illustrate a broader array of circularity strategies.
It is important to note that the actual impact of these platforms on consumer behaviour
and overall material throughput is difficult to estimate. In particular, when platforms pri -
marily offered visibility and advertising for sustainable businesses, it is unclear whether
they genuinely shifted consumer behaviour toward more sustainable choices or whether
they inadvertently encouraged additional consumption. This echoes concerns about CE
platforms’ potential rebound effects identified in previous studies [ 55, 58]. Moreover, a
platform’s ability to leverage network effects depends on reaching a critical mass of users
on all sides of the multi-sided market, which is a fundamental challenge in platform logic
[45, 50]. This emerged as a significant barrier in the case platforms, limiting their ability to
influence on consumer behaviour.
To address the difficulty of reaching a critical mass of users, the findings of this study
emphasize the importance for platform operators to consider the dominant mentalities and
motivations of both supply- and demand-side users and to communicate with each group
accordingly, regardless of their own environmental motivations or the platform’s profit ori-
entation. The study also highlights the influence of underlying cultural norms, values, and
preferences that shape users’ image of the platform.

Contextualizing Rationales of Different Actor Groups
Culturally embedded beliefs, morals, and customs characteristic of the given societal con -
text have been found to play a key role in CE transitions [ 1]. Japan’s relatively collectivist
culture [30, 32, 34, 37, 38] creates both barriers and opportunities for attracting consumers
to CE platforms. On one hand, the emphasis on social harmony can deter individuals from
adopting “unusual” consumption practices, as early adopters may face negative associa -
tions, as reflected in expressions like ishiki takai-kei [65]. This has been associated with
the tendency to avoid self-promoting behaviour [ 40]. Such social hesitation may limit the
effectiveness of peer referrals and word-of-mouth marketing, which are often vital to audi -
ence building in MSPs [66]. It could pose a significant barrier for CE platforms, where early

adopters play a key role in spreading the platform use and circular consumer behaviour
[3]. To overcome this challenge, the case platforms often marketed themselves using imag-
ery of health, freshness, and discounts rather than overt sustainability messaging, aligning
with earlier descriptions of Japanese entrepreneurs’ “masked” approach to CE [ 23, 34]. It
highlights consumers’ focus on more practical, consumerism-oriented factors than environ-
mentalism in adopting CE platforms [23]. At the same time, it was expected that once sus-
tainable behaviors gain broader social acceptance, the same collectivist dynamics accelerate
the scaling of CE platforms and the mainstreaming of circular consumption.
The findings indicate that platform owners and operators were generally motivated
by ecologically dominant rather than business-dominant logic. This is consistent with
previous research focused on entrepreneurs operating CE-oriented businesses [ 34]. The
ecologically dominant logic appeared to be connected to the sense of mottainai , which
expresses regret over waste [ 12, 24, 26], aligning with previous studies on Japanese CE
businesses [ 25, 34]. Moreover, in addition to purely business or ecological motives,
interviewees brought up social values related to collective good, such as benefitting local
community. Similar findings in previous studies have been associated with the Sanpo-
yoshi philosophy [ 12, 40].
However, the platform operators felt that their ecologically dominant approach
differed from the general Japanese business culture, including the attitudes of the supply-
side users, which they considered to be primarily guided by business motivations. They
emphasized the importance of communicating the economic benefits to potential supply-
side users, including new revenue streams from surplus goods that would otherwise
go to waste, cost savings from reduced packaging, and increased visibility, which
helped businesses to attract new customers and strengthen their brand image. Yet, the
businesses were often hesitant to join platform and, for example, concerned about risks

Theoretical Contribution and Practical Implications
This study contributes to MSP literature by broadening the understanding of how consumer-
oriented platforms can advance the CE. We develop an empirical–conceptual framework
that illustrates the mechanisms linking circularity outcomes, platform logic, actor motives,
and cultural context, highlighting the inherently relational nature of CE platforms.
Specifically, the study extends knowledge on CE platforms in the food sector. Whereas
prior research has focused predominantly on food-sharing platforms [e.g. 14–18], we
examine a more diverse set of CE platforms and demonstrate how they pursue different
circularity strategies, including narrowing, slowing, and closing resource loops [ 54, 55].
We further contribute to discussions on CE platforms’ profit orientation and provider
type, by demonstrating that, despite differences in business logics and revenue structures
among for-profit, non-profit, and public-sector platforms, the underlying platform logic
remains similar. This finding also has policy implications, suggesting that CE platforms
can support public-sector sustainability objectives, provided that their platform logic is
well understood.
Furthermore, we highlight the role of cultural context in audience building for CE plat -
forms—an aspect that has received limited attention in prior research. The study focuses on
Japan, where CE platform research in the food sector has been scarce compared with West-
ern, and particularly in European contexts [18, 19] and discusses the findings in relation to
depictions of Japanese consumer and corporate culture as well as entrepreneurial motiva -
tions [12, 23, 25, 32, 34, 38, 40–42]. However, while national cultural frameworks can offer
a useful starting point for examining CE practices in business level, we do not treat them as
explanatory factors; rather, we use them as conceptual tools to draw attention to contextual
blind spots in existing CE platform research.
Finally, the findings have practical implications, as we highlight that effective audience-
building and incentive structure should be aligned with the dominant motives of targeted
supply and demand-side users within the given cultural context, regardless of platform’s
profit orientation and platform operator’s own ecological or business orientation towards
CE strategies. This has strategic relevance to platform operators, as prior research indicates
that CE platforms are often designed with individual consumers in mind while overlooking
socio-cultural contexts [5]. This is particularly important in food sector, where CE transi -
tions are deeply embedded in everyday socio-cultural practices [ 7, 8]. Platform operators
can address concerns regarding CE practices through different normalization strategies.
Moreover, this study offers empirically grounded insight into platforms’ abilities to facilitate
different forms of CE-oriented business and consumption in the food sector, giving impetus
to improvements and innovations.

Conclusion
This study has explored the intersection of platform and circular economy in the Japanese
food services sector through a multiple-case study of seven B2C platforms that aim to facili-
tate sustainable consumption practices. It examined how these platforms balance aspirations
of profits and circularity, and how the platform logic supports the CE transition in consumer
behaviour in the given cultural context.
The findings highlight the delicate balance between economic and environmental
rationales in consumer-oriented CE platforms. They point out that the platform
logic has potential to support CE by connecting relevant user groups, which lowers
transaction barriers and facilitates sustainable choices, and by leveraging network
effects, which contributes to mainstreaming sustainable consumption. However, these
benefits largely depend on reaching a critical mass of users, which forms a threshold
that many MSPs struggle to meet. Moreover, when the main benefit platforms offer
to supply-side users is visibility and marketing, there is a risk of rebound effects,
where increased exposure may drive additional consumption rather than reduce
overall resource use. The study also underscores that consumption is deeply social and
culturally embedded phenomenon. Thus, cultural norms and values can significantly
shape how consumer-oriented CE platforms are operated and scaled. Platforms that
acknowledge and adapt to these dynamics are better positioned to contribute to the
transition toward circularity.
As with all qualitative case research, certain limitations should be acknowledged.
First, our findings are context-specific and as such, not directly transferable to other
cultural settings. Second, the research drew on a focused but limited sample, which
constrains generalizability. In addition, interview-based data may be subject to self-
presentation bias; for instance, some entrepreneurs may have emphasized their
ecological motivations more strongly than their commercial interests. Further research
is needed to complement qualitative insights with quantitative or longitudinal data.
Future research could also deepen insights into the cultural aspects of CE platforms by
including platform consumers in the analysis, enabling a more direct examination of
how cultural norms shape adoption and use. Comparative cross-cultural studies would
further help assess the generalizability of findings and the longer-term behavioural
impacts of CE-oriented platforms.
