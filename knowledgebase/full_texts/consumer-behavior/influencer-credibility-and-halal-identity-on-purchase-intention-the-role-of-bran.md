This study investigates the effect of influencer credibility on purchase intention, with
brand engagement as a mediating variable and digital religious expression as a
contextual factor. Drawing on the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB), this research
seeks to explain how trust in social media influencers influences consumer behavior
within religiously oriented digital communities. Data were collected through an online
survey of Indonesian social media us ers who actively participate in online religious
communities, particularly in the halal fashion sector. The analysis employed Partial Least
Squares–Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM). The results indicate that influencer
trustworthiness has a significa nt positive effect on brand engagement (p = 0.000) but
does not directly affect purchase intention (p = 0.450). Brand engagement plays a crucial
mediating role in the relationship between influencer credibility and purchase intention
(p = 0.000) and is ide ntified as a strong predictor of consumers’ buying intentions (p =
0.000). However, the moderating effect of digital religious expression is not supported (p
= 0.164), suggesting that digital religiosity does not strengthen the influence of
influencer credibility on purchase intention. These findings extend TPB by highlighting
the indirect mechanism through which influencer credibility shapes consumer behavior
via engagement. The study contributes to the literature by integrating influencer
credibility, brand engagement, and digital religious expression into the TPB framework.
Practically, the findings offer insights for marketers targeting Muslim consumers by
emphasizing engagement -based influencer strategies. Limitations include the cross -
sectional design and cultural specificity of the sample, indicating opportunities for future
longitudinal and cross-cultural research.

Contribution/Originality: This study offers originality by integrating influencer credibility, brand engagement,
and digital religious e xpression within the Theory of Planned Behaviour. It reveals engagement as a key mediator
and highlights contextual insights from Indonesia’s halal fashion market .

2. LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1. TPB Stands for the Theory of Planned Behaviour
The Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) posits that individuals' a ttitudes, subjective norms, and perceived
behavioural control influence their actions and intentions. The theory of planned behavio ur (TPB) has proven very
useful in consumer research and marketing when explaining choices based on values, such as buying it ems for
religious or moral reasons. This is especially true when discussing choices rooted in values. In traditional TPB
applications, the impact of digital social environments, where norms are constantly created and reinforced through
online interactions, is not always fully understood. For example, online interactions continually set and strengthen
norms. Along with seeking social acceptance through human connections, Muslim consumers also desire social
acceptance through digital displays of faith that link consumption to moral identity.
Alalwan (2018) and Shah and Sayuti (2011) assert that recent advancements in the Theory of Planned Behaviour
(TPB) have elucidated the importance of contextual variables as boundary conditions that either amplify or attenuate
behavioural intentions. These factors include culture, the use of technology , and how much time people spend on
social media. In this case, the credibility of influencers may act as an informal norm by changing what people expect.
Conversely, the degree of engagement individuals possess with a company reflects their perception of agency in
influencing their own conduct. Meanwhile, the expression of religious beliefs on digital platforms could change how
people act and think by raising moral issues in the context of online interactions. This integration changes the Theory
of Planned Behaviour (TPB) from a psychological model of intention to a socio-digital framework that includes online
interactions and identity performance as factors that lead to behaviour.

2.2. The Credibility of Those Who Have Influence
(Erdogan, 1999) and Ohanian (1990) says that an influencer's credibility depends on three things: how good they
are at what they do, how good they look, and how trustworthy they are both on and off the job. Lou and Yuan (2019)
assert that the incorporation of symbolic congruence and social authenticity into digital contexts enhances credibility
beyond traditional endorsement concepts. Research by Lim, Mohd Radzol, Cheah, and Wong (2017)  and Sokolova
and Kefi (2020)  indicates that influencers perceived as authentic and aligned with group values exert a greater
influence on public opinion. Conversely, this effect may not consistently  manifest or be unequivocally evident.
Believers assess both knowledge and adherence to spiritual and ethical standards in religious markets, highlighting
the robust connection between credibility and moral legitimacy (Ishak, Che Omar, Khalid, Ab. Ghafar, & Hussain,
2020). This underscores that adherents assess both adherence and understanding.
There is a growing body of evidence (Qureshi, Shahzadi, & Hussain, 2024 ) that suggests the credibility of an
influencer has an indirect impact on the desire to make a purchase. This influence can be exerted either through
involvement or by modifying the qualities of the situational identity. The findings of this contradict prior studies that
established the trustworthiness of influencers as a direct predictor. This study contributes to the discourse by
contextualizing trustworthiness within a moral -symbolic framework. Influencers serve as communicators and
normative agents who establish behavioral standards. The TPB viewpoint contends that individuals' perceptions of
societal pressure to follow trusted authority figures are influenced by the legitimacy of those authorities.

2.3. Active Participation with a Brand
According to Brodie, Hollebeek, Jurić, and Ilić (2011) and Hollebeek, Glynn, and Brodie (2014), the term "brand
engagement" describes the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral commitment that customers demonstrate while
interacting with brands. Engagement, as opposed to transactional loyalty, refers to active engagement and the
collaborative construction of one's identity, particularly within social media settings. In Islamic marketing, brand
engagement is highly significant because it reflects how a person's religious beliefs and personal connection to a
product are perceived. People eat halal food for various reasons, some  practical and some moral (Abbasi et al., 2018;
Hasanah et al., 2025).
Dessart, Veloutsou, and Morgan-Thomas (2016) conducted an analysis of studies, illustrating that engagement
serves as a mediator in the relationship between trust and intention. Engagement is when a person's perception of an
influencer's authenticity changes into real participation in ecos ystems driven by influencers. This change connects
credibility to action. The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) states that how much control someone perceives they
have over their behavior indicates how well they can follow through on their goals through contact. To measure this,
they examine how much people interact with the brand. Consequently, it is posited that the credibility of influencers
enhances engagement, thereby increasing the likelihood of a purchase.

2.4. Expressions of Religion Conducted Online
According to Campbell and Tsuria (2021), "digital religious expression" is the act of expressing and negotiating
religious ideas in online settings.  This process entails the enactment, mediation, and validation of religion through
digital interaction.  This phenomenon indicates that conventional distinctions between private spirituality and public
discourse are being contested, resulting in the emergence of a hybri d realm where religiosity can be both individual
and communal.  Butt, Khong, and Alam (2021)  assert that digital religiosity within Muslim consumer culture
integrates moral discourse into the marketplace, thereby influencing perceptions of authenticity and halal compliance.
This study examines the contextual moderator that can alter the psychological associations posited by the
Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB), offering a critical analysis of digital religious expression. Butt et al. (2021) assert
that individuals exhibiting heightened digital religiosity may assess influencer messages through a moral lens.  This
perception may lead to heightened engagement when the content aligns with faith -based principles and diminished
engagement when the information is perceived as incongruent.  Consequently, the dissemination of ideas via digital
platforms not only fortifies prevailing social norms but also transforms the ethical parameters of trust and influence.
In theory, it serves as a boundary condition that governs the influence of subjective norms and perceived behavioral
control on the intention to act in a specific manner.

2.5. Desire to Make a Purchase
"Purchase intention" is the term for the mental steps that happen before someone actually buys something. Aslan
(2023) and Alalwan (2018) assert that the probability of purchase is influenced by evaluative judgments, emotional
reactions, and perceived social factors. For Muslim consumers, intention is highly moralized, and they make decisions
based on their faith, morals, and community expectations.  Research conducted by Nazir (2023) indicates that religion
and halal assurance significantly influence purch asing intentions.  Nonetheless, these effects are progressively
mitigated by digital narratives and influencer communications.
The integration of the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) and digital religiosity has revealed that the intention
to purchase arises not solely from rational evaluation, but from socialized cognition, characterized by the confluence
of perceived norms, engagement, and religious identity.  Consequently, the study redefines purchase intention as the
behavioral manifestation of both dig ital identity performance and normative adherence within online Muslim
communities.

2.6. Formulation of the Hypothesis
The following hypotheses, which are founded in the extended TPB framework, are proposed by the study on the
basis of the theoretical synthesis that was presented earlier.
In the first hypothesis, the legitimacy of an influencer has a favorable impact on the intention to purchase.
There is a favorable correlation between the credibility of an influencer and the level of inte raction with a
company.
The level of interaction with a brand has a favorable impact on the intention to make a purchase.
When it comes to the connection between the credibility of an influencer and the intention to buy, brand
engagement serves as a mediator when it comes to the connection.
The credibility of an influencer is linked to the desire to buy, and digital religious expression has an impact on
this link.
These hypotheses collectively position influencer credibility as a normative stimulus, bran d engagement as a
behavioral mechanism, and digital religious expression as a contextual amplifier, all of which collaboratively impact
purchase intention.  This integrated model augments the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) within the realm of
digital religiosity and Islamic consumer behaviour, linking psychological theory with socio -cultural transformation
in digital markets.

3. METHOD
3.1. Design of the Study
This study utilizes a quantitative, explanatory research design to examine causal relationships among influencer
credibility, brand engagement, digital religious expression, and purchase intention, based on the Theory of Planned
Behaviour (Ajzen, 1991).  The explanatory design was chosen to enable hypothesis testing and the verification of
structural r elationships among latent variables derived from established theoretical frameworks (Creswell &
Creswell, 2018).  To do Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS -SEM), we used a program called
SmartPLS 4.  This software i s useful for doing research that is both predictive and theory -building, as well as for
looking at models that have complicated mediating and moderating effects (Hair, Hult, Ringle, & Sarstedt, 2019).
Utilizing PLS-SEM aligns with the study's objective to quantify latent components encompassing psychological,
behavioral, and environmental dimensions that are not directly observable. PLS-SEM is better than covariance-based
Support Vector Machines (SEM) for working with non-normal data and sample sizes that are small to medium. This
is an important point to remember for behavioral research that looks into new digital trends like influencer marketing
and online religiosity.

3.2. Context of the Research and Sampling
The research took place in Indonesia, which has the most Muslims in the world and is one of the fastest-growing
markets for halal fashion. The situation is  especially important because Indonesia has a unique mix of digitalization,
religiosity, and modern consumerism. Jakarta, Bandung, and Surabaya were the three big cities where Muslim teens
hung out and used the internet. The data came from these three major cities.
In accordance with the following three inclusion criteria, respondents were selected through the use of purposive
sampling: Muslim consumers between the ages of 18 and 40, active users of social media who follow fashion
influencers, and acquaintances with at least one halal fashion brand are the three criteria that fall under this category.
By utilizing this strategy, it is ensured that the individuals who are participating have sufficient prior information to
evaluate the things that are being invest igated. According to Hair, Sarstedt, Hopkins, and Kuppelwieser (2014),  the
minimum number of replies required for PLS-SEM is 250, and we found that we had received 250 valid responses. It
is ten times the number of indicators that are present in the construct that is the most complicated. The preservation
of demographic variety with regard to gender, education, and occupation was done in order to improve the study's
external validity.

3.3. Instrumentation and Measurement
All of the concepts were operationalized using established, validated scales from prior research.  We utilized six
items from Ohanian (1990)  to assess influencer credibility. These items examined trustworthiness, expertise, and
appeal. Five in dicators that looked at cognitive, emotional, and behavioral aspects were used to measure brand
engagement (Hollebeek et al., 2014). Campbell and Tsuria (2021) say that four indicators were used to measure digital
religious expression.  These signs were online engagement, value articulation, and faith -based communication. To
ascertain purchase intention, four components from Dodds, Monroe, and Grewal (1991)  were employed, focusing
specifically on willingness and consideration for a purchase.
We used a five-point Likert scale for each indicator, where one represented the strongest disagreement and five
the strongest agreement. Three academics knowledgeable about Islamic consumer behavior and marketing reviewed
the content to ensure accuracy. A pilot test with thirty participants indicated that the items were clear and reliable
before full implementation (Cronbach's A lpha was greater than 0.70). We employed Cronbach's Alpha, Composite
Reliability (CR), and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) to assess the construct's reliability and validity in subsequent
research steps. Hair et al. (2019) study established thresholds at +0.70 and +0.50, respectively.

3.4. Examination of Data and Model Fit
The analysis was conducted in two phases: initially, the measurement model was assessed, followed by an
evaluation of the structural model through inspection.  We used the Fornell-Larcker criterion to test the measurement
model by looking at indicator load ings, reliability indices, convergent validity, and discriminant validity.  The fact
that all of the item loadings were greater than 0.70 and all of the AVE values were greater than 0.50 showed that
convergent validity was present.  The validation of discriminant validity was established when the square root of the
Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for each construct exceeded the correlations among the constructs.
To ascertain the significance of path coefficients, bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples was empl oyed for the
structural model. Indirect impact estimation and interaction term analysis in SmartPLS were used to examine
mediation and moderation, respectively. We referenced Cohen (1990) thresholds of 0.02 (small), 0.15 (mid), and 0.35
(big) to determine effect sizes (f2). The blindfolding process was also used to assess predictive relevance (Q2).
Furthermore, model fit indices were calculated to ensure adherence to the advanced PLS -SEM reporting
standards. The Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) was 0.061, which is lower than the su ggested
criterion of 0.08. This indicates that the model fit was good. According to Hair et al. (2019)  study, the Normed Fit
Index (NFI) was 0.911, which exceeds the acceptable level of 0.90 and demonstrates that the model performs well.
The endogenous components explain a substantial amount of information, evidenced by R² values of 0.786 for Brand
Engagement and 0.764 for Purchase Intention. The combined use of these indices confirms that the model is
consistent, valid, and strong overall.

tests of path coefficients, helps researchers identify which predictors to prioritize for model simplification and which
variables have substantial explanatory value. The F² test thus con firms the structural model's statistical relevance
and offers meaningful insights into the relationships among the study variables.

5. DISCUSSION
5.1. How Managers Comprehend the Magnitudes of Effects
When viewed from a managerial perspective, the findings provide a great deal of guidance and information.
According to the findings, the credibility of influencers has a considerable impact on brand engagement ( β = 0.887,
f² = 3.672). This suggests that authentic influencers greatly increase the level of involvement and emotional
commitment of consumers.  The establishment of long -term collaborations with influencers whose values are
congruent with halal principles should be a top priority for marketers. This is because such alignment increases the
perception of trust and encourages sustainable engagement loops.
As demonstrated by the fact that the medium influence of brand engagement on purchase intention (f2 = 0.209)
demonstrates that it can be strategically utilized to convert trust into sales results.  The possibility of Muslim
customers making a purchase can be significantly increased with the implementation of campaigns that are centered
on involving people in the process, such as interactive question and answer sessions, user-generated material, and the
sharing of testimonials.
Given the fact that the contextual role of digital religious expression is very minor but substantial (f2 = 0.187),
it is clear that it is essential to brand in a manner that is sensitive to religious beliefs.  In spite of the fact that it d oes
not immediately alter how individuals intend to behave, incorporating religious beliefs into internet stories can make
a brand appear more legitimate and assist it in fitting in with the community.  Instead of making overtly religious
appeals, which could make them appear less genuine, businesses that wish to connect with Muslim clients can use
subtle spiritual clues, such as tone, imagery, and representations of modesty.

5.2. A Combination of Theory and Practice with Integration
By demonstrating that social validation, rather than religiosity itself, promotes online behavioral intentions,
these findings contribute to the theoretical integration of the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) with digital
religiosity. Their results enhanc e the theoretical framework. The credibility of an influencer reflects a subjective
norm, while engagement signifies behavioral control. Conversely, digital religious expression functions as a cultural
filter rather than a causal agent.
The research unders cores the necessity of transcending mere endorsement rationale in influencer marketing
within the halal fashion sector. Creating participatory ecosystems where customers can help shape the meaning of a
brand through digital participation leads to better behavior than just seeing real endorsements. Engagement is not a
measure of output; it is a strategic input that keeps brands alive in markets that care about value.
The p-value of 0.450 shows that how trustworthy an influencer is has no effect on whether or not someone wants
to buy a product. This shows that how credible the audience thinks the material is doesn't have a big effect on how

Within the context of the TPB paradigm, brand engagement is recognized as a particularly significant normative

endorsers as good examples. The results of this study show how important it is to build trust with influential people,
especially when it comes to engagement, which is now a main goal of digital marketing.
The statistical analysis shows that the p -value is 0.000, which indicates a strong link between interacting with
the brand and the desire to buy. The conclusion is that people are more likely to pur chase when they engage with a
business by liking, commenting on, or sharing content. Engagement is the first step in building trust and
commitment, both of which are important factors to consider when making a purchase.
A compelling emotional connection to  a brand is a significant indicator of the likelihood that a person would
purchase a product or service. According to the theory of planned behavior, engagement not only demonstrates that
a person has a favorable attitude toward a brand but also has the ab ility to forecast how that person will behave in

connect themselves with advocates of the brand, which ultimately strengthens the legitimacy o f the company

6. CONCLUSION
The goal of this study is to find out how social media marketing, the credibility of influencers, branding, digital
religious expression, purchase intention, and brand engagement all work together. These help us understand the
most important statistical pa rameters that are significant to these interactions. Out of all the hypotheses evaluated,
the one asserting that the credibility of the influencer has a significant impact on the level of engagement with the
brand and the desire to make a purchase carried the greatest weight. Furthermore, brand engagement acts as a
complete mediator in the connection between the trustworthiness of influencers and the intention to make a purchase.

There is no significant association between the moderator variable and the direct relationship between the credibility
of the influencer and the intention to make a purchase. One of the major mechanisms that turn influence into action
is engagement, which sheds light on the baffling lack of customer action despite the trust that inf luencers wield.
From a theoretical point of view, the findings have the potential to improve and contribute to the Theory of
Planned Behaviour (TPB). Brand involvement appears to be a significant attitude component that affects behavioural
intentions, aligning with the TPB, which emphasizes attitudes and subjective standards. Customers are more likely
to behave in ways that are consistent with societal expectations when they believe that important people trust them.
This is because the subjective norm incre ases when customers receive this information. Although credibility is
essential, it is not sufficient to convince people to purchase a product without first inspiring them to feel an emotional
or active connection to the product. Therefore, it is even more  important to have a comprehensive understanding of
consumer behavior that considers both individual cognitive evaluations and societal indicators related to customer
behavior.
Nevertheless, there are a few qualifications to these findings.  First, it is p ossible that the findings are only
applicable to the Indonesian market because the sample size is rather small and the researchers primarily focused on
Indonesian clients.  In addition, the utilization of cross-sectional data makes it impossible to draw any conclusions on
the causal relationships between the variables.  People might worry about response bias when it comes to the data
from the self-reported survey on subjective things like trust and intent.
The operationalization of the concepts of "moderat ing effect" and "digital religious expression" uncovers
additional opportunities that were not utilized, while also elucidating the complex relationships that exist between
these constructs and consumer contexts. The theory of planned behavior (TPB) serves  as an effective guiding
framework; however, incorporating additional models, such as the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM) and the
Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) framework, would enhance our understanding of the psychological aspects of
the phenomenon. It is strongly suggested that this research be expanded to include long -term metrics for tracking
consumer participation and intent over time. Taking into consideration the level of client skepticism, the standard of
the material, and the reliability of the platform would provide us with additional information regarding the challenges
associated with influencer marketing. It is possible that cross -cultural analysis will make it easier to evaluate the
cultural sensitivity that is necessarily present in these interactions. Incorporating qualitative research techniques into
this investigation, such as doing a netnography or conducting in -depth interviews, would help improve
comprehension of the context and contribute to the triangulation of the quantitative findings.
