Abstract
Marketing scholarship has primarily focused on influencing consumer behavior towards sustainability, yet many sus -
tainability challenges require market-level changes across multiple stakeholders. Addressing this gap, we examine how
heterogeneous stakeholders collectively shape markets for sustainability. We conduct a multi-case study of B2B markets
whose impact on climate change and biodiversity is actively contested. Drawing on institutional field theory, we concep -
tualize market shaping for sustainability as linked to issue fields where diverse stakeholders engage to influence market
trajectories. Our abductive analysis identifies four types of multi-stakeholder market-shaping efforts pursuing markedly
different—at times opposing—market-level outcomes: status quo maintenance, targeted reform, infrastructure reform,
and market contraction. To explain this variation, we delineate four pathways through which stakeholders enact sustain -
ability-related issues—two for challengers and two for incumbents. By linking these pathways to intended market-level
outcomes, we advance theory on market-based sustainability interventions and offer actionable insights for navigating
multi-stakeholder market-shaping processes.

Introduction
Building on decades of previous work, Chandy et al. (2021)
issued a compelling call for marketing scholars and practi -
tioners to shift their focus towards how “better marketing”
can meaningfully contribute to a “better world.” In response,
sustainability has emerged as a central concern in market -
ing research and practice, as reflected in the Marketing Sci-
ence Institute’s 2024 Research Priorities. Much prior work
on sustainability in marketing focuses on how to influence
consumer behavior (White et al., 2019) through specific
marketing strategies such as green marketing (Cronin et al.,
2011), adjustments to the marketing mix (Leonidou et al.,
2013), sustainability-oriented marketing communications
(Friske et al., 2023), and cause-related marketing (Schamp
et al., 2023). While these studies offer valuable insights
into how marketing can steer individual choices towards
sustainability, there is growing recognition that consumer-
centric research alone is insufficient to address the scale and
urgency of global sustainability challenges (Nenkov, 2024).
At the same time, this focus has left business-to-business
(B2B) markets relatively underexamined, despite their eco-
nomic significance (Kim et al., 2024).

To complement consumer-centric research, we follow
scholars who call for greater attention to meso-level sus -
tainability interventions that influence the broader systems
in which firms operate (Kim et al., 2024; Nenkov, 2024).
These interventions extend beyond influencing consumer
behavior but do not pursue full-scale societal transforma -
tion. For such meso-level interventions, markets represent
a particularly important yet underexplored lever for sustain-
ability. Recent work urges marketing scholars to examine
how markets can be proactively shaped toward more sus -
tainable outcomes (Kim et al., 2024; Sheth & Parvatiyar,
2021). Similarly, scholars in other disciplines call for a shift
“from a reactive market-failure-fixing approach towards a
proactive market-shaping one” (Mazzucato, 2022, p. 93).
Marketing scholars have long examined how firms can
broaden their strategic repertoire from merely adapting to
market changes to proactively shaping markets through mar-
ket-driving strategies (Jaworski et al., 2000; Humphreys &
Carpenter, 2018; Maciel & Fischer, 2020) or market-shaping
strategies1 (Azimont & Araujo, 2007; Nenonen et al., 2019).
While early work emphasized firm performance, recent
research highlights market shaping as means to advance also
sustainability outcomes (Sheth & Parvatiyar, 2021; Stor -
backa et al., 2022; Syväri et al., 2025; Warrier & Sangle,
2025). In practice, sustainability-oriented market shaping is
increasingly visible across industries. For example, Tesla has
reshaped the global automotive market by normalizing elec-
tric vehicles as desirable, high-performance alternatives to
combustion engines—spurring both accelerated electric vehi-
cle development and infrastructure investments. Similarly,
the EU’s right-to-repair directive (2024/1799) is transforming
consumer electronics markets by incentivizing manufacturers
to design more durable and repairable products, while simul-
taneously revitalizing the electronics repair sector.
Despite this growing interest, a key gap remains. Prior
research predominantly examines marketing shaping activi-
ties by individual firms (Jaworski et al., 2000; Kindström
et al., 2018) or relatively homogeneous coalitions within a
single market (Baker & Nenonen, 2020; Maciel & Fischer,
2020). As a result, it offers limited insights into how het -
erogeneous stakeholders—such as firms, policymakers, and
NGOs—collectively shape markets. This has two concern -
ing consequences. First, it overlooks the diverse—and often
conflicting—interpretations of sustainability and shaping
intentions across stakeholder groups (Doganova & Karnøe,
2015; Keränen et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024). Second, it

provides limited guidance on how to navigate these interests

These limitations become particularly visible in large-
scale sustainability transitions. Consider, for example, the
shift toward electric vehicles. While car manufacturers and
consumers play central roles in this process, the sustainability
of the transition is also shaped by other stakeholders—such as
grid operators, regulators, and civil society organizations—
who influence battery sourcing, charging infrastructure, and
energy-mix requirements. Such multi-stakeholder dynam -
ics remain largely invisible when market shaping is studied
from the perspective of a single firm or a coalition of firms.
Without extending its scope and addressing these tensions,
sustainability research in marketing risks promoting well-
intentioned but overly simplistic solutions that neglect the
complexity of values and competing visions of sustainability.
To address this gap, we conduct a multi-case study of four
B2B markets in which heterogeneous stakeholders engage in
market shaping for sustainability. All studied markets share
a common material base in forest-based resources, where
environmental issues such as climate change and biodiver -
sity loss are actively contested. Adopting an interpretivist
abductive research design (Mantere & Ketokivi, 2013),
our objective is to uncover situated mechanisms (Cornelis -
sen & Werner, 2026) that explain the varied enactments of
sustainability by stakeholders seeking to shape the markets
towards ‘better’ futures. Specifically, we ask: What forms
do multi-stakeholder market-shaping efforts for sustainabil-
ity take, and how are sustainability-related issues enacted
in such efforts? To answer these questions, we draw on the
concepts of exchange fields and issue fields from institu -
tional field theory (Zietsma et al., 2017), which allow us to
move beyond firm-centric accounts and capture how market
shaping unfolds across interacting fields.
Our findings identify four types of multi-stakeholder
market-shaping efforts linked to issue fields, distinguished
by their field settlement-orientation and the market-level
outcomes they pursue: (1) status quo maintenance, (2) tar -
geted reform, (3) infrastructure reform, and (4) market con-
traction. These effort types not only differ markedly in their
intended outcomes but, in some cases, directly oppose one
another. To explain this variation, we identify four pathways
of sustainability enactment—two associated with market
challengers and two with market incumbents. Our analysis
shows that these pathways stem from differences in stake -
holders’ field embeddedness and their perceptions of the
legitimacy gap surrounding the market. These differences,
in turn, lead stakeholders to adopt distinct modes of sustain-
ability enactment—fundamentalist, pragmatic, instrumen -
tal, or transformative. Taken together, our findings help to
understand why efforts to shape markets for sustainability

by heterogeneous stakeholders can steer the same market in
fundamentally different directions.
Our work makes three contributions to the literature on
meso-level sustainability interventions, particularly market-
based interventions such as market shaping for sustainability.
First, we extend market-shaping research by foregrounding
issue fields as critical arenas in which sustainability-related
shaping unfolds, showing how field settlements emerge as
central shaping objects and how shaping efforts are condi -
tioned by stakeholders’ field embeddedness and issue sets.
This perspective also offers a novel analytical lens for other
meso-level interventions by highlighting how sustainability
agendas emerge and are contested across interacting fields.
Second, we enhance the conceptualization of multi-stake -
holder market-shaping efforts for sustainability by defining
them as loosely coupled configurations of shaping activities
oriented toward field settlements and specific market-level
outcomes. We also show how heterogeneous stakeholders’
shaping activities coalesce into such efforts despite limited
coordination. In doing so, we reconceptualize market shap-
ing for sustainability as a distributed and emergent process in
which actors pursue shared or competing outcomes through
parallel and sometimes even interfering activities. Third, we
uncover a situated mechanism—differing legitimacy per -
ceptions—that explains variation in how stakeholders enact
sustainability in shaping efforts. By formalizing this mecha-
nism in a set of research propositions, we advance theory
by explaining why multi-stakeholder efforts to shape mar -
kets for sustainability often diverge—and at times directly
conflict. For practitioners, we offer actionable guidance for
different stakeholder groups—incumbent firms, challenger
firms, regulators, as well as NGOs and activists—on how
to effectively navigate multi-stakeholder market-shaping
processes.
Theoretical background
Market shaping as a meso-level sustainability
intervention
Like many marketing scholars, we define sustainability as
“meeting the needs of the present without compromising
the ability of future generations to meet their own needs”
(World Commission on Environment and Development,
1987). Achieving sustainable development requires balanc-
ing environmental, social, and economic dimensions—an
idea reflected in frameworks such as the triple bottom line
and the 3Ps (people, planet, profit).
To complement prior research, which has primarily
focused on marketing’s role in making consumer behav -
ior more sustainable (e.g., White et al., 2025), our study
responds to recent calls to shift attention to meso-level
interventions (Kim et al., 2024; Nenkov, 2024), such as
extended producer responsibility schemes (Gui et al., 2016),
sustainable supply chain management (Carter & Liane
Easton 2011), and market-shaping for sustainability (Sheth
& Parvatiyar, 2021). These studies share a common focus:
investigating how larger systems, in which firms are embed-
ded—such as supply chains, industries, markets, cross-sec -
tor partnerships—can be made more sustainable. A recent
review of strategic marketing for sustainability shows that
the meso-level remains significantly underexplored rela -
tive to consumer-focused marketing research, even though
it represents a critical avenue for advancing sustainability
(Kim et al., 2024).
Among the streams of meso-level sustainable marketing
research, we adopt a market shaping for sustainability per -
spective (Ottosson et al., 2020; Sheth & Parvatiyar, 2021;
Syväri et al., 2025). This allows us to examine intervention
efforts by multiple heterogeneous stakeholders both hori -
zontally and vertically within exchange systems that con -
nect competing producers with multiple customers. While
other streams, such as sustainable supply chain manage -
ment (Carter & Liane Easton 2011; Khan et al., 2021) and
extended producer responsibility research (Gui et al., 2016;
Tian et al., 2020), also adopt systemic views, they typically
focus on distinct vertical systems, such as supply chains
or reverse logistics networks. This orientation limits their
ability to capture actions by stakeholders operating across
competing or overlapping supply chains. In contrast, mar -
ket shaping for sustainability takes product and service
markets—comprising multiple, intersecting supply chains
and networks—as its analytical starting point, making it
better suited to capture competing and intersecting multi-
stakeholder dynamics. Moreover, this perspective does not
privilege any particular pillar of sustainability and accom -
modates both material products and immaterial services
across diverse business contexts, including B2C and B2B
settings.
Research on market shaping conceptualizes markets as
complex multi-actor exchange systems and focuses on the
strategic actions that bring about meaningful changes in
these systems (Jaworski et al., 2000; Humphreys & Carpen-
ter, 2018; Nenonen et al., 2019; Pontikes & Rindova, 2020).
Typical activities to shape markets include launching new
innovative products or services, introducing new pricing
models or channels, renegotiating work division in the value
chain, educating customers and other stakeholders, creating
new category labels, and influencing customer preferences,
regulation or technical standards (Jaworski et al., 2000;
Humphreys & Carpenter, 2018; Nenonen et al., 2019; Flaig
et al., 2021). As such, market shaping expands the strate -
gic repertoire of firms beyond merely adapting to changes

or leveraging existing competitive opportunities, by argu -

al., 2020; Pontikes & Rindova, 2020; Helfat, 2021; Fischer,

studies typically centered on firm-level outcomes such as
improved financial performance or market share (Gavetti et
al., 2017; Keyhani et al., 2015; Kumar et al., 2000), more
recent studies are increasingly exploring market shaping as
a tool for advancing sustainability outcomes (Sheth & Par -
vatiyar, 2021; Syväri et al., 2025; Warrier & Sangle, 2025).
Defined as “purposive efforts by a focal actor to trans -
form market characteristics to generate positive impact in
critical and relevant areas for society and the planet” (Syväri
et al., 2025, p. 3), market shaping for sustainability has been
explored in relation to both social (Agarwal et al., 2018;
Kullak et al., 2022) and environmental dimensions of sus -
tainability (Veal & Mouzas, 2011; Doganova & Karnøe,
2015; Ottosson et al., 2020; Syväri et al., 2025). In particu-
lar, prior research has examined how markets can be shaped
for sustainability through the introduction of environmen -
tally friendly technologies and products (Ottosson et al.,
2020; Werner et al., 2022; Mehtälä et al., 2024; Eriksson
et al., 2026) or policy interventions (Veal & Mouzas, 2011;
Mattsson & Junker, 2023). Activities used to shape markets
for sustainability are largely the same as the one applied
to shape markets for business benefits, but several studies
report challenges in reaching the desired market-level out -
comes due to conflicts with existing regulation and social
norms; challenges in enrolling and coordinating across
stakeholders; and existing, less sustainable markets remain-
ing dominant (Ottosson et al., 2020; Mattsson & Junker,
2023; Mehtälä et al., 2024; Syväri et al., 2025).
To address the above challenges identified by the mar -
ket-shaping for sustainability research, we need a theoreti -
cal foundation that elevates the analysis beyond firm-level
activities to interventions involving multiple stakeholders.
The literature on institutional fields (Zietsma et al., 2017)
offers such a foundation, which we will turn to in the next
section.
Institutional fields as a lens to understand market
shaping efforts
To examine what forms multi-stakeholder market-shap -
ing efforts take, and how sustainability is enacted in such
efforts, we draw on institutional theory, which has increas -
ingly informed research on markets and market shaping (e.g.
Humphreys, 2010; Scaraboto & Fischer, 2013; Ertimur &
Coskuner-Balli, 2015; Baker et al., 2019). Specifically, we
adopt the concept of institutional fields (Zietsma et al., 2017).
A field is “recognized area of institutional life” (DiMag -
gio & Powell, 1983, p. 148) comprising “a community of
organizations that partakes of a common meaning system
and whose participants interact more frequently and fate -
fully with one another than with actors outside the field”
(Scott, 1995, p. 56). Following Zietsma et al. ( 2017), we
distinguish between two types of fields—exchange fields
and issue fields. This conceptual distinction is important
because issue fields can overlap multiple, distinct exchange
fields and bring together heterogeneous stakeholders to
contest over issues and ways to address them (Buchanan et
al., 2023). As such, this theoretical lens allows us to move
beyond individual firms and their market-shaping activities
within a focal market and instead focus on field-spanning
multi-stakeholder market-shaping efforts.
Exchange fields stabilize and coordinate exchange among
a focal population of actors (e.g., firms) and their exchange
partners (e.g., suppliers, intermediaries, and custom -
ers; Zietsma et al., 2017). Actors within these fields share
a local order— understandings and practices considered
rational and appropriate in the context (Cloutier & Cou -
ture, 2024)—supported by an institutional infrastructure
comprising formal and informal governance mechanisms,
organizational templates, categories and labels (Greenwood
et al., 2011; Zietsma et al., 2017). The institutional infra -
structure can vary in its degree of elaboration and coherency
across exchange fields (Hinings et al., 2017). When highly
elaborated and coherent, the exchange field is considered
institutionalized or “mature.” Three types of exchange fields
are particularly relevant for us: industry exchange fields
(markets), professional exchange fields (e.g., public policy),
and social movement exchange fields (Zietsma et al., 2017).
Issue fields, in contrast, form around issues (Zietsma
et al., 2017; see also Hoffman, 1999), defined as “events,
developments, and trends that members of the industry
recognize as having important consequences for the indus -
try and which receive continued attention” (Hoffman &
Ocasio, 2001, p. 427). Issue fields arise when actors from
one or more exchange fields challenge the meanings and
practices of another—for example, when social movement
actors seek to change an industry (Zietsma et al., 2017). In
essence, an issue field emerges when stakeholders question
the legitimacy of the local order within an exchange field—
such as a market—in relation to a focal issue, for example,
an environmental concern such as water pollution or climate
change (Cloutier & Couture, 2024). Here, legitimacy is not
an organizational attribute but rather reflects stakeholders’
perceptions of the adequacy of business behavior relative
to their expectations, and these perceptions may diverge
(Panwar et al., 2014). Local orders within markets often
serve business interests, motivating incumbents to pre -
serve them, while challengers who view them as outdated,
unfair, or morally wrong seek change. Issue fields, thus,
bring together a diverse set of stakeholders with distinct

identities and commitments to institutional infrastructure
located in different exchange fields (Zietsma et al., 2017).
Consequently, the issue underlying an issue field may carry
different meanings for different stakeholders, leading to var-
ied interpretations and enactments that shape the nature and
direction of market-shaping efforts.
Because issue fields span multiple exchange fields,
they lack their own local orders or institutional infra -
structure (Zietsma et al., 2017). Instead, they rely on field
settlements—”common frameworks aimed at addressing
issues” (Buchanan et al., 2023, p. 700). Field settlements
function as templates for action (e.g., certifications or stan -
dards) through which stakeholders propose ways to address
contested issues (Cloutier & Couture, 2024). Unlike insti -
tutionalized local orders, these settlements are not self-rein-
forcing; they depend on ongoing negotiation among actors
(Buchanan et al., 2023). Furthermore, multiple, competing
settlements may coexist within an issue field, reflecting
alternative approaches to resolving the focal issue.
This theoretical lens highlights two key features of mar -
ket shaping for sustainability. First, the arenas and objects
of market shaping are likely to be broader when shaping
markets for sustainability. While shaping markets for busi -
ness benefits is largely confined to relevant product/service
markets and their constituent elements, shaping markets for
sustainability suggests that issue fields with their diverse
stakeholders and constantly negotiated field settlements
must also be taken into account. Second, as these issue
fields emerge when the legitimacy of the focal market is
questioned, the varying perceptions of legitimacy across
multiple stakeholders warrant keen attention.
Methodology
Method and research context
The lack of empirical evidence and dedicated theoretical
explanations related to our research questions motivates
a discovery-oriented, interpretivist abductive approach to
theory-building (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007; Mantere
& Ketokivi, 2013; Sætre & Van de Ven, 2021). We adopt
a multi-case study method, which is particularly appro -
priate for addressing ‘how’ questions. Prior research on
market shaping, market formation, and market transfor -
mation (Molner et al., 2019; Nenonen et al., 2019) has
frequently employed this approach. More specifically, our
study is a special type of multi-case study—an embedded
case study—where all the cases share a common context
(Scholz & Tietje, 2002). This helps in reducing “noise” in
the empirical material from environmental and contextual
variation—and thus eliminating alternative explanations
(Bechky & O’Mahony 2016).
The multi-case study comprises of B2B product/service
markets that share a common material base in forest-based
resources. Geographically, we limit our study to Finland
and Sweden. The reasoning behind choosing this research
context is six-fold. First, this context enables us to address
the limited attention given to B2B markets in sustainable
marketing research (Kim et al., 2024). Second, forest-based
resources play a key role in addressing the climate crisis
by providing renewable alternatives to fossil fuels and
offering carbon sequestration and storage (Nabuurs et al.,
2022). Third, much of the biodiversity loss occurs in forests
(Nabuurs et al., 2022), while markets connected to forest-
based resources also serve as important economic engines
in Nordic countries, particularly in Finland and Sweden
(Fridén et al., 2024). Fourth, multiple actors are seeking
to shape these markets towards increased sustainability
(Näyhä, 2019; Halonen et al., 2022). Fifth, these shaping
efforts are currently unfolding, allowing us to gather empiri-
cal material in real-time (Dawson et al., 2025). Sixth, the
context presents an empirical surprise or mystery needed
for discovery-oriented abductive theory-building (Alvesson
& Kärreman, 2007; Sætre & Van de Ven, 2021): How is it
that, despite broad agreement on the importance of advanc-
ing sustainability across markets connected to forest-based
resources in the Nordic countries—which are small and
highly consensus-oriented—there remain bitter disagree -
ments regarding how such market shaping should unfold
(Halonen et al., 2022; Dawson et al., 2025)?
Selection of cases
The selection of the cases took place in three steps. First,
we reviewed recent (published in 2018–2022) academic
studies that have discussed markets related to Nordic forest-
based resources and their likely future development. This
review yielded 176 relevant articles and identified eight dis-
tinct markets related to forest-based resources with ongo -
ing market shaping for sustainability that were discussed
in more articles than just one. Second, we reviewed Finn -
ish and Swedish popular press articles (published in 2022),
seeking to identify contemporary sustainability-related
debates, interventions, or tensions linked to these markets.
This review yielded 266 unique and relevant articles, and
uncovered one additional market related to forest-based
resources with ongoing market shaping for sustainability.
It also provided some additional details to the previously
identified eight markets. Web Appendix 1 provides greater
detail regarding the identification and selection of the mar -
kets included in the multi-case study.

Collection of empirical material
To understand how sustainability-related issues are enacted
in multi-stakeholder market-shaping efforts and what forms
such market-shaping efforts take, we collected empirical
material from two main sources: in-depth semi-structured
interviews and secondary materials. We continued empirical
material collection until we reached data, theoretical, and
thematic saturation (Saunders et al., 2018).
First, we conducted in-depth semi-structured interviews
with 100 informants between October 2023 and March
2025 (please see Web Appendix 2 for the interview guide).
The interviewees were initially selected based on the popu-
lar press articles collected during the case sampling phase
(please see Web Appendix 1). For instance, for the forest
management services market, academic articles and popular
press articles mentioned forestry companies, forest man -
agement organizations, industry and forest owner associa -
tions, as well as NGOs as relevant stakeholders to interview.
Popular press articles, in particular, were helpful in identi -
fying specific organizations and individuals to contact. The
identification of interviewees took place over time, allowing
us to follow up on surprising findings during the on-going
interpretation and analysis of the empirical material. We
also employed snowballing to identify additional interview-
ees (Heckathorn, 1997), asking each interviewee for sug -
gestions for additional discussion partners at the end of the
interview.
In terms of the interviewee profile, we sought to include
informants who were particularly knowledgeable about the
market-shaping efforts taking place. Many interviewees
represented stakeholders who were somehow involved in
identified market-shaping efforts (e.g., firms creating new
markets, affected stakeholders in the value chain, NGOs
advocating changes in the markets), but we also sought
interviews with informants that were able to provide a “big
picture” view of one or several cases (e.g., researchers,
financial institutions, industry associations).
Most informants were interviewed alone, but occasion -
ally two or three informants were present in the same inter-
view. The length of the interviews varied between 31 and
117 min, the average being 69 min. In total, the interview
material comprises of approximately 104 h of recorded
interviews. All interviews were transcribed in verbatim in
the language spoken in the interview to avoid translation or
interpretation errors. Table 1 provides information about the
interviewees and the secondary material for each case.
Second, we collected two types of secondary materials
to complement the interviews: (1) policy papers and reports
from official studies commissioned by national or EU-level
public actors in 2022–2025 and (2) newspaper articles dis -
cussing the identified shaping efforts in Finnish and Swedish
popular press in 2022–2025. The policy papers and reports
from official studies were particularly helpful in illuminat -
ing the actions of public officials and politicians. Newspa -
per articles, on the other hand, were helpful in illuminating
the “big picture” as well as the general public opinion—and
sometimes also alternative or even dissident perspectives.
In practice, we started the identification and sampling of
the policy papers and reports by searching the public infor -
mation repositories of the national governments of Finland
and Sweden as well as EU’s. The search period was 2022–
2025 and the search terms are listed in Web Appendix 3. We
read the abstracts of the papers and reports and based on
that decided whether the document in question is relevant
for our study. After this, we expanded our search to immedi-
ately relevant public agencies; for example, it was plausible
that the Swedish Energy Agency would be a relevant pub -
lic agency for the district heating generation case. Hereaf -
ter, we expanded our search to other agencies and earlier
years using two-pronged snowballing. First, if a new public
agency was mentioned in some on the analyzed documents
and/or interviews, when searched their databases using the
same search period (2022–2025) and search terms. Second,
we expanded our search period if the analyzed documents
and/or interviews made references to earlier public policy
papers and reports that appeared crucial for the analysis of
the document/interview in question. For instance, it was not
possible to fully appreciate the Government of Sweden’s
budget bill of 2022 that mentioned bio-CCS (carbon cap -

Analysis of the empirical material
Our analysis of the empirical material was guided by the
principles of abductive reasoning—and particularly its
interpretative branch—and hence we sought plausible
explanations to our research questions (instead of validity
or universal generalizability) through researcher reflexiv -
ity when making theoretical interpretations (Mantere &
Ketokivi, 2013; Sætre & Van de Ven, 2021). Practically,
we employed methodological bricolage approach (Pratt et
al., 2022), which involved combining four overlapping ana-
lytical moves that we tailored to our research questions and
supported on-going researcher reflexivity and sensemak -
ing. As one of the main criteria for evaluating interpretative
abductive research is researcher reflexivity and sensitivity
to the empirical material (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007;
Mantere & Ketokivi, 2013; Sætre & Van de Ven, 2021), we
have included examples on how the empirical material sur -
prised us and encouraged us to reconsider our prior assump-
tions during the four analytical moves in Web Appendix 5.
First, we started analysis of the empirical material by cre-
ating case descriptions of each of the four cases (Eisenhardt
& Graebner, 2007). Initially, these case descriptions drew
predominantly on secondary sources of empirical material
(newspaper articles, policy papers, research reports), which
were used to create tentative overarching descriptions of the
cases: the characteristics of the focal market, descriptions
of the main actors, and summaries of the initially identi -
fied shaping efforts. We wrote in-depth reflection notes after
each interview, which were initiated immediately after the
interview and supplemented while transcribing the audio
files. We used these interview notes to constantly update our
case descriptions.
Second, we conducted open coding of all the interviews,
and those secondary materials deemed particularly infor -
mative during the creation of case descriptions (Corbin &
Strauss, 2008; Locke et al., 2020). We did not code the field
notes from observations, as the role of the observations was
to ensure sufficient contextual immersion (Stremersch et al.,
2023) rather than serve as empirical material to be analyzed.
However, we consulted the field notes during the later ana -
lytical moves. During the open coding, we focused on three
aspects: (1) the activities conducted to shape market and the
market-shaping efforts to which they were plausibly linked,
(2) the issues that actors identified with, and (3) the possible
interactions or links between issues or between issues and
the initial market-shaping efforts. The coding was conducted
by three members of the research team, who all used NVivo
software to facilitate the coding. NVivo is a qualitative data
analysis software that supports systematic organization, cod-
ing, and comparison of large sets of textual, audio, and visual
data. Throughout the coding, the three researchers constantly

Third, we carried out within-case analysis to illuminate
plausible mechanisms that explain how sustainability is
enacted in multi-stakeholder market-shaping efforts (Sætre
& Van de Ven, 2021; Cornelissen & Werner, 2026). For each
case, we initially outlined all the shaping efforts present in
the market, ranging between four and five in all cases. We
described these shaping efforts by mapping out the shaping
activities used as well as identifying the overall aim of the
effort and the actors involved. Next, we turned our atten -
tion to the actors. We mapped the core issues central to each
actor and identified the exchange field(s) in which they were
embedded. After this, we outlined the issue field relevant to
the case (e.g., “Sustainable Forest Management” in case 1),
the core shaping object in this issue field (e.g., forest man -
agement methods in case 1), and the current constellation
type (e.g., unified in case 1). The final step in this analytical
move involved identifying which issues were manifested in
each shaping effort—and which were subdued or excluded
from it— analyzing how the shaping effort related to the
core shaping object, and articulating how “sustainability”
was enacted in the shaping effort. The results of this third
analytical move are detailed in the Web Appendices 6–10.
Four, we conducted cross-case analysis to identify what
forms sustainability-oriented multi-stakeholder market-
shaping efforts take to find common patterns related to the
mechanisms explaining how sustainability is enacted in
such market-shaping efforts (Eisenhardt, 2021). In practice,
this was done by creating visualizations and tables of all
the cases, covering the exchange and issue fields, shaping
efforts, and the relationships between fields and shaping
efforts. Comparing and contrasting the four case specific
visualizations and tables allowed us to cluster the identi -
fied market-shaping efforts from all cases into a typology
of issue-field-oriented market-shaping efforts. After this,
we sought to explain the variation across these efforts by
delineating four pathways through which stakeholders enact
sustainability-related issues in shaping efforts.
We took several steps to increase the trustworthiness of our
analysis and the plausibility of our findings (Sætre & Van de
Ven, 2021). First, we collected empirical material both in real
time (interviews) and retrospectively (secondary materials) to
mitigate the biases of the interviewees and the producers of
secondary materials. Second, we interviewed informants rep-
resenting various perspectives (stakeholders, functions, levels
of seniority) to each case. This variety of viewpoints further
mitigated the biases of individual interviewees and provided
holistic, triangulated accounts of all identified shaping efforts
and issues. Third, we carried out member checking (Lincoln
& Guba, 1985): Each interviewee was given the opportunity
to review and comment on their interview transcript, and
we provided them with several updates about our evolving
research process. Fourth, the entire research team of four met
regularly (once or twice a month) for lengthy meetings or
research retreats (length ranging between three hours and five
days) to constructively critique the emerging interpretations
of the empirical material (Sætre & Van de Ven, 2021). Fifth,
the research team composition was designed to ensure trust-
worthiness and plausibility: All members of the research team
had comprehensive and complementary pre-understanding
of the empirical context (markets related to forest-based
resources), and each member of the team represented differ-
ent but complementary theoretical schools of thought.
Findings
This section presents the findings of our multi-case study on
market shaping for sustainability by heterogeneous stake -
holders. Based on our abductive cross-case analysis, we
first identify four types of multi-stakeholder market-shaping
efforts. We then explain variation in their intended market-
level outcomes by delineating four pathways through which
market challengers and incumbents enact sustainability-
related issues in these efforts, depending on the legitimacy
gap they perceive. Detailed within-case analyses that pre -
ceded the cross-case analysis are presented in Web Appen -
dix 6, with summaries in Web Appendices 7–10.
Four types of multi-stakeholder market-shaping
efforts for sustainability
All four studied markets feature numerous sustainability-
related market shaping activities by stakeholders ranging
from large corporations to small-scale entrepreneurs, from
industrial interest organizations to NGOs, and from policy -
makers to environmental activists. Some of these activities
take the form of conventional marketing practices, such as
using advertising and public relations to influence custom -
ers and to position specific offerings as sustainable alterna -
tives. However, many shaping activities extend beyond the
traditional marketing mix as they are carried out by a wide
range of stakeholders and aimed at influencing the broader
market system rather than individual customer decisions.
These activities include advocating and campaigning for
specific sustainability issues to a wider variety of audiences,
advising or educating other stakeholders, developing new
business models and technologies, and engaging in policy -
making processes across regional, national, international
levels. Actors also engage in activities that shape markets
by creating and diffusing standards, such as new industry
practices and calculation methods, that define how sustain -
ability issues are understood and addressed.

Our field-level analysis connects these isolated activi -

have emerged at the intersection of the focal market and
adjacent exchange fields. Across our four cases, all identi -

climate change and biodiversity loss (see Web Appendices
7–10). Within these issue fields, field settlements—nego -

objects. The field settlements in our cases were foundational
in defining the appropriate practices underlying markets:
the forestry method (Case 1), construction method (Case 2),
heat generation method (Case 3), or carbon removal method
(Case 4).

(Case 1), the long-term issue field of ‘sustainable forestry’

approaches to addressing contested sustainability issues.

the stakeholders behind the effort. In this effort type, these

them (e.g., rotational forestry or burning as a heat genera -

activities that improve these settlements in response to sus -

to reinforce rather than alter the focal market and its local
order.

Targeted reform  The second market-shaping effort type
seeks to reform the focal market by promoting alterna -

a settlement in the first place. These efforts are settlement-
specific in their orientation to influence field settlements,
meaning that they aim to promote specific, alternative
field settlements that serve the interests of the stakeholders
behind the effort.
Both challengers—stakeholders embedded in alternative
exchange fields—and incumbents can drive targeted reform
efforts. In the case of challengers’ case, multiple, slightly dif-
ferent versions of the target settlement may be promoted due
to limited coordination. This pattern appears in Effort 3 in the
forest management services market (Case 1; Web Appendix
7). Here, challengers seek to replace rotational forestry with
alternative methods that avoid clear-cutting, the proposing
slightly different solutions, such as continuous cover forestry
or close-to-nature forestry. This variation reflects the hetero-
geneity of stakeholders, who are typically embedded in social
movement exchange fields that bring together actors from
multiple domain around shared concerns.

within markets for forest management services (Case 1; see
effort 4 in Web Appendix 7) and district heating generation

Sustainability enactment in market-shaping efforts:
Pathways and propositions
All the identified shaping effort types engage with the sus -
tainability issues central to each issue field and draw on
these issues to justify the market-level outcomes they pur -
sue. However, these outcomes vary widely and, in some
cases, directly oppose one another. To explain this varia -
tion in the intended market-level outcomes of the identified
shaping efforts types, we abductively specify four pathways
of sustainability enactment in such efforts (see Fig. 1). Two
pathways capture sustainability enactment by market chal -
lengers and two by market incumbents. Because market
challengers and incumbents are primarily embedded in dif -
ferent exchange fields, they interpret and respond to issues
based on different local orders (i.e., institutionalized under-
standings and practices), which leads them to hold distinct
issue sets (see details in Web Appendices 7–10). An issue
set denotes the collection of interrelated issues that stake -
holders recognize and act upon.
For an issue field to emerge, challengers’ issue sets must
include sustainability-related issues that are not initially
prominent in the focal market and are therefore typically
absent from incumbents’ issue sets. This does not imply that
incumbents’ issue sets are entirely devoid of sustainability
concerns; rather, they lack those issues that challengers con-
sider important. This misalignment prompts stakeholders
in alternative exchange fields to challenge the legitimacy
of the focal market’s local order, which they view as inad -
equate for addressing issues in their issue sets. These unad-
dressed issues form the basis of the issue field, as illustrated
in Fig. 1 by arrow (1) from the “Issue set X” to the “Issue
field.” Alternative exchange fields may include industry,
professional, or social movement fields, provided that their
local orders differ from those of the focal market.
Although we expected challengers’ and incumbents’ sus-
tainability enactment—and their resulting shaping efforts—
to differ, we also observed variation within each group,
despite stakeholders initially sharing a similar orientation
toward sustainability-related issues. In our abductive analy-
sis, the type of legitimacy gap perceived by the stakeholders
(challengers or incumbents) emerged as a key mechanism
explaining the two distinct modes of sustainability enact -
ment within each group, as well as the differing market-
level aims in their shaping efforts. Following prior work
by Panwar et al. ( 2014), we define a legitimacy gap as the
difference between a stakeholder’s expectations of business

a loss of legitimacy in the eyes of key stakeholders, such

to the market’s long-term viability. In response, incumbents
engage in transformative enactment of sustainability-related
issues from the issue field by reframing them not as external

tence of the market. As a result, these issues become inte -
gral to their issue sets and guide their shaping efforts. For

them to promote regenerative forestry as an alternative to
rotational forestry.

Taken together, our analysis shows that the enactment of
sustainability in market-shaping efforts is conditioned by
stakeholders’ embeddedness in exchange fields and the issue
sets they hold. Crucially, these enactments depend on how
market incumbents and challengers perceive the legitimacy
gap between the focal market and societal expectations.
These differing legitimacy perceptions act as the situated
causal mechanism (Cornelissen & Werner, 2026), which
give rise to four distinct enactment pathways—fundamen -
talist, pragmatic, instrumental, and transformative—which,
in turn, underpin qualitatively different intended market-
level outcomes of shaping efforts. By linking stakeholder-
level enactment pathways to these intended outcomes, our
findings illuminate how heterogeneous interpretations of
sustainability translate into collective—yet often compet -
ing—efforts to maintain, reform, or even contract markets.
This connection provides the basis for our theoretical con -
tribution, which we develop further in the following section.
Discussion
To address the limited attention in marketing research to
meso-level interventions by heterogeneous stakeholders
that seek to shape markets for sustainability, we conducted
a multi-case study on four B2B markets whose impact on
climate change and biodiversity is actively contested. Our
findings identify four types of multi-stakeholder market-
shaping efforts linked to issue fields and delineate four
pathways through which stakeholders enact sustainability-
related issues in these efforts. We show that these pathways
lead to markedly different market-level outcomes, depend -
ing on stakeholders’ field embeddedness and their percep -
tions of the legitimacy gap surrounding the market, and we
formalize this mechanism in a set of research propositions.
In doing so, we advance theory on market-based sustainabil-
ity interventions by linking stakeholder-level enactments
to market-level outcomes and offer actionable insights for
diverse stakeholders on how to navigate multi-stakeholder
market-shaping processes, as elaborated below.
Theoretical contributions
Our work offers three main contributions to the growing lit-
erature on meso-level sustainability interventions, particu -
larly research on market-based interventions such as market
shaping for sustainability. First, we extend market-shaping
research by foregrounding issue fields as critical arenas for
sustainability interventions. Prior work has predominantly
examined market shaping for improved firm outcomes
within discrete product or service markets (Humphreys,
2010; Humphreys & Carpenter, 2018; Nenonen et al., 2019;
Maciel & Fischer, 2020). In contrast, we show that sustain-
ability-related shaping is linked to issue fields that span
multiple exchange fields and bring together heterogeneous
stakeholders. Within these issue fields, field settlements—
agreed-upon frameworks and templates for action—act as
central shaping objects.
This perspective does not only advance market-shaping
research by broadening its analytical lens but also offers a
novel unit of analysis for studying other meso-level sustain-
ability interventions (Nenkov, 2024) such as sustainable
supply chain management (Carter & Liane Easton 2011;
Khan et al., 2021) and extended producer responsibility (Gui
et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2020) by conceptualizing the wider
context in which intervention efforts toward a focal system
emerge and unfold. Importantly, our work illuminates that
such meso-level sustainability interventions are conditioned
by stakeholders’ field embeddedness and the issue sets
they hold. This conceptualization encourages scholars and
practitioners to consider how field embeddedness across
markets (Esbjerg et al., 2024) and other fields (Mountford
& Geiger, 2021) structures and influences meso-level sus -
tainability interventions. In particular, this highlights that
stakeholders’ distinct issue sets—encompassing concerns
such as profit-making, environmental considerations, social
equity—are influenced by the fields in which these stake -
holders are embedded. As such, our findings urge scholars
to move beyond focal system-bound analyses of meso-level
sustainability interventions (e.g., studies focusing on a sin -
gle supply chain or market), and examine how issue fields
introduce, contest, and reconfigure sustainability agendas
and how the various field-spanning intervention efforts
interact with one another.
Second, we enhance the analytical precision with which
collective market-shaping efforts for sustainability are con -
ceptualized. In multi-stakeholder contexts, we define such
efforts as loosely coupled configurations of shaping activi -
ties through which heterogeneous stakeholders seek to
influence field settlements and steer the focal market toward
specific outcomes . While earlier research has focused on
distinguishing market shaping from reactive strategies to
markets and on cataloguing the various activities used to

Humphreys & Carpenter, 2018; Nenonen et al., 2019;
Pontikes & Rindova, 2020), more recent work has begun

advance this work by showing that shaping efforts by het -

intended outcomes and field settlement orientation, rather

These insights also extend prior research on collabora -
tive market shaping—which has largely focused on coor -
dinated efforts among relatively homogeneous actors—by
theorizing how loosely coordinated multi-stakeholder
efforts emerge despite divergent interests and often lim -

coordinated forms of market shaping described in prior
research—whether among competitors (Baker & Nenonen,
2020; Maciel & Fischer, 2020; Beninger & Francis, 2021)
or along a single value chain (Dion et al., 2025)—represent
special cases within a wider landscape of collective multi-
stakeholder market shaping. In contrast to assumptions of
shared intent and coordinated action, stakeholders in our
cases often pursued similar outcomes without direct collab-
oration, contributing through parallel and sometimes even
interfering activities. This highlights the need to reconcep -
tualize market shaping for sustainability as a distributed and
emergent process among heterogeneous actors with mark -
edly different intended outcomes ranging from maintaining,
to reforming, and even contracting markets.
Third, we advance theory on market-based sustainability
interventions by explaining variation in how stakeholders
enact sustainability in such efforts. While prior research
acknowledges that stakeholders interpret sustainability dif -
ferently (Kim et al., 2024) and that tensions exist across
sustainability dimensions (Doganova & Karnøe, 2015;
Keränen et al., 2023; Syväri et al., 2025), it offers limited
explanation for the origins of these differences. We address
this gap by uncovering a situated mechanism—perceived
legitimacy gaps—and the contextual condition that triggers
it—stakeholders’ exchange field embeddedness—that sys -
tematically influence how stakeholders enact sustainability-
related issues in their shaping efforts. These differences give
rise to four distinct enactment pathways—fundamental -
ist, pragmatic, instrumental, and transformative—which,
in turn, generate qualitatively different market-shaping
efforts pursuing different market-level outcomes. By for -
malizing these relationships in a set of research proposi -
tions, we move beyond descriptive accounts of stakeholder
heterogeneity and specify a causal mechanism that links
legitimacy perceptions to both stakeholder behavior and
market-level consequences. In doing so, we explain why
multi-stakeholder efforts to shape markets for sustainability
often diverge—and at times directly conflict—a relationship
that prior research has not theorized.
Practical implications
Incumbent firms confronted by other stakeholders’ efforts to
shape markets for sustainability should pay particular atten-
tion to the broader debates surrounding the sustainability
issues. These debates are unlikely to “go away” until the
underlying sustainability issues are internalized by the mar-
ket. Engaging with these debates requires the ability to inter-
act constructively with highly heterogeneous stakeholders,
many of whom may come from different backgrounds and
hold starkly contrasting views about the market’s future.
This, in turn, may demand the development of new capa -
bilities such as systems thinking, paradoxical thinking, and
democratic thinking (Hillebrand et al., 2015).
When choosing a market-shaping strategy, incumbents
must assess the gap between stakeholders’ expectations
and their own business practices as objectively as possible.
This requires high levels of reflexivity as well as genuine
empathy toward other stakeholders—including an apprecia-
tion that challengers often assess the market to be shaped
at the level of ideals rather than objective facts about its
current state. Reframing the market under debate can also
be productive: taking a genuinely long-term perspective
(e.g., assessing impact over coming decades rather than
years) and/or broadening the assessment from the individ -
ual product or service market to related markets and/or the
wider societal and environmental systems in which they are
embedded to illuminate the multiplicity of consequences
and values at stake.
The appropriate strategy will ultimately depend on the
type of gap between stakeholders’ expectations and incum-
bents’ business practices. When key stakeholders lack full
information about incumbents’ practices or their conse -
quences, incumbents should invest in stakeholder education
together with credible, legitimate partners, and communi -
cate transparently about their own interests and potential
gains from doing so. However, when genuine reformation
of the market is needed to bridge the gap between stake -
holders’ expectations and incumbents’ business practices,
this is likely to translate to transformational changes to
incumbents’ business models. Such fundamental business
model changes are likely to take considerable time but
may be necessary to ensure the firms’ long-term survival.
When pursuing market reformation, it will often be sensible
to build coalitions with suitable challengers such as other
firms, regulators, or NGOs. An illustrative example from
a related context is Danone’s multi-year transition toward
regenerative agriculture practices, which entailed radical

changes to procurement standards, supplier relationships,
and production processes (Bambridge-Sutton, 2023).
Challengers coming from outside the focal market
should assess the gap between the market’s sustainabil -
ity performance and their own expectations—and on that
basis decide whether to pursue market reformation or mar -
ket contraction. Challenger firms offering more sustainable
alternatives should develop a systemic understanding of the
market they seek to shape. Our findings suggest that market-
shaping for sustainability should extend beyond the specific
product or service being sold. While challenger firms may
be primarily interested in commercializing their offerings,
such transactions typically occur at scale only after the
market system has been reconfigured to support their new
products or services. For example, Behr ( 2010) showed
that firms commercializing solar PV (photovoltaic) panels
initially concentrated on improving panel performance but
soon encountered barriers limiting wider adoption tied to
permissions, grid-connection rules, and the number of qual-
ified electricians. Over time, it became evident that substan-
tial progress required changes in these systemic aspects, not
just advances in the product itself. Triggering and scaling
up such systemic changes is often a long-term endeavor—
taking years or even decades—and challenger firms must
be equipped to sustain such efforts. Similar to incumbents
seeking to reform their markets, challenger firms are likely
to benefit from creating coalitions with other stakeholders
such as regulators, NGOs, or even incumbent firms pursu -
ing market reformation. Some challenger firms may instead
pursue market contraction if they consider that the focal
market cannot be sufficiently reformed to meet their sustain-
ability ideals. However, this pathway is realistically avail -
able only to those firms able to pivot their business toward
other product and service markets.
For regulators, our findings suggest four implications.
First, regulators should broaden their perspective and recog-
nize that, in addition to correcting market failures, their role
also includes proactively shaping markets for sustainabil -
ity (Mazzucato, 2022). Second, regulatory efforts appear
most effective when they focus on clearly defined aspects
of sustainability (e.g., carbon dioxide emissions) and take
the form of relatively simple interventions that affect mul -
tiple product or service markets (e.g., carbon taxes) or target
a specific market (e.g., requiring a minimum share of sus -
tainable aviation fuel blends). Third, although such focused
interventions may yield meaningful results, sustainability is
a multi-dimensional phenomenon, and many markets will
require regulatory approaches that address more complex
aspects of sustainability, such as biodiversity. In agriculture,
for example, biodiversity-related regulation may include
requirements for crop rotation or maintenance of pollina -
tor habitats. In such cases, regulatory efforts should also
consider targeting relevant business and use practices (e.g.,
farming practices), which may provide a more powerful
lever than regulating individual product or service mar -
kets. Fourth, incumbents seeking to maintain their current
markets often possess considerable lobbying power—and
vested interests—to oppose sustainability-related regula -
tion. Regulators should take this resource and power imbal-
ance into account when interpreting feedback on proposed
regulation: those opposing change and seeking to maintain
status quo are likely to submit numerous, well-developed
consultation responses, whereas challenger firms, NGOs,
and activists may not even be aware that the consultation
is taking place.
Finally, NGOs and activists  may find the sustainability-
oriented market-shaping effort types presented in Table 2
as a useful guide for strategizing their actions. While many
NGOs and activists may naturally—and justifiably—gravi -
tate toward market contraction, our findings suggest that
such efforts often struggle to gain traction with core play -
ers, including regulators, due to the economic sacrifices
involved. Thus, at least in the short to medium term, more
effective progress may come from aligning with stakehold -
ers engaged in shaping efforts aimed at reforming the mar -
ket. For example, NGOs confronting overconsumption in
fashion have promoted contraction-oriented initiatives such
as “Buy Nothing on Black Friday,” yet more wide-reaching
results could be achieved by collaborating with reform-ori -
ented stakeholders, such as certification bodies advancing
circular textile standards. Engaging with market reformation
efforts opens three interesting opportunities for NGOs. First,
it provides an avenue for raising the salience of the underly-
ing sustainability issue—which in turn can bolster parallel
contraction efforts. Second, NGOs are often uniquely posi -
tioned to influence social norms and templates for action
and can use this influence to push reformation efforts toward
more ambitious sustainability outcomes. Third, NGOs can
deliberately divide roles among themselves, with some
organizations supporting reformation efforts while others
continue to pursue market contraction. This combination
may intensify pressure on incumbents engaged in main -
tenance efforts and ultimately produce more sustainable
outcomes—even where full market contraction remains an
unrealized goal.
Limitations and future research
Although our empirical setting focuses on markets grounded
in forest-based resources, the framework we develop is not
context-specific. Because it builds on the generalizable
concepts of exchange and issue fields, it supports analytic
rather than empirical generalization (Yin, 2010). As such,
the framework applies to settings in which B2B markets

1 3
Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science
are embedded in institutionally mature societal contexts
and become contested through issue fields that mobilize
heterogeneous stakeholders. Such conditions are common
in primary and extractive industries central to environmen -
tal sustainability, such as agriculture, fisheries, energy, and
mining, where production systems are deeply intertwined
with contested ecological concerns. The framework also
extends to social sustainability contexts in which issue fields
form around social concerns such as modern slavery in sup-
ply chains (Han et al., 2024), where stakeholders contest
the legitimacy of prevailing practices and seek to reshape
system-level arrangements.
There are, however, four important boundary condi -
tions (Whetten, 1989; Tsang & Colpan, 2025) that limit the
transferability of our findings to other contexts. First, all the
focal markets in our study are B2B markets, that is, the core
exchanges in these exchange fields take place between B2B
actors. This means consumer agency within them is chan -
neled primarily through public opinion, political choice, and
engagement in civil society organizations in our context. In
B2C markets, consumers also have other opportunities to
shape markets for sustainability such as product/service
choices (Olson, 2013), consumer boycotts (Lasarov et al.,
2023), and other forms of consumer activism (Gollnhofer et
al., 2019). B2C markets may also offer alternative interven-
tion pathways to companies and regulators such as extended
producer responsibility (Gui et al., 2016) and offering
reverse logistics (Budak, 2020).
Second, our explanation is dependent on the presence
of a sustainability-related contestation that gives rise to an
issue field. Thus, our framework and typology should not
be extended to contexts where sustainability-related market
shaping occurs without contestation—or to contexts where
the issue field is not linked to sustainability. By exten -
sion, it remains unclear whether the explanatory utility of
legitimacy gaps (Panwar et al., 2014) in predicting which
stakeholders gravitate toward the same market-shaping
efforts extends beyond sustainability-related contexts. Other
explanations—such as power asymmetries (Bogomolova et
al., 2026), technological trajectories (Kumar et al., 2026),
or policy windows (Schiff, 2023)—may be more relevant
when stakeholders shape markets for reasons unrelated to
sustainability.
Third, we conducted our research in the Nordic coun -
tries. These relatively small, high-income, liberal democ -
racies are characterized by stable societal institutions,
which provide a conducive context for the emergence and
persistence of exchange fields (e.g., industry, professional
and social movement)—the starting point of our theoreti -
cal framework—even though these fields themselves may
vary in their degree of institutional elaboration and coher -
ence. Nordic societies are also characterized by high levels
of interpersonal and institutional trust. This context may
affect how stakeholders perceive legitimacy gaps and, con -
sequently, how they enact sustainability in market shap -
ing. Future research should therefore examine whether the
legitimacy gaps and enactment pathways identified here
also characterize lower-trust or institutionally less stable
contexts.
Four, our conceptualization of market shaping empha -
sizes strategic intent at the market level. This focus nec -
essarily de-emphasizes emergent or unintended forms of
market change. Sustainability-related shifts also arise from
the compound effects of incremental adjustments and ongo-
ing organizational routines and not only from deliberate
attempts to shape markets; such dynamics fall outside the
scope of our analysis.
Beyond these boundary conditions, our cross-sectional
research design limits our ability to observe how market-
shaping efforts and sustainability enactments evolve over
time. The study relies mainly on retrospective interviews
and secondary materials, and, for policymakers and consum-
ers, heavily on documentary sources due to limited access
to direct informants. This constrains our ability to capture
real-time changes, shifting interpretations of sustainability,
and iterative adjustments among stakeholders.
Our findings suggest that field embeddedness, issue sets,
and perceived legitimacy gaps influence stakeholder align -
ment in efforts to shape markets for sustainability. As these
concepts originate in the institutional fields literature—
largely independent from the market-shaping scholarship—
they open up interesting opportunities to cross-pollinate
research meso-level sustainability interventions. To date,
streams such as sustainable supply chain management,
extended producer responsibility, and market shaping have
evolved largely in isolation. For example, future research
could systematically compare different meso-level sus -
tainability interventions to assess the effectiveness of sus -
tainability policies and how this efficacy may vary across
contexts. A more fine-grained analysis of how field embed-
dedness conditions shaping efforts, for instance through
structural network analysis (Borgatti et al., 2009), would
further nuance our understanding of related mechanisms.
Finally, future research should also explore whether and how
legitimacy gaps can be strategically managed to accelerate
sustainability transitions. For instance, how might firms,
policymakers, or NGOs influence legitimacy perceptions
among stakeholder groups to foster convergence around
sustainability goals? Investigating both market and market-
ing strategies aimed at altering legitimacy judgments could
provide actionable insights for both theory and practice.
Our typology of shaping efforts and our identified enact-
ment pathways raise important questions about how these
efforts play out and interact over time. First, additional

research is needed to establish whether the identified mech-
anism is robust to changes in actors’ field embeddedness
over time. Second, future research is needed to establish
whether and how contraction, reformation, and maintenance
efforts coexist, conflict, or reinforce one another within the
same field. Do aggregated effects of multiple shaping efforts
accelerate or hinder sustainability transitions? Longitudinal
studies and system-level modelling could shed light on the
interplay between competing or complementary market
strategies, offering a more holistic view of market-shaping
processes in complex, multi-stakeholder environments.
Finally, while most market-shaping research emphasizes
market expansion or reform, our study identifies market con-
traction as a distinct and underexplored strategic aim behind
shaping efforts. Future research could examine the condi -
tions under which market contraction becomes a viable or
necessary strategy, as well as the specific activities through
which actors pursue it. Questions worth exploring include:
What competitive, or societal triggers lead stakeholders to
advocate market contraction? How can stakeholders stra -
tegically orchestrate contraction in ways that minimize
negative consequences and reduce the risk of backlash?
Addressing these questions would deepen our understand -
ing of market shaping when sustainability requires reducing
consumption or phasing out harmful markets.
