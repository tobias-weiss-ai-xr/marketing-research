Abstract

Background: Consumer rights protection remains an important issue, as many consumers still lack awareness of
and willingness to defend their consumer rights. Consumer education is expected to improve awareness and
encourage action in defending consumer rights.
Objective: This study examined the relationship between the AIDA components (awareness, interest, desire,
action) and the struggle to defend consumer rights, with a focus on the role of consumer behavior education.
Method: This study involved 204 university students divided into two groups: 45 students who had taken a
consumer behavior course and 159 students who had not. Data were analyzed using descriptive analysis and Partial
Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM).
Results: Students who had taken a consumer behavior course showed higher levels of awareness, interest, desire,
and action related to defending consumer rights compared to those who had not. Statistically significant differences
between the two groups were found specifically in awareness and action, while differences in interest and desire
were not significant. Interest was significantly associated with both desire and action in both groups.
Conclusion: These findings suggest that consumer behavior  education is associated with greater awareness and
action in defending consumer rights. Governments and related institutions are therefore encouraged to strengthen
consumer education efforts, although further research using longitudinal or experimental de signs is needed to
confirm causal relationships.

Keywords: AIDA; consumer education; consumer rights; Structural Equation Modeling

1. Introduction
In 2023, the Indonesian Consumers Foundation (YLKI) reported 943 consumer complaints, with the
financial services sector accounting for 38.2% of the total cases (Bisnis.com, 2024). The primary issues
in this sector were related to online loans, which comprised 50% of financial service complaints. The e-
commerce sector also recorded a significant share of complaints (13.1%), including refund problems
(23.4%), fraud or breach of agreement (14.8%), and undelivered goods (5.5%). Other frequently
reported sector s included telecommunications (12.1%), housing (6.7%), and electricity (2.4%)
(Liputan6.com, 2024).
Consumers are often in a disadvantaged position due to weak bargaining power, partly because they
do not fully understand their rights or tend to regard this lack of understanding as a common, low -
priority issue. When problems or losses arise from the use of a particular product, consumers frequently
find it difficult to obtain redress from business actors, reflecting an imbalance of bargaining power
between the two parties. Several factors contribute to this condition, one of the most significant being
consumers' low awareness of their rights. According to Mazlan et al. (2014), consumer education plays
a key role in teaching consumers about their rights. Enforcement of consumer protection in Indonesia
requires support from all stakeholders, including consum ers themselves, given that low awareness of
consumer rights remains a primary source of consumer vulnerability ( Ayuningtyas, 2015). Consumers
must therefore be aware of, able to exercise, and willing to defend their rights.
Although Indonesia's legal framework for consumer protection has become increasingly
comprehensive, many business actors continue to exploit legal loopholes to maximize profit, often to
the detriment of consumers and in disregard of consumer protection principles. Compounding this issue,
a considerable proportion of Indonesians still lack a clear understanding of the role and importance of
consumer protection laws (Widiarty , 2024). Strengthening the enforcement of consumer protection in
Indonesia therefore depends not only on the availability of adequate regulation, but also on consumers'
willingness to recognize and actively assert their rights when aggrieved. This raises a  question that
remains underexplored in the consumer protection literature: what process leads a consumer from simply
being aware of their rights to actually taking action to defend them?
A recent systematic review of three decades of consumer protection research offers relevant context
for this question. Goel et al. (2025), analyzing 137 top-ranked academic manuscripts published between
1991 and 2021, found that the dominant research theme s in this field have centered on advertising, e -
commerce, and financial services, with consumer awareness typically treated as an isolated outcome
rather than as one stage within a broader behavioral  process leading to action. This points to an
underexplored area: the sequential psychological pathway through which consumers move from
awareness of their rights to concrete rights-defending behavior.
The AIDA model (Awareness, Interest, Desire, and Action) offers a useful framework to examine
this pathway. Originally developed to explain how promotional stimuli move a potential customer
through sequential stages toward a purchasing decision (Ghirvu, 2013), AIDA conceptualizes response
to a stimulus as a cumulative process rather than a single event. Applied to the context of consumer
rights, this study treats the struggle to defend consumer rights as an analogous sequential process.
Consumers must first become aware of their rights, develop interest in understanding them further, form
a desire to see them upheld, and ultimately take concrete action when those rights are violated. Bhowal
(2010) applied this framework to examine students' engagement in defending their rights through word
of mouth and found varying levels of awareness, interest, desire, and action among respondents, but did
not examine what factors explain these variations, leaving open the question of whether such variation
is associated with exposure to consumer education.
To address this gap, the present study uses exposure to a university-level consumer behavior course
as a proxy for formal consumer education, comparing students who have and have not taken the course.
The course covers consumer purchasing behavior and cons umer protection, including a specific focus
on consumer rights, providing a suitable setting to examine whether structured education is associated
with differences across the AIDA pathway.
Building on this gap, the objectives of this study are to: (1) compare the levels of awareness, interest,
desire, and action in defending consumer rights between students who have and have not taken the

consumer behavior course; (2) examine the influence of consumer education on each AIDA dimension;
(3) test the sequential relationships within the AIDA model (awareness to interest, interest to desire, and

This study contributes to the literature in two ways. First, it applies an AIDA -based Consumer
Response Index (CRI) to measure consumer responses to their rights as a staged process rather than a
single outcome, addressing the gap identified by Goel et al. (2025) regarding the limited attention given
to behavioral pathways in consumer protection research. Second, by comparing students exposed and
not exposed to formal consumer education, this study provides evidence on whether structured education
is associated with differences across the entire AIDA pathway or onl y at specific stages, with
implications for how consumer education curricula and policy interventions are designed. The findings
are expected to support the development of data -driven consumer protection policies and inform
improvements in consumer educati on curricula, thereby strengthening consumers' position relative to
business actors.
2. Literature Review
2.1. AIDA Concept
According to Li and Yu (2013), the AIDA concept, Awareness, Interest, Desire, and Action, was
first developed by E. St. Elmo Lewis in 1898 to explain how promotional stimuli move a potential buyer
toward a purchase decision. Ghirvu  (2013) further explains that the model's underlying purpose is to
attract the attention of potential clients, arouse their interest and desire, and ultimately prompt action.
Johar et al. (2015) similarly position AIDA as one of the core models of consumer  decision-making,
consisting of a sequential progression from awareness to interest, desire, and action.
Within this hierarchy, awareness represents the cognitive stage in which a consumer's attention is
first captured; interest reflects active engagement with information about a product, service, or issue;
desire marks the point at which the consumer becomes convinced that the object of the message fulfills
a need; and action is the resulting behavioral response (Ghirvu, 2013). Importantly, however, Fazri
(2009) cautions that in practice, only a limited number of messages succeed in moving consumers
through all four stages, as many consumers stall at the cognitive or affective level without progressing
to actual behavior. This observation is central to the present study: it suggests that awareness alone is
an insufficient indicator of consumer empowerment, and that the transitions between AIDA stages, not
merely their individual levels, warrant closer empirical attention.  This is precisely the gap this study
addresses in the context of consumer rights.
2.2.  Consumer Education
Prior studies converge on the finding that consumer education is positively associated with rights
awareness, but diverge in how they explain the mechanism and consequences of this relationship.
Mahardika and Yuliati (2022) found that urban high school students demonstrated greater awareness of
consumer education materials than their rural counterparts, attributing this gap to unequal access to
information. Simanjuntak (2021) reported an even more severe pattern at the population level, with 87%
of respondents lacking awareness of their consumer rights altogether. Widyastuti and Prastitya  (2020)
offer a behavioral explanation for the consequences of this gap: consumers with limited rights awareness
tend to tolerate product or service flaws rather than contest them, a tendency that sustains a cycle of low
accountability among businesses.
Taken together, these studies establish a consistent pattern: unequal exposure to consumer education
corresponds with unequal awareness, and low awareness is linked to passive, non -assertive consumer
behavior. However, none of these studies directly test w hether increased awareness translates into the
later behavioral stages of interest, desire, and action; their outcome variable is typically awareness or

general knowledge itself, not a staged behavioral response. This is where studies on the downstream
effects of consumer education become relevant. Hasibuan and Judijanto  (2023) found that individuals
with higher levels of consumer education showed a stronger understanding of their rights and
responsibilities, which translated into more informed purchasing decisions, suggesting a link between
education and behavioral outco mes, though not analyzed through a staged framework. Sánchez and
Martínez (2020) extend this further, showing that well -informed consumers are more likely to engage
with and hold brands accountable actively, indicating that awareness can, under some condit ions,
escalate into assertive action. Mazlan et al. (2014) similarly argue that consumer education specifically
teaches consumers about their rights and responsibilities as part of the consumption process, while Johar
et al. (2015) note that consumer behavior, shaped by such education, affects each stage of the purchasing
decision process and, by extension, consumer responses to exposure.
Collectively, this body of literature establishes that consumer education improves rights -related
awareness and is associated with more assertive consumer behavior, but leaves open how this
improvement unfolds across the sequential stages between awareness and action, a question the AIDA
framework is well suited to answer, and which this study examines directly through the following
hypotheses:
H1a: There is a significant difference in awareness of consumers' rights between those students who
followed the course of consumer behavior and those who did not follow the course.
H1b: There is a significant difference in interest in fighting for consumers' rights between those
students who followed the course of consumer behavior and those who did not follow the course.
H1c: There is a significant difference in the desire to fight for consumers' rights between those
students who followed the course of consumer behavior and those who did not follow the course.
H1d: There is a significant difference in the action of fighting for consumers' rights between students
who took the consumer behavior course and those who did not.
2.3. Relationship of AIDA’s Components
Although AIDA was originally formulated to describe purchasing behavior, its underlying logic, that
a behavioral response progresses through a cognitive stage (awareness), an affective stage (interest and
desire), and a conative stage (action), is not inherently limited to commercial transactions. Chrzanowska
and Chudzian (2014) demonstrate that the AIDA model is an appropriate method for analyzing the
impact of exposure on consumer decision -making more broadly, while Johar et al. (2015) treat AIDA
as a general model of consumer decision-making rather than one confined to purchase decisions alone.
Defending consumer rights can be understood as a parallel form of consumer decision -making: a
consumer exposed to information about their rights must similarly become aware of the issue, develop
interest in it, form the desire to act on it, and ultimately decide whether to take concrete action, such as
filing a complaint or seeking redress. On this basis, the present study applies the AIDA framework
analogously to the domain of consumer rights advocacy, treating the struggle to defend consumer rights
as a staged behavioral outcome rather than a single, undifferentiated response.
Whether the AIDA stages are in fact sequentially and causally linked, however, remains a point of
disagreement in the literature. Naomi (2011) argues that the link between awareness and action is often
absent because underlying knowledge is not used as a r eference point when consumers actually take
action, implying that awareness and action are only loosely coupled. In contrast, Johar et al. (2015) find
that awareness, interest, desire, and action exert a significant joint effect when tested together, and Lau
et al. (2021) similarly report that a consumer's awareness of a product or service shapes subsequent
action, mediated by interest and desire. Chrzanowska and Chudzian (2014) support this sequential view,
treating AIDA as a valid method for tracing how exposure translates into decision-making outcomes.

This unresolved tension, whether AIDA components function as a tightly sequential chain or as
loosely connected stages, is central to the present study's design. Rather than assuming the sequential
logic of AIDA holds uniformly, this study empirically test s each transition (awareness to interest,
interest to desire, desire to action) separately, and compares these relationships between students with
and without exposure to formal consumer education. This approach allows the study to determine not
 only whether consumer education influences each AIDA stage, but also whether education
strengthens the linkages between stages, directly addressing the debate between Naomi (2011) and Johar
et al. (2015) and Lau et al. (2021). This leads to the following hypotheses:
H2a1: Consumer education significantly influences awareness of consumers' rights.
H2b1: Consumer education significantly influences the interest in fighting for consumers' rights.
H2c1: Consumer education significantly influences the desire to fight for consumers' rights.
H2d1: Consumer education significantly influences the action of fighting for consumers' rights.
H2a2: Consumer education significantly influences awareness of consumers' rights.
H2b2: Consumer education significantly influences interest in fighting for consumers' rights.
H2c2: Consumer education significantly influences the desire to fight for consumers' rights.
H2d2: Consumer education significantly influences the action of fighting for consumers' rights.
H3a1: Awareness of consumers' rights significantly influences the interest in fighting for consumers'
rights.
H3b1: Interest in fighting for consumers' rights significantly influences the desire to fight for
consumers' rights.
H3c1: The desire to fight for consumers' rights significantly influences the action of fighting for
consumers' rights.
H3a2: Awareness of consumers' rights significantly influences the interest in fighting for consumers'
rights.
H3b2: Interest in fighting for consumers' rights significantly affects the desire to fight for consumers'
rights.
H3c2: The desire to fight for consumers' rights significantly influences the action of fighting for
consumers' rights.
3. Method
3.1. Research Design, Location, and Sampling
This study employed a cross -sectional survey design involving undergraduate students at IPB
University. Because this design captures data at a single point in time, the relationships examined in this
study are interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. IPB University was selected as the study
location because its Department of Family and Consumer Sciences offers a consumer behavior  course
explicitly covering consumer protection and consumer rights, providing a relevant setting to compare
students with and without exposure to this course.
The sampling technique used was stratified random sampling, with strata defined by semester level
(four, six, and eight) and by course -taking status (having taken or not having taken the consumer
behavior course). The number of samples was determined using the Slovin formula, based on a
population of 10,046 undergraduate students across the three semester levels, yielding a minimum
required sample of 200 respondents. The proportion of samples drawn from each stratum was calculated
proportionally to the size of each subpopulation. Of 230 questionnaires distributed following the

randomization process, 215 were returned, and 204 were retained for analysis after data cleaning,
comprising 45 students who had taken the consumer behavior course and 159 who had not.
It should be noted that the two comparison groups are unequal in size, reflecting the natural
proportion of students who elect to take the consumer behavior course relative to the wider student
population, rather than a deliberate balancing of group sizes.  Because course enrollment  was not
randomly assigned, the two groups may differ not only in course exposure but also in other
characteristics, such as semester level, prior consumer complaint experience, general media exposure,
or pre -existing legal knowledge, that could independen tly influence awareness, interest, desire, and
action toward consumer rights. Stratification by semester level was applied during sampling to reduce
imbalance on this specific factor; however, other potential confounding variables were not directly
controlled in the sampling design. This is acknowledged as a limitation of the quasi -experimental
comparison used in this study, and findings regarding differences between the two groups should be
interpreted with this caveat in mind.
3.2. Variables and Measurement
This study distinguishes between two related but conceptually separate constructs, which are clarified
here to avoid ambiguity. The first is course -taking status, a binary grouping variable used to divide
respondents into the two comparison groups (student s who have and have not taken the consumer
behavior course). This variable serves as the basis for the between-group comparisons tested in H1a to
H1d. The second is consumer education exposure, a latent construct used as an exogenous variable in
the structural model, operationalized through two dimensions: the type of media accessed for consumer
rights information (six binary items, 1 = ever, 0 = never, covering print media, electronic media, internet,
leaflets/banners/posters, direct counseling by NGOs/private organizations/government/universities, and
advertisements on consumer rights) and the intensity of exposure to these media (six items on a four -
point scale: 3 = often, 2 = occasionally, 1 = rarely, 0 = never), adapted and modified from Simanjuntak
et al. (2014). This distinction matters because consumer education exposure, as measured here, captures
general information exposure across multiple channels and is not limited to the formal consumer
behavior course; the course itself is treated separately as the basis for group comparison. This dual
operationalization allows the study to examine both between -group differences associated with formal
coursework (H1a to H1d) and the influence of broader consumer  education exposure on each AIDA
dimension within each group (H2a to H2d).
The remaining constructs were measured as follows. Awareness of consumer rights refers to
respondents' awareness of the rights stipulated in the Republic of Indonesia Law Number 8 of 1999
concerning Consumer Protection, measured using three open -ended items scored from 0 to 8. Interest
in defending consumer rights and desire to defend consumer rights were each measured using four items
on a five -point Likert scale (5 = strongly agree to 1 = strongly disagree). Action to defend consumer
rights was measured u sing eight binary items (1 = ever, 0 = never). All items for the AIDA -related
constructs were adapted and modified from Naomi (2011). Total scores for each construct were
transformed into a 0 to 100 index using the formula:

3.3. Instrument Testing
The instrument was pretested on 20% of the research sample to assess reliability and validity prior
to full-scale data collection. Reliability, assessed using Cronbach's alpha, was as follows: media type (α
= 0.643, six valid items), exposure intensity (α = 0.736, six valid items), awareness of consumer rights
(α = 0.797, three valid items), interest in defending consumer rights (α = 0.850, four valid items), desire
to defend consumer rights (α = 0.759, four valid items), and action to defend consumer rights (α = 0.753,
ten valid items).
3.4. Data analysis
Data were analyzed using both descriptive and inferential techniques with SPSS for Windows and
Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS -SEM) using SmartPLS  2.0. PLS-SEM was
selected for three main reasons relevant to this study's data structure. First, PLS -SEM does not require
a multivariate normal distribution of the data, which is appropriate given the mixed measurement scales
used across constructs. Second, PLS-SEM performs well with relatively small subgroup sample sizes,
such as the course -taking group in this study, where covariance -based SEM would be less suitable.
Third, PLS -SEM is a variance -based, prediction -oriented approach, which aligns with this  study's
objective of examining associative pathways between consumer education exposure and the sequential
AIDA dimensions, rather than confirming a strictly theory-driven causal model.
Independent samples t -tests were used to examine differences in awareness, interest, desire, and
action to defend consumer rights between students who had and had not taken the consumer behavior
course (testing H1a to H1d). PLS -SEM was used to examine whether consumer education exposure,
awareness, interest, and desire were significantly associated with subsequent AIDA dimensions,
specifically interest, desire, and action to defend consumer rights, separately for each group (testing H2a
to H2d and H3a to H3c). Model estimation followed a two-step procedure: first, a measurement model
(outer model) evaluation confirming the reliability and validity of the indicators, followed by a structural
model (inner model) evaluation to test the hypothesized relationships (Gye-soo, 2016).

effect across the full AIDA sequence, and should not be interpreted as evidence that consumer education
drives the complete awareness-to-action pathway in either group.

5. Discussion
Mazlan et al. (2014) state that consumer education is one of the primary channels through which
consumers learn about their rights. Consistent with this view, the present study found significant
differences in awareness of consumer rights and in action to defend consumer rights between students
who had and had not taken the consumer behavior course, supporting Hypothesis 1a and Hypothesis 1d.
Hasibuan and Judijanto  (2023) similarly found that well -informed consumers are more likely to
advocate for their rights and assert their interests. Zannettino et al. (2016) further suggest that consumer
education raises awareness of the broader psychosocial effects of consumer decisions, which may
encourage participation in advocacy-related behavior. The awareness and action findings of this study
are broadly consistent with this line of reasoning, though the cross -sectional design does not allow the
direction of this relationship to be established with certainty.
In contrast, no significant difference was found between the two groups in interest or desire, and
Hypotheses 1b and 1c were not supported. This partial pattern is consistent with Fazri's (2009)
observation that relatively few consumers progress through al l stages from awareness to action, and
suggests that the effect of formal consumer education in this sample was concentrated on the endpoints
of the AIDA sequence (awareness and action) rather than distributed evenly across all four stages.
The most theoretically significant finding of this study, however, is that awareness showed no
significant influence on interest in either group, and Hypotheses 3a1 and 3a2 were both rejected. Rather
than treating this as a minor or null result, this findi ng should be read as evidence that awareness of
consumer rights and the motivation to act on that awareness are governed by distinct psychological
mechanisms. This interpretation is consistent with a well-established line of research on the gap between
knowledge and behavior, sometimes referred to as the knowledge-behavior gap or attitude-behavior gap,
which holds that possessing information about an issue does not, by itself, generate the attitudinal or
motivational states needed to act on it. Within the framework of the Theory of Planned Behavior (Ajzen,
1985), this gap can be explained by the fact that behavioral intention, and by extension interest and
subsequent action, is shaped not only by knowledge but also by attitudes toward the behavior, perceived
social norms, and perceived behavioral control, factors that a purely cognitive measure of awareness
does not capture. In the context of this study, students may have known that consumer rights exist and
what those rights are (as reflected in t he awareness score) without necessarily believing that asserting
those rights was socially expected, personally worthwhile, or practically feasible, any of which could
suppress the translation of awareness into interest.
This interpretation gains further support when situated within the Indonesian consumer rights
context. Fibrianti et al. (2023) found that, compared to consumers in other national contexts, Indonesian
consumers tend to display a weaker orientation toward self-protection even where general awareness of
consumer issues exists. Relatedly, Flora et al. (2024) point to a broader submissive cultural orientation
among Indonesian consumers, in which social norms around avoiding confrontation with business actors
may discourage consumers from acting on their rights even when they are cognitively aware of them.
Read together with the present findings, this suggests that the awareness -interest gap observed in this
study may reflect not merely an individual psychological phenomenon but a culturally reinforced
pattern, in which awareness of one's legal rights does not readily translate into the social confidence or
perceived legitimacy needed to actively pursue them. This possibility cannot be tested directly with the
present data, but it offers a plausible explanation for why a formal, information-based intervention such
as a university course was sufficient to raise awareness and, indirectly, action, yet insufficient to shift
the intermediate motivational stage of interest.
This finding carries an important implication for how the AIDA model should be interpreted in the
context of consumer rights advocacy: rather than functioning as a uniformly sequential chain in which
each stage directly produces the next, the model appears to operate as two loosely connected segments
in this population, a cognitive stage (awareness) that education can influence relatively directly, and a
motivational-behavioral segment (interest, desire, action) that is more strongly self -reinforcing

. Simanjuntak et al. / TIJAB (The International Journal of Applied Business), 10( 3) (2026) 325–343 339

internally (as shown by the strong interest-to-desire and desire-to-action linkages discussed below) but
not reliably triggered by awareness alone. This qualifies the framework's applicability and suggests that
consumer education interventions aiming to pr omote rights -defending action may need to address
motivational and normative barriers directly, rather than relying on the assumption that raising
awareness will automatically cascade into interest and action.
Within this motivational -behavioral segment, interest showed a significant positive influence on
desire, and desire showed a significant positive influence on action, in both groups, supporting
Hypotheses H3b1, H3c1, H3b2, and H3c2. These findings are consistent with Lau et al. (2021), who
reported that consumer awareness of a product or service shapes subsequent action through the
mediating stages of interest and desire, and with Pelse et al. (2015), who found that once consumers
reach the stage of desire fulfillment accompanied by a positive response, they are more likely to proceed
to action. The consistency of this segment across both groups, regardless of course -taking status,
suggests that once interest in defending consumer rights is present, the progression tow ard desire and
action follows a broadly similar pattern among students, independent of formal educational exposure,
reinforcing the interpretation that the critical bottleneck in this behavioral  process lies earlier,
specifically, in the transition from awareness to interest, rather than later in the sequence.
The Consumer Response Index (CRI) results illustrate the practical extent of this bottleneck. Among
the 45 students who had taken the consumer behavior course, 25 students (55.6%) were categorized as
aware of their consumer rights, yet only 7 ultimately reported taking action. Among the 159 students
who had not taken the course, 53 students (33.3%) were categorized as aware, of whom only 15 reported
taking action. In both groups, the majority of students who reached the awareness stage did not proceed
to action, consistent with Fazri's (2009) conclusion that only a limited proportion of consumers complete
the full progression from awareness to action, and  consistent with the awareness -interest disconnect
identified above as the most likely point of attrition in this pathway.
Taken together, these findings indicate that this study provides partial, rather than complete, support
for the AIDA model as applied to consumer rights advocacy. The model's explanatory value in this
context lies primarily in the later stages of the process (interest, desire, action), while its assumption that
awareness directly generates interest does not hold in this population. Future research should examine
what specific attitudinal, normative, or contextual factors mediate the transition from awarenes s to
interest among Indonesian student consumers, potentially incorporating constructs from the Theory of
Planned Behavior directly, in order to better explain this gap and inform more effective consumer
education strategies.
6. Conclusions
6.1. Conclusions
This study found that awareness, interest, desire, and action to defend consumer rights were higher,
on average, among students who had taken the consumer behavior  course compared to those who had
not. Significant differences between the two groups, however, were found specifically in awareness and
action; no significant difference was observed in interest or desire. This indicates that consumer
education is associated with differences in the earliest and final stages of the AIDA process, rather than
uniformly across all four dimensions.
The structural model results provide partial, rather than complete, support for the AIDA model in the
context of consumer rights advocacy. Interest was significantly and positively associated with desire,
and desire was significantly and positively associated with action, in both groups, indicating a consistent
behavioral pathway once interest is present. Consumer education showed a significant positive
association with desire and action among students who had taken the consumer behavior  course, and
with awareness among students who had not. In contrast, awareness showed no significant association
with interest in either group. This is the most important finding of this study: awareness of consumer
rights, by itself, does not appear sufficient to generate the interest needed to translate that awareness into
rights-defending behavior. Consumer education initiatives that focus solely on raising awareness may

340 Simanjuntak et al. / TIJAB (The International Journal of Applied Business), 10( 3) (2026) 325–343

therefore be insufficient; efforts to strengthen consumers' motivation and confidence to act on what they
already know may be equally, if not more, important.
The Consumer Response Index (CRI) results illustrate this attrition in practical terms. Among
students who had taken the consumer behavior course, only 7 of the 25 students categorized as aware of
their consumer rights ultimately reported taking action to defend them. Among students who had not
taken the course, only 15 of the 53 students categorized as aware did so. In both groups, the majority of
students who reached the awareness stage did not progress to action, reinforcing the conclusion that
awareness alone is not a reliable predictor of rights-defending behavior.
Because this study used a cross -sectional survey design, these findings describe associations rather
than causal effects. It cannot be concluded that consumer education directly causes changes in
awareness, interest, desire, or action; the observed differences may also reflect other characteristics that
differ between students who elect to take the consumer behavior course and those who do not, such as
prior interest in the subject, semester level, or general disposition toward proactive behavior.
6.2. Theoretical and Practical Contributions
The results of this study make an important contribution to the AIDA model (Awareness, Interest,
Desire, Action) in the context of consumer rights protection. The AIDA model shows that awareness is
an important first step in building interest, which then d rives desire, and finally results in action. This
study emphasizes the relevance and validity of the model by showing that consumer education
significantly influences each dimension in AIDA, especially in building students' desire and action to
fight for consumer rights.
Consumer education has a significant influence on the interest and desire to act to fight for consumer
rights. This supports the idea that consumer education can raise awareness and create greater interest in
consumer protection. Furthermore, the SEM results show a positive relationship between interest, desire
and action, which corroborates the progressive flow of the AIDA model. Thus, these findings extend
the application of the AIDA model to the consumer education context, while underscoring the
importance of integrating AIDA theory in the design of educational programs to increase awareness and
proactive action towards consumer rights.
The findings of this study provide important implications for the management of consumer education
and consumer rights protection. First, educational institutions need to improve the effectiveness of the
consumer behavior course curriculum by adding interactive elements such as real case simulations or
advocacy projects. This aims to not only raise awareness but also encourage students' interest and desire
to act to fight for consumer rights. Second, the government and consumer protection organizations need
to utilize effective public communication channels, such as social media and educational seminars, to
raise consumer awareness
Third, to encourage the transition from awareness to action, educational institutions and related
organizations can provide hands-on experiences, such as consumer advocacy programs or easy access
to report violations of consumer rights. In this way, studen ts can be empowered as active agents of
change in consumer protection. Finally, collaboration between the government, educational institutions,
and consumer protection organizations is needed to design data-driven programs that support increased
consumer awareness, interest, and action in a more integrated manner. This strategy is important to
ensure that consumer education not only generates awareness, but also real action in the fight for
consumer rights.

6.3. Limitation and Recommendation
This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design does not allow causal claims to be
made about the effect of consumer education on the AIDA dimensions; the relationships identified
should be interpreted as associations. Second, the sam ple was drawn exclusively from undergraduate
students at a single university (IPB University), which limits the generalizability of the findings to other
student populations, universities, or the broader Indonesian consumer population. Third, the two
comparison groups differed substantially in size (45 versus 159 students) and were not randomly
assigned to course -taking status, meaning that unmeasured differences between the groups, beyond
consumer education exposure itself, may partly explain the observed differences.
Future research should consider several directions. Longitudinal or experimental designs would
allow stronger causal inference regarding the effect of consumer education on consumer rights
awareness and behavior . Studies involving multiple universities or a broader, non -student population
would improve the generalizability of findings beyond a single institutional context. Given this study's
central finding that awareness does not readily translate into interest, future research should examine the
specific attitudinal, normative, or confidence -related barriers that prevent aware consumers from
becoming motivated to act, potentially drawing on frameworks such as the Theory of Planned Behavior
to identify these mechanisms more precisely. Finally, future consumer education programs and curricula
may benefit from moving beyond information delivery alone to include practical components such as
complaint-filing training, applied legal literacy exer cises, and activities designed to build consumers'
confidence in asserting their rights directly with business actors, since these elements more directly
target the interest and desire stages that formal education alone did not appear to influence in this study.
