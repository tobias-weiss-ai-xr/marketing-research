Abstract: Health-oriented food choices are increasingly important to health economics,
public health policy, and preventive health management because dietary markets influence
individual well-being, access to healthier products, and the societal costs of unsustainab le
consumption. Existing scholarship provides insufficient evidence on whether private health
motives, ecological attitudes, ingredient scrutiny, expectations of responsible producers, and
perceived market constraints play different roles in the adoption a nd regularity of organic
consumption. The study evaluates the behavioral and market factors associated with
becoming an organic -food consumer and moving towards more frequent consumption.
Evidence was obtained from a Polish -language internet questionnaire administered during
May 2024 to 91 participants, comprising 71 consumers, 19 non -consumers, and one
unclassified respondent; women represented 62.6%, and 60.4% were aged 19–25 years. The
empirical design integrated frequency summaries, multiple -response as sessment, scale
reliability diagnostics, a paired Wilcoxon signed -rank procedure, Spearman rank
associations, cumulative ordinal logistic modeling, binary logistic modeling, Firth bias -
reduced estimation, and 5,000 case -resampling replications, implemented in RStudio using
R version 4.4.0 (4.4.0). Organic products were consumed at least several times monthly by
65.6% of respondents with valid classifications, whereas 21.1% reported no consumption;
health motivation averaged 4.32 compared with 3.55 for ecolo gical motivation, and the
paired test yielded 767.5, a statistical probability below one-thousandth and a rank-biserial
correlation of 0.783. Affordability emerged as the principal market -access concern: 76.4%
identified high prices as a barrier; the price -reduction measure averaged 4.51 points; and
promotion, labeling, availability, and assortment formed a coherent facilitation scale with
a reliability coefficient of 0.76. In the main ordinal specification, support for environmentally
responsible producer conduct multiplied the likelihood of entering a higher consumption
category by 3.30; confidence limits at 95 per cent extended between 1.30 and 8.37, with
statistical probability equal to 0.012, while the bias -reduced binary model additionally
linked ingredient scrutiny to consumer status by a factor of 1.85, bounded between 1.08 and
3.36 at the 95 per cent level, with statistical probability equal to 0.025. These results support
health-policy and health -management initiatives that combine affordability, cr edible
certification, accessible retail provision, and producer accountability, while future inquiry
using larger probability -based panels should assess whether such interventions reduce
barriers to healthier, environmentally sustainable diets.

INTRODUCTION

Current food production and dietary
arrangements impose interconnected ecological,
health, and financial burdens, placing everyday food
choices within contemporary sustainability policy.
World Bank estimates attribute close to 16 gigatons
of annual emissions to the agrifood economy,
representing roughly 30% of the worldwide total
(Sutton et al., 2024 ). The societal consequences
extend substantially beyond climate damage. The
Food and Agriculture Organization of the United
Nations (FAO, 2023)  assessed the hidden health,
ecological, and social burden generated by current
food systems at more than USD 10 trillion annually,
or almost one -tenth of worldwide economic output.
Unhealthy diets account for the dominant share of
these losses, whereas emissions, nitrogen release,
land conversion, and water exploitation account for a
further substantial share. Sustainable transformation
must therefore e ncompass agricultural organization,
commercial food provision, and individual dietary
conduct rather than remain confined to energy or
manufacturing policy.
Certified ecological production offers one
potential means of improving biodiversity, soil
condition, water protection, input efficiency, and
agricultural resilience. Its territorial coverage across
EU member states, however, remains markedly
below the level envisaged in current policy. Eurostat
(2026) reported that certified ecological cultivation
accounted for 10.9% of utilized farmland in 2024,
compared with the policy ambition to reach 25% by
2030. Meeting that objective would require a
pronounced acceleration in sectoral development.
Under the Orga nic Action Plan, demand formation
and confidence in certified products constitute one
principal area of intervention, together with farm
conversion and value -chain reinforcement
(European Commission, 2021 ). The European Court
of Auditors (2024)  likewise concluded that stronger
market conditions are required to make conversion
economically attractive to producers. It warned that
existing measures are insufficient to support
commercial development within the sector.
Food purchasing also has a direct preventive -
health dimension. Every day dietary decisions
influence exposure to perceived food-safety risks, the
quality of habitual nutrition, and the longer -term
burden of preventable diet -related conditions. From
this perspective, the organic segment is relevant not
merely as an environmental market niche but as a
setting in which individuals seek products associated
with reduced synthetic inputs, clearer production
standards, and closer alignment with personal well -
being. Health consciousness and concerns about
chemical residues have repeatedly been identified as
important drivers of organ ic purchasing, although
favorable perceptions do not necessarily result in
sustained behavioral change. Whether health-related
expectations are translated into actual purchasing,
therefore, depends on their interaction with
ingredient scrutiny, confidence in producers, and the
economic accessibility of the available product offer.
This interaction places organic food choice within the
field of health economics because individual behavior
is conditioned by prices, information asymmetries,
and unequal capacity to obtain products perceived as
conducive to well -being. The analysis does not
assume that organic certification itself guarantees
superior health outcomes; rather, it examines how
perceived health value, food -related risk avoidance,
and market accessibi lity shape consumer behavior
within a preventive-health context.
A preventive -health perspective also requires a
distinction between initiating a potentially healthier
choice and maintaining that choice over time.
Consumers may enter the organic market due  to
concerns about pesticides, preservatives, food quality,
or production methods. However, continued purchasing
can remain constrained by price premiums, narrow
product ranges, unclear labeling, and doubts about the
credibility of environmental or health claims. Such
frictions are relevant to health management because
they may interrupt beneficial dietary routines and
create unequal opportunities for people with different
financial resources or levels of market access. Previous
evidence indicated that willingness to pay is sensitive to
perceived benefits, product attributes, and the
magnitude of the organic premium, while
communication and trust affect whether favorable
intentions become observable behavior. Public
intervention and managerial practice must
consequently address not only consumer awareness but
also credible certification, transparent information,
affordability, and retail conditions that make health -

Viewed jointly, these ecological, preventive, and

favorable p references. Health considerations may
encourage consumers to choose organic products,
while environmental attitudes may connect
individual food choices with broader sustainability
goals. Although consumers may view organic food
favorably, repeated purchasing can still be disrupted
by cost pressures, restricted retail access, insufficient
product variety, doubts about authenticity, and
uncertainty over whether an item is genuinely
organic.
The health-economic relevance of the study lies in
the interaction be tween preference formation and
the constraints governing access to health -oriented
goods. Price premiums, information asymmetry,
certification uncertainty, and uneven retail
availability can affect both the private capacity to act
on perceived health benef its and the distribution of
such opportunities across consumer groups.
Although the present study does not estimate
healthcare savings or clinical outcomes, it examines
behavioral and market mechanisms that may
influence the feasibility and continuity of p reventive
food choices.
The analysis consequently considers whether
health motives, ecological orientations, scrutiny of
product composition, expectations regarding
producer accountability, and perceived commercial
conditions differentiate entry into the organic market
and subsequent purchasing frequency. Particular
attention is also given to the obstacles and enabling
measures that may facilitate health -oriented dietary
behavior by making market provision more
accessible and trustworthy.
LITERATURE REVIEW
Health-oriented consumer behavior provides a
useful conceptual foundation for understanding the
development of organic food markets and their
relevance to preventive health management. From this
perspective, food purchasing is not merely a routine
consumption decision but a form of self -protective
behavior through which individuals attempt to reduce
exposure to pesticides, artificial additives, genetically
modified ingredients, and other perceived food -
related risks. Research based on the Health Belief
Model indicated that organic food intentions are
shaped by perceived susceptibility to health threats,
the expected severity of health threats, perceived
benefits and barriers, and consumers’ confidence in
their ability to adopt healthier diets (Alagarsamy et al.,
2023). Studies of health and wellness foods have
similarly shown that health consciousness, nutritional
concerns, and beliefs about product safety influence
purchasing decisions, particular ly when consumers
associate food choices with long -term physical well -
being (Ali et al., 2021). Favorable views of organic food,
perceived social expectations, and consumers’
confidence in their abil ity to make such choices can
strengthen the translation of health -related concerns
into purchasing intentions (Chu, 2018; Kamboj et al.,
2023; Paul & Rana, 2012 ). Organic consumption can
therefore be interpreted as a market -based
component of preventive health behavior. However,
the strength of this relationship depends on whether
consumers p ossess sufficient knowledge, resources,
and access to transform health intentions into
repeated purchases.

The health orientation of organic consumers is
reflected not only in stated motivations but also in
broader dietary patterns and lifestyles. Organic food
consumers tend to display healthier dietary profiles,
greater nutritional awareness, and stronger
adherence to health -promoting practices, suggesting
that organic purchasing is frequently embedded in a
more comprehensive lifestyle rather than
undertaken as an isolated action ( Goetzke & Spiller,
2014). Health consciousness and nutritional
knowledge can improve consumers’ ability to assess
product attributes, interpret food information, and
recognize the preventive value of diet. However, their
effects may differ between product categories and
consumer groups ( Bazzani et al., 2020 ). Evidence
from Poland indicates that attention to specific
nutritional information, such as salt content on food
packaging, is associated with demographic
characteristics, health interests, and consumers’
involvement in food decisions ( Bryła, 2020 ).
However, consume rs do not always evaluate health
risks rationally. Consumers’ judgments about food
safety can be shaped by limited knowledge, habitual
routines, mental shortcuts, and a mismatch between
perceived danger and observed buying patterns
(Wang et al., 2018 ). Therefore, the capacity of
organic-food systems to support preventive well -
being is determined not merely by the importance
individuals attach to health but also by whether they
can interpret nutritional and product -related
evidence and translate it reliably into routine
purchasing decisions.
Health considerations coexist with a broader set
of motives that determine whether positive
perceptions develop into market behavior. Early
studies of organic consume rs identified personal
health, food quality, naturalness, taste, and avoidance
of chemical residues among the most important
purchasing motives, while price, limited availability,
and insufficient information remained substantial
constraints ( De Magistris & Gracia, 2008 ). More
recent evidence confirms that health benefits,
perceived food safety, and product quality continue
to be central drivers of organic purchasing. However,
environmental responsib ility and social values have
become increasingly important ( Gundala & Singh,
2021). Consumers’ willingness to purchase also
depends on perceived value, which reflects an
implicit comparison betwe en expected health,
quality, and ethical benefits and the monetary or non-
monetary sacrifices required to obtain organic food
(Lim et al., 2014 ). Positive beliefs and attitudes may
consequently r emain insufficient when consumers
perceive organic products as expensive,
inconvenient, or only marginally different from
conventional alternatives, as demonstrated by
evidence from the Czech market ( Zagata, 2012 ).
Among Generation Z, satisfaction, trust, perceived
value, and favorable attitudes are particularly
important for converting initial organic purchases
into repurchase intentions ( Bhutto et al., 2023 ).
Young consumers may also respond positively to
novel organic products, but their acceptance depends
on whether health -oriented innovations remain
understandable, attractive, and compatible with their
consumption habits ( Radzymińska & Jakubowska,
2019).
Because many health -related characteristics of
organic food cannot be directly evaluated before
purchase, labels, brands, packaging, and retail
communication perform an essential  informational
function. Organic labels may reduce uncertainty and
improve product evaluations, but their effects vary
across global, local, and private brands because
consumers interpret certification in combination
with brand credibility and familiarity (Bauer et al.,
2013). Sustainability attributes can add value to
organic and local foods, yet consumers do not
necessarily distinguish clearly between
environmental, territorial, ethical, and health-related
claims ( Annunziata & Mariani, 2018 ). Trust is
therefore a critical intermediary between
information and preventive purchasing behavior,
particularly when consumers question the
authenticity of certification, production standards, or
retailer claims ( Hamzaoui Essoussi & Zahaf, 2009 ).
Green marketing practices, store reputation, product
presentation, and access to credible i nformation can
strengthen organic purchases by making perceived
health and environmental benefits more visible at the
point of sale (Nguyen et al., 2019). Retailers may also
contribute to healthier lif estyles by improving
product availability, organizing health -oriented
assortments, and supporting consumer education,
which is especially relevant in markets such as
Poland, where purchasing environments influence

The health orientation of organic consumption
increasingly intersects with environmental and
altruistic values. Environmental concern can
strengthen the influence of positive attitudes on
organic purchasing because consumers may view
organic food as simultaneously beneficial to personal
well-being and less harmful to ecosystems ( Cachero-
Martínez, 2020 ). This connection is important for
preventive health management because
environmental quality, sustainable agriculture, and
human health are perceived as mutually dependent
rather than separate consumption domains. Climate -
oriented dietary changes similarly require
consumers to connect individual food decisions with
collective environmental outcomes , although
engagement differs according to values, worldviews,
and willingness to modify established habits (de Boer
et al., 2016 ). Attitudes toward organic food have
consequently been linked to b roader support for
sustainable development and responsible production
systems ( Oroian et al., 2017 ). Pro -environmental
values may also encourage altruistic consumer
behavior, in which organic purchases are justified by
benefits to farmers, animals, future generations, and
society rather than solely by private health gains
(Grzybowska-Brzezińska & Grzywińska -Rąpca,
2025). Nevertheless, sustainability p erceptions do
not produce uniform changes across all food
categories. For example, organic meat and meat
substitutes can evoke different evaluations because
consumers balance health, naturalness,
environmental impact, and product familiarity in
different ways (Siegrist & Hartmann, 2019 ). Organic
markets should therefore be understood as spaces in
which preventive health motives interact with
environmental responsibility, ethical concerns, and
culturally embedded food preferences.
The relative importance of these drivers and
barriers varies across countries and at different
stages of market development. Romanian studies
show that consumers generally associate organic
food with health, safety, superio r quality, and
sustainable development, but purchasing frequency
is constrained by high prices, limited accessibility,
insufficient knowledge, and doubts about
authenticity (A. G. Petrescu et al., 2017; D. C. Petrescu
et al., 2017). Similar patterns have been identified in
Northern Spain, where favorable attitudes coexist
with incomplete knowledge of certification and
differences between consum ers’ declared
preferences and actual behavior ( Rodríguez-
Bermúdez et al., 2020 ). Comparative evidence from
Brazil, the United States, and Europe indicated that
health and quality motives are widely shared.  In
contrast, the intensity of price sensitivity,
environmental concern, institutional trust, and
market accessibility differs substantially between
regions ( Sica & Franco, 2024 ). Within the Visegr ad
countries, health protection and avoidance of harmful
substances are important reasons for purchasing
organic food. At the same time, price, inadequate
availability, and uncertainty about labels continue to
impede regular consumption ( Soroka et al., 2021 ).
The COVID-19 pandemic heightened attention to food
safety, immunity, and personal health, but its market
effects were not uniform. Polish and United Kingdom
consumers differed in their purchasing patte rns and
responses to pandemic -related risks, reflecting
differences in market maturity, income, access, and
established organic consumption habits
(Wojciechowska-Solis et al., 2022 ). These findings
demonstrate that preventive health motivations are
filtered through national retail structures,
socioeconomic conditions, and levels of trust in food
governance.
Previous research has presented organic food
consumption as a multidimensional health -oriented
behavior shaped by perceived health risks,
nutritional knowledge, attitudes, trust, product value,
social influence, and access to credible market
information. T he literature has also shown that
health motivations frequently overlap with
environmental and altruistic values. However, their
translation into repeated purchasing is constrained
by price premiums, availability, information
asymmetry, and the intention –behavior gap.

lifestyles, much of the available evidence is cross -

METHODOLOGY
Research Design and Data Collection

The study employed an exploratory, cross -
sectional quantitative design to examine the
motivations, market conditions , and environmental
attitudes associated with organic food consumption.
Data were collected through a structured Polish -
language online questionnaire in May 2024
(Appendix B). The survey instrument combined
fixed-choice items, five -point response scales , and
questions permitting several selections. It gathered
information on respondents’ familiarity with and use
of organic food, how often and what types of products
they purchased, where they obtained them, how
much they spent, their health -related and ecolog ical
motives, price perceptions, scrutiny of ingredients,
views on producers’ conduct, obstacles to purchasing,
and possible measures for expanding demand.
Demographic information included gender, age,
education, place of residence, self -assessed financial
situation, and household composition.
A non -probability sample of 91 respondents
completed the questionnaire. Respondents were
approached via readily available digital channels,
such as online community pages, established
consumer networks , and individual ly distributed
email or messaging invitations. Eligibility required
residence in Poland and the ability to make or
influence food -purchasing decisions within the
household. As no comprehensive sampling register
could be used, respondents were selected thro ugh
non-random accessibility -based procedures. The
findings are therefore not designed to represent the
Polish population. Given the strong concentration of
younger, urban , and comparatively highly educated
participants, the data should be interpreted as
evidence from an exploratory consumer group rather
than as population -level estimates. Three
participants initially reported not consuming organic
food but subsequently indicated they did so several
times per year and completed all consumer -specific
questions. Because the frequency response provided
more specific behavioral information, these
respondents were reclassified as occasional
consumers. Nineteen participants who reported no
consumption and did not complete the consumer -
specific section were retained as non-consumers. One
participant provided neither consumption status nor
frequency and remained unclassified. The final
analytical classification, therefore, comprised 71
consumers, 19 non -consumers, and one respondent
with an undefined consumption status.

Questionnaire Structure and Measurement
Organic-food behavior was operationalized in two
forms. First, respondents indicated whether they
consumed such products at all. This yes -or-no
response was then integrated with the declared
purchasing frequency to create a four -level outcome
reflecting consumption intensity: category 0 denoted
non-use, category 1 occasional use several times
annually, category 2 purchases several times
monthly, and category 3 consumption on a weekly or
daily basis. The three respondents with an
inconsistent binary response but a positive annual
consumption frequency were assigned to category 1.
The unclassified respondent was excluded from
analyses that required a defined consumption
outcome, yielding 90 valid observations.
For the analysis restricted to the  71 identified
purchasers, buying regularity was represented by a
three-level ordered outcome: 1 indicated purchases a
few times annually, 2 denoted several purchases per
month, and 3 referred to weekly or daily use. In the
additional dichotomous specifica tion, purchasers
received a value of 1, whereas non -purchasers were
assigned 0.
Health and environmental motivations were
measured among consumers using two five -point
items, ranging from 1 (strong disagreement) to 5
(strong agreement). Respondents evaluat ed whether
they selected organic food for its perceived health
and environmental effects. Perceived organic food
price was measured on a five-point scale, with higher
values representing a higher price assessment.
Attention to product composition, includin g
pesticides and other chemical substances, was coded
from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”).
Attitudes towards producer behavior were
measured using questions asking whether food
producers should increase organic production and
act more pro -environmentally. Res ponses were
originally coded as “no ,” “no opinion ,” and “yes.” For
the principal regression models, support for pro -
environmental producer behavior was recoded as a
binary indicator: 1 for “yes,” 0 for “no,” or 0 for “no
opinion.” This transformation was used because only
2 respondents selected the negative response,
rendering the three -category predictor statistically
unstable.
Participants’ perceived financial standing was
represented on an ordered scale extending from the
least favorable to the most favorable category. Sex,
age group, educational attainment, place of residence,
and household structure were included only to
characterize the respondents. Owing to the limited
number of observations, these characteristics were
not introduced jointly into the main regression
specifications, which were deliberately kept compact.
Organic food was recorded according to
respondents’ own interpretation of the term. The
certification status of products obtained from private
gardens, individual farmers, or open markets was not
independently verified. The consumption variables
should therefore be understood as measures of self -
reported organic -food behavior rather than as
measures restricted exclusively to formally certified
products.
Data Screening and Classification of Organic
Food Consumers

were not expected to sum to 100%.
During data preparation, the response category

could increase demand for organic food: lower prices,
better promotion, better labeling, greater availability,
and greater assortm ent. Each item was rated on a

The principal multivariable analysis employed a

estimated using the 90 respondents with a valid
consumption classification.

Consumer-Only Ordinal Models
For the subsample of 71 purchasers, two further
ordinal regression specifications were fitted. The
outcome captured three levels of buying regularity: a
few times annually, several times monthly, and at
least once per week. The first specification examined
health-related and ecological motives, whereas the
second expanded the model by incorporating
scrutiny of product composition and respondents’
perceived financial standing.
The purpose of these models was to distinguish
factors associated with consumption frequency
among existing consumers from those distinguishing
consumers from non -consumers. Nested models
were compared using a likelihood -ratio test, while
relative fit was evaluated using the Akaike
information criterion. The proportional -odds
assumption and numerical convergence were
assessed using the same procedures as for the full -
sample ordinal model.

The results identify a distinction between the
motivational, market, and normative dimensions of
organic food consumption. At the motivational level,
health considerations were considerably more
important than environmental concerns. At the market
level, respondents perceived organic food as expensive
and identified lower price s, wider availability, and
greater assortment as the most promising mechanisms
for increasing demand. At the normative level,
stronger support for environmentally responsible
producer behavior was associated with more intensive
organic food consumption.
The absence of a significant relationship between
perceived price and consumption intensity suggests
that price may function as a broadly recogni zed
structural barrier rather than as a factor that clearly
distinguishes frequent from infrequent consumers.
Frequent consumers may continue purchasing
organic products despite perceiving their prices as
high, whereas less frequent consumers may regard
the same price level as a stronger practical constraint.
This possibility should be examined using
multivariable mo dels that incorporate financial
situation, demographic characteristics , and
attitudinal variables.
Viewed through the green -transition lens,
broader uptake of organic food is likely to depend on
measures that address both consumer behavior and
the conditions prevailing in the marketplace. Health -
based communication may be effective in attracting
consumer attention, while stronger environmental
messaging may be needed to connect private
consumption choices with broader sustainability
outcomes. At the same ti me, policies and business
strategies aimed at affordability, product availability,
credible labeling , and environmentally responsible
production may help convert favorable attitudes into
more regular sustainable consumption behavior.
Because the study used  a small, non -probability
and cross -sectional sample, the findings should be
interpreted as exploratory associations rather than
population estimates or causal effects. The
subsequent multivariable analysis examined whether
the observed association with pr o-environmental
producer expectations remained significant after
accounting for financial situation, price perceptions ,
and ingredient attention.

DISCUSSION
The findings demonstrate that health -related
considerations occupy a central place in organic food
choice. However, they also suggest that health and
environmental motives may operate differently
across stages of consumer behavior. Health
motivation received a significantly higher evaluation
than environmental motivation, with mean scores of
4.32 and 3.55, respectively ( V = 767.5, p < 0.001;
rank-biserial r = 0.783). Likewise, 83.1% of
consumers identified a positive influence on health as
a purchase determinant, whereas 39.4% selected
environmental benefits. This result corresponds with
previous evidence that perceived health benefits,
food safety, naturalness, and avoidance of pesticides
or chemical residues are among the most persistent
reasons for purchasing organic food ( De Magistris &
Gracia, 2008 ; Gundala & Singh, 2021 ). It is also
consistent with Health Belief Model research showing
that perceived health risks and expected benefits can
encourage intentions to adopt organic food choices
(Alagarsamy et al., 2023 ). The prominence of health
considerations, therefore, supports interpreting
organic purchasing as a form of health -oriented
consumer behavior relevant to preventive health
management.
At the same time, health motivation did not
significantly explain differences in purchasing
frequency among existing consumers ( OR = 1.35,
p = 0.228 in the simpler model; OR = 1.35, p = 0.224 in
the extended model). Environmental motivation
showed a stronger positive association and
approached conventional significance in the
extended specification ( OR = 1.55, p = 0.0496).
However, this effect was unstable across models, and
the additional predictors did not improve model fit.
The evidence should  therefore not be interpreted as
demonstrating that environmental motivation is a
robust determinant of purchasing frequency. Rather,
it tentatively indicates that private health
considerations and broader ecological values may
influence different aspects of behavior. Health may
provide an immediately understandable justification
for choosing organic products. In contrast,
environmental concerns may become more relevant
when food purchasing is incorporated into ongoing
lifestyle routines and broader sustain ability
commitments. This interpretation is compatible with
studies showing that environmental concern
strengthens the relationship between favorable
attitudes and organic purchasing, particularly when
ecological values are linked to consumer identity and
repeated behavior (Cachero-Martínez, 2020; de Boer
et al., 2016 ; Grzybowska-Brzezińska & Grzywińska -
Rąpca, 2025 ). However, because the health - and
environmental-motivation questions were answered
only by existing consumers, the present analysis
cannot directly establish whether health motivation
causes initial market entry. It primarily sh ows that
perceived health value is highly salient among

does not always translate consistently into behavior,

biases, and contextual constraints (Wang et al., 2018).

insufficient to sustain regular purchasing without
favorable prices, trusted certification, and convenient
market access. The  binary-model evidence should
remain exploratory, as the bootstrap confidence
intervals included the null value and several

transparency ( Śmigielska, 2013 ). Consequently,

They should combine motivational communication
with structural improvements in affordability,
certification credibility, and retail access.

variation within a small, relatively young, and
comparatively advantaged sample prevents it from
explaining differences between respondents. Price
premiums can create unequal opportuniti es to
maintain food choices perceived as beneficial to
personal well-being, especially among lower -income
households that were underrepresented in the
sample. Future studies should therefore estimate
income gradients, actual expenditure, organic -price
premiums, and demand elasticities rather than
relying solely on general price perceptions. They
should also distinguish certified organic products
from food obtained from gardens, farmers, or
markets that respondents may perceive as natural or
ecological but that lack formal certification.

CONCLUSIONS
The analysis explored whether personal health
concerns, ecological values, scrutiny of food
ingredients, views on producers’ environmental
accountability, and perceptions of market
accessibility influence entry into the organic -food
market and subsequent purchasing regularity. It also
considered which interventions could encourage
more consistent, sustainable dietary behavior during
the green transition.
The empirical work drew on a single -wave
convenience survey comprising 91 participants.
Following data screening, 71 individuals were
classified as organic -food purchasers, 19 as non -
purchasers, and 1 case could not be assigned to either
group. The analytical procedure integrated
descriptive summaries, evaluation of multiple-choice
responses, internal -consistency assessment, paired
Wilcoxon testing, Spearman rank associations,
cumulative-link modeling for ordered outcomes,
separate ordinal specifications for consumers,
standard binary logistic estimation, Firth -corrected
logistic analysis, and bootstrap -based robustness
checks.
Organic food consumption was relatively common
among the surveyed group: 65.6% of respondents
with valid information reported consuming organic
products at least several times per month, where as
21.1% were classified as non -consumers. Health
motivation was substantially stronger than
environmental motivation, with mean scores of 4.32
and 3.55, respectively, and the difference was
statistically significant ( 𝑉 = 767.5, 𝑝 < 0.001, rank -
biserial 𝑟 = 0.783). High price was identified as the
principal consumption barrier by 76.4% of
respondents, while lower prices received the highest
evaluation among demand-enhancing measures, with
a mean score of 4.51; promotion, labeling, availability,
and assortment formed a reliable Market Facilitation
Scale ( 𝛼 = 0.76). In the principal ordinal model,
support for environmentally responsible producer
behavior was associated with 3.30 times higher odds
of belonging to a more intensive consumption
category (95% CI [1.30,  8.37], 𝑝 = 0.012), while the
model as a whole improved significantly over the
intercept-only specification ( 𝜒2(4) = 9.75, 𝑝 =
0.045; Nagelkerke pseudo -𝑅2 = 0.110). In the Firth

95% CI [1.08, 3.36], 𝑝 = 0.025) and support for pro -
environmental producer behavior ( 𝑂𝑅 = 3.75, 95%
CI [1.23, 11.61], 𝑝 = 0.020) remained positively
associated with consumer status. However, the wider

supplementary binary findings should be interpreted
cautiously.
These findings concern consumer perceptions
and self -reported purchasing behavior. They should
not be interpreted as evidence that organic
certification itself produces superior clinical
outcomes or that increasing organic consumption
necessarily reduces the incidence or cost of diet -
related disease.

The findings suggest that policies intended to
expand organic food consumption should combine
affordability measures with improvements in market
access, information credibility, and environme ntal
communication. Public authorities and retailers could
support price -reduction schemes, temporary
incentives, or targeted assistance for lower -income
consumers. At the same time, producers and
distributors should improve product availability,
assortment, and visibility in conventional retail
channels. Clearer certification, traceability, and
labeling standards may help address the reported
lack of trust and difficulty distinguishing organic from
conventional food. Communication strategies should
retain the strong health -related appeal of organic
products while more explicitly linking these private
benefits to environmental protection, sustainable
agriculture, and responsible production. The
persistent association between consumer behavior
and expectation s regarding producer responsibility
further indicates that firms should demonstrate
measurable environmental commitments rather than
rely solely on promotional claims.
The principal contribution to health economics
and management lies in distinguishing per ceived
health-oriented motivation from the economic,
informational, and organizational conditions that
enable it to become repeated behavior. The findings
show that preventive dietary choices cannot be
managed solely through awareness -building:
affordability, credible market information, producer
accountability, and accessible retail provision form
part of the behavioral environment within which
health-related preferences are either maintained or
constrained.
For health economics and management, the
principal practical implication is that health-oriented
food behavior should be evaluated as both an access
problem and a motivational problem. Future policy
analysis should therefore compare the costs,
behavioral effects, and distributional consequences
of alte rnative interventions, rather than assuming
that awareness campaigns or general price
reductions will affect all consumer groups equally.
Several qualifications should be considered when
interpreting these findings. The respondent group was
small, recruited through non-random procedures, and
composed largely of younger, urban, and
comparatively highly educated participants, reducing
the extent to which the estimates can be generalized to
the broader population. Because the evidence was
collected at a single point in time, the analysis supports
only associative interpretation and cannot establish
causality. Reported purchasing behavior may also
contain recall inaccuracies, socially desirable
responses, or inconsistent understandings of what
constitutes organi c food; products sourced from
household gardens, individual farmers , or open
markets, for example, may not have carried formal
organic certification. In addition, some concepts relied
on single-question indicators. At the same time, the
relatively small non-consumer subgroup reduced the
stability of the binary estimates, as shown by the
breadth of the bootstrap intervals.
Subsequent studies should draw on larger
probability-based samples and clearly separate
formally certified organic goods from products that
consumers merely regard as natural or
environmentally friendly. Measurement quality could
be strengthened through the use of validated multi -
item instruments and by linking questionnaire
evidence to observed purchase records or household
spending data. Panel, experimental, and comparative
international designs would also help determine
whether price -support measures, stronger
certification arrangements , and targeted
environmental communication produce durable
changes in organic-food purchasing behavior.
