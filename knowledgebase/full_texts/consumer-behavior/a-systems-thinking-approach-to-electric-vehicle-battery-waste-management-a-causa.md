Abstract
The increasing adoption of electric vehicles in Indonesia has increased the
urgency of managing end -of-life battery waste through a more structured and
sustainable system. This study devel ops a causal loop diagram (CLD) to
examine the dynamics of electric motorcycle battery waste management, with a
focus on the interactions among users, manufacturers, informal actors, and
government institutions. The study synthesizes evidence from academic
literature, policy documents, and media reports to identified variables and
causal relationships, into integrated CLD, and confirms its structure through
triangulation and consistency checks. The uniqueness of this study lies in its
system-thinking approa ch that combines documentary synthesis, causal
modeling, and structural triangulation to capture both formal and informal
pathways in the battery waste supply chain. The conceptual model was further
validated through consistency checking across multiple so urce types and
review of the resulting feedback loops and leverage points. The findings show
that battery waste management is shaped by reinforcing and balancing loops
involving consumer behavior, regulatory strength, formal collection capacity,
informal sector dominance, and manufacturer end-of-life decisions. The model
highlights leverage points for reducing environmental risks and increasing
value recovery, particularly through stronger producer responsibility,
improved formal infrastructure, and targete d incentives for circular pathways.
This study contributes a validated conceptual framework for understanding
battery waste governance and provides a basis for future policy design and
system dynamics simulation.

2.0   METHOD

2.1   Research Design and Multi-Site Selection
This study uses a qualitative systems thinking
framework with Causal Loop Diagram (CLD)
modeling to analyze the electric motorcycle battery
waste management system in Indonesia during the
EoL phase. A multi -site selection (manufac turers,
users, informal actors, and government) is adopted
because system behavior cannot be understood from
a single perspective; each site represents a subsystem
with distinct feedback structures.

2.2   Data Collection and Variables
Data were assembled  from documented practices in
scientific articles, technical reports, and regulations.
Variables are qualitative and conceptual, derived
from literature reviews and policy reports. The
variables were retained based on their consistency
across at least two source categories, ensuring
contextual relevance to Indonesia. Triangulation
integrated findings from academic literature, policy
documents, and field reports to maintain an audit trail
for coding.

3.1   Empirical Data Collection
Empirical evidence assembled from literature and
national regulations r eveals that battery waste
management operates within interconnected but
fragmented frameworks. While formal actors —
manufacturers and licensed recyclers —are central to
Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) schemes,
their effectiveness depends on industrial -scale
infrastructure and robust reverse logistics  [22], [23],
[24], [25], [26],[27].  Globally, system performance
is shaped by how well these actors link manufacturer
decisions with secondary market demand and
regulatory recycled-content requirements [28], [29].
Consumer behavior studies highlight that users are
not passive; they weigh buyback prices, service
convenience (e.g., home pick -up), and trust in the
recycling provider when choosing between channels
[17], [21], [30], [31]. In Indonesia, this choi ce is
complicated by a fragmented network where official
channels (dealers and authorized workshops)
compete with highly accessible but unregulated
"shadow networks" of junk dealers and small
workshops [7],[27], [28].  Despite the rapid push for
EV adoption [1], [19], [30] the absence of dedicated
end-of-life (EoL) management has concentrated
economic and environmental risks within the
informal sector. System dynamics research shows
that minimal enforcement and high purchase prices
for used batteries drive flows into this informal sector,
where manual dismantling causes critical material
loss and serious pollution [27], [28],[33].

Indonesian law currently governs EV battery waste
under general "Hazardous and Toxic Waste (B3)"
categories, lacking the battery -specific EPR
mandates common in the EU, India, and China
[17],[29],[35].  Strengthening this framework
requires not only emission monitoring and technical
standards for recycling facilities [34],[35] but also
the alignment of various technical policies, s uch as
Presidential Regulation 79/2023 and Ministry of
Industry Regulation 6/2022, which focus more on EV
acceleration than on waste management
[36],[37],[38],[39].

The empirical findings suggest that a structure
limited to four actors (producers, users,  informal
actors, and government) creates feedback loops that
consistently undermine circular economy goals. This
context points to a critical systemic need: a fifth
actor—a formal battery management entity. This
entity would function as a dedicated node in the CLD,
integrating informal roles into compliant schemes
and bridging the current gap between policy demands,
manufacturing needs, and market preferences.

4.0   CONCLUSION
This study demonstrates that electric motorcycle
battery waste management in Indonesia is a systemic
challenge that requires navigating interconnected
feedbacks rather than isolated interventions.  By
strengthening the Extended Producer Responsibility
(EPR) principle and restructuring the roles of formal
and informal actors, the government can transform a
growing environmental risk into a resilient circular
economy. Success depends on aligning regul atory
enforcement with attractive, consumer -centric
service networks, ensuring that material recovery
remains economically viable and environmentally
safe as the EV sector continues to expand.
