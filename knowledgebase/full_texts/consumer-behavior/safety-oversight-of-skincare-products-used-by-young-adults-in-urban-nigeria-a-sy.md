Abstract
This study examines the safety oversight of skincare products used by young adults in urban
Nigeria, with a specific focus on Ikeja, Lagos State. Due to social influences, changing beauty
standards, and more market access, young adults are using skincare p roducts more frequently,
which has led to serious public health concerns. This conceptual review summarizes the body of
research on consumer behavior, regulatory frameworks, skincare product safety, and risk factors
related to the use of dangerous products . The ubiquity of unregulated and counterfeit goods, the
availability of dangerous substances like mercury, hydroquinone, and corticosteroids, and the role
of unofficial markets and internet platforms in promoting their dissemination are all given special
emphasis. The study explores further into the oversight role of institutions like the National Agency
for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) and highlights concerns including
weak enforcement, limited consumer awareness, and inadequate monitoring systems. Results show
that consumer knowledge, cultural norms, and market dynamics all have an impact on safety
oversight in addition to regulatory measures. In order to promote product safety and protect the
health of young adult consumers in urban areas, the study emphasizes the significance of improved
regulatory enforcement, improved public awareness, and cooperative stakeholder involvement.

Skincare products
Skincare products are substances or formulations applied externally to the skin with the aim of
cleansing, protecting, moisturizing, treating, or enhancing the appearance of the skin. These
products fall under the category of cosmetics and are designed for application to the skin’s surface
without affecting the body’s internal physiological functions. Unlike pharmaceutical products,
they are not primarily intended to treat or cure medical conditions, although they can have an
impact on overall skin health. (Marlina et al., 2022).
Skincare products include a broad variety of items such as soaps, facial cleansers, creams, lotions,
oils, toners, exfoliating agents, sunscreens, and skin-lightening preparations. These products may
be produced from natural substances, synthetic compounds , or a mixture of both. The safety of
skincare products is largely influenced by the nature of their ingredients, the concentration of
active components, and adherence to approved regulatory standards (Yones & Muthaiyah, 2023).
The world of cosmetics has a rich history that stretches back thousands of years. Picture ancient
Egypt around 4,000 BC, where dark eyeliner was a staple, or imagine European societies applying
foundation and blush to achieve that flawless, pore less look.  Fast forward to the late 19th and
early 20th centuries, and the beauty industry blossomed into a vital career opportunity for women,
particularly African-American women, who pioneered products tailored for diverse hair textures.
Today, the global beauty i ndustry is an astonishing $425 billion powerhouse, encompassing
everything from hair and skincare to vibrant makeup palettes. The tale of beauty is as vibrant and
multifaceted as the array of lipsticks and nail polishes at your favourite makeup counter (Sinha et
al., 2023).
The Nigerian skincare market has a rich history, reflecting a dynamic interplay between traditional
remedies and contemporary cosmetic innovations that have shaped consumer behaviour over time.
Early household staples, such as Stella Pomade and Jet Cream, were widely utilized for
moisturizing and protecting the skin, while Venus Cream, Tura Moisturizing Body Lotion, and
Nixoderm Skin Ointment gained recognition for addressing targeted dermatological concerns,
including dryness, acne, hyperpigmentation, and fungal infections (Zolkin et al., 2023). Alongside
locally manufactured items, global brands such as Vaseline and Morgan’s have maintained a
significant presence, offering an extensive array of creams, lotions, petroleum jelly, and
therapeutic formulations that cater to diverse skin needs (Zolkin et al., 2023).
 More recently, the market has experienced the emergence of specialized products, including skin-
lightening creams, anti -aging serums, sunscreens, and organic formulations, which reflect
evolving beauty standards and heightened consumer awareness (Kaushik et al., 2023). Distribution
channels have expanded beyond traditional markets to include supermarkets, pharmacies, beauty
stores, and online platforms, increasing accessibility for urban populations. The combination of
traditional, imported, and specialize d skincare products highlights the wide variety of options
available in Nigeria. It also points to the important role of regulatory agencies in making sure these
products are safe, effective, and of good quality—especially for young adults (Voica et al., 2023).
The use of skincare products among young adults has grown markedly in recent years. This
increase is largely driven by heightened awareness of personal appearance, the influence of social
media, aggressive marketing strategies by cosmetic companies, and ev olving societal beauty

Young Adults and Skincare Practices
Young adults are generally defined as individuals between the ages of 18 and 35, a stage
characterized by increased independence, social engagement, and heightened self-awareness. This
age group often places considerable importance on personal appearance and is more likely to adopt
various skincare routines to address both cosmetic and dermatological needs (Ngwenya et al.,
2023). Factors such as gender, education level, and occupation influence their skincare behaviours,
as different demographic groups prio ritize specific products and practices to meet their unique
needs (Guarnotta et al., 2022).
Skincare practices among young adults are shaped by a combination of behavioural patterns and
lifestyle habits. Many engage in multi -step routines that involve cleansing, moisturizing, toning,
and occasionally using specialized products such as sunscreens, serums, or skin-lightening creams
(Ngwenya et al.,  2023). The frequency of product use varies, with some individuals applying
products multiple times a day while others follow more sporadic routines. Purchasing habits also
differ, as some young adults prefer shopping at pharmacies, beauty stores, or supermarkets, while
others increasingly rely on online marketplaces for convenience and access to international brands
(Svobodova et al., 2023).
In addition to individual habits, social and cultural influences play a significant role in shaping
skincare practices. Peer recommendations, family traditions, and guidance from friends often
impact product choice, while advertising campaigns and celebrity endorsements create aspirational
standards of beauty (Rathee et al., 2023). Social media platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, and
YouTube have amplified these influences, exposing young adults to global trends, product
reviews, and new brands at an unprecedented pace (Tilikidou & Delistavrou, 2023).
Cultural and generational factors further influence how young adults engage with skincare. In
Nigeria, traditional practices such as the use of shea butter, palm oil, and herbal formulations
coexist alongside modern cosmetic products (Kaushik et al., 2023). Some young adults continue
to rely on these indigenous remedies for daily skin care, while others favour contemporary
international brands perceived as high -quality or trendy. This intersection of traditional and
modern practices reflects the evolv ing be auty standards among urban youth (Ngwenya et al.,
2023). Awareness and knowledge about skincare safety also vary widely within this age group.

Safety of Skincare Products
Safety of skincare products refers to the ability of these products to be used on the skin without
causing harmful health effects. Although skincare items are primarily intended for cosmetic
purposes, certain ingredients may pose risks if used improperly, in excessive amounts, or without
proper testing. Common formulations such as creams, lotions, soaps, and skinlightening agents
may contain active compounds that can cause skin irritation, allergic reactions, or more serious
health outcomes when misused or unregulated (Bernauer et al., 2021).
Several ingredients commonly found in unsafe skincare products have been linked to adverse
effects. For example, mercury and hydroquinone are sometimes present in unregulated
skinlightening creams, despite their known potential to cause kidney damage, neur ological
disorders, and dermal disorders such as ochronosis when used in high concentrations (Bernauer et
al., 2022). Excessive corticosteroids can thin the skin and suppress immune responses, while
certain preservatives and fragrances can trigger allergic or inflammatory responses in susceptible
individuals. These risks are compounded when products are sourced from informal markets or lack
clear labelling information, increasing the potential for misuse (Apte & Pahan, 2023).
In addition to ingredient -related risks, product safety is also influenced by usage patterns and
consumer behaviour. Young adults may engage in practices such as mixing products, applying
multiple formulations simultaneously, or using products beyond their  recommended duration
(Fiume et al.,  2023). The desire for rapid cosmetic results, such as skin lightening or acne
clearance, can further increase exposure to potentially harmful substances. These practices
heighten the likelihood of cumulative skin damage and long-term adverse effects.
Environmental and storage conditions also play a role in skincare product safety. Exposure to
excessive heat, poor storage hygiene, or prolonged shelf life can lead to product degradation or
microbial contamination. Contaminated skincare products may cause  skin infections, rashes, or
inflammation when applied. This risk is particularly relevant in tropical climates, where heat and
humidity can accelerate product spoilage. (Silva & Tamburic, 2022).
Overall, the safety of skincare products extends beyond their intended cosmetic function and
encompasses formulation quality, user awareness, and patterns of application. Understanding these
safety considerations is essential, as unsafe skincare practices can result in both immediate and
long-term consequences for skin health. This underscores the importance of informed product use,
especially among young adult populations who represent a major consumer group in urban areas
(Portugal-Cohen et al., 2023).
Consumer knowledge and awareness play a pivotal role in skincare product safety. Studies have
shown that many young adults lack sufficient understanding of product ingredients, potential risks,
and correct usage methods (Fiume et al.,  2023). Misconceptions about “natural” or imported
products being inherently safe often lead to indiscriminate use, including prolonged application or

Cultural and social factors also affect skincare practices and safety outcomes. Skin lightening, for

of beauty and peer pressure. The pursuit of rapid results often drives individuals to use high
concentrations of active compounds or combine multiple products, exacerbating the likelihood of

shared. Cumulative exposure to harmful ingredients is an additional concern. Even when
individual products are used according to manufacturer instructions, repeated application over time
can lead to chronic effects, such as skin thinning, hyperpigmentation, or sys temic toxicity. These
cumulative effects are particularly concerning when multiple products are used concurrently or
when users alternate between different formulations with overlapping active ingredients (Bernauer
et al., 2022). Monitoring long-term use patterns and educating consumers about the potential for
cumulative harm are essential components of promoting safety.
Preventive strategies are critical to mitigating the risks associated with skincare products. Safe
practices include verifying product authenticity, adhering strictly to usage guidelines, performing
patch tests before full application, and avoiding the com bination of multiple potent products.
Public health campaigns, educational programs, and clear labelling can empower young adults to
make informed decisions, reducing the incidence of skin damage and adverse health outcomes
(Silva & Tamburic, 2022). Encour aging awareness about the role of environmental factors, such
as proper storage and temperature control, further enhances product safety and efficacy.
Finally, research indicates that effective safety oversight requires collaboration between
consumers, regulatory agencies, manufacturers, and public health institutions. While regulations
and quality control measures are necessary, individual behavior and awareness are equally vital in
preventing adverse effects. By fostering a culture of informed product use and responsible
consumption, the risks associated with skincare products can be significantly minimized,
ultimately supporting the long-term health and well-being of young adults in urban areas such as
Ikeja, Lagos State (Portugal-Cohen et al., 2023).

Safety Oversight of Skincare Products
Safety oversight of skincare products refers to the structured processes, regulations, and
monitoring mechanisms established to ensure that products applied to the skin are safe for human
use. It involves the collective actions of regulatory bodies, manufacturers, distributors, and health
institutions to prevent the circulation of harmful or substandard cosmetic items. Central to safety
oversight is the enforcement of standards that guide product formulation, ingredient selection,
manufacturing practices, l abelling requirements, and post -market surveillance. Regulatory

cause harm, that consumers have access to accurate information, and that manufacturers are held
accountable for quality and safety . In environments where oversight is weak, consumers are at
higher risk of exposure to unsafe products, resulting in skin damage and systemic health issues.
Therefore, robust safety oversight is critical for maintaining trust in the skincare industry and
promoting responsible production and consumption practices.

Components of Safety Oversight
Components of safety oversight encompass the various structured elements and processes that
ensure skincare products are safe, properly regulated, and suitable for consumer use. One of the
foundational components is regulatory approval, which involves the assessment and registration
of skincare products before they enter the market. This process typically includes laboratory
analysis to verify ingredient safety, product stability, and compliance with established cosmetic
standards. Another essential compone nt is manufacturing oversight, which focuses on ensuring
that producers adhere to good manufacturing practices, maintain hygienic conditions, and use
quality raw materials (Ferreira, M., et al.,2022). Regulatory authorities often conduct routine and
unannounced inspections of factories to confirm compliance. Labeling requirements also form a
critical part of safety oversight, as proper labels provide consumers with essential information such
as product ingredients, usage instructions, warnings, batch numbers, and expiry dates (Wikipedia
2025). Accurate labeling helps users make informed choices and avoid products that may trigger
allergies or adverse reactions. Market surveillance is another major component, involving
monitoring products already in circulation to identify harmful, counterfeit, or unregistered skincare
items. This surveillance may include random sampling, laboratory testing, and targeted raids on
non-compliant vendors. Post-market monitoring enables the detection of emerging issues that may
not have been apparent during pre -market testing. Enforcement mechanisms such as product
recalls, bans, fines, and legal action ensure that manufacturers and vendors remain accountable
and deter non-compliance (Vieira, D et al., 2024).
Public awareness and consumer education programs are also integral to safety oversight, as they
inform the public about safe product use, how to identify approved products, and where to report

Regulatory Framework for Skincare Products in Nigeria
The regulation of skincare products in Nigeria is anchored on institutional and legal mechanisms
designed to control the manufacture, importation, distribution, and marketing of cosmetic
products. The primary regulatory authority in this regard is the Nati onal Agency for Food and
Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC), which mandates product registration, approves
labelling information, and conducts inspections of production facilities to ensure compliance with
established standards before products are released into the market (Fiume et al., 2023).
Supporting this framework is the Standards Organization of Nigeria (SON), which develops
quality standards and specifications for cosmetic products and raw materials. These agencies
collectively aim to ensure that skincare products circulating within the N igerian market meet
minimum safety and quality requirements. Regulatory control also extends to advertising and
product claims, with measures in place to prevent misleading or unsubstantiated assertions that
may influence consumer decisions (Masood & Zaidi, 2023).
Despite these regulatory provisions, enforcement remains a significant challenge due to the
proliferation of informal retail outlets, street markets, and online platforms where unregistered
products are commonly sold. Limited surveillance capacity and weak  border control further
complicate regulatory efforts (Mardon et al.,  2023). As a result, strengthening institutional
coordination, enhancing monitoring mechanisms, and improving public awareness are essential
for effective safety oversight, particularly i n high-consumption urban areas such as Ikeja, Lagos
State.  To further strengthen regulatory oversight, attention must be given to the capacity-building
of regulatory personnel. Training inspectors, quality control officers, and laboratory technicians
on the latest cosmetic safety standards ensures that evaluations  of products are thorough and
accurate. Adequate staffing, continual professional development, and access to modern testing
methodologies are critical in preventing substandard products from reach ing consumers and
maintaining the integrity of the regulatory system (Rwanda FDA., 2025)
Inter-agency collaboration is another vital component of effective regulation. While NAFDAC and
SON are the primary authorities, coordination with customs services, local government agencies,
and law enforcement helps to control the importation and distrib ution of unapproved skincare
products. Such partnerships enable timely detection of counterfeit products, support market raids,
and facilitate the sharing of intelligence about emerging risks in both formal and informal markets
(Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC), 2024).
The integration of digital technologies into regulatory practices has emerged as a promising
approach to improve compliance and enforcement. Electronic product registration, barcode
tracking, and mobile applications for consumer verification allow regulato rs to monitor products
more efficiently and reduce the circulation of counterfeit items. Digital reporting systems also
empower consumers and healthcare professionals to report adverse reactions or suspected non -
compliant products, creating a feedback loop that strengthens overall oversight public engagement
and education remain central to achieving compliance (Kazan et al., 2023). Regulatory authorities

Risk Factors for Unsafe Product Use Among Young Adults
Risk factors for unsafe product use among young adults are influenced by social, psychological,
economic, and informational factors. One major risk factor is heavy exposure to social media,
where skincare trends spread rapidly without scientific verification. Influencer endorsements often
blur the line between advertising and personal experience, leading to blind trust in products
(Djafarova & Rushworth, 2023). Many young adults lack adequate knowledge about cosmetic
ingredients and their potential side effects. This knowledge gap makes it difficult to assess whether
a product is suitable for their skin type (World Health Organization. 2022)
The desire for quick and visible results increases the likelihood of overusing skincare products or
combining multiple treatments, which can damage the skin and cause adverse reactions (Davis,
Callender, & Feldman, 2023). Acne -related stress during late ad olescence and early adulthood
further heightens vulnerability, as individuals may resort to aggressive treatments to achieve rapid
improvement (Zaenglein et al.,  2021). Easy access to online cosmetic platforms encourages
impulsive buying behavior, and many online marketplaces sell counterfeit or unregulated products
that pose health risks (Djafarova & Rushworth, 2023). Economic limitations may also drive young
adults toward cheaper, low-quality alternatives. Misleading labels, such as “natural” or “herbal,”
can create a false sense of safety, leading consumers to underestimate potential risks (World Health
Organization [WHO], 2022).
Peer pressure and fear of missing out (FOMO) strongly influence young adults’ skincare
purchasing decisions (Djafarova & Rushworth, 2023). Cultural beauty standards promote
unrealistic expectations of flawless skin, which increase the likelihood of engagin g in unsafe
skincare practices (Davis, Callender, & Feldman, 2023). Limited access to dermatological care
discourages professional consultation, leading many young adults to rely on trial -and-error
methods rather than expert guidance (Zaenglein et al., 2024). Poor understanding of proper product
application and dosage, including skipping patch tests, elevates the risk of allergic reactions and
adverse skin effects (World Health Organization [WHO], 2022). Mental health factors, such as
low self-esteem and anxiety, contribute to excessive or unsafe use of skincare products (Fardouly
et al.,  2021). Algorithm -driven content on social media repeatedly exposes users to similar
products, reinforcing purchasing behaviors and normalizing excessive routines. Weak regu lation
of online cosmetic advertising allows exaggerated or misleading claims to persist, further
increasing the risk of unsafe product use among young adults (Goossens & Aerts, 2022).
Limited knowledge of harmful ingredients is a major risk factor for unsafe skincare product use
among young adults. Many young consumers are unaware of potentially harmful chemicals such
as parabens, sulfates, and hydroquinone, as well as their associated side effects, including skin

Misleading marketing terms such as “natural,” “organic,” or “chemical -free” often create a false

dermatitis—are frequently ignored despite their allergenic potential.

assessment processes (World Health Organization, 2022). Young adults r arely consult

may lack scientific credibility (Mardon et al., 2023). Marketing claims such as “clinically proven”

understanding of sunscreen ingredients and sun protection factors may result in insufficient UV
protection and increase d risk of skin damage. Consequently, trial -and-error experimentation
frequently replaces informed decision-making. Limited awareness of harmful ingredients further
impairs consumers’ ability to distinguish safe from unsafe products, leaving young adults
vulnerable to adverse skin reactions, long -term dermatological damage, and exploitation by
aggressive marketing strategies (Davis et al., 2019).
The influence of peers and beauty influencers plays a significant role in shaping young adults’
skincare habits and product choices. Young adults often place greater trust in the opinions of
friends, online communities, and social media personalities than in professional or dermatological
advice (Juliano, 2022). Peer recommendations and shared experiences can create pressure to
conform to prevailing beauty trends, which may result in impulsive or unnecessary skincare
purchases. Social media platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube amplify this influence
through viral content, challenges, and algorithm -driven recommendations that repeatedly expose
users to specific products, reinforcing purchasing behaviours and normalizing excessive skincare
routines. (Pramanik et al., 2021)
The influence of peers and beauty influencers significantly shapes skincare behaviours among
young adults. Peer recommendations often carry more weight than professional advice, creating
pressure to conform to popular skincare trends (Bandura, 2021). Socia l media platforms such as
Instagram and TikTok intensify this influence by rapidly spreading viral skincare content and
product endorsements (Montag et al.,  2021). Beauty influencers are frequently viewed as
trustworthy due to perceived authenticity, which  increases the persuasive power of their

Concept of Consumer Awareness and Knowledge
Consumer awareness relates to the knowledge and understanding that buyers have regarding:
Consumer awareness of skincare ingredients means understanding what chemicals or natural
compounds are present in a product before use. Knowing the ingredient list he lps consumers
identify potential allergens, irritants, or harmful substances that may not suit their skin type. Many
harmful compounds, such as mercury and hydroquinone, have been found in some skin-lightening
products despite regulations forbidding their use in cosmetics in Nigeria and other countries.  Lack
of disclosure or missing ingredient lists is a common red flag for unsafe products, making it
difficult for consumers to assess risks. Consumers who read labels and educate themselves are
better positioned to avoid products with endocrine disruptors or banned substances.  Awareness
also involves recognizing scientific ingredient names and researching unfamiliar terms.
Ultimately, informed buyers can choose products that match their skin needs and avoid those with
ingredients linked to long-term harm (Kapitan et al., 2022).

Safe Usage Practices
Safe usage practices encompass how and when skincare products should be applied to minimize
adverse effects, and consumers should follow instructions on packaging regarding frequency,
amount, and method of application. For example, over-use of potent actives like hydroquinone or
steroids without medical supervision can lead to skin thinning, irritation, or systemic health issues,
and proper usage also includes avoiding mixing incompatible products that might irritate the skin
or reduce effectiveness. Consumers should pay attention to warnings, recommended usage areas,
and whether products are suitable for sensitive skin, and applying products correctly helps ensure
intended cosmetic benefits while reducing the risk of inflammation or allergic reactions.
Awareness campaigns often highlight how misuse can exacerbate conditions like acne or
dermatitis, and ultimately, consumers who adopt safe practices are more likely to enjoy skincare
benefits without unnecessary harm or discomfort (Baki, 2022).

Recognition of Certified/Approved Products
Knowing how to recognize certified or approved skincare products improves consumer safety, as
in Nigeria, products must be registered and display a valid NAFDAC registration number to be
considered legally approved for sale, and consumers who check for thi s number and verify it can
avoid purchasing substandard, counterfeit, or dangerous products. Without regulatory approval,
there is no guarantee that a product has undergone quality and safety testing, as approval symbols
signal that a product has passed certain safety and quality benchmarks, meaning certified products
are less likely to contain banned ingredients such as mercury or high-dose steroids (World Health
Organization [WHO], 2019). Consumer education around certification reduces demand for
dangerous alternatives, and retailers and e -commerce platforms also play a role by verifying
certifications before listing products, which ultimately empowers consumers to distinguish
legitimate brands from harmful ones.

 Health Risks Associated with Bleaching Agents, Steroids, and Mercury
Awareness of the health risks associated with certain cosmetic ingredients is critical for safe
skincare choices, as substances such as mercury, steroids, and excessive hydroquinone are
sometimes found in unregulated skin-lightening products and pose serious risks. Mercury exposure
can damage the kidneys, nervous system, and may lead to long-term toxicity even with topical use
(World Health Organization [WHO], 2019), steroids used without supervision can thin the skin,
suppress immune response, and cause hormonal imbalances, and hydroquinone overuse is linked
with ochronosis (permanent skin darkening), irritation, and other adverse outcomes. Consumers
unaware of these risks may suffer from chronic skin conditions or systemic health issues, and
education about these dangers discourages unsafe skin-bleaching practices prevalent in some urban
markets. Recognizing harmful agents encourages consumers to seek safer, certified alternatives,
and health-focused awareness initiatives help reduce demand for dangerous pr oducts and protect
long-term well-being (Baki, 2022).

Conclusions
The safety oversight of skincare products in urban Nigeria represents a complex intersection of
regulatory capacity, consumer behavior, and market dynamics. The results of this review indicate
that the risks associated with skincare product use among young  adults are not only a function of
product composition but also of behavioral patterns, sociocultural influences, and information
asymmetry within the market environment. The safety oversight of skincare products in urban
Nigeria represents a complex intersection of regulatory capacity, consumer behavior, and market
dynamics. Safety oversight must be viewed as a multifaceted process that goes beyond regulatory
compliance to include consumer education, market surveillance, and adaptive policy responses.
This is crucial because young adults operate in a highly mediated consumption space that is shaped
by social media, peer pressure, and aspirational beauty standards, which frequently prioritize rapid
results over safety considerations. Additionally, the coexistence of traditional and modern skincare
practices introduces additional layers of complexity in assessing safety outcomes.
