# Creative destruction: Human-AI competition in an experimental market

Creative destruction: Human-AI competition in an
experimental market
John J. Conlon1 and Peter Schwardmann2
1Department of Economics, The Ohio State University, Columbus, OH 43210, USA.
2Department of Social and Decision Sciences, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, PA 15213,
USA.
Abstract.We study two hypotheses for how a human role in production could persist despite
growing AI capabilities. Humans might have a durable advantage in taste, designing better products
even as AI agents automate routine tasks, or consumers might intrinsically prefer that humans have
a hand in production. In our experiment, participants act as producers, designing computer games
with an AI coding agent, and as consumers who buy them. Across three studies (N = 1,025), we
find no evidence for either hypothesis. Games designed by an AI on its own outperform those
of human producers, including both amateurs and professional creatives. Human producers do
not anticipate the AI’s advantage. Revealing whether a game is human-designed does not raise
consumers’ willingness to pay for the product.

Introduction
Economic production increasingly involves collaboration between humans and artificial intelli-
gence (AI) systems. The share of US workers reporting using AI on the job doubled to 40% be-
tween 2023 and 2025, and its uptake in the workforce has been faster than that of the personal
computer or the internet (1,2). Firms deploy large language models (LLMs) and other AI systems
for tasks as varied as coding, job interviewing, customer support, clinical documentation, medical
imaging, management consulting, sports umpiring, and debt collection (1,3–10), and human em-
ployees spend an increasing share of their time managing and directing AI agents (11–14). These
expanding capabilities have enormous potential upsides (3,7,8,15), but they also raise concerns
about AI eventually displacing human labor. In contrast to earlier technological disruptions, LLMs
increasingly perform tasks that once seemed quintessentially human: communication, art, reason-
ing, and knowledge work more broadly. AI automation therefore poses not only a financial risk
to workers whose jobs it might displace (16–18), but a threat to a primary source of meaning and
pride in people’s lives.
Two hypotheses are commonly advanced for why a human role in production might persist
even as AI capabilities grow. First, on the supply side, humans might have a durable advantage
intaste: they may be better at knowing, or more creative in designing, the products that other
humans will prefer (19,20). If so, while AI agents will increasingly perform more routine tasks
(e.g., coding), human guidance will remain crucial in directing them toward productive ends (21,
22). Some commentators go even further, arguing that by removing technical barriers through
automation, AI can unlock the creative and productive potential of a much larger set of workers
(23–25). Second, on the demand side, consumers might intrinsically prefer that humans retain a
hand in production (26–30). That is, human labor might remain integral to the production process
either for instrumental reasons—because they contribute to the creation of better products—or for
intrinsic reasons, because consumers demand human-made productsper se.
Testing these hypotheses experimentally requires constructing a market where human producers—
collaborating with AI agents—compete with entirely AI systems, and where we can manipulate
what consumers know about the human involvement (or lack thereof) in the production process.
We construct such a market in three preregistered studies that feature the production of, and deci-

sions over whether to purchase, more than 1,600 unique computer games (which can be explored
at https://human-ai-games.github.io). Our experimental market then allows us to answer three re-
search questions:
RQ1:Do human producers, whether amateurs or professionals, have an advantage over AI
in taste?
RQ2:Do producers believe that they have such an advantage?
RQ3:Do consumers intrinsically prefer human-made products?
RQ1 and RQ3 test our two main hypotheses. RQ2 asks whether producers themselves believe the
first hypothesis, and therefore whether its failure would come as a surprise to them. Crucially,
all key choices in our market are incentivized: producers earn what consumers are willing to pay
for their games, consumers pay for the games they choose to play, and producers’ predictions are
rewarded for accuracy.
Experimental design
Our three studies involve 1,025 US-based participants recruited from Prolific, an online research
platform (296 in Study 1, 180 in Study 2, and 549 in Study 3; summary statistics in table S1). As
shown in Fig. 1, Study 1 contains the full market with participants acting first as producers and
then as consumers of computer games. Participants in Study 2 were professionals in creative in-
dustries who engaged only in the production phase. Participants in Study 3 only made consumption
decisions. See supplementary materials for full methods.
Study 1: Participants as producers and consumers
As producers, participants first designed three games in collaboration with an AI coding agent
powered by a large language model (Anthropic’s Opus 4.8), accessed live via an interface em-
bedded into the experiment. Each task started with a plain black-and-white “basic game”—for
example, falling blocks resembling Tetris, or a paddle game similar to Pong—which participants
improved through three rounds of messages to the agent. Upon receiving the participant’s message,
the coding agent would modify the code for the game, the updated version of which appeared on
3

the screen, replacing the previous version. Between rounds, participants tested out the current state
of the game to develop ideas for how to change it. At various points during the task, a participant’s
willingness to continue designing the game and their prediction of the price it will fetch on the
market were elicited. Fig. 2 provides an example of the experimental interface in the production
phase.
After the production phase, participants entered the consumer phase of the study. After testing
another participant’s game and the basic game it came from, participants were asked their willing-
ness to pay (WTP), using an incentive-compatible payment rule (31), to play the other participant’s
game rather than the basic game for at least one minute. They made consumption decisions for up
to four games, and their stated WTPs constituted the market incentives producers faced: a con-
sumer’s WTP for a game was paid to its producer as a bonus.
Two-thirds of producers were randomized to see an AI design a game on its own, without
human input, either after their first or second design task. These producers were truthfully told that
we prompted an LLM (Anthropic’s Sonnet 4.6) to redesign the same basic game they had just seen
“to be much more creative, engaging, and fun to play,” and that its single response was passed
to the same coding agent they had just interacted with. Participants then saw the LLM “type” its
message into a text box to be sent to the coding agent, before getting to test the game the coding
agent created in response (see Fig. S1 for an example). The AI-designed games then entered the
pool of games the consumer phase drew from alongside the human-designed games.
Comparing consumers’ WTP for human- and AI-designed games addresses RQ1. To address
RQ2, we asked producers to predict consumers’ WTP for their own games and for the AI’s, both
before and after they had witnessed the AI design a game.
Finally, we randomized whether consumers were told how games were produced. In the Blind
arm, they were given no information, while in the Disclosed arm they were told whether the game
was designed “by another participant who worked with an AI, just like you did” or “by an AI
on its own”. Comparing the difference between WTPs for AI- and human-made games across
the two disclosure arms addresses RQ3. In particular, an intrinsic preference for human-made
products would manifest as a greater advantage or smaller disadvantage of human-made games in
the Disclosed arm of the experiment.

Study 2: Professional producers
In Study 2, we recruit 180 participants with jobs in creative industries whose experience might
boost their performance. Of these, 51 are game developers or work in the video game industry,
70 are graphic designers, and 59 are content creators, identified through Prolific’s occupational
screeners. In a post-experiment survey, 80% of them report that their job has recently involved
them “creating something for other people to use, look at, read, or play — for example writing,
visual design, a game, or software”. We therefore refer to these participants as “professionals.” The
professionals completed only the production phase of the experiment.
Study 3: Dedicated consumers
In Study 3, a separate 549 participants completed only the consumer phase of the experiment. The
pool of games these consumers faced comprised the 503 games created by the professionals in
Study 2, 388 “amateur” games made by Study 1’s participants, and 297 AI-designed games from
both prior studies. Study 3 participants also rated each game on the dimensions of fun, creativity,
polish, and clarity on 1–9 Likert scales. Table S2 shows that these ratings are highly predictive of
participants’ WTP.
The consumers in Study 3 were randomized into three treatments. First, a Blind treatment did
not disclose whether each game was designed by a human or AI, though all participants were aware
that they might be shown games of both types. Second, a Disclosed + Paid treatment told partic-
ipants whether each game was AI- or human-designed, and for the latter made salient that their
WTP would be paid to the producer. Finally, a Disclosed + Unpaid treatment also told participants
whether a game was AI- or human-designed, but participants were (truthfully) told that their WTP
would not affect the producer’s payment.
Results

valued by producers and that the products they make are valued by consumers. It is, therefore, the

AI-designed games outperform the games designed by both amateurs and professionals
RQ1 asks whether human producers, whether amateurs or professionals, have an advantage over
AI in taste and creativity. If so, we should find that human-designed games garner a higher WTP
than those that the AI produces on its own. In fact, we find the opposite (Fig. 3A). Pooling across
disclosure arms, consumers in Study 1 are willing to pay $0.47 on average for AI-designed games
against $0.37 for human-designed games—a gap of 10.0 cents, or 0.31 standard deviations (SD)
(p <0.001). Two features of the design are worth noting. First, producers and consumers were
the very same people, and therefore producers could consult their own preferences to tailor the
games they made to consumers (who by construction had the same preferences in expectation).
Second, AI-only production was deliberately handicapped, as AI could not test out the game or
iteratively improve it, and we only used a single prompt across all AI-designed games. Fig. 3A
also shows that the AI’s advantage carries over to the dedicated consumers of Study 3, and to the
games of professional producers, whose games garner a 0.16 SD lower WTP than AI games on
average (p <0.01).
Consumers’ quality ratings suggest where the AI’s advantage comes from (Fig. 3B). Study 3’s
consumers rate AI-designed games as more fun, more creative, and more polished than the games
of amateurs and professionals alike. On our index combining the four ratings, professionals’ games
score 0.21 SD below AI games (p <0.001) and amateurs’ games 0.34 SD below (p <0.001,
significantly worse than professionals’ games,p= 0.02). Strikingly, consumers value AI games
for their creativity, the dimension in which we might have expected humans to hold an advantage.
One potential explanation is that coming up with ideas and judging whether they will improve
a product is costly and difficult for humans, whereas an AI can ideate immediately and cheaply.
The prompts sent to the coding agent are consistent with this channel: professionals’ combined
messages per game are longer than amateurs’ (89 vs. 60 words), and the AI’s single messages are
longer still (235 words). The AI may therefore outperform humans in part by generating more
ideas, without compromising on their average quality.
Recall that two-thirds of producers had an opportunity to watch the AI design a game itself. A
natural question is whether human producers can outperform the AI after they have had a chance
to learn from it (e.g., by learning how best to prompt the coding agent). We do find some evidence
8

of human games improving after watching the AI produce (we discuss this result further below),
but even human games made after the producer saw the AI design a game garner a lower WTP
than the AI games themselves, by 0.21 SDs in Study 1 (p <0.01) and by 0.15 SDs in Study 3
(p= 0.01).
Finally, the AI’s advantage is not confined to the middle or bottom of the WTP distribution. In
Study 1, the AI–human gap in WTP is positive and its 95% confidence interval excludes zero at
every decile but the lowest; in Study 3, AI games outperform both amateur and professional games
at every decile above the lowest (Fig. S3), though effects are not all statistically significant. This
result is noteworthy in the right tail of the distribution, where we might have expected hundreds
of distinct human designers to generate more variance—and so better extremes—than one model
sent the same prompt many times. Instead, we find no evidence for a standout group of human
designers whose games beat the best of the AI’s.
In answer to RQ1, we find no evidence that humans have an advantage over AI in taste. The
AI’s games outperform those of amateurs, of professionals, and of humans who have had the
opportunity of learning from watching the AI design a game.
Producers expect to beat the AI
RQ2 asks whether our participants believe that they have an advantage in taste. We ask producers
after they have finished designing their game to predict consumers’ WTP to play it. Producers also
predict WTP for AI-designed games, both before and after they see the AI’s design idea and its
implementation. Fig. 4 plots the predicted and real differences between the WTP fetched by the
producer’s and the AI’s games.
Both amateur and professional producers initially predict that the AI-designed games are at
a significant disadvantage, of 9 and 17 cents respectively (0.3–0.5 SDs, one-samplettest,p <
0.001). The predicted gap turns in the AI’s favor as soon as producers read the AI’s idea, and
stays there after they have played its game. This suggests that the AI outperforms humans in idea-
generating. Further, this fact is plain for participants to see upon reading the AI’s idea, even before
experiencing the game or learning about market outcomes. However, it is not expected ex ante.
In answer to RQ2, the AI’s advantage comes as a surprise to the market participants themselves.

that the absence of an intrinsic preference for human-designed games in Study 1 merely reflected
a sense of rivalry between producers.
A natural concern is that the null effects of disclosing provenance obtain only because con-
sumers in our Blind arm are already able to tell which games were AI- and which were human-
designed. To test this, in Study 3 we asked participants in the Blind treatment the probability that
each game they faced was designed by a human. We find that consumers are wholly unable to dis-
tinguish the two, assigning a 59.3% chance to AI games and a statistically indistinguishable 58.2%
chance to human games (p= 0.67). This result is consistent with prior work showing that people
cannot easily distinguish human- vs. AI-generated text (34). Finally, in both Disclosed treatments,
we ask participants after providing their WTP whether they can recall whether the game was AI- or
human-designed. Reassuringly, they answer this question correctly for 92.8% of games, confirming
that they attend to the information about who designed each game.
In answer to RQ3, we find no evidence that consumers intrinsically prefer human-made prod-
ucts. When we experimentally divulge the extent to which humans had a hand in production, their
demand for the product remains unmoved and, in particular, they continue to significantly prefer
AI to human products.

Discussion
We test two hypotheses for how a human role in economic production could persist despite growing
AI capabilities and fail to find evidence for either: fully AI-produced games outperform those of
13

amateurs and professionals alike, even when their (lack of) human involvement is revealed and
made salient. Moreover, the AI’s advantage is not anticipated by the human producers.
Implications
Our results suggest that AI could displace precisely the sort of work humans would most like
to keep. The job in our experiment was a good one: producers took pride in their games and
enjoyed designing them. Conventional measures would not register this loss. GDP records the
consumption value of what is produced and the wages paid to produce it, but not the satisfaction
and meaning workers derive from producing it. A world in which AI made our participants’ games
would therefore look like a richer one, even as something valued by the producers disappeared. Our
producers expected their games to outsell the AI’s and revised this belief only once they had seen
the AI at work. Such surprise is likely to compound the personal costs of displacement: workers
who do not anticipate being replaced have little reason to retrain or change occupations in advance,
so their costs of adjusting are likely to be larger when replacement arrives. This speaks for frank
and early public discussion of what AI production means for the labor market. Finally, our results
offer little hope for strategies that emphasize a human hand in production. Labeling products as
human-made did not raise what consumers were willing to pay for them, and neither did telling
consumers that their payment would go to the human producer.
Limitations
No naturally occurring market lets us varyonlywhether humans have a hand in making a product
or what consumers know about it. A controlled experiment makes this variation possible, but poses
the challenge of creating a paradigm where producers are familiar with and able to produce the
product being sold and consumers actually demand the product. The production of computer games
with a coding agent meets this challenge. Our market is nonetheless a compressed version of the
markets it is meant to inform: prices were measured in small denominations, consumption lasted
for a short period, and production lasted minutes rather than weeks. At the same time, many aspects
of our design would seem tofavorhuman competitiveness: we handicapped AI-only production
relative to what we allowed human designers, recruited a customer base unusually similar (indeed,
identical) to and therefore likely sympathetic toward human producers, and made the level of AI
14

involvement in production very salient. We expect our results to speak to creative work that sustains
many mid-level jobs. It is less clear how far they extend to superstar markets, such as music or
books, or to markets further afield, such as scientific research. Moreover, we cannot rule out that
other interventions activate a preference for human production more effectively than disclosure, for
example by creating familiarity between individual producers and consumers. Understanding the
market forces likely to sustain or drive out humans from economic production in creative work is
an urgent question for policy makers and social scientists. Our experiment provides early evidence
and, we hope, motivates follow-up studies that address its limitations.

Materials and Methods
Participants and logistics
Study 1’s 296 US-based participants were recruited from Prolific between 6 and 10 July 2026.
Participants who answered more than three comprehension questions incorrectly were excluded
automatically by being asked to return the study early, which removed 9.7% of potential partici-
pants. Table S1 reports summary statistics. As is common on Prolific, the sample skews somewhat
more female, younger, better educated, and more liberal than the US population. Large majorities
report using AI tools (83.4%) and playing video games, including phone games, at least monthly
(83.8%), and 19.3% report using AI for coding or software outside the experiment. The median
study duration was 54 minutes. All three studies were preregistered on the AEA RCT Registry
(AEARCTR-0019010) and approved by Carnegie Mellon University’s Institutional Review Board
(IRBSTUDY2016_000000482); all participants gave informed consent. Screenshots of every page
of all three instruments are available at this link.
Elicitation of willingness to pay
All WTP questions use a Becker-DeGroot-Marschak mechanism (31). A random price is drawn
and displayed by a spinner landing on a value between $0.00 and $1.00; the participant pays the
S1

price and receives the good if the price falls below her stated WTP (framed as her “maximum
price”), and otherwise pays nothing and receives the default option. Payments are deducted from
bonuses earned later in the study.
Demo phase
Participants are first introduced to the types of games involved in the experiment. There are seven
“basic games”: simplified black-and-white versions of well-known genres (falling blocks to be ar-
ranged into rows, a paddle that bounces a ball, and so on). Each participant is shown two randomly
selected basic games, must play each for at least ten seconds, and then states a WTP to play one
rather than the other for at least one minute. This phase familiarizes participants with the goods
and with the WTP elicitation mechanism before they enter the production or consumption phases.
Producer phase
Participants design three computer games with an LLM coding agent powered by Anthropic’s
Opus 4.8, accessed live during the experiment through the API. Each task begins from a basic
game drawn at random from those not used in the demo phase. Participants send messages to the
coding agent, which edits the game’s code in response; before each new message they must test
the current version by playing it for at least ten seconds. Design ends after three rounds of edits,
and participants know this limit in advance. Figure 2 shows the production interface: the live game
appears on the right, the message history and elicitations on the left.
Predicting consumer WTP .Producers know their finished game will be offered to another par-
ticipant, who may pay up to $1.00 to play it rather than the basic game the task began from, and
that they earn that WTP as a bonus. They predict their consumer’s WTP twice, once after seeing
the basic game but before designing, and once after the final edit, earning $0.10 for each prediction
within $0.02 of the realized WTP.
Producer surplus.After testing the basic game but before writing their first message to the
coding agent, producers state their WTP to design the game rather than skip the task, in which
case the game is not offered to any consumer and the task pays no bonus. This is our measure of
ex anteproducer surplus: the price a producer will pay to hold the job rather than skip it, which
S2

captures both the payment they expect from consumers and any non-pecuniary value of designing.
The elicitation is implemented with 1% probability, shown by a spinner landing on “Counts” or
“Doesn’t Count.” An analogous question is repeatedly asked before and after each response from
the coding agent. Each of these questions also counts with a 1% probability. If a question counts,
a price is drawn as for the other WTP questions: if her WTP exceeds the price, she pays it and
continues designing; otherwise she stops designing, and the game is not offered to a consumer. In
either case, she is not asked any further such questions during that design task.
Pride and enjoyment.After the third edit, participants state two incentivized WTPs: for a cus-
tomized link to their game, so that “you can play it later or share it with friends,” and for inclusion
of their game on a public website searchable by all participants. Purchased links and the website
are delivered at the end of the study and by message on Prolific. They then answer unincentivized
slider questions on how much they enjoyed designing the game and how proud they are of it, with
text updating as the slider moves (e.g., “I am [not at all/slightly/moderately/very/extremely] proud
of the game I designed”).
AI-only production
Two-thirds of participants are randomly selected to watch the AI design a game without human
input; half of these do so after their first design task and half after their second, generating random
variation in both whether and when a participant sees autonomous AI production. Participants
are truthfully told an LLM (Sonnet 4.6) is asked to “redesign [the basic game] to be much more
creative, engaging, and fun to play. Feel free to build on the basic design or completely redesign
it.” The full prompt additionally supplies technical context—for example that another model will
implement the description as a computer game played for about a minute in a frame with external
Start/Restart controls—followed by the basic game’s HTML, and asks for a single short paragraph
of plain prose. The reply is sent to the same coding agent participants use. Participants watch the
LLM “type” its idea into an interface resembling their own, must test the resulting game for at least
30 seconds, and predict the consumer’s WTP for it three times: before seeing the idea, after seeing
the idea but before implementation, and after playing the implemented game. Fig. S1 shows this
phase, with an example idea and the game produced from it.
S3

Consumer phase
Each participant is then offered up to four games drawn from a single other participant’s producer
phase, matched in real time: finished games enter a pool, and consumers draw from the producer
who most recently finished. Consequently 1,018 of 1,023 games were shown to exactly one con-
sumer; the remaining 5 had no consumer because their producers were among the last to take
the study, and those producers were paid $1.20 per unmatched game. Because producers generate
slightly fewer than four games in expectation, some consumers see fewer than four: 154 partici-
pants valued four games, 123 three, 15 two, 3 one, and 1 (the first participant) none. For each game,
the consumer states a WTP to play it rather than its associated basic game, then plays the chosen
game for at least a minute.
Disclosure treatment.We randomize at the participant level whether game origin is disclosed.
In the Disclosed arm, consumers are truthfully told, both before each game and in a caption shown
while they play, whether it “was designed by another participant who worked with an AI, just
like you did” or “was designed by an AI on its own—without a participant working with it like
you did.” In the Blind arm every game is described only as “produced during another participant’s
survey.”
Study 2: professional producers
Study 2 ran the producer half of the Study 1 design on 180 participants recruited from Prolific
between 4 and 11 August 2026 through its occupational screeners: current game developers or de-
signers, content creators, graphic designers, and employees of the video-game industry, excluding
Study 1 participants. This experiment was largely identical to Study 1 except that the consumer
phase was dropped. Professionals were paid a Study 3 consumer’s stated WTP for each of their
games as a bonus, mirroring how Study 1’s producers were paid from Study 1’s consumer phase.
Table S1 reports summary statistics.
Study 3: dedicated consumers
Study 3 ran the consumer half of the design on 549 new Prolific participants (31 August to 2
September 2026; median duration 20 minutes) who had taken part in neither earlier study. Each
S4

consumer valued four games drawn from the following pool: all 503 games designed by the profes-
sionals, 388 games sampled at random from Study 1’s participants, and all 297 AI-designed games
from the two producer runs (25% of the pool). No consumer saw two human-designed games built
from the same basic game.
Consumers were randomized with equal probability to three arms that differed only in one
sentence shown before each game. In the Blind arm, consumers were told that the pool mixes
games designed by participants working with an AI and games designed by an AI on its own. In the
two Disclosed arms every game was labeled with its origin. In the Disclosed + Paid arm, consumers
were truthfully told for human-designed games that their producer would receive their stated WTP
as a bonus; in the Disclosed + Unpaid arm they were told (also truthfully) that their WTP would
not affect what the producer earned because another consumer’s already had. Blind-arm consumers
were asked after rating each game how likely it was to have been designed by a human, on a 0–
100 slider paid by a matching-probability mechanism (35); Disclosed-arm consumers were instead
asked, right after stating their WTP, to recall whether the game had been labeled human- or AI-
designed.
Statistical analysis
Unless otherwise noted,pvalues are from two-sidedttests comparing group means, with standard
errors two-way clustered by consumer and producer to account for repeated observations of each.
Each AI-designed game is its own producer cluster; in Study 1 the two dimensions coincide, since
each consumer valued one producer’s games and each producer’s games went to one consumer.
Quantile comparisons in Fig. S3 are differences between empirical quantiles within each study,
with 95% percentile intervals from 10,000 bootstrap samples that resample consumers. In Fig. 4,
we use robust standard errors for predicted gaps and for amateurs’ actual gaps. Summary indices
of the four quality ratings standardize each rating and combine them with weights proportional to
the row sums of their inverse covariance matrix (36), then standardize the result. All statistical tests
are two-sided.
