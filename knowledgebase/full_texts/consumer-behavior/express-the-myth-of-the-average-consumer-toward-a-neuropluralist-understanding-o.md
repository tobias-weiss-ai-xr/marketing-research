The Myth of the Average Consumer:

Abstract
This article challenges a foundational assumption in marketing and public policy: that consumers process stimuli in broadly similar,
stable, and predictable ways. The authors argue that the expectation of “average” neurological functioning introduces systematic
error into marketplace design, privileging some and stigmatizing others. They advance instead a neuropluralist perspective in
which neurological variation is continuous, context-dependent, and population-wide, rather than con ﬁned to clinically de ﬁned
groups. To operationalize this shift, the authors ﬁrst introduce STAR (Sense, Think, Affect, and Relate), an organizing framework
that structures disparate research streams about neurological processing into four dimensions covering how consumers sense,
think, feel, and relate. STAR can be used to identify how assumptions about neurological processing norms become embedded in
market systems, producing system –consumer misalignment. They then propose neuropluralist design, a design orientation that
incorporates variation ex ante rather than addressing it through ex post accommodations. Together, STAR and neuropluralist
design form a paired detect –design framework that reframes marketplace failure from individual de ﬁciency to a person –environ-
ment ﬁt issue. The framework redirects marketing and policy toward systems that support multiple valid neurological pathways of
participation, advancing a more accurate, equitable, and inclusive foundation for market design.

public policy, neuropluralism

reconﬁguring market systems in the context of neurological plu-

Neurological Pluralism: Multiple Valid Ways
of Processing the World
Differences in neurological processing are typically represented
through diagnostic classi ﬁcation systems such as the
Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders
(American Psychiatric Association 2023) and the International
Classiﬁcation of Diseases (World Health Organization 2024).
These systems organize variation into discrete conditions, such
as autism spectrum disorder, attention deﬁcit/hyperactivity disor-
der (ADHD), and dyslexia. In clinical and institutional contexts,
such classiﬁcations are essential: They guide diagnoses, enable
access to care and accommodations, and provide individuals
with language and legitimacy for their lived experiences
(Cicero et al. 2024; Widiger and Samuel 2005). For many, diag-
nosis is transformative, bringing validation, explanation, and sup-
port (Go Jefferies and Ahmed 2022; Mirabito et al. 2025).
Nevertheless, the categorical structure of psychiatric manu-
als has been widely critiqued. Individuals who share a diagnosis
can differ substantially in traits, support needs, and coping strat-
egies (Cicero et al. 2024; Machin et al. 2019; Widiger and
Samuel 2005). Others can experience meaningful challenges
without meeting formal diagnostic thresholds for treatment
(Iasiello, Van Agteren, and Muir-Cochrane 2020). Boundaries
between conditions are ﬂuid, and traits framed as de ﬁcits in
one context are valued as strengths in another (Cicero et al.
2024; Cuthbert and Insel 2013; Kotov et al. 2017). More impor-
tantly, categorical representations impose a false dichotomy that
conﬁnes neurological difference to the one in ﬁve individuals
who identify as neurodivergent (Healy 2024), implicitly de ﬁn-
ing them against “neurotypical” individuals whose functioning
aligns with dominant social expectations (Figure 1, Panel A).
For marketing and public policy, this distinction is critical.
Markets do not interact with diagnostic categories; they interact
with consumers whose perceptual, cognitive, affective, and rela-
tional processes vary dynamically across contexts and life
stages (Legault, Bourdon, and Poirier 2019; Wise 2023).
Clinical populations make these dynamics more visible, but
do not de ﬁne their boundaries. Variation in neurological pro-
cessing exists on a continuum across all consumers. It can be
temporary or enduring, shaped by complex interacting biologi-
cal, psychological, social, and ecological in ﬂuences (Figure 1,
Panel B) (Bronfenbrenner and Morris 2007). Internally experi-
enced factors, both genetic and acquired (e.g., hormones, aging,
illness, injury, neurodivergence) alter sensory perception, cog-
nitive capacity, emotional regulation, and social interactions
(Grady 2000; Hogervorst, Craig, and O ’Donnell 2022;
Keynejad et al. 2019; Lim and Dinges 2010; Reis, Antoni,
and Travado 2020; Sandi 2013). Grief, stress, and substance
use can disrupt neurological processing that meditation and
exercise can improve (Dowling, Weiss, and Condon 2008;
Gallardo-Gómez et al. 2022; Maté and Maté 2022; Perkins,
Sehmbi, and Smith 2022; Saavedra Pérez et al. 2015).
Meso-level environments (e.g., family dynamics, workplace
support, friendships, digital activity) and macro-level contextual
factors (e.g., culture, nature, war, pollution, poverty, racism)
further shape how individuals perceive and process stimuli
(Costa et al. 2019; Firth et al. 2019; Gürog ̆lu 2022; Johnson,
Riis, and Noble 2016; Kapp 2011; Kühn et al. 2021; Matson
et al. 2011; Park and Huang 2010).
In short, neurological processing is not uniform. Too often,
however, marketplace practices and policy presume neurotypi-
cal processing styles as the norm. These biases are rarely
explicit (Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan 2010), yet they shape

STAR: Identifying Consumer –Marketplace
Neurological Misalignment
Drawing on insights from multiple literature streams, including
psychiatry, neuroscience, technology, and business, we ﬁrst
establish the prevalence of neurological plurality within the
marketplace. STAR is presented as an organizing framework
that integrates previously separate streams of research into
four core neurological processing dimensions. STAR thus pro-
vides a uni ﬁed lens to specify when and where fallible norms
about neurotypicality are reproduced. Although STAR is not
intended as an exhaustive taxonomy, these four dimensions
are foundational to neurological functioning (Gross 1999;
Michelini et al. 2024). Consumer research in each area is well
established, spanning work on sensory processing (Krishna
2012), cognition (Bettman 1974; Howard and Sheth 1969),
emotions (Bagozzi, Gopinath, and Nyer 1999), and social inter-
action (Bagozzi 2000; Hamilton et al. 2021). STAR is the ﬁrst
step in our paired detect–design framework, reorienting analysis
of marketplace failure away from individual de ﬁcits to system –
consumer misalignment, opening opportunities to design for
neurological plurality using NPD.
A comprehensive review of all the literature within each
STAR dimension is beyond the scope of this article. Rather,
our goal is to identify recurring implicit assumptions about
how consumers sense, think, feel, and relate that are embedded
within market systems and how those assumptions break down
under the reality of neurological plurality. We focus on three
representative assumptions within each STAR dimension that
consistently shape consumer experiences and serve as common
sources of consumer –system mismatch. Together, these
assumptions provide a parsimonious yet suf ﬁciently broad
foundation for diagnosing how neuronormative expectations
are reproduced across market environments. Misalignment
arises when these assumptions fail, disrupting coordination
between consumers and market systems. Table 1 provides a
summary.
It is important to note that the four core processing dimen-
sions in STAR operate in close combination. For example, emo-
tional salience in ﬂuences interpretation of social signals
(Chevallier et al. 2012). Sensory overload interferes with work-
ing memory and decision-making (Robertson and Simmons
2015). Cognitive ﬂexibility shapes emotion regulation and rela-
tional competence (Gross 1999; Zelazo and Müller 2002). In the
discussion that follows, however, we isolate them temporarily,
without implying distinct boundaries or independence, to better
surface implicit assumptions embedded in market systems. We

Sense: Assumptions of Shared Sensory Processing
Sense refers to how consumers perceive stimuli from external
environments and internal bodily states, including interoceptive
signals such as hunger, heart rate, and temperature (Khalsa et al.
2018; Krishna 2012). Marketplace practice and policy are typi-
cally calibrated as if consumers share similar sensory thresh-
olds, with exposure, detection, and attention assumed to
operate in broadly stable and predictable ways (Krishna
2012). In practice, sensory processing varies substantially
across individuals and contexts (Velasco et al. 2026), meaning
identical stimuli can be experienced differently as impercepti-
ble, distracting, or overwhelming. We identify three recurring
assumptions about sensory experience in the marketplace.

sensory thresholds, re ﬂecting the premise that consumers will
detect and tolerate stimuli in similar ways (Bitner 1992;

inputs (Khalsa et al. 2018; Padhaiskaya and Weijo 2026). These
internal signals vary across individuals and contexts, signiﬁcantly
shaping how environments are experienced. For example, spatial
choices such as high ceilings, narrow aisles, or dense layouts may
generate discomfort or disorientation for some consumers, but
overly simpli ﬁed layouts may prove insuf ﬁciently stimulating
to others (Padhaiskaya and Weijo 2026; Velasco et al. 2026).

Sensory attention. Market environments assume that attention
can be directed in predictable ways, with salient cues reliably
capturing focus to guide behavior. Visual hierarchy, motion,

Patterns of attention shift across development, with children dif-

distraction (Cabeza, Nyberg, and Park 2017; Rueda et al. 2005).

fatigue, stress, and multitasking (Eysenck et al. 2007; Lim

hyperfocusing on details (Ashinoff and Abu-Akel 2021;

characterized by multiple layered or competing sensory cues

Think: Assumptions of Shared Cognitive Processing
Think refers to postperceptual cognitive processes through
which consumers interpret, organize, and act on marketplace
information (Bettman, Luce, and Payne 1998; Payne,
Bettman, and Johnson 1993). Marketplace design typically
assumes that consumers possess relatively stable cognitive
capacity and control, and construct meaning in broadly similar
ways. However, cognitive processing is neither uniform nor sta-
ble; it varies across individuals and contexts as a function of
resource availability, environmental demands, and situational
factors (Lim and Dinges 2010; Payne, Bettman, and Johnson
1993; Sandi 2013). We identify three recurring assumptions
about cognitive functioning.
Cognitive capacity. Cognitive capacity refers to the amount of
information and complexity a consumer can process at a given
moment. Marketplace design assumes a universal baseline of
cognitive resources, including suf ﬁcient memory span, process-
ing speed, and tolerance for cognitive load. However, capacity
varies widely across the population. Traumatic brain injury
(Fry, Greenop, and Schutte 2010) and neurological diseases
like epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, and Parkinson ’s (Neue,
Linden, and Muschalla 2022) provide clinical examples, but sta-
ble traits (e.g., personality; Simon, Lee, and Stern 2020) and
transient states (e.g., sleep loss, anxiety, menopause;
Hogervorst, Craig, and O ’Donnell 2022; Lim and Dinges
2010; Sandi 2013) illustrate the wide heterogeneity in cognitive
capacity. Developmental stages associated with aging (Grady
2000; Park and Reuter-Lorenz 2009) and ecological variables
such as pollution (Costa et al. 2019) further alter capacity.
Cognitive capacity is also challenged by contextual demands,
from the mere presence of smartphones (Ward et al. 2017) to
complex market designs, such as dense retail assortments, time-
pressured negotiations, complex contracts, and hidden disclo-
sures (Ashby and Todd Maddox 2005; Loken 2006). When
demand surpasses available resources, cognition is compro-
mised, leading to mistakes, disengagement, or retreat.
Cognitive control. While cognitive capacity is like a computer ’s
memory (how much you can hold), cognitive control is the pro-
cessor (how effectively you manage and use it). Market systems
assume consumers can ef ﬁciently and consistently allocate and
regulate cognitive resources to maintain goal-directed behavior
(Bettman, Luce, and Payne 1998; Jacoby, Speller, and Kohn
1974) by inhibiting distractions, updating working memory,
and persisting across time (Burghoorn et al. 2026). However,
cognitive control varies with many factors, such as aging, stress,
and nature exposure (Sandi 2013; Xu and Ding 2026; Yoon,
Cole, and Lee 2009), certain mental health conditions (e.g.,

Meaning making. Marketplace environments implicitly assume
that consumers similarly interpret, categorize, and reason about
information to arrive at comparable understandings. This
assumption underlies disclosures, pricing formats, advertising
messages, and choice architectures, all of which are designed
for a presumed “average” decision-maker who extracts meaning
consistently and ef ﬁciently to optimize outcomes. However,
meaning-making processes vary substantially, shaping how
information is interpreted and acted on. Some consumers priv-
ilege detail-ﬁrst integration and rule-based categorization, yield-
ing narrower categories and heightened sensitivity to feature
variation (Happé and Frith 2006). Others rely on visual-spatial
patterning rather than phonological coding (Von Károlyi 2001).
Sensitivity to framing, numeracy, and probabilistic reasoning
varies widely (Peters 2012), as does depth of processing
(Petty and Cacioppo 1986). Some consumers rely on intuitive
shortcuts, while for others such heuristics trigger resistance
(Friestad and Wright 1994). Humor, sarcasm, and ﬁgurative lan-
guage are frequently interpreted differently (Kinzler 2021).
Integration patterns can further differ under time pressure and
environmental load (Barkley 1997).
Affect: Assumptions of Shared Emotional Processing
Affect refers to how consumers interpret, regulate and experi-
ence emotions in response to marketplace stimuli (Sharma
et al. 2023; Watson and Spence 2007). Emotionally evocative
cues such as urgency, scarcity, and reward are widely used to
guide consumer behavior, with the expectation that they will
elicit comparable levels of intensity (Lerner et al. 2015;
Loewenstein et al. 2001) and be consistently regulated to sup-
port goal-directed behavior (Lazarus 1991; Loewenstein et al.
2001; Russell 1980; Wilson and Gilbert 2005). Emotional expe-
rience, however, varies in type, intensity, valence, and regula-
tory capacity (Hamm et al. 2021; Hu et al. 2020). Systems
designed around a narrow range of affective functioning fail
to acknowledge emotional demands that may overwhelm
some consumers or fail to engage others. Three common
assumptions about emotional functioning are discussed.
Emotional identiﬁcation. Market systems, such as customer feed-
back systems, sentiment-based targeting, and AI-mediated
interactions, assume consumers can accurately identify and
interpret emotional states (Calvo and D ’Mello 2010).
However, the ability to identify emotions develops over time,
with children and adolescents demonstrating distinct levels of
emotional differentiation (Nook et al. 2018). Clinical factors
such ADHD (Rapport et al. 2002) affect recognition, while
trauma can reduce emotional clarity, producing numbing
(Cisler and Olatunji 2012; Putica et al. 2021). Emotional iden-
tiﬁcation becomes even more dif ﬁcult under conditions of
uncertainty or high stress, where feelings are complex, ambigu-
ous, and rapidly shifting (Mirabito et al. 2025). These differ-
ences are further in ﬂuenced by variation in interoceptive
awareness and emotional granularity (Barrett 2017; Ip, Yu,
and Gendron 2024; Khalsa et al. 2018).
Emotional regulation. Market environments are built on implicit
expectations about consumers ’ capacity to monitor and adjust
their emotional responses, once they have been identi ﬁed
(Gross 1999; Watson and Spence 2007). However, emotional
regulation varies systematically with age, trauma, illness, and
pain (Cracco, Goossens, and Braet 2017; Kim, Kim, and Gil
2022; Mal ﬂiet et al. 2017; Mueller et al. 2024; Putica et al.
2021). Everyday situations, from sleep and nutrition to stress,
meditation, and chatbot interactions, in ﬂuence emotional regu-
latory capacity (Han 2017; Head 2025; Maté and Maté 2022;
Nan and Hinz 2021; Spencer et al. 2017; Tan, Wachsmuth,
and Tugade 2022; Vandekerckhove and Wang 2017). Context
also proves critical; high-pressure environments such as ﬁnan-
cial decision-making (Mende et al. 2017) and emotionally
charged contexts such as healthcare navigation (Mirabito et al.
2025) can disrupt emotional regulation, prompting perceived
overreactions, disengagement, or withdrawal.
Emotional experience. Assumptions about shared emotional
experiences are embedded in common design practices, such
as limited-time offers, countdown timers, loss framing, and
evocative messaging (Cialdini 2007; Dhar and Nowlis 1999).
However, rather than being uniformly experienced, experienced
emotions can differ in valence and intensity across individuals
and contexts. Some consumers, like those with Tourette syn-
drome, experience rapid escalation or heightened intensity in
response to urgency or loss cues (Coffey and Park 1997), while
others, like autistics, exhibit more muted or delayed responses
(Kinnaird, Stewart, and Tchanturia 2019). Physiological states
such as ﬂuctuating hormones, illness, or medication side effects
also intensify or blunt affective responses, with consequent
implications for how marketplace stimuli are perceived and
acted on (Coffey and Park 1997; Kinnaird, Stewart, and
Tchanturia 2019; Pearson, Lightman, and Evans 2009;
Rapport et al. 2002; Reis, Antoni, and Travado 2020; Smidt
and Suvak 2015; Van Zutphen et al. 2015). Stimuli intended
to motivate action may instead produce avoidance, disengage-
ment, or distress, while lower-intensity cues may fail to engage
consumers who require stronger affective input (Cisler and
Olatunji 2012).

Relate: Assumptions of Shared Relational Processing
Relate refers to how consumers interpret social cues, navigate
interaction norms, and engage with others in marketplace con-
texts (Bagozzi 2000; Hamilton et al. 2021). Market environments
are typically organized around the expectation that consumers
share common conventions for communication, role behavior,
and interaction sequencing. Yet social interpretation varies across
individuals and contexts, shaped by culture, identity, prior expe-
rience, and situational demands (Markus and Kitayama 1991;
Ting-toomey and Kurogi 1998). Three implicit assumptions
deﬁne interpersonal marketplace participation.
Interpersonal style. Marketplace interactions assume that inter-
personal cues such as tone, timing, and phrasing carry shared
and stable meanings. Service encounters and digitally mediated
exchanges are designed as if these cues reliably communicate
intent, credibility, and interpersonal stance across consumers
and settings (Schütz et al. 2020). However, the interpretation
of these cues is constructed by the individual, shaped by factors
such as cultural background, communication style, prior inter-
action history, and sensitivity to power dynamics (Kinzler
2021; Markus and Kitayama 1991; Ting-toomey and Kurogi
1998; Toccalino et al. 2022). A curt reply may be understood
as ef ﬁcient or dismissive; a delayed response as neutral or dis-
respectful; reduced eye contact as disengagement or concentra-
tion (Sirianni et al. 2026). Misunderstanding can arise across
interacting parties whose expectations differ, consistent with
the double empathy problem (Milton, Gurbuz, and López
2022). When interaction depends on shared interpretation of
cues, divergence in meaning creates misalignment in how intent
and response are understood.
Shared norms. Market systems assume that consumers navigate
interactions through common structures, or scripts, governing
turn-taking, disclosure, questioning, and response sequencing.
Check-in systems, customer service encounters, complaint pro-
cedures, and chatbot ﬂows often presuppose direct requests,
concise problem statements, and linear question-and-answer
exchanges (Bagozzi 2000; Hamilton et al. 2021). Alignment
with these structures, however, varies across cultures, institu-
tional familiarity, and life experiences. Some consumers favor
indirect requests, longer pauses, or a clear explanation of con-
text before disclosure (Kinzler 2021; Markus and Kitayama
1991). Nonverbal communication patterns also vary. For exam-
ple, maintaining eye contact is a core feature of Western inter-
personal interactions but is considered disrespectful in some
indigenous cultures (Matson et al. 2011; Simmons 2023).
Even within the same individual, alignment can shift across
contexts. When marketplace designs require adherence to a nar-
row interaction structure, different communication norms create
misalignment.
Relational dynamics. Market environments assume that consum-
ers interpret and respond to social authority in comparable ways
(Anselmo, Mannaioli, and Sardo 2026). Service encounters,
veriﬁcation procedures, disclosures, and complaint systems
are often designed as if role expectations are neutral and consis-
tently interpreted across consumers (Keating et al. 2025). Yet
factors such as social identity, power, stigma, and prior institu-
tional experience shape how authority, legitimacy, and requests
for information are understood within an interaction (Cowen
2011; Markus and Kitayama 1991; Mayeli and Matuskey
2026). The same employee request or directive may be experi-
enced as routine by one consumer and as intrusive or inappro-
priate by another. When service encounters, online and
ofﬂine, presume shared expectations about trust, legitimacy,
disclosure, and deference, misalignment in interaction unfolds.
STAR Discussion and Operationalization
Collectively, STAR shows that market environments built around
assumptions of a universally shared “normal” level of sensory,
cognitive, affective, and relational processing fail to account for
widespread natural neurological variety. While examples from
clinical contexts make these dynamics particularly visible, varia-
tion exists on a continuum across all consumers, shaped by com-
plex interacting bio-psycho-socio-ecological in ﬂuences. A key
feature of this variability is its nonlinear and nonuniform nature.
Consumers do not occupy a single, stable position along a contin-
uum of ability but exhibit dynamic jagged pro ﬁles across STAR
dimensions (Figure 2, solid spiky line). The marketplace, how-
ever, is built on assumptions of an “average” consumer proﬁle
(Figure 2, inner shaded circle). Long embedded in marketing the-
ory and policy, the mythical “average consumer” is not a useful
abstraction but a source of systematic design error.
Consumer–marketplace misalignment occurs every time a
consumer’s spiky neurological pro ﬁle diverges from market-
place assumptions of normal neurological processing. For
example, when marketplace stimuli exceed their sensory thresh-
olds (Point X in Figure 2), consumers may miss critical informa-
tion or disengage completely. When marketplace demands
exceed their cognitive control (Point Y in Figure 2), consumers
may struggle to sequence steps, misinterpret information, or
inadvertently agree to conditions they would likely reject under
less demanding circumstances. Providers face parallel conse-
quences from these misalignments, including reduced trust
and engagement, misinterpretation of consumer behavior, inter-
actional inef ﬁciency, ineffective designs, and poorer service
outcomes. In policy contexts, these dynamics are particularly
consequential. For instance, long and ambiguous disclosures
put pressure on cognitive resources, reducing implementation
efﬁcacy.
These misalignments are neither trivial nor idiosyncratic.
They are systematic consequences of designing for an imagined
average consumer, disadvantaging those whose sensory, cogni-
tive, affective, or relational pro ﬁles diverge, temporarily or per-
manently, from assumptions embedded in marketplace
environments, with consequences for well-being, access, and par-
ticipation. Markets built for a single“average” mind are as unten-
able as offering one clothing size and requiring consumers alter
themselves to ﬁt. Just as markets design for a range of physical

body types (Rose 2016), they must also design for neurological
heterogeneity, removing the need for consumers to adapt to
inhospitable marketplaces (Go Jefferies 2023). Addressing this
challenge begins with the detection of implicit assumptions.

Detection alone, however, is insufﬁcient. Once hidden assump-
tions have been identi ﬁed, practitioners and policymakers must
determine how market environments can be (re)designed for neu-
rological plurality. We take up this challenge next through neuro-
pluralist design (NPD). Whereas STAR identiﬁes why and where
misalignment occurs, NPD offers a set of principles to guide the
development of products, services, communications, and policies
that are responsive to neurological diversity from the outset rather
than through after-the-fact accommodation. We conclude with an
illustration of how this paired detect –design framework could
work in the context of public policy development and implemen-
tation for inﬂuencer marketing.
Neuropluralist Design: Designing for
Neurological Plurality
NPD is a strengths-based design orientation that considers neu-
rological variation the rule, not the exception. Theoretically
grounded in work on pluriversal design (Escobar 2018), NPD
positions marketplace exclusion as a product of environments
calibrated to a narrow range of ideal functioning. Rather than
designing for an “average” user’s competencies, then retro ﬁt-
ting for those who diverge, NPD advances systems that support
multiple, equally valid ways of processing the world (Perry
2011). In this way, NPD departs from existing design paradigms
that seek equity in access: Universal design emphasizes solu-
tions that can be used by the widest possible range of users with-
out adaptation (Milton, Martin, and Melham 2016; Ostroff
2011), while inclusive design often retains a shared baseline
to which variation can be added (Lteif et al. 2025; Patrick and
Hollenbeck 2021). In both cases, design remains anchored to
the designer ’s implicit assumptions about how an imaginary
“ideal” user behaves (Norman 1999). Variation is treated as a
peripheral exception, to be managed through ad hoc, after-
market accommodations.
NPD comprises eight principles (Table 3) that seek to decou-
ple design from problematic assumptions about normal human
behavior (Cook and Stein ﬁeld 2026; Escobar 2018; Tunstall
2023). By questioning how “typical” ways of sensing, thinking,
feeling, and relating are de ﬁned within particular systems, NPD
foregrounds the norms that shape whose functioning is consid-
ered “normal” in behavioral science (Costanza-Chock 2020;
Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan 2010; Tunstall 2023). Rather
than implying that differently functioning individuals should
be remediated to ﬁt with nonrepresentative behavioral norms
(Costanza-Chock 2020; Go Jefferies 2023; Hamraie 2016),
NPD advances plural solutions that support multiple forms of
participation in marketplaces (Cook and Stein ﬁeld 2026;
Doyle and McDowall 2022; Lteif et al. 2025).

STAR–NPD: A Paired Detect –Design
Framework
STAR and NPD are intended as a paired detect –design frame-
work for creating more inclusive and equitable marketplaces.
STAR helps identify hidden assumptions about how consumers
sense, think, feel, and relate, revealing points at which market-
place systems may be calibrated to a narrow range of neurolog-
ical functioning. NPD then translates these insights into design
principles intended to prevent, reduce, or eliminate system –con-
sumer misalignment. NPD is intentionally domain-neutral: The
principles can be applied to marketplace design, service deliv-
ery, technology platforms, customer experience, organizational
practices, public policy, and more. Because the Journal of
Public Policy & Marketing is concerned with how marketing
knowledge informs policy, we illustrate the paired detect –
design framework in a policy setting focused on in ﬂuencer mar-
keting (see Table 4).
Inﬂuencer marketing provides a useful context because its
rapid growth has outpaced the development of effective con-
sumer protection mechanisms, with scholars highlighting per-
sistent gaps in transparency, accountability, and regulatory
oversight (Anselmo, Mannaioli, and Sardo 2026). Valued glob-
ally at over $40 billion (Mordor Intelligence 2026), it also rep-
resents a signi ﬁcant and rapidly evolving marketing practice.
We organize our illustrative example around the formulation,
implementation, and evaluation (Andrews et al. 2022) of a pol-
icy that seeks to solve the problem of misleading in ﬂuencer
posts (e.g., paid sponsorship is not declared).

Policy Formulation: Shift From Individual De ﬁcits
to System Design
In problem formulation, traditional policy approaches ask
whether an “average” consumer would be misled, locating
harm in individual de ﬁcits. In contrast, the paired detect –design
framework reorients design problems from ﬁxing consumers to
ﬁxing the systems that fail to tolerate, let alone celebrate, neu-
rological plurality. STAR is used to ﬁrst identify systemic mis-
matches between marketing stimuli and variation in sensory,
cognitive, affective, and relational processing, particularly in
high-load contexts such as rapid scrolling, multitasking, or
fatigue. Shifting attention from who is vulnerable to where sys-
tems fail consumers reveals forms of avoidable harm that
remain invisible under standards centering on an “average” con-
sumer; NPD then redirects policy design away from vulnerable
individuals to the contexts (e.g., late-night use, dynamic envi-
ronments) and tactics (e.g., hidden disclosures, urgency cues)
that systematically produce misalignment, informed by diverse
and intersecting user experiences.
Policy Implementation: From Disclosure Compliance
to Assurance of Understanding
Once a policy has been implemented, practices that appear
benign under assumptions of uniform processing, such as ﬁne-
print disclosures, urgency-based messaging, sensory-intensive
formats, or complex technical terms and conditions that con-
sumers face (Anderson et al. 2016; Shamir 2008), may satisfy
formal (legal) requirements for transparency while nonetheless
producing systemically unequal outcomes. When the sensory,
cognitive, emotional, or relational demands required to per-
ceive, interpret, and act on information exceed the range of
STAR functioning present in the population, formally available
information may become functionally inaccessible to some con-
sumers. From a STAR –NPD perspective, the central policy
implementation question is therefore not simply whether infor-
mation is accurate but whether it remains meaningfully accessi-
ble and actionable across diverse processing pro ﬁles. This
perspective expands the focus of consumer policy beyond infor-
mational accuracy to informational accessibility. That is, the
task of public policy is not merely to ensure that information
exists, but that it can be perceived, interpreted, and acted on
across diverse processing pro ﬁles. In so doing, policy imple-
mentation moves from context-agnostic rules to context-
sensitive standards that conform with NPD principles around
sensory ﬂexibility, cognitive plasticity, emotional modulation,
and relational respect.

Proactive Auditing
In policy evaluation, the STAR –NPD framework shifts public
policy beyond detecting compliance with disclosure require-
ments (e.g., limited liability reporting of commercial sponsor-
ship) toward ensuring robust performance of market
interactions designed for neurological variation. This encour-
ages anticipatory rather than reactive interventions. For exam-
ple, rather than assuming disclosures will be noticed and
correctly interpreted, NPD calls for multimodal, layered com-
munications (e.g., essential, clarifying, full information) that
remain interpretable across users and contexts, or for incorpo-
rating safeguards when urgency cues or emotionally salient
appeals are used in high-load contexts. Evaluation of policy
thus moves from complaint-driven enforcement —premised
on users recognizing and reporting harm —to experience-
based auditing that evaluates how systems function in prac-
tice. This includes enabling low-friction, multichannel report-
ing, assessing outcomes across contexts, reducing stigma in
reporting, and using cross-case and intersectional evaluations
to ensure that marketplace designs perform equitably across
diverse consumer pro ﬁles. Collectively, marketing governance
shifts from top-down expert assessment to participatory, user-
informed evaluation.
Discussion
This research advances a neuropluralist perspective on con-
sumer behavior, marketing systems, and public policy by treat-
ing variation in how consumers sense, think, feel, and relate as a
fundamental and pervasive feature of human populations, rather
than a property of identi ﬁable clinical subgroups. From this per-
spective, the concept of a universally representative “average”
consumer is untenable because individuals occupy dynamic,
context-dependent, and jagged pro ﬁles across processing
dimensions. Consumer –marketplace misalignment, then, is
not an anomalous disruption to otherwise well-functioning sys-
tems but a predictable outcome of designs built on narrow
assumptions about homogenous neurological processing
dimensions. Our reframing shifts attention away from identify-
ing and accommodating outliers toward interrogating the
assumptions embedded in market and policy design, positioning
neurodiversity not as a marginal concern but a structural
approach to address market failure by ensuring access, partici-
pation, and well-being for diverse processing pro ﬁles.
Our central contribution lies in articulating this shift through
a paired detect–design framework that links the identi ﬁcation of
problematic neuronormative assumptions embedded in market-
ing and public policy with a corresponding design-level
response. STAR organizes a vast body of work on differences
in neurological processing to facilitate the identi ﬁcation of
assumptions about shared sensory thresholds, stable cognitive
capacity, predictable emotional regulation, and common social
grammars. When these assumptions fail to hold, outcomes
such as overload, misinterpretation, exclusion, and relationship
breakdown are no longer considered anomalous, but foreseeable
consequences of designing for an imagined “average” consumer
to enable ef ﬁcient processing of market signals.
NPD provides the corresponding design response to biases
revealed by STAR. Rather than adapting existing systems to
accommodate those who fall outside assumed norms, NPD
begins with heterogeneous processing as the baseline condi-
tion of market interactions and policy design. It shifts the
unit of concern from individuals to systems, the timing of
intervention from post hoc accommodation to anticipatory
design, and the normative goal from fairness within existing
standards to the recon ﬁguration of those standards themselves.
NPD emphasizes ﬂexibility, multiple pathways, and context
sensitivity, enabling market environments to function across
diverse and dynamic processing pro ﬁles to better serve all
marketplace consumers.
Collectively, the STAR–NPD framework suggests notions of
fairness be reframed as the capacity of marketplace systems to
function effectively across a broad range of neurological pro-
ﬁles. Traditional regulatory frameworks (e.g., the U.S. Federal
Trade Commission ’sd e ﬁnition of deceptive advertising) are
grounded in how a “reasonable” or “average” consumer would
respond, implicitly evaluating practices against a narrow band
of cognitive, sensory, affective, and social processing. Yet a
marketing communication may be compliant in one setting
but misleading in another, depending on how contextual
demands interact with processing variation. In contrast, a neuro-
pluralist perspective reframes core ethical principles such as
truthfulness, authenticity, respect, equity, and social responsi-
bility (Baker and Martinson 2001) as properties that remain
interpretable across diverse modes of neurological processing
and contexts, rather than by how well they serve an assumed
average consumer. This shifts the goal of marketplace design,
from achieving fairness within existing norms to reconstructing
the norms themselves, recognizing that norms are not neutral
but re ﬂect historically privileged ways of functioning
(Higgins and O ’Leary 2026).
Our research builds on consumer behavior scholarship that
has long documented individual differences across sensory,
cognitive, emotional, and relational dimensions. However,
this work has largely developed in parallel streams that treat het-
erogeneity as a moderator of otherwise universal effects. By
advancing neurological pluralism, we suggest that the founda-
tions of consumer behavior are themselves heterogeneous,
recasting individual differences from explanatory modi ﬁers to
the processing dimensions that structure how consumers engage
with market environments.
Our framework also extends critiques of behavioral science
by showing that, just as Western, educated, industrialized,
rich, and democratic (WEIRD) samples are “among the least
representative populations one could ﬁnd for generalizing about
humans” (Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan 2010, 61), assump-
tions about a single “average” consumer overlook systematic
variation in neurological processing. As the title of our article
indicates, the role of myth-busting is critical to improving
what we know about consumer behavior in markets (Ewert
14 Journal of Public Policy & Marketing 0(0)

2019). By centering the neurodiversity literature ’s conceptuali-
zation of natural neurological variation as a universal character-
istic of the human species, our approach emphasizes the
dynamic innate and contextual motivators shaping epistemolo-
gies of consumer behavior research. We also advance the neuro-
diversity literature by calling on ﬁrms and policymakers to
move beyond super ﬁcial recognition of neurodiversity toward
substantive design changes that re ﬂect the full range of human
processing (Acevedo and Stolz 2026; Chapman 2023).
Designing for variability is not without tension, particularly
for ﬁrms. Many marketing systems are optimized for ef ﬁ-
ciency, standardization, and scalability, relying on simpli ﬁed
models of consumer processing while tolerating evidence of
market failures. Calibrating systems for heterogeneous pro-
cessing will require multiple pa thways, adaptive interfaces,
and more complex communication strategies (Sirianni et al.
2026), potentially increasing costs and reducing short-term
efﬁciency. There is also a risk of overcomplicating systems,
introducing new forms of friction or cognitive burden.
Shifting toward designs that anticipate variability will also
require new capabilities, new metrics of success, and, in
many cases, a rethinking of what constitutes effective and
fair market practice.
These challenges poi nt to important directions for future
research. Empirical work is needed to operationalize the
STAR framework across contexts, to test NPD interventions,
and examine trade-offs between ﬂexibility and ef ﬁciency.
Additional research should explore how variability mani-
fests over time and across situ ations, and how systems can
adapt dynamically to changing processing pro ﬁles. While
the STAR provides a tractable starting point, it is not
exhaustive, and future research may re ﬁne or extend the
dimensions.
Ultimately, the central implication of this work is straightfor-
ward but consequential: The era of designing for the mythical
“average consumer” has reached its limits. Markets are not fail-
ing because consumers deviate from the norm; they are failing
because accepted norms are untenable if they rely on nonrepre-
sentative research and specious reasoning (Henrich, Heine, and
Norenzayan 2010). Contradictory interpretive paradigms coex-
ist in tension and are used to construct different realities of the
same phenomenon under analysis; alternative paradigms gener-
ate contrary beliefs and are consistent with a pluriversal ontol-
ogy (Escobar 2018). By advancing STAR as a detection
framework and NPD as a corresponding design paradigm, this
research offers a new way to understand and address market
failure under conditions of irreducible human variability.
When systems are designed for variability, they become more
inclusive, resilient, and effective.
