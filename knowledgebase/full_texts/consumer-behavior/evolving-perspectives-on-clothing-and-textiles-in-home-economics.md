 This article examines the conceptual evolution of clothing and textiles
within Home Economics, now recognized as Family and Consumer
Sciences (FCS). Historically framed around early 20th -century domestic
skills, garment construction, and frugal household m anagement, textile
education has transformed into a multifaceted, interdisciplinary research
discipline. Driven by mid -century industrialization, post -war consumer
behavior, and the formal institutional rebranding codified at the 1993
Scottsdale Conference , modern FCS leverages a human ecology
framework to address critical global challenges. Grounded in this
paradigm, contemporary textile pedagogy intersects material science,
circular economy models, digital technologies, and socio-cultural identity
frameworks. The field challenges persistent public misconceptions that
reduce textile education to mere manual needlework. Instead, modern
FCS curricula incorporate STEAM concepts -including fiber chemistry
and 3D digital design -alongside fast fashion critiques, t extile recycling,
adaptive apparel, and supply chain transparency. Furthermore, emerging
research directions embrace artificial intelligence, bio-fabricated textiles,
and policy-driven circular economy directives, establishing FCS as a vital
platform for e nvironmental stewardship, consumer empowerment, and
technological adaptation.
Keywords: Family and Consumer Sciences (FCS); Textile Education;

II. Historical Foundations:

During the early 20th century, clothing and textile
education within Home Economics was primarily
oriented around home garment construction,
mending, efficiency, and frugal resource management
(Welters & Lillethun, 2020). Grounded in the
foundational principles established at the Lake Placid
Conferences (1899 -1909), pioneer Ellen Swallow
Richards and early leaders framed household labor not
as menial work, but as a scientific endeavor aimed at
applying chemistry, economics, and physical science
to daily living (Welters & Lillethun, 2020). Within this
framework, garment creation and maintenance were
central to domestic stability and hygiene.
In an era before mass -produced synthetic materials
and affordable ready -to-wear clothing became
ubiquitous, home garment construction served as a
critical economic safeguard for families (Stage &
Vincenti, 1997). Home Economics curricula
emphasized hand an d machine sewing techniques,
pattern drafting, fiber identification, and textile care
(Nickols et al., 2009). Mending -including darning,
patching, and altering existing garments-was taught as
an essential skill to extend textile lifespans and
minimize hous ehold expenditure (Dreilinger, 2021).
This focus reflected a culture of frugal resource
management, where domestic efficiency directly
influenced a family’s financial resilience and social
presentation.
Furthermore, early 20th -century textile instruction
integrated scientific principles of sanitation and
industrial efficiency (Dreilinger, 2021; Welters &
Lillethun, 2020). Tailoring techniques and textile

Following World War II, clothing and textile
education within Home Economics underwent a
fundamental transition, shifting from domestic
garment construction toward consumer behavior,
ready-to-wear clothing selection, and synthetic fiber
education (Nickols et al., 2009; Stage & Vincenti,
1997). Post-war industrialization, standardized sizing,
and the mass expansion of manufacturing rendered
factory-produced apparel widely accessible and
affordable. As a result, home dressmaking transitioned
from an essential economic necessity into an elective
pastime (Cornell University Library, n.d.).
Recognizing that families were increasingly acting as
market purchasers rather than primary producers,
home economists systematically redirected their
curricula toward evaluating mass-produced garments,
wardrobe planning, budgeting, and critical consumer
literacy (Cornell University Library, n.d.; Stage &
Vincenti, 1997).
Simultaneously, the mid -20th century chemical
revolution introduced synthetic polymers -such as
nylon, polyester, and acrylic -which dramatically
altered global textile markets and domestic
maintenance routines (Hardin, 1990). The arrival of
"wash-and-wear" fabrics promised unprecedented
convenience, resistance to wrinkles, and altered
performance expectations for household appare l
(Hardin, 1990). To adapt to these innovations, Home
Economics integrated polymer chemistry and textile
testing into its academic programs. Educators
prioritized teaching students how to identify chemical
fiber properties, interpret laundry care labels, evaluate
fabric blends, and assess garment construction quality
in department store selections (Hardin, 1990; Nickols
et al., 2009).
Furthermore, this era marked the formal
incorporation of social-psychological perspectives into
clothing research. Scholars began investigating how
apparel selection influenced body image, social status,
professional mobility, and family resource
management in an increasingly consumer -oriented
society (Nickols et al., 2009; Stage & Vincenti, 1997).
By pivoting from physical stitching to scientific
evaluation, consumer behavior, and material analysis,
the discipline established a vital bridge between
modern industrial productions and informed
household consumption.
During the late 20th century, clothing and textile
research within Home Economics expanded
significantly beyond consumer selection into a
multifaceted discipline encompassing physical textile
science, social -psychological dynamics, and cultural
identities (Johnson et al., 2014; Nickols et al., 2009).
The establishment of dedicated academic journals -
such as the Clothing and Textiles Research Journal
(CTRJ) in 1982 -provided a formal venue for rigorous
empirical research, elevating the scholarly standing of
apparel studies (Nickols et al., 2009).
On the scientific front, researchers deepened their
investigation into fiber performance, fabric quality
testing, thermal comfort, and polymer chemistry to
meet industrial standards and consumer demands.
Simultaneously, home economists recognized that
dress operates as a fundamental human
communication system (Kaiser, 1997; Roach -Higgins
& Eicher, 1992). Applying theories from sociology and
social psychology, scholars examined how clothing
serves as a primary stimulus influencing impression
formation, nonve rbal communication, gender roles,
self-esteem, and body image (Johnson et al., 2014;
Kaiser, 1997). Apparel was no longer viewed merely as
functional coverage, but as a symbolic medium

III. Contemporary Perspectives & Core Pillars:

1. Sustainability & Circularity:
Contemporary Family and Consumer Sciences (FCS)
re-evaluates textile education by challenging the
linear "take-make-waste" paradigm of the fast fashion
industry. Modern FCS curricula incorporate critical
fast fashion critiques, emphasizing how hyper -
consumption, chemical processing, and low -cost
garment production drive severe resource depletion
and environmental degradation (Niinimaki et al.,
2020). To mitigate these ecological footprint issues,
FCS scholars advocate for circular textile systems that
prioritize closed-loop life cycles.
Education within the discipline has expanded to
encompass advanced post -consumer strategies,
including mechanical and chemical textile recycling
alongside creative upcycling (Goel et al., 2025;
Schumacher & Forster, 2022). Furthermore, design
pedagogy incorporates zero-waste techniques-such as
pattern-making methods that eliminate offcut waste -
to prevent material loss before garments reach the
production phase (Niinim aki et al., 2020). Beyond
physical design, FCS fosters critical consumer literacy
through e co-labeling education. By teaching
individuals how to accurately evaluate third -party
environmental certifications, raw material origins, and
supply chain transparency metrics, FCS empowers
consumers to navigate greenwashing and make
ethically grounded purchasing decisions (Edirisooriya,
2025; Goel et al., 2025). Through these core pillars,
modern textile studies in FCS bridge material science,
consumer behavior, and environmental stewardship to
build a sustainable textile future.
2. Textile Science & Smart Fabrics:
In modern Family and Consumer Sciences (FCS),
textile studies bridge advanced material science,
consumer wellness, and everyday technological
applications. Rather than treating apparel merely as
passive coverage, contemporary FCS curricula
integrate techni cal textiles, bio -based polymers, and
smart fabrics to address real -world functionality in
daily life (Nickols et al., 2009). The discipline examines
how high -performance materials -such as moisture -
wicking synthetics, thermal -regulating phase-change
materials, and bio-polymers like polylactic acid (PLA)
and lyocell -redefine comfort, durability, and eco -
efficiency for consumers (Iris Publishers, 2025;
Patsnap, 2025).
Simultaneously, the convergence of electronic
engineering and textile design has elevated wearable
technology as a major research pillar within FCS.
Modern smart fabrics incorporate conductive yarns,
micro-sensors, and flexible electronics into everyday
garments to facilitate continuous health diagnostics,
biometric tracking, and workplace safety monitoring.
By evaluating performance fabrics and smart textiles
through a human -ecology lens, FCS researchers
explore how these innovations impact personal well -
being, adaptive apparel for individuals with
specialized needs, and consumer literacy (Nickols et
al., 2009). Ultimately, this intersection of material

3. Socio-Cultural & Identity Aspects:
In modern Family and Consumer Sciences (FCS),
clothing and textiles are examined as symbolic media
through which individuals negotiate personal identity,
social inclusion, and cultural belonging (Johnson et al.,
2014; Roach-Higgins & Eicher, 1992). Moving beyond
functional coverage, contemporary FCS research
investigates the socio -psychological impact of dress
across four primary dimensions:
• Gender Dynamics:  Deconstructing traditional
binary dress codes and exploring how apparel
choices challenge or reinforce evolving gender
roles (Johnson et al., 2014).
• Body Positivity:  Evaluating the psychological
relationships between body image, self -esteem,
and clothing fit, promoting inclusive sizing that
validates diverse body types (Johnson et al., 2014;
Kaiser, 1997).
• Adaptive Clothing for Disabilities:  Integrating
universal design principles to create accessible,
dignified apparel —utilizing magnetic closures,
flat seams, and sensory -friendly fabrics —that
empowers individuals with physical or cognitive
disabilities to dress independently and participate
fully in social life (Brown & Nicholls, 2021;
Gupta, 2011).
• Cultural Expression Through Dress:  Analyzing
how ethnic dress, subcultural styles, and global
textile heritage preserve cultural identity and
foster intercultural literacy in pluralistic societies
(Roach-Higgins & Eicher, 1992).
By examining dress as a holistic human ecological
system, FCS research demonstrates how inclusive
apparel design directly enhances psychosocial well -
being, agency, and social equity (Brown & Nicholls,
2021; Kaiser, 1997).

4. Digital Transformation & Consumer Literacy:
In contemporary Family and Consumer Sciences
(FCS), digital transformation serves as a crucial bridge
between technological innovation, consumer literacy,
and sustainable apparel management (Casciani et al.,
2022). The exponential growth of e -commerce has
fundamentally reshaped the fashion marketplace,
necessitating new forms of digital consumer literacy to
navigate complex online environments.
To mitigate high return rates and sizing uncertainties
inherent to digital retail, FCS research explores 3D
virtual fitting rooms (VFRs) and augmented reality
try-on technologies. These tools enhance cognitive
product evaluation, reduce transport-related emissions
from garment returns, and improve overall consumer
satisfaction (Casciani et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2022).
Simultaneously, digital wardrobe management
applications empower consumers to inventory
personal garments, track cost -per-wear metrics, and
curate creative outfits from existing wardrobes,
directly curbing impulse hyper -consumption
(Casciani et al., 2022).  Beyond the consumer micro -
system, digital transformation enables end -to-end
supply chain transparency through blockchain
tracking, QR -code disclosure, and digital product
passports (Heim, 2022). By integrating these digital
tools into textile pedagogy, FC S equips consumers to
evaluate material origins, verify labor ethics, and
demand systemic corporate accountability across
modern apparel ecosystems (Heim, 2022; Lee et al.,
2022).

Furthermore, FCS curricula foster ethical buying
choices by guiding consumers across four key phases
of textile interactions: acquisition, use, maintenance,
and disposal (Ribeiro et al., 2023). Students learn to
prioritize high -durability fabrics, consider  fair-trade
labor standards, support second -hand circular

everyday garment maintenance to systemic ecological

V. Future Directions & Research Agenda:

Emerging Trends:
Within contemporary Family and Consumer Sciences
(FCS), future clothing and textile research operates at
the nexus of advanced computational technology,
biotechnology, and regulatory frameworks. Artificial
Intelligence (AI) is transforming textile science
through predictive modeling, computer vision for
automated fabric sorting, and algorithmic zero -waste
pattern generation, significantly reducing pre -
consumer chemical and material waste.
Concurrently, bio -fabrication redefines material
production by shifting from extraction to cultivation.
Fungal mycelium networks, algae -derived fibers, and
bacterial cellulose present fully biodegradable
alternatives to petroleum -based synthetics and
resource-intensive leathers, sequestering carbon while
drastically reducing water usage during growth.
However, scaling these technological and biological
innovations requires alignment with policy -driven
sustainable fashion strategies. FCS researchers
increasingly investigate the systemic impact of
emerging regulations -such as Extended Producer
Responsibility (EPR) mandates, eco-design directives,
and digital product passport requirements -which
enforce supply chain transparency and circular
accountability across global markets. By positioning AI
optimization, bio -fabricated materials, and policy
compliance within a holistic human ecology
framework, FCS research establishes a comprehensive
roadmap for accelerating the transition toward a
circular, climate-resilient global textile ecosystem.
Gaps in Literature:
Despite the expanding focus on sustainable fashion
within Family and Consumer Sciences (FCS), a critical
gap persists in the empirical literature regarding the
long-term impact of textile literacy on household
waste reduction (Abbate et al., 2023; Tang, 20 23).
While existing scholarship extensively measures
immediate educational outcomes -such as increased
consumer awareness, short-term intention to buy eco-
friendly apparel, or initial adoption of repair
techniques-longitudinal studies tracking actual post -
consumer waste behavior over extended multi -year
periods remain exceptionally scarce (Abbate et al.,
2023).
Current research models often rely on self -reported,
short-term surveys that fail to capture whether
educational interventions translate into sustained
lifestyle changes, such as reduced overall textile
acquisition, prolonged garment lifespans, or consistent
diversion of post -consumer textiles from municipal
solid waste streams (Abbate et al., 2023; Tang, 2023).
Furthermore, the literature lacks standardized metrics
to quantify how specific sub -components of textile
literacy-such as care label comprehension , fiber
chemistry knowledge, or mending proficiency -
directly correlate with decreased household waste
volume per capita over time (Abbate et al., 2023). To
establish a rigorous research agenda, future FCS
studies must prioritize longitudinal, data -driven
methodologies that track household textile disposal
patterns over years, bridging the gap between
theoretical consumer literacy and measurable, long -
term environmental outcomes.

VI. Conclusion:

The evolution of clothing and textiles within Home
Economics (Family and Consumer Sciences) illustrates
a profound paradigm shift from private domestic
efficiency to systemic global responsibility. Over the
past century, the discipline successfully transit ioned
from teaching traditional garment construction and
mending to delivering an interdisciplinary curriculum
grounded in human ecology, material science, and
consumer literacy. Modern FCS reframes textile
education as an essential mechanism for
environmental stewardship, empowering individuals
and communities to combat the ecological degradation
associated with fast fashion through circular design,
textile recycling, and critical consumerism.
Simultaneously, the integration of STEAM
frameworks—such as 3D digital design, smart fabrics,
and bio -fabrication-ensures the field remains at the
forefront of technological adaptation and industrial
innovation. Furthermore, by addressing socio-cultural
dynamics, gender roles, and adaptive clothing for
diverse needs, FCS promotes individual well -being,
equity, and social inclusion. Reclaiming the narrative
of textile studies in FCS is necessary to dismantle
reductionist stereotypes of domestic craft. Ultimately,
