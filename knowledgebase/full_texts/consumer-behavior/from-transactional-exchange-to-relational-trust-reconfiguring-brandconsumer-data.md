Abstract. As algorithms and large-scale data increasingly shape the digital economy,
consumer behavioral data have become an important strategic resource for firms. However,
under the logic of surveillance capitalism, extensive collection and commercialization of
consumer data may raise concerns regarding privacy, information asymmetry, and reduced
consumer autonomy, potentially undermining the foundations of long-term brand–consumer
trust. Existing research has largely emphasized the efficiency and commercial value of data
while paying comparatively less attention to the relational and ethical dimensions of data
use. Against this background, this study examines how brands may reconfigure consumer
data relationships in the context of surveillance capitalism. Drawing on surveillance
capitalism theory, relationship marketing, and trust formation, the study develops a
Relational Trust Framework. The framework conceptualizes a shift toward three interrelated
dimensions—shared ownership, shared governance, and value co-creation—and proposes a
potential pathway from data transparency to perceived fairness, trust formation, and long-
term relationship development. Theoretically, the framework extends transactional
approaches to consumer data by incorporating relational trust and consumer agency into the
analysis. Practically, it provides a conceptual basis for brands, digital platforms, and
regulators to develop more transparent, ethical, and sustainable approaches to data-driven
marketing.
Keywords: surveillance capitalism, relational trust, data governance, value co-creation, data
ethics
1. Introduction
The rapid advance of deep learning and distributed computing has driven digital marketing toward
smarter analysis and decision-making [1]. Behavioral traces, preferences, and location data from
digital touchpoints fuel customer profiling, personalization, and predictive analytics [2]. Yet as firms
grow more reliant on consumer data, research and practice have overwhelmingly prioritized short-
term gains—financial returns, conversion rates, and personalization efficacy—while neglecting how
data practices shape long-term brand–consumer relationships [3]. Under surveillance capitalism,

implications for marketers, platform designers, and regulators.
2. The transactional data exchange paradigm and its inherent tensions
Traditional marketing exchange theory generally conceptualizes interactions between firms and
consumers as transactional relationships based on value exchange. In data-driven marketing
environments, this transactional logic extends to the collection and use of consumer data: consumers
provide personal information in exchange for free services, personalized experiences, convenience,
or other immediate benefits, while firms use the resulting data for marketing analysis and
commercial activities [7]. However, as algorithmic extraction becomes increasingly embedded in
digital business models, the limitations of this paradigm become more visible.
2.1. The privacy paradox: a manifestation of the limitations of transactional data exchange
The privacy paradox refers to the discrepancy between consumers' stated concerns about personal
information and their actual data disclosure behavior. Consumers may express substantial concerns
about privacy risks while continuing to disclose personal information when using digital services in
order to obtain convenience, personalized experiences, or other immediate benefits [6].
Conventional explanations have often attributed this phenomenon to limitations in consumers'
information-processing abilities or to deviations from fully rational decision making. From the data-
relationship perspective adopted in this study, however, the privacy paradox may also reflect
limitations inherent in transactional data exchange.
Under conditions of substantial information asymmetry, consumers may have limited knowledge
of how their data are subsequently transferred, processed, reused, or transformed into additional
commercial value [8]. Consequently, data disclosure may not always reflect fully informed and
autonomous choice but may instead represent a pragmatic compromise under conditions in which
meaningful alternatives are limited. This suggests that the transactional paradigm may provide
insufficiently balanced value exchange when the longer-term use and commercial implications of
consumer data remain difficult for consumers to observe.
2.2. Transparency and control: the gap between formal empowerment and substantive agency
To respond to concerns about data misuse and privacy risks, firms and digital platforms have
introduced mechanisms such as privacy policies, disclosure notices, and data-preference settings.
However, formal transparency and empowerment do not necessarily translate into meaningful
consumer control. Complex legal terminology, limited visibility into algorithmic processes, and
highly technical data practices may make it difficult for consumers to understand how their data are

3. Data alienation and competitive distortion in the context of surveillance capitalism
In the era of surveillance capitalism, data have become more than a supporting marketing resource,
they have increasingly become an important source of capital accumulation, predictive capability,
and competitive advantage [4]. As firms place greater strategic value on data volume, analytical
capability, and predictive accuracy, data may acquire an increasingly dominant role in competitive
decision making. This development may contribute to a form of data fetishism, whereby the scale of
data resources and the accuracy of prediction are treated as central determinants of market position,
potentially reshaping the underlying logic of competition.
3.1. The reshaping of traditional competitive dynamics by data-driven strategies
Porter's competitive framework emphasizes the role of industry structure and resource advantages in
shaping competitive barriers. In digital markets, data-driven strategies further modify how some
competitive forces operate [2]. In terms of buyer bargaining power, firms may use large-scale
consumer data to construct detailed customer profiles, thereby increasing their informational
advantage over consumers and potentially enabling practices such as personalized pricing. With
respect to the threat of new entrants and substitutes, leading platforms may accumulate data
resources, technological capabilities, and network effects that create substantial barriers to entry
[11]. Within industry rivalry, competition may also increasingly involve firms' capabilities to
acquire, analyze, and predict consumer behavior.
These developments suggest that data capabilities have become an increasingly important source
of competitive advantage in digital markets. However, when firms place excessive emphasis on data
accumulation and behavioral prediction, they may become more oriented toward short-term data
extraction and predictive efficiency while paying less attention to social responsibility, data ethics,
and the long-term quality of consumer relationships.
3.2. Reduced consumer data agency and the erosion of trust foundations
As consumer data become increasingly concentrated among a limited number of powerful platforms,
consumers' agency and practical control within data interactions may be weakened. Surveillance
capitalism relies on the continuous extraction and algorithmic modeling of everyday behavioral
information, transforming such activities into resources that can support prediction, targeted
marketing, and commercial decision making [4]. In this process, consumers may become
increasingly positioned as sources of extractable behavioral data rather than active participants in the
marketing process.
The relative opacity of algorithmic recommendation systems may further increase uncertainty
about how consumer information shapes commercial decisions. Consumers may therefore

4. The relational trust framework: a paradigm shift toward relational data exchange
The preceding analysis suggests that conventional transactional data exchange may create tensions
related to information asymmetry, consumer control, and long-term trust. To address these
limitations, this study proposes the Relational Trust Framework, which reconceptualizes consumer
data relationships from primarily unilateral value extraction toward longer-term relationship
development.
The framework does not reject the commercial value of consumer data or suggest that firms
should discontinue data-driven marketing. Instead, it emphasizes that the use of consumer data
should be embedded in relationships characterized by greater transparency, reciprocity, participation,
and mutual value creation. From this perspective, consumer data can be understood not merely as a
commercial asset controlled by firms, but also as a relational resource embedded within ongoing
brand–consumer interactions.
4.1. Three core pillars of the framework: shared ownership, shared governance, and value co-
creation
The Relational Trust Framework is built upon three interrelated pillars: shared ownership, shared
governance, and value co-creation. Together, these dimensions represent a shift from unilateral data
extraction toward more reciprocal and relationship-oriented forms of data interaction.
(1) Shared Ownership. Shared ownership challenges the assumption that consumer data should be
treated primarily as a firm-controlled commercial asset. Rather than reducing the issue to a
conventional property claim, this dimension emphasizes consumers' rights, agency, and legitimate
interests in relation to their personal and derived data. Firms may continue to use consumer data for
legitimate commercial purposes, but such use should be grounded in clearly communicated
authorization and appropriate mechanisms for value sharing [8].
(2) Shared Governance. Shared governance extends beyond formal privacy disclosure and
consent mechanisms by emphasizing meaningful consumer participation in data-related governance.
Firms may employ explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), distributed ledger technologies, and
dynamic privacy management tools to make data practices more visible and understandable and,
where feasible, enable consumers to adjust their data-sharing preferences [10]. The objective is not
to assume that consumers can exercise complete control over every aspect of data processing, but
rather to strengthen the practical agency available to them.
(3) Value Co-creation. Value co-creation conceptualizes data interaction as a reciprocal value-
creation process rather than a unilateral extraction mechanism [11]. Consumers contribute
behavioral and preference information that may support improvements in product design and service
responsiveness, while firms provide personalized value, information, or exclusive benefits in
response to consumer data contributions. Such reciprocity may create a more sustainable basis for
ongoing data interaction.

4.2. A four-stage pathway for relational trust development
Building on the three core pillars, this study proposes a potential pathway through which relational
trust may develop: data transparency → perceived fairness → trust formation → long-term
relationship development.
Data Transparency. Firms should provide clear and understandable information regarding the
purposes, scope, and general mechanisms of data collection and use. Greater transparency may
reduce information asymmetry and uncertainty by enabling consumers to develop more informed
expectations about data practices.
Perceived Fairness. Data transparency may influence consumers' perceptions of fairness. Such
perceptions include distributive fairness, referring to whether the benefits associated with data
sharing appear proportionate to consumers' contributions, and procedural fairness, referring to
whether the rules and processes governing data use are perceived as reasonable and legitimate.
Trust Formation. Greater perceived fairness may reduce consumers' concerns about the risks
associated with data sharing and contribute to more positive evaluations of the firm's intentions and
practices. In turn, these perceptions may create conditions conducive to trust formation.
Long-term Relationship Development. When trust is established within the data relationship, it
may support continued engagement, stronger loyalty, and more sustained brand–consumer
relationships. Thus, data practices may generate value not only through short-term targeting and
behavioral prediction but also through the development of long-term relational value.
Importantly, this four-stage sequence is proposed as a conceptual mechanism rather than an
empirically established causal relationship. Its effectiveness may depend on contextual factors such
as privacy sensitivity, technological literacy, and industry characteristics.
4.3. Recalibrating marketing strategy: STP and brand positioning
The Relational Trust Framework integrates data-related trust into core marketing practices—
segmentation, targeting, positioning, and CRM—without replacing them. It shows how established
strategies can be recalibrated to support relational value, not just transactional efficiency.
Segmentation and targeting should go beyond behavioral tracking and predictive analytics. Firms
can incorporate consumers' privacy sensitivity and data-trust preferences when defining segments
and selecting targets. Positioning, meanwhile, can foreground commitments to data ethics and
responsibility—making data integrity a distinctive brand differentiator. Brands can reposition
themselves—not as algorithmic recommenders alone, but as trustworthy data custodians. This shift
imposes ethical boundaries on technology use and strengthens perceptions of brand responsibility
and relational intent. In CRM, firms should move past lifetime-value models centered on short-term
conversion and individual-level data extraction. Instead, they can cultivate high-trust digital
communities where lifetime value and relationship value co-evolve.

5. Discussion
5.1. Managerial implications
The Relational Trust Framework grounds data ethics and trust in actionable marketing practice. For
brands, ethical data use is not just a compliance issue—it is a strategic lever for strengthening
customer relationships and informing marketing decisions.
Product: Embed Privacy by Design into development, integrate data controls (e.g., granular
consent, real-time data dashboards) directly into core product functionality.
Price: Introduce value-return mechanisms—such as tiered membership benefits, premium service
access, or personalized rewards—in exchange for data contributions. This signals reciprocity and
reinforces fair exchange.
Place: Design intuitive, visual data-management centers at key digital touchpoints (e.g., app
settings, website portals) to make data flows transparent and controllable across platforms.
Promotion: Replace intrusive, behaviorally targeted ads with permission-based, preference-
aligned communication—prioritizing relevance and respect over reach and reactivity.

For digital platforms, the framework suggests moving beyond closed and firm-centered data
control toward more transparent and participatory governance mechanisms. Platforms may improve
consumer agency by providing clearer authorization, data management, and withdrawal
mechanisms, as well as greater visibility into how data are used. For regulators, governance should
not focus exclusively on ex post compliance. Regulatory arrangements may also encourage
proactive data practices related to transparency, accountability, and consumer data rights, thereby
supporting a market environment in which responsible data use can coexist with commercial value
creation.
5.2. Future research
Future research may further explore the development of a post-surveillance marketing ecosystem, in
which digital marketing gradually evolves from a model centered primarily on data control toward
one emphasizing digital trust and responsible data practices.
First, cross-cultural quantitative and experimental studies can examine the applicability and
boundary conditions of the proposed pathway from data transparency to perceived fairness and trust
formation across different cultural and privacy contexts. Second, future studies can investigate how
emerging technologies—including generative artificial intelligence, zero-knowledge proofs,
federated learning, and decentralized identity (DID)—may contribute to the implementation of
shared governance and value co-creation [12]. Third, longitudinal case studies across industries such
as finance, healthcare, and e-commerce can examine the long-term effects of ethical data strategies
on firms' financial performance and brand equity.
6. Conclusion
As digital technologies reshape marketing, consumer data have become vital for customer analysis,
personalization, and decision-making. Yet under surveillance capitalism, continuous data extraction
raises privacy concerns, exacerbates information asymmetry, and erodes consumer control—
potentially undermining long-term brand–consumer relationships. This study identifies the limits of
transactional data exchange and introduces the Relational Trust Framework to shift brand–consumer
data interactions from unilateral extraction toward transparency, reciprocity, and longevity.
The framework rests on three interrelated pillars: hared ownership: Affirms consumers' rights,
agency, and legitimate interests in their personal and derived data. Shared governance: Enables
meaningful consumer participation in data decisions—going beyond boilerplate consent and
disclosure. Value co-creation: Treats data exchange as reciprocal—consumers contribute insights for
product/service improvement, firms deliver functional, experiential, or relational value in return.
Together, these pillars reframe consumer data not as a commodity for extraction, but as a relational
resource that sustains mutual value.

From this foundation, this paper proposes a conceptual pathway from data transparency to
perceived fairness, trust formation, and long-term relationship development.Transparency reduces
uncertainty and clarifies data practices, shaping perceptions of distributive and procedural fairness.
Fairness, in turn, fosters trust, trusted brands earn sustained engagement, loyalty, and relational
value. Crucially, this is a theoretical proposition—not an empirically verified causal chain.
Theoretically, the framework advances data-driven marketing research by integrating trust and
relationship theory into data governance—shifting focus from efficiency to relational sustainability.
Managerially, it shows how data ethics can be embedded across core marketing activities: product
design, pricing, digital distribution, promotion, branding, and CRM. For platforms and regulators, it
underscores transparency, accountability, and substantive consumer participation. Limitations
include: (1) the conceptual nature of the study—no empirical validation, (2) context dependence of
shared ownership/governance across industries and regulatory regimes, and (3) variability in
consumer responses due to privacy sensitivity or digital literacy. Future work should test the
framework via surveys, experiments, longitudinal cases, and large-scale quantitative analysis to map
its mechanisms and boundary conditions. Overall, the Relational Trust Framework offers a
foundational lens for evolving digital marketing from transactional exchange to transparent,
reciprocal, and trust-based relationships.
