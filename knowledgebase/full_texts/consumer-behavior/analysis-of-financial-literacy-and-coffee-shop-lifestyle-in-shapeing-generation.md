Abstract. Generation Z in major Indonesian cities shows relatively adequate financial literacy,
yet the rapid growth of the coffee shop industry remains accompanied by recurring and partly
unplanned consumption patterns. This study explores how financial literacy a nd coffee shop
lifestyle interact to shape the consumptive behavior of Generation Z in Semarang, a secondary
city rarely covered by qualitative research on this topic. Using a qualitative case study approach
with a phenomenological orientation, the study involved six purposively selected informants:
three university students, two young Generation Z workers with varying visit frequency and
spending levels, and one coffee shop manager as a business -side triangulation source. Data
were collected through semi -structured interviews and analyzed thematically, with
trustworthiness maintained through source triangulation, triangulation across four theoretical
lenses, and member checking with all informants. Findings indicate that informants' financial
literacy was polarized between those with systematic budgeting discipline and those who relied
on rough estimates, and that strong financial literacy curbs impulsive purchases but does not
reduce visit frequency, as coffee shops function as a third place for work, study , and
socializing. Consumptive behavior emerges most vulnerably during add-on ordering moments
triggered by social pressure or FOMO and eased by digital payment methods that obscure
awareness of cumulative spending. These findings suggest that financial literacy interventions
should target micro decision moments at the point of transaction rather than general financial
knowledge alone, offering practical insight for Generation Z, coffee shop operators, and
financial education institutions.

Keywords: Coffee Shop Lifestyle; Consumptive Behavior; Financial Literacy; Generation Z;
Third Place.

1. Background
Ideally, Indonesia's Generation Z who grew up as digital natives with full access to
financial applications, e -wallets, and financial literacy information on social media —should
be the demographic most capable of managing their finances rationally. The 2024 National
Survey on Financial Lit eracy and Inclusion (SNLIK) conducted by the Financial Services
Authority (OJK) and the Central Statistics Agency (BPS) recorded a financial literacy index
of approximately 65.4 percent for the Indonesian population. Furthermore, the 18 –25 age
group, which  comprises the majority of the informants in this study, recorded the third -
highest composite financial literacy index nationally at 70.19 percent (OJK & BPS, 2024).
Theoretically, this young urban generation should be financially literate enough to manage
lifestyle expenses proportionally.
However, the coffee shop lifestyle is growing rapidly and absorbs a significant portion
of spending among this age group. The Indonesian coffee shop industry is currently
recognized as one of the largest globally in terms of outlet numbers, with an estimat ed
462,000 locations and an annual market growth of approximately 10 percent (Seasia Stats,
2026; USDA, 2025). This phenomenon is reinforced  by a functional shift: coffee shops are
no longer merely places to purchase beverages but have evolved into "third spaces" for
working, studying, and socializing. This shift is clearly reflected in the visitation patterns of the

This gap raises a fundamental question: why does a relatively adequate level of financial
literacy among Generation Z not necessarily prevent repetitive and unplanned consumption
patterns in coffee shops? Among the six informants interviewed—comprising five Generation
Z individuals (students and young professionals) and one coffee shop manager representing
the business owner's perspective —diverse patterns emerged. Some informants with strict
budget discipline still consider coffee shop visits a routine nece ssity, while others
acknowledged impulsive behavior driven by FOMO (Fear of Missing Out) and the
convenience of digital payments. This variation suggests that the relationship between
financial literacy and consumptive behavior is nonlinear, thus requiring further qualitative
exploration.
Previous studies on financial management within the productive age group have heavily
focused on capital management and financial performance at the business level (Juliani  &
Nugraheni, 2025; Laia & Nugraheni, 2025). Other quantitative studies examining the
relationship between financial literacy and Generation Z consumer behavior across various
cities have yielded inconsistent results. Some researchers identified a significant effect (Arestha
et al., 2024; Insani et al., 2026; Paramitalaksmi et al., 2022, in Yogyakarta), whereas others
found no significant direct impact of financial literacy, although lifestyle remained a consistent
influential factor (Putrantona et al., 2024; Ananda Putri et al., 2025; Mahendra et al., 2023, in
Surabaya; Idris et al., 2025; Paramita et al., 2024). This  recurring cross-city pattern—where
lifestyle serves as a more reliable predictor of consumer behavior than financial literacy
alone—reinforces the urgency of a qualitative investigation. Qualitative research specifically
linking personal financial literac y to third -space lifestyles in second -tier cities like Semarang
remains relatively limited; this study attempts to address that exact gap.
The urgency of this research is founded  on three rationales. Practically, the aggressive
expansion of the coffee shop industry creates persistent consumption pressures for
Generation Z in major cities, including Semarang. Socially, a deeper understanding of the
mechanisms driving this consumer behavior can assist Generation Z, parents, and financial
education institutions in designing more targeted interventions. Theoretically, this research
enriches the study of personal financial management by providing a qualitative perspective
that captures the nuanced, firsthand experiences of the informants.

2. Theoretical Study
The Theory of Planned Behavior (Ajzen, 1991) explains that individual behavior,
including consumption behavior, is influenced by intentions formed by attitudes toward the
behavior, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. This theory is relevant  in
explaining why informants who are aware of their budget limits are still motivated to visit
coffee shops due to friends' invitations or social media trends.
Lusardi and Mitchell (2014) define financial literacy as a form of investment in human
capital namely, the knowledge and skills in managing finances that influence the quality of
economic decisions throughout life. This theory serves as the primary framework for mapping
variations in literacy levels among the informants in this study.
Rook (1985) defines impulse buying not simply as an unplanned  purchase, but rather
as a five -stage psychological process: a sudden urge, psychological disequilibrium, inner
conflict, cognitive devaluation, and disregard for consequences. This framework is utilized to
analyze the experiences of informants who purchas ed additional items beyond their initial
plans.
Such sudden psychological urges are often facilitated by the welcoming environments
of social venues. Oldenburg (1989) conceptualizes coffee shops and similar locations as a
"third space" an area outside the home and workplace or campus that serves as an arena for
informal social interaction. This concept remains highly relevant in recent studies of urban
coffee shops, which demonstrate that the function of the third space is shifting to me et the
needs of digital-native consumers, evolving from flexible workspaces into spaces for building
social connections (Ferreira et al., 2021; Pramasari et al., 2024). This theory explains why many
informants interpret coffee shops not simply as places to consume, but as worthwhile work
or study spaces, even if they incur a cost.

These four theories are utilized as interpretive lenses for the qualitative interview data,
rather than as statistically tested causal models. Consequently, three main propositions are
proposed:
(P1):  Robust financial literacy does not always prevent consumptive behavior when coffee
shops are interpreted as functional necessities.
(P2):  Social norms and FOMO (Fear of Missing Out) are the primary triggers for impulsive
purchases that override budgetary constraints.
(P3):  The convenience of digital payments weakens financial awareness within the third space,
meaning that theoretical financial literacy is not automatically reflected in actual
consumer behavior.

3. Research Methods
This study utilized a qualitative approach with a phenomenological case study design to
capture the informants' subjective meanings regarding their experiences with financial literacy
and the coffee shop lifestyle. The research was conducted in Semarang Ci ty and involved six
purposively selected informants: three students and two young Generation Z workers,
alongside one coffee shop manager who served as a source of triangulation from a business
perspective. The five consumer informants were selected  based on varying characteristics,
including a high frequency of coffee shop visits, high spending, active e -wallet or paylater
usage, robust financial literacy, and prior experiences with impulsive purchasing.
Data collection was conducted through semi -structured interviews utilizing interview
guides that covered informant profiles, financial literacy (budget management, priority scales,
savings awareness, and risk mitigation), the coffee shop lifestyle (frequency, motivation, price
sensitivity, and social-visual influence), and consumer behavior (impulse buying, consumption
rationality, financial impact, and self -evaluation). The collected data were evaluated using
thematic analysis through the stages of data familiarization, coding, theme search and review,
and report preparation (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Data validity was maintained through source
triangulation (comparing the five consumer informants against the single manager informant),
theory triangulation (employing the four theoretical lenses), and member checking. During the
member checking process, transcription results and initial interpretations were reconfirmed
with all six informants to ensure accuracy and strict alignment with their intended experiences.

4. Results And Discussion
Polarized Financial Literacy: Knowledge Does Not Always Equate to Control
These findings suggest that the financial literacy levels of Generation Z in this study are
sharply polarized  between a systematically disciplined group and an estimate -based group.
Furthermore, this polarization does not entirely correlate with age or employment status.
Informant 4, a 25-year-old financial analyst, explicitly described his financial management:
"I always make a budget at the beginning of the month... I also record all my expenses
through an app so I can evaluate my spending at the end of each month." (Informant 4)
In contrast, Informant 5, a 20-year-old student, admitted:
"Honestly, I am not very disciplined in managing my finances... I often only realize I
am running low on money when the end of the month approaches." (Informant 5)
This contrast is not merely about age, but rather about systems. Informant 4 utilizes
explicit tracking tools, while Informant 5 relies on mental estimates without formal record -
keeping. This aligns with Lusardi and Mitchell's (2014) thesis that financial literacy is a form
of human capital that requires conscious investment. Notably, even Informant 4, the most
disciplined of the group still views coffee shop visits as a planned routine rather than an
expense to be entirely avoided. This pattern indicates that robust financial literacy does not
necessarily equate to reducing coffee shop consumption, but rather to managing it effectively
within a predetermined budget.
Coffee Shops as Functional Third Spaces, Not Merely a Lifestyle
Within the second theme, the primary motive for visiting coffee shops among the
majority of informants was functional, which later shifted into a social habit:
"Initially, I went there to work on assignments because the atmosphere was more
comfortable than in my boarding house. Over time, it became a habit because my friends often
invited me to meet at coffee shops." (Informant 1)
This perspective was corroborated from a business standpoint by Informant 6, a coffee
shop manager:

"Coffee shops are now more than just places to buy coffee. Many customers come to
study, do assignments, or work remotely... Coffee shops have evolved into social spaces that
support a variety of activities." (Informant 6)
This pattern aligns with Oldenburg's (1989) concept of the "third space," where coffee
shops occupy a voluntarily chosen position outside the home and the campus or office. The
shift from functional needs to social habits demonstrates how the third space gradually
absorbs social functions beyond its original purpose—a process that informants do not always
recognize as a form of increased consumption. Informants 3 and 4, both young professionals,
explicitly cited work comfort, internet connectivity, and the availability of power outlets as
primary considerations, rather than aesthetics. Meanwhile, Informants 1, 2, and 5, who are
students, identified aesthetic factors as secondary considerations.
From FOMO to the Convenience of Digital Payments: The Hidden Path to
Consumptive Behavior
The third theme reveals that the informants' consumptive behavior does not stem solely
from a lack of financial knowledge, but rather from social pressure (FOMO) and the
convenience of digital payments:
"Sometimes I see friends posting photos of themselves at new coffee shops... I end up
going along even though I don't really need to." (Informant 1)
"Because paying using an e-wallet feels so convenient, I sometimes don't really realize
my total spending at that moment." (Informant 3)
Informant 6 confirmed this pattern through the lens of business transaction data:
"When there is a cashback promo or discount from a digital payment app, the number
of transactions usually increases quite sharply. Many customers admit to taking advantage of
these promos, unknowingly purchasing more than they originally planned." (Informant 6)
This behavioral pattern neatly maps onto the subjective norms within the Theory of
Planned Behavior (TPB) and Rook's (1985) five -stage process. The convenience of e -wallets
acts as an accelerator, reducing perceived behavioral control by obscuring physical  financial
signals. Informant 4, possessing the highest financial literacy among the participants, admitted
to rarely experiencing FOMO and consistently evaluating his financial situation before
accepting an invitation. This indicates that financial litera cy, when coupled with an explicit
record-keeping system, can act as a buffer against impulsive purchases, even if it does not
eliminate the coffee shop visits entirely.
Discussion
The three themes discussed above address the research problem formulation: the
relationship between financial literacy and consumer behavior is not linear. Instead, it is
moderated by whether the financial literacy is accompanied by an explicit tracking sy stem, as
well as the extent to which individuals are exposed to FOMO and the friction -free nature of
digital payments.
These findings are partially consistent with Insani  et al. (2026), who determined that a
cashless society and financial literacy significantly influence Generation Z's "latte factor"
behavior in Surabaya. However, these qualitative findings also serve to complement the
quantitative research conducted by Prawitasari et al. (2025) in Semarang, which concluded that
FOMO did not have a statistically significant effect. The qualitative data in this current study
demonstrates that FOMO was explicitly mentioned by four out of the five Generation Z
informants as a trigger for unplanned visits. Contrasting the statistical insignificance found in
broader quantitative modeling with the qualitative realities of individual behavior makes for a
highly compelling academic argument. It confirms the added value of a qu alitative approach
in capturing nuanced experiences that may not appear statistically significant in aggregate data,
yet remain profoundly significant at the individual level.

5. Conclusion And Suggestions
This study concludes that financial literacy and the coffee shop lifestyle shape
Generation Z's consumer behavior in Semarang through complex interactions, rather than a
simple cause -and-effect relationship. Robust financial literacy has been shown  to reduce
spontaneous impulse purchases; however, it does not eliminate the frequency of coffee shop
visits themselves, as these venues have evolved into functional third spaces for working,
studying, and socializing. The most consistent points of consume r vulnerability arise during
secondary purchase moments, which are triggered by social pressure and facilitated by digital
payment systems. Therefore, efforts to mitigate consumptive behavior among Generation Z

cannot rely solely on increasing general financial knowledge; they must also target micro -
decision moments directly at the point of transaction.
This study is constrained by a small number of informants within a single city; thus, the
findings are highly contextual and are not intended for statistical generalization. The depth of
the thematic analysis also relies on the informants' openness during interviews, which may
potentially be influenced by social desirability bias. While these constraints restrict the broad
applicability of the conclusions, they do not compromise the validity of the qualitative patterns
identified through source and theoretical triangulation.
Practically, we recommend that Generation Z implement an explicit expense -tracking
system, particularly for digital transactions. Furthermore, coffee shop managers could consider
implementing transparent notifications for cumulative spending per visit. Financial education
institutions are advised  to design literacy materials that target specific behavioral contexts
rather than merely providing general knowledge about financial products. Future research
should expand the informant pool across multiple cities, quantitatively test the moderating
role of digital payment convenience, and conduct longitudinal studies on the same Generation
Z cohorts as they transition from university to the workforce.
