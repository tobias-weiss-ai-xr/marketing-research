Panic buying, the sudden, affectively driven surge in purchasing essential goods
in excess of rational need, has been widely documented during health crises
but remains underexplored as a response to economic policies such as tariffs.
Critically, panic buying must be distinguished from rational stockpiling: while
anticipatory purchasing in response to tariff-induced price increases may reflect
sound intertemporal decision-making, panic buying is characterized by purchases
exceeding individually optimal levels and driven by negative affect, perceived
scarcity, and social contagion rather than objective risk assessment alone. This
article extends the integrated psychodynamic model of panic buying developed
by Cooper and Gordon (1) to address tariff-induced consumer behavior, clarify-
ing its psychological underpinnings and broader significance. The article exam-
ines how well-established frameworks from behavioral economics, including
Prospect Theory, scarcity heuristics, and affect-based models, account for much
of the surface phenomenology of tariff-induced panic buying, while arguing that
psychodynamic mechanisms explain a clinically significant residual: namely, why
a subset of individuals exhibits panic buying that is disproportionate to objec -
tive threat, resistant to rational persuasion, and recurrent across successive eco-
nomic crises. The article introduces three core psychodynamic mechanisms
operating across a five-phase model: (1) developmental vulnerability rooted in
economic trauma; (2) threat activation involving projection and splitting; and (3)
behavioral enactment via displacement and transitional object dynamics. Each
mechanism is distinguished conceptually, linked to specific phases of the model,
and operationalized with proposed measurement strategies. By providing a clini-
cally actionable framework that complements existing behavioral and economic
accounts, the analysis contributes to both therapeutic practice and public policy
during periods of economic uncertainty.

Introduction
Panic buying, defined as a sudden surge in the purchase of essential goods in response to
perceived threats or adverse events, characterized by purchases exceeding individually optimal
levels and driven by heightened negative affect, has emerged as a significant behavioral phe-
nomenon during crises such as pandemics, natural disasters, and major policy changes (1, 2).
While panic buying has been extensively documented during the COVID-19 pandemic, its

occurrence in response to economic policies, specifically tariffs,
remains underexplored in the psychological literature. Tariffs, which
are government-imposed duties on imported goods, can lead to price
increases and supply chain disruptions, fostering uncertainty and fear
among consumers that closely parallel the psychological conditions
precipitating panic buying during health emergencies.
Existing research has established that panic buying is driven by
negative emotions such as anxiety, fear, and institutional mistrust,
particularly during periods of uncertainty or perceived scarcity (3, 4).
These emotions are further amplified by media coverage and social
contagion, leading to atypical consumer behaviors that disrupt supply
and demand. The psychodynamic framework, as articulated by
Cooper and Gordon (1), conceptualizes panic buying as a defense
mechanism rooted in developmental history, unresolved trauma, and
the need to regain control during destabilizing events.
Despite growing interest in the psychological underpinnings of
panic buying, there is a notable gap in the literature regarding its
occurrence in response to tariffs and other economic policy shifts.
Most studies have focused on health crises, with limited attention to
the unique triggers and psychodynamic processes associated with eco-
nomic stressors. This gap is significant: tariffs evoke similar patterns
of uncertainty, mistrust, and perceived scarcity, potentially activating
deep-seated anxieties and maladaptive coping strategies. Moreover,
tariff-induced panic buying unfolds over a longer temporal arc than
acute health crises, posing distinct challenges for both therapeutic
intervention and public policy response.
The present article addresses this gap by extending the psychody-
namic conceptualization of panic buying to include responses to tar-
iffs. Grounded in Cooper and Gordon’s ( 1) integrated six-phase
treatment model, the article argues that while behavioral economic
frameworks explain much of the surface phenomenology of panic
buying, psychodynamic mechanisms explain a clinically significant
residual, particularly the subset of cases characterized by dispropor-
tionate severity, resistance to rational reassurance, and recurrence.
Attention is also given to the role of socioeconomic status and cultural
context, which have been underrepresented in existing frameworks.
In doing so, this work aims to inform therapeutic interventions and
contribute to the broader discourse on consumer behavior during
economic crises.
Distinguishing panic buying from
rational stockpiling
A critical preliminary step, raised by recent consumer behavior
research, is distinguishing panic buying from rational or strategic
stockpiling. When consumers reasonably anticipate that tariffs will
increase prices for durable goods or non-perishables, purchasing
larger quantities in advance constitutes an economically rational inter-
temporal consumption decision (5, 6). Such behavior reflects standard
forward-looking optimization: the consumer correctly perceives a
future price increase and acts to minimize total expenditure. This is
stockpiling, not panic buying.
Panic buying, by contrast, is defined by three features that distin-
guish it from rational stockpiling: (1) purchases exceed the individu-
ally optimal quantity required to address the expected shortage or
price increase; (2) the behavior is driven primarily by heightened
negative affect, anxiety, fear, and perceived loss of control, rather than
accurate risk assessment; and (3) the behavior is responsive to social
contagion and media narratives independent of objective supply data
(2, 7). This distinction has direct empirical implications: rational
stockpiling should track objectively announced tariff levels and
expected price changes, whereas panic buying should correlate with
measures of consumer anxiety, media exposure, and institutional dis-
trust over and above objective economic indicators.
Importantly, the same purchasing behavior, buying larger-than-
usual quantities of a product, may reflect rational stockpiling in one
consumer and panic buying in another, depending on the underlying
psychological processes. It is precisely this within-behavior heteroge-
neity that psychodynamic frameworks are particularly equipped to
address: by examining the developmental histories, characteristic
defense mechanisms, and affective states of individual consumers,
clinicians can identify which individuals are engaging in dispropor-
tionate, affectively driven purchasing and intervene accordingly. A
necessary corollary of this framework is that “disproportionality” as a
criterion requires an explicit, independently specified benchmark of
individually optimal purchase quantity — for instance, the quantity
justified by announced tariff levels, expected price changes, household
consumption rates, and storage capacity. Without such a benchmark,
the residual that psychodynamic mechanisms are proposed to explain
risks being defined negatively, as whatever behavioral economic
models fail to predict, rather than measured independently. Future
empirical work operationalizing this framework should specify and
validate such a benchmark a priori, so that the psychodynamic con-
tribution can be assessed against a defined standard rather than a
theoretical remainder.
Theoretical background
Competing frameworks and the unique
contribution of psychodynamic theory
Before introducing the psychodynamic framework, it is necessary
to consider what established frameworks from behavioral economics,
decision science, and consumer psychology already explain, and
where they leave explanatory gaps that a psychodynamic account fills.
Prospect Theory (8) offers a parsimonious account of many panic
buying behaviors. Loss aversion predicts that consumers will respond
more intensely to the prospect of losing access to goods (through scar-
city or price increases) than to equivalent potential gains, and that
losses will be experienced as roughly twice as painful as equivalent
gains are pleasurable. Applied to tariff contexts, Prospect Theory
explains why consumers overreact to tariff announcements relative to
the objective probability and magnitude of price increases. However,
Prospect Theory does not explain individual differences in the severity
of the response: why do some consumers exhibit buying behavior far
exceeding what loss aversion alone would predict, while others, facing
identical objective conditions, do not panic buy at all?
Scarcity heuristics and availability-based cognition offer comple-
mentary accounts of how media framing of shortages creates per -
ceived scarcity that drives purchasing independent of actual supply
conditions (9). Affect-based models of decision-making demonstrate
that negative emotional states systematically bias risk perception and
choice toward protective behaviors (3). Herd behavior and social con-
tagion models explain how observing others stockpile creates

normative pressure to do likewise, regardless of individual need (3).
Together, these frameworks provide a robust account of the aggregate
pattern of tariff-induced panic buying.
What they do not explain is the clinically significant subset of
panic buying behavior that is: (a) disproportionate to the objective
economic threat as defined by any rational model; (b) resistant to
accurate information about supply conditions, which should, on a
rational or heuristic model, reduce panic; (c) recurrent across succes-
sive economic crises in the same individuals, suggesting a stable psy-
chological vulnerability rather than a situational response; and (d)
associated with significant psychological distress beyond the buying
behavior itself, such as generalized anxiety, somatic symptoms, or rela-
tionship strain. It is precisely this residual, unexplained by behavioral
economic frameworks, that psychodynamic theory is uniquely posi-
tioned to address, by tracing the behavior to developmental histories
and unconscious processes that operate independently of rational
appraisal.
Panic buying in economic contexts: from
health crises to trade policy
Existing empirical models of panic buying emerged predomi -
nantly from health crises, particularly COVID-19, where supply chain
disruptions and existential threats triggered widespread hoarding
behaviors (1, 2). Tariff-induced panic buying shares these core psy-
chological drivers while diverging in its economic triggers and tem-
poral dynamics. Tariffs create anticipatory scarcity: consumers fear
future price increases or shortages before disruptions materialize (10).
Unlike pandemic-driven buying, which centers on immediate survival
needs, tariff-related panic buying often targets durable goods and non-
perishables, reflecting a response to perceived long-term economic
instability. Research on dysfunctional safety behavior during health
crises further demonstrates how fear and perceived threat reliably pro-
duce hoarding patterns that parallel those expected in tariff con -
texts (11).
The psychological processes driving tariff-related panic buying
include uncertainty amplification through media coverage of trade
policy developments (4); projection of systemic mistrust, whereby
consumers externalize fears onto governments or foreign entities,
viewing tariffs as evidence of institutional incompetence or betrayal
(2); and social contagion, in which observing others stockpile creates
normative pressure that reinforces buying as a socially sanctioned
coping strategy (3). While direct empirical documentation of tariff-
induced panic buying as distinct from rational stockpiling remains
limited, the psychological conditions that produce panic buying in
health-crisis contexts, uncertainty, institutional mistrust, social con-
tagion, and negative affect, are plausibly present when major tariff
announcements are made, and the parallel is sufficiently close to war-
rant hypothesis generation and empirical investigation.
Psychodynamic foundations: Cooper and
Gordon’s integrated model
Cooper and Gordon’s (1) integrated psychodynamic model identi-
fies panic buying as a defense mechanism against feelings of helpless-
ness and existential threat. Their framework emphasizes how
developmental histories marked by resource deprivation or economic
trauma increase vulnerability to regressive behaviors during destabi-
lizing events. The model comprises six clinical phases: (1) intake,
where current symptoms and developmental history are gathered; (2)
identification of related themes between history and current presenta-
tion; (3) exploration of how the current context activates themes from
developmental history; (4) psychoeducation including mindfulness
training; (5) skill acquisition and rehearsal; and (6) consolidation and
termination.
The five-phase model proposed in the present article (Table 1) is
a descriptive-phenomenological framework, mapping the sequence of
psychological events from pre-existing vulnerability through behav-
ioral enactment and reinforcement, that operates in parallel with, and
is distinct from, Cooper and Gordon’s (1) six-phase clinical treatment
model. The five phases describe what happens psychologically during
a tariff-induced panic buying episode; Cooper and Gordon’s six phases
describe what the clinician does in response. The two frameworks are
complementary and sequentially related: clinical phases 1–2 corre-
spond to assessment of the phenomenological phases 1–2; clinical
phases 3–5 address the mechanisms active in phenomenological
phases 3–4; and clinical phase 6 addresses the prevention of re-entry
into the phenomenological phase 5 reinforcement cycle.
Limitations of current models and the need
for extension
While Cooper and Gordon’s (1) framework provides a robust
clinical scaffold, three gaps are particularly salient in economic con-
texts. First, regarding temporal dynamics: most models focus on acute
crises, whereas tariff effects unfold over months or years, complicating
both assessment and intervention timing. Second, regarding class and
cultural variability: current frameworks underrepresent how socio-
economic status and cultural norms, for instance, collectivist versus
individualist orientations, modulate responses to trade policies. Third,
regarding policy feedback loops: few studies examine how panic
buying itself influences tariff outcomes, such as artificially inflated
demand that may justify prolonged trade barriers, deepening eco -
nomic distrust in a self-reinforcing cycle (12).
Central hypothesis
The primary hypothesis advanced in this article is that a clinically
significant subset of consumer purchasing behavior following tariff
announcements constitutes panic buying, as distinguished from ratio-
nal stockpiling, and that this panic buying is generated by a psycho-
dynamic process in which tariff-related economic threat activates
unconscious defenses rooted in developmental histories of resource
deprivation, producing affectively driven purchasing behavior that
exceeds individually optimal levels and is resistant to rational
reassurance.
Three secondary, testable hypotheses follow from this framework.
First, the severity of panic buying in response to tariff announcements
will be predicted by measures of developmental economic trauma
(e.g., childhood exposure to poverty, food insecurity, or economic
instability) over and above objective indices of tariff impact, consumer
anxiety, and media exposure. Second, specific psychodynamic pro-
cesses will differentially mediate the relationship between tariff threat
and panic buying: projection and splitting will mediate the relation-
ship between institutional mistrust and threat amplification; displace-
ment will mediate the relationship between felt helplessness and
purchasing behavior; and transitional object dynamics will moderate
the relationship between attachment insecurity and stockpile quantity.

Third, panic buying severity will predict recurrence across successive
tariff-related events in the same individuals, consistent with repetition
compulsion, whereas rational stockpiling will not show this cross-
event stability after controlling for objective economic conditions.
These hypotheses are distinguishable from predictions generated
by behavioral economic frameworks: Prospect Theory predicts that
loss aversion, not developmental history, will be the primary predictor
of response magnitude; scarcity heuristics predict that media framing,
not attachment insecurity, will drive stockpile quantity. The psycho-
dynamic framework generates unique predictions that can be falsified
independently of these alternatives.
Three core psychodynamic mechanisms in
tariff-induced panic buying
The present framework consolidates the psychoanalytic literature
into three core mechanisms, each conceptually distinct, operating at
different phases of the model, and generating distinct empirical pre-
dictions and clinical implications. This consolidation responds to the
concern that a proliferation of constructs without clear differentiation
weakens explanatory parsimony. While the broader psychoanalytic
literature offers many relevant concepts, the three mechanisms below
are selected because they address different phases of the panic buying
sequence: developmental vulnerability, threat activation, and behav-
ioral enactment.
Mechanism 1: developmental vulnerability
— regression and repetition compulsion
The first mechanism operates at the developmental phase, prior to
any specific tariff event. Individuals who experienced significant eco-
nomic hardship during formative years, poverty, food insecurity,
parental financial instability, may develop persistent threat schemas in
which resource scarcity is encoded as a proximate and recurring
danger. When confronted with tariff-related economic news, these
individuals are prone to regression: a return to earlier modes of func-
tioning in which primitive accumulation behaviors (hoarding) were
adaptive responses to genuinely scarce resources (13). Critically, this
regression is typically not experienced consciously; the individual per-
ceives their stockpiling as rational precaution rather than as a re-
enactment of earlier coping strategies.

Repetition compulsion, the unconscious tendency to re-enact
unresolved past experiences in an attempt to master them, provides a
complementary account of why this vulnerability manifests as a stable,
cross-situational pattern (13). Consumers with developmental eco-
nomic trauma do not engage in disproportionate panic buying
because each new tariff episode is uniquely threatening; they do so
because the pattern of response is encoded from earlier experiences
and is repeatedly activated by cues of economic threat. This explains
the recurrence of panic buying in the same individuals across multiple
crisis episodes, a pattern that loss aversion and scarcity heuristics do
not predict once objective conditions are controlled. Empirically, this
mechanism is operationalizable through the Adverse Childhood
Experiences (ACEs) scale adapted for economic trauma, or the
Economic Hardship Questionnaire, administered in conjunction with
longitudinal purchasing data.
Mechanism 2: threat activation — projection
and splitting
The second mechanism operates at the trigger and amplification
phases, mediating between the receipt of tariff-related information
and the intensity of the threat response. Projection involves the exter-
nalization of internal states, particularly fears of inadequacy, helpless-
ness, and vulnerability, onto external objects or actors. In tariff
contexts, consumers project their own anxieties onto governmental
institutions or foreign trading partners, experiencing tariff policies as
hostile or incompetent acts by untrustworthy others rather than as
routine policy instruments with complex intended and unintended
consequences (2). This projection amplifies the perceived threat and
generates what is clinically recognizable as paranoid anxiety about
economic actors, anxiety that exceeds what objective analysis of trade
policy would warrant.
Splitting, the tendency to perceive institutions and situations in
polarized, all-or-nothing terms, operates in conjunction with projec-
tion to create a cognitive-affective environment in which panic is both
more likely and more intense. In tariff contexts, splitting manifests as
rigid dichotomization: domestic goods are idealized, foreign goods or
trading partners are demonized, one’s government is experienced as
either wholly reliable or wholly malevolent. This black-and-white cog-
nition forecloses the nuanced, probability-weighted appraisal that
characterizes rational stockpiling. Crucially, projection and splitting
are conceptually distinct from the affect-based decision models of
behavioral economics: whereas affect-based models treat negative
emotion as an input that distorts otherwise rational cognition, the
psychodynamic account treats projection and splitting as motivated,
structured distortions organized around specific relational and devel-
opmental anxieties. Empirically, this mechanism is operationalizable
through institutional trust scales administered proximate to tariff
announcements, combined with measures of splitting (e.g., the
Splitting Index or object relations measures) and anxiety (STAI).
Mechanism 3: Behavioral enactment —
displacement and transitional object
dynamics
The third mechanism operates at the behavioral phase, explaining
how threat activation is translated into specific purchasing behavior.
Displacement involves redirecting anxiety from its true source, diffuse
economic insecurity and felt helplessness in the face of macroeco -
nomic forces beyond individual control, toward a manageable behav-
ioral outlet. Stockpiling provides the illusion of agency: the individual
cannot control tariff policy but can control the contents of their
pantry. This mechanism is conceptually distinct from the herd behav-
ior and social contagion accounts offered by behavioral economics,
which explain why individuals conform to others’ purchasing behav-
ior; displacement explains why individuals who are not influenced by
social contagion, and who may even recognize their purchasing as
excessive, continue nonetheless, because the behavior functions to
manage an internal state rather than respond to an external norm.
Transitional object dynamics (14) provide the most clinically gen-
erative account of the specific form that displaced anxiety takes in
panic buying. Winnicott’s concept of transitional objects, physical
embodiments of security that provide comfort during separation or
threat, illuminates why stockpiled goods carry affective significance
beyond their practical value. The hoarded cans of food or cases of
household goods are not merely rational preparations; for the devel-
opmental-vulnerability subset, they are symbolic containers for anxi-
eties that cannot be more directly addressed. This framing generates
a unique clinical prediction: the symbolic content of hoarded goods
(their specific categories and quantities) will be more strongly pre-
dicted by attachment insecurity and felt threat to caregiving security
than by objective measures of supply disruption or price change.
Empirically, this mechanism is operationalizable through the Panic
Buying Scale (PBS; 4), supplemented by measures of attachment style
and items assessing the felt security provided by stockpiled goods.
From clinical observation to
population-level phenomena: scope and
limits of the framework
A central question raised by any extension of clinical psychoana-
lytic concepts to consumer behavior is whether mechanisms originally
derived from individual therapeutic observation can legitimately be
applied to population-level patterns. This concern is well-founded and
warrants explicit address.
The present framework does not claim that all panic buying, or
even most panic buying, is explained by psychodynamic mechanisms.
As the section on competing frameworks makes clear, prospect theory,
scarcity heuristics, affect-based models, and social contagion explain
a substantial portion of aggregate panic buying behavior. The psycho-
dynamic framework advances a more limited claim: that within any
population of consumers responding to a tariff announcement, there
is a clinically significant subset for whom the severity, persistence, and
resistance to rational reassurance of their panic buying behavior
reflects the operation of developmentally rooted unconscious pro-
cesses. This subset will be identifiable by their scores on the proposed
measures (developmental economic trauma, attachment insecurity,
institutional distrust, splitting) and will exhibit the specific patterns of
recurrence and disproportionality that the three mechanisms predict.
This claim is empirically tractable at the population level.
Structural equation modeling or multi-level modeling applied to con-
sumer panel data, combining purchasing behavior with the proposed
psychological measures, could test whether developmental economic
trauma and attachment insecurity predict panic buying severity over
and above Prospect theory variables (loss aversion, reference-point
shifts) and social contagion indicators. Survey-based experimental

The framework also has implications beyond the individual clini-
cal encounter. If a predictable subset of the consumer population is
prone to disproportionate panic buying due to psychodynamic vul-
nerability, then population-level interventions, such as governmental
communication strategies, can be calibrated to address not only ratio-
nal stockpiling but also the affective and relational dimensions that
drive the panic-buying subset. Governments communicating about
tariff impacts, for instance, should attend not only to the accuracy of
their supply projections but also to how the framing of institutional
reliability (countering projection) and supply adequacy (reducing
splitting) is received by consumers with high institutional distrust.
An integrated multiphase model for
tariff-induced panic buying
Table 1 presents the integrated five-phase model, incorporating
the three core psychodynamic mechanisms described above. Each
phase is linked to its dominant psychological mechanism, the specific
defense or process active at that phase, separate clinical and policy
interventions, and proposed empirical measures. As noted in the
Theoretical Background, this five-phase descriptive model maps the
phenomenological sequence of a panic buying episode and is comple-
mentary to, not a replacement for, Cooper and Gordon’s (1) six-phase
clinical treatment model.
The model highlights the interplay between macro-level policy
and micro-level psychodynamics. Critically, the Reinforcement phase
incorporates the policy feedback loop that is largely absent from clini-
cal models: panic buying artificially inflates demand and can create
real shortages where none initially existed, thereby validating consum-
ers’ initial fears and perpetuating the cycle (12). This feedback mecha-
nism implies that clinical intervention at the Behavioral phase, before
reinforcement is established, is more effective than intervention after
the cycle has consolidated, and that policy-level interruption of
demand spikes is both an economic and a mental health intervention.
Socioeconomic and cultural dimensions
Economic anxiety and risk aversion are heightened during peri-
ods of policy uncertainty, influencing both consumer behavior and
broader political attitudes (15). Crucially, socioeconomic status may
modulate both the objective exposure to tariff impacts and the psy-
chological vulnerability described in Mechanism 1. Individuals and
families with lower incomes face greater material exposure to tariff-
induced price increases and less financial resilience to absorb them,
amplifying the psychological urgency of stockpiling. At the same time,
the practical capacity to stockpile is itself a function of economic privi-
lege: buying in bulk, having storage space, and having access to trans-
portation and cash are resources differentially available to more
affluent consumers. Panic buying can therefore be paradoxically more
psychologically pressing yet more materially constrained for those
with fewer resources, a dynamic with implications for how popula-
tion-level interventions are designed and targeted.
Cultural context shapes the phenomenology of tariff-induced
panic buying in ways that modulate the three core mechanisms.
In collectivist cultural frameworks, the social contagion
dimension of the Amplification phase is likely intensified: the psy -
chological imperative to protect one’s household by conforming
to community behavior carries greater moral weight, and failure
to stockpile may generate social shame as well as personal anxiety.
In individualist frameworks, displacement dynamics may be
intensified, as the inability to single-handedly secure one’s family
against macroeconomic forces is experienced as a personal failure.
These cultural differences in phenomenology suggest that the
relative weight of the three mechanisms may vary cross-culturally,
a prediction that structured cultural comparison studies could
examine.
Political distrust and the anticipation of governmental inad -
equacy further fuel the projection and splitting mechanisms, as
consumers externalize economic insecurity onto institutional
actors perceived as unreliable ( 12). For populations with histories
of systemic oppression or disproportionate exposure to negative
governmental action, for whom institutional distrust is histori -
cally grounded rather than merely dispositional, tariff policies
may carry layered meanings that intensify threat activation inde -
pendent of developmental economic trauma. Clinicians should
remain attentive to this distinction: not all institutional distrust
reflects psychopathological projection; some reflects accurate his -
torical appraisal, and interventions should be calibrated
accordingly.
Clinical applications
The integrated model suggests phase-specific clinical approaches
that build on Cooper and Gordon’s (1) six-phase treatment frame-
work. During intake, genogram analysis can identify intergenerational
patterns of economic trauma and resource anxiety, operationalizing
the developmental vulnerability assessed in Phase 1. Psychoeducation
that normalizes the psychological response to economic uncertainty,
framing it as an understandable response to developmental history
rather than a pathological defect, can reduce shame and increase
engagement.
At the Trigger and Amplification phases, the identification of pro-
jection and splitting provides specific therapeutic targets. Reality test-
ing, examining the evidence for and against catastrophic appraisals of
tariff impacts, addresses projection. Mentalization techniques that
support clients in holding complexity and ambiguity, in their percep-
tions of governmental actors, foreign trading partners, and economic
uncertainty, address splitting. Both approaches are consistent with
Cooper and Gordon’s (1) emphasis on working through interpersonal
dynamics and correcting cognitive distortions.
At the Behavioral phase, the transitional object framing provides
a clinically generative entry point. Exploring what stockpiled goods
mean to the client, what they feel like they provide, what the client
imagines would happen without them, opens inquiry into the under-
lying attachment history and developmental deprivation that drives
the behavior. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), as pro-
posed by Cooper and Gordon (1), is particularly suited to displace-
ment: anchoring clients in present-moment experience interrupts the
future-oriented, anticipatory anxiety that drives anticipatory stockpil-
ing and helps clients distinguish current reality from past deprivation.

clinical history, by examining whether the client has engaged in

Discussion
This article has proposed and elaborated a psychodynamic
account of panic buying in response to tariffs that is explicitly
positioned as complementary to, not a replacement for, well-
established behavioral economic frameworks. The central argu -
ment is not that prospect theory, scarcity heuristics, and social
contagion are insufficient to explain panic buying in general, but
that they leave a clinically significant and empirically identifi -
able residual unexplained, a subset of consumers whose panic
buying is disproportionate, recurrent, and resistant to rational
reassurance, and that this residual is explicable through three
core psychodynamic mechanisms operating across five sequen -
tial phases.
The consolidation of the psychoanalytic literature into three
mechanisms, developmental vulnerability (regression and repeti -
tion compulsion), threat activation (projection and splitting), and
behavioral enactment (displacement and transitional object
dynamics), addresses the concern that the psychodynamic frame -
work proliferates constructs without sufficient differentiation.
Each mechanism operates at a distinct phase, generates distinct
predictions, implies distinct clinical interventions, and is opera -
tionalizable with distinct measurement strategies. Together they
form a coherent mechanistic account rather than a post-hoc inter -
pretive collection.
The temporal dimension of tariff-induced panic buying dis -
tinguishes it meaningfully from acute crisis buying and has
important clinical implications. Because tariff effects unfold grad -
ually and are announced in advance, there is a pre-event window,
between announcement and implementation, during which the
Trigger and Amplification phases are active but the Behavioral
phase has not yet fully consolidated. Clinical and policy interven -
tion during this window, targeting projection, splitting, and the
conditions for transitional object formation, may be more effec -
tive than post-hoc intervention after the reinforcement cycle has
been established.
It should be acknowledged that this article presents a hypoth -
esis-generating framework rather than an empirical validation.
The three mechanisms and the secondary hypotheses derived
from them have not yet been empirically tested in tariff contexts.
The proposed measurement strategies are offered as starting
points; psychometric development specifically for economic crisis
contexts is needed. Future research should examine consumer
panel data spanning tariff announcement periods, combining pur -
chasing behavior with the proposed psychological measures in
structural equation or multi-level designs. Longitudinal studies
tracking the same individuals across successive tariff-related
events would provide the strongest test of the recurrence predic -
tions derived from the repetition compulsion hypothesis.
Limitations
Several limitations of this framework should be acknowl -
edged. First, the psychodynamic mechanisms proposed have not
been empirically tested in the context of tariff-induced panic
buying; the model is hypothesis-generating and should not be
understood as empirically validated. Second, the proposed mea -
surement strategies require psychometric development for eco -
nomic crisis contexts, and the available instruments (ACEs, STAI,
PBS) were not designed specifically for this application. Third, the
framework is most applicable to cases in which panic buying is
clinically significant in severity and distress; many instances of
above-baseline purchasing following tariff announcements will
reflect rational stockpiling and require neither clinical attention
nor psychodynamic interpretation. Fourth, the psychodynamic
approach requires more time and therapeutic depth than brief
interventions permit, which may limit its applicability in resource-
constrained settings. Fifth, while this article argues for the impor -
tance of cultural and socioeconomic context in modulating the
three mechanisms, it does not provide culturally specific clinical
adaptations supported by outcome data, which remains an impor -
tant direction for future development. Sixth, a methodological
tension exists in the operationalization of putatively unconscious
processes through self-report instruments: by definition, mecha -
nisms such as regression, projection, and splitting operate below
the threshold of conscious awareness, and respondents cannot be
expected to report directly on their activation. The proposed mea -
sures — attachment style inventories, institutional trust scales,
splitting indices — capture constructs associated with these mech -
anisms rather than the mechanisms themselves, and their validity
as proxies for unconscious processes requires explicit psychomet -
ric justification in future empirical work. Behavioral and physio -
logical measures, as well as clinician-rated assessments in
therapeutic contexts, may provide more direct access to these
processes and should be incorporated into research designs along -
side self-report.
Conclusion
The psychological dimensions of panic buying in response to tar-
iffs represent an underexplored but practically significant area for
clinical and scholarly attention. This article has extended Cooper and
Gordon’s (1) integrated psychodynamic model by consolidating the
relevant psychoanalytic literature into three core mechanisms, devel-
opmental vulnerability, threat activation, and behavioral enactment,
that operate sequentially across five phenomenological phases and
generate distinct, falsifiable predictions distinguishable from those of
established behavioral economic frameworks.
The framework advances a bounded claim: not that psychody-
namic mechanisms account for all panic buying, but that they explain
a clinically significant subset characterized by disproportionate sever-
ity, resistance to rational reassurance, and cross-event recurrence.
Understanding this subset through a psychodynamic lens enables
clinicians to address the developmental roots of maladaptive con -
sumer behavior rather than its surface manifestations alone, and
enables policymakers to design communications that address not
