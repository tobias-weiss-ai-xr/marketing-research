Abstract
The challenge in sustainability is the green divide, the difference between consumers’
stated preferences in support of sustainability and their actual purchases. The green
divide may be reduced through AI -driven approaches that builds affirmative
associations with customer behaviors . The study surveyed 549 rural and urban
consumers in India, to determine the relationship between AI personalization and the
green divide. Survey responses were collected using offline and online methods, and
the survey data were analysed using factor ana lysis and PLS -SEM. Survey results
showed that AI personalization is associated with ethical consumerism, especially
with ethically trained AI. Value orientation was also significant, as rural consumers
from collectivist societies aligned more with an AI re sponse that sponsored a social
cause, while urban consumers from individualist societies aligned more with self -
serving perspectives. The results suggested that to achieve SDG 12, differentiated AI
solutions considering level of urbanization and individualism/collectivism of regions
of India are necessary. The researchers employed Nudge Theory and Algorithmic
Trust Theory to develop the Trust Bridge Hypothesis, predicting that trust is an
antecedent of AI customization and ethical consumption. This is a res ponse to
challenge of India’s green divide, especially for retailers and policymakers utilizing
delivery-oriented nudges.

Keywords: AI-driven personalization, Cultural values, Ethical consumer behavior, Nudge
Theory, Trust in AI.

Introduction
A global survey indicated that 73% of people expressed the intention to make ethical
purchases, however, only 13% followed through (White et al., 2019). This gap is
widening in India with the combination of rapid urbanisation and the multicultural
framework of the country. Within a current cultural frame, the narrowing of the gap is
likely to be associated with the use of AI personalization. However, such
personalization is a reliance on culturally bound trust and value systems. In this context,
it is likely that AI recommendations are the means to achieve ethical purchasing (Sipos,
2025). However, trust in AI is not universally established (Shin, 2021). Hence, the use
of AI to stimulate culturally based values to positive behavior means sustainable
purchasing is challenging.

The use of AI personalization to reduce the green divide in India has a distinct gap in
the literature. There is a lack of literature that extends the boundary of trust in AI and
ethical consumerism (Nguyen and Lee, 2022), especially in the algorithms’ bla ck box
(He and Jiang, 2022). While scholars recognise the importance of culture in people’s
responses to a nudge (Thavara and Yadav, 2025), there is little study of the trust -
behavior gap and the individualism-collectivism continuum. Finally, a poorly designed
feasibility of AI personalization is likely to be a contributing factor to the increasing
marginalisation of the culturally and geographically divided communities in India
(Gungunawat et al., 2024).

Rather than proposing a novel theory, this paper integrates and contextualizes Thaler et
al.’s (2009) Nudge Theory, Khamitov  et al.’s (2024) Algorithmic Trust Theory, and
Hofstede’s (2001) Cultural Dimensions Theory within a unified contingency
framework. Using a sample of 549 Indian consumers, we show that: (1) Trust is an
important mediation factor; the AI personalization phe nomenon and the (currently)
unquantifiable relationship between ethical consumption may continue to be
suboptimal until the barrier is removed. (2) It is an uneven scenario, and trust-building
may not yield the same outcome. The values of the different seg ments of the
sub­samples of consumers combined to show that Urban Individualists were found to
be more aligned with the trust-building outcome as long as self-interest was prioritized.
In contrast, trust -building was found more aligned with the outcome for Rural
Collectivists once the appeals were community oriented.

This study provides three key contributions. First, this study presents an AI trust
building framework with a culturally differentiated approach, applying varying degrees
of AI segment -specific disclosure to address the transparency trust paradox. Second,
the study offers a culturally differentiated pragmatism segmented trust framework for
retailers to maintain culturally relevant AI use to avoid potential greenwashing. Third,
the study proposes the integration of explainable AI, rural inclusivity, and the
sustainable consumption framework of the SDG 12 for India.

The design of the current research is cross -sectional, so all reported correlations are
associative and not causal. Influence, effect, and impact are considered to be gaps due
to the nature of the research design. Thus, this study concentrates on the associations of
(i) AI -driven personalization and sustainable decision -making, (ii) the sustainable
ethical-consumption gap and the trust gap of sustainable ethical consumption, and (iii)
the sustainable ethical-consumption gap and the sustainable behavior AI trust gap and

Literature Review
The objective of this literature review is to examine the intersection of artificial
intelligence, consumption, and culture to clarify the more abstract unanswered
questions. The study structures the literature to clearly define these questions and the
areas that this research will further examine. These domains are (1) the behavioral and
sociological aspects regarding the intention -action gap regarding sustainable
consumption, (2) the gap regarding the role of ICT and AI regarding the gap regarding
trust, and (3) the role of cultural values, especially regarding individualism and
collectivism and their reconstructions regarding cross -border AI, regarding the gap
regarding the trust that has been neglected, yet important. This paper primarily
addresses the gap regarding the cultural context  and the role AI personalization plays
regarding sustainable ethical consumption. Most prior studies have concentrated upon
the Western context focusing more specifically upon the rural and urban divide.

Critical Synthesis of Trust, Transparency, and Fairness Debates

There are three majors, yet disentangled inconsistencies found in literature. The first
focuses on the fact that Khamitov et al. (2024) suggest trust is a unitary construct, and
Burton et al. (2019) argue there is an inverted U -curve where personalization leads to
trust and an erosion of that trust. The study attempts to understand the way in which
various consumer cohorts from India perceive manifestation of incoherence where trust
is eroded. The second focuses on the trust and transparency paradox (He and Jiang,
2022); it is theorized that a fully functioning trust transparency framework may, in fact,
contribute to trust er osion and may, conversely, assist in reducing skepticism of
altruism among collectivists. The third focuses on the trust Ubal et al. (2024), and the
erosion of trust where price sensitiveness is in excess of 15 -18%. However, there has
been no literature focusing on whether these values are moderated by culture. The study
aims to provide a better understanding of the interactive effect and provide a content
synthesis for the work conducted in the past.

Fostering Trust and Ethics in AI Consumer Engagement

Trust is likely cultivated through ethical transparency where AI is not integrated.
However, the promise for transparency in terms of AI may likely lead to skepticism and
a decrease in trust in the AI systems. The majority of the research on the interaction of
consumers with AI make use of some variation of the technology acceptance model
(TAM), diffusion of innovation, and privacy calculus to frame their analysis (Ubal et
al., 2024). The use of AI recommendation systems in omnichannel commerce is likely
to positively relate to the desired outcomes of sustainable behavior (Karri et al., 2024).

Using an integrative review, the study establishes the constructs here. There exists a
common uncritical, mutual usage of trust, transparency, and fairness. Nudge theory
considers AI to be a personalized habit disruptor (Han, 2021). Trust is, and perhaps
always will be, the most paramount. Several studies reveal the mediatory function of
trust between personalization and ethical consumption (Sipos, 2025). The trust
construct, in many of these cases, is sketched in a highly rudimentary manner. Is
transparency a forerunner to trust, a facet of trust, an ethical obligation, that exists side
by side with trust? According to Hofstede’s dimensions, within a certain equilibrium,
collectivism likely supports fairness and transparency, while individualism likely
supports autonomy (Shin, 2021). This suggests that the most essential determinants of
trust may be contextually and culturally bound.

‘Trust’ as a Mediator is a Contingent and Fragile Construct

Sustainable behavior relating to trust in the system is aligned with recommendations of
the ‘fair trade’ type, which are value -consistent with the user, hence illustrating the
positive impact of recommendations on user trust in the system (Khamitov et al., 2024).
Burton et al. (2019) describe an inverted U -curve, whereby a certain level of
customization can create a trust threshold, while trust can decline when consumers
perceive the system as creepy. Furthermore, the lack of comprehensibility negat ively
relates to the perceived system’s usefulness (Ismatullaev  and Kim, 2022) along with
the provision of sustainable recommendations (He and Jiang, 2022), which can create
the paradox of transparency in sustainability. Sustained high trust and the offered
sustainable alternatives of high price (despite the recommend ation), being above 15 -
18%, can result in the recommended sustainable purchase losing positively perceived
AI recommendation trust (Ubal et al., 2024). In this sense, the trust in AI
recommendations and the rational economic problem are closely interwoven (Huang et
al., 2018).

Cultural Values as a Theoretical Moderating Variable

Culture defines the response to different technologies. Despite the acknowledgment of
cultural variability in AI nudges’ acceptability, a gap in theory exists in addressing how
culturally responsive AI nudges will affect the trust -behavior linkage, a core
psychology component (Thavara & Yadav, 2025). For instance, surrounded by Western
individualist cultures (e.g., the USA, the UK), driven by a focus on the individual, and
Eastern collectivist cultures (e.g., India and China), driven by concern for the
community, nudges make the trust-behavior linkage emphatically more pronounced for

Convergence, Synthesized Framework, and Research Openings

Previous synthesized frameworks that incorporate Nudge Theory and behavioral
models (Karri et al., 2025a) neglected the incorporation of culturally contingent
theories. Although the AI Ethics -Culture-Fit Model developed in this paper is a
contingency theory rather than a full grand theory, it aims to achieve the maximum
integrative potential of existing theories and set boundary conditions. AI
personalization (IV) is hypothesized to have a positive relationship with ethically
aligned behavior  (DV), mediated by trust (MV), and this relationship is culturally
contextualized (i.e., individualism -collectivism). This theory aims to fill the gap in
existing literature by identifying the less emphasized relations of AI, and to some
extent, the ethics, cultural questions of trust, as well as the moderating role of culture
in the context of the theory.

Theoretical Foundations

This paper develops an integrated contingency model, the primary contribution of
which is the contextualisation and integration of Nudge Theory (Thaler and Sunstein,
2009), Algorithmic Trust Theory (Khamitov et al., 2024), and Hofstede (2001) Cultural
Dimensions Theory, focused on the precise, contingent contextual settings of the
intersection of AI-driven personalization and the facilitation of ethical consumption.

Conceptual Framework and Variable Operationalisation

This framework consists of four key elements. The AI‑Driven Personalization
(Independent Variable, IV) is defined as consumers’ assessments of a company’s skill
at personalising product suggestions, taking into account individual preferences and
prior purchasing behaviors. The Trust in AI Mediating Variable (MV) is explained as
a psychological mediating phase along the continuum of fairness, reliance, and data -
privacy concerns. Here, transparency precedes the formation of trust. The Dependent
Variable (DV) Ethical Consumer  Behavior – indicates the purchasing activities of
sustainable goods and services and the self -disclosed purchasing of verifiable
sustainable goods and services.

As a Moderator (MOD), Cultural Values focuses on individualism and collectivism,
positing a somewhat individualistic orientation in urban, and somewhat collectivistic
orientation in rural, India (Kumar et al., 2023). While, Hofstede (2001) described
individualism and collectivism as opposites, later studies (Boer and Fischer, 2013)
support the coexistence of these constructs, noted especially as ‘cultural ambivalence’
in developing regions like India. The study provides a unidimensional
operationalisation ( from individualistic to collectivistic), based on three additional
factors: (a) confirmation of one-dimensionality through factor analysis (HTMT = 0.066
for AI Personalization‑Cultural Values) (b) India’s accelerated urbanisation resulting
in significant intra-individual value transformations, and (c) the requirement for a single
unidirectional moderator to retain statistical power within a parsimonious framework
for H4.

The model articulates a specific conditional pathway: AI personalization is linked to
behavioral change only in the context of trust, and the cultural values in question
determine the strength of the mediated relationship. This categorizes prior studies th at

Methodology
Research Design

This study proposes a model to analyse the relationships of AI -driven personalization,
trust in AI, cultural values, and ethical consumer behavior, employing cross -sectional
explanatory surveys. This method enables the analysis of the model’s predictive

Karri et al., 2026
Asian Journal of Business Research, Volume 16, Issue 3, 2026 149
relationships and fit. Nonetheless, the study has to acknowledge the major limitation of
the design, which is the lack of true causal inference.

Consequently, any relationship in the model is stated in associative terms (e.g. “is
correlated with,” “is associated with,” “predicts”) and should not be considered as proof
of genuine causation. To mitigate the effect of common method bias, the study
employed some of the statistical and procedural techniques such as employing different
types of scale, assuring anonymity of the respondents, and Harman’s single -factor test
(which indicated that no single factor explained more than 35% of the total variance,
undershooting the 50% limit).

Data Gathering

The primary data were gathered from questionnaires that were administered both
electronically (via e -mail and on social networks) and in person to obtain responses
from urban and rural participants from both Urban and Rural India. Each question was
designed to use a five -point Likert scale. A pilot study was conducted to test face
validity and reliability. In order to obtain appropriate statistical power, it was estimated
that a sample between 400 to 600 responses was adequate. With respect to an overall
sample of 633 responses, 84 were dropped from the sample because of extreme
responses and/or non-responses, resulting in an overall sample of 549 responses. The
final sample consisted of retail consumers (52% female, 48% male). The sample was
constructed in such a manner that individuals who possessed at least a modicum of
participation in the digital or retail economy.  The study was performed within the
ethical constructs of data collected, preserving the anonymity and confidentiality of the
participants, and all of the participants provided informed consent.

Analytical Procedures

The results from the three phases of data analysis are discussed. The first phase
comprised the use of SPSS for descriptive statistics and data screening. In the second
phase, the inner-outer model of PLS -SEM was evaluated in SmartPLS  after an initial
exploratory factor analysis (EFA) in SPSS, to assess the underlying factor structure.
This analysis was concerned with indicator reliability (item loadings), internal
consistency (Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability, CR), convergen t validity
(average variance extracted, AVE), and discriminant validity (using the heterotrait -
monotrait, HTMT, ratio). To address concerns regarding the constructs of trust,
transparency and fairness (formulated as three separate constructs), the study performed
author-developed post-hoc analyses. Specifically, a modified measurement model in
which ‘latent variable transparency’ was formed using the covariance of items TAI3
and TAI7, resulted in a poorer fit (SRMR = 0.089 compared to the original model
SRMR = 0.071; Δχ² = 34.2, p < 0.01). In the structural model, all items (hypotheses)
showed a variance inflation factor (VIF) less than 2.5, suggesting there was no problem
of multicollinearity. Since PLS-SEM is an efficient method to strike a balance between
predictive modeling and modeling complex and latent variables (Hair et al., 2017), it
was adopted to analyze the structural (inner) model. The path, mediation and
moderation hypotheses were also evaluated using PLS -SEM. The moderation
hypothesis (H4) was also tested using the interaction construct method. The model’s
significance was assessed through bootstrapping (5,000 subsamples) to estimate
standard error and t-statistics.

Discussion
The study was to explore AI personalization and its relationship with India’s green
divide, focusing on trust as a mediator and cultural values as a moderator. The
application of Nudge Theory, Algorithmic Trust Theory, and Cultural Dimensions
Theory is a w orking model, and thus, the contribution to the literature is to a
contextualised contingency framework, as opposed to a broad theory. All hypotheses
were statistically significant (p < 0.001; refer to Table 6) and indicated a range of
practical significance, as marked with f² (Table 5). The subsequent text differentiates
between practical significance and statistical significance.

First, the study discovered the role of trust being a cross cultural, mediating variable.
The study found that AI personalization had a positive influence on trust (H1: β = 0.372,
f² = 0.161, medium effect) and that trust played a significant role in the p rediction of
ethical consumption (H2: β = 0.396, f² = 0.244, medium -large effect). More
specifically, the study demonstrated the trust behavior relationship, and, particularly,
the significant and substantive moderating effect of culture on the trust behavior
relationship (H4: β = 0.371, f² = 0.194, medium effect). This study provides positive
connections, in a trust framed relationship, that were more positive for rural and
collectivist customers, and reciprocal and self -benefiting for urban and indiv idualistic
consumers. This study demonstrates the absence of AI nudges on the role of standard
trust theory cross culturally in relation to trust nudges, and provide the terminology of
‘cultural framing’ relating to the rational alignment on trust.

Secondly, with the integrated AI Ethics -Culture Fit Model, the green divide can be
reframed. Previous works model the gap as an intention-action dichotomy (White et al.,
2019). In contrast, and with the support of the mediated moderation findings, this model
reframed the gap as a ‘trust-culture puzzle.’ Ethical consumption cannot be reduced to
intention; it is the culturally adaptable trust, generated by AI, that broadens the
phenomenon of ethical consumption. The size of the interaction effect (f² = 0.194)
indicates that the culturally adaptive personalization is both statistically significant, and

Asian Journal of Business Research, Volume 16, Issue 3, 2026 156
practically significant.

Thirdly, it adds to the ethical AI literature as it demonstrates the multivariate and
distinct nature of trust in AI. It has been shown that the demand for transparency is one
of the phenomena that “triggers trust” in AI (He and Jiang, 2022), and this study shows
that transparency and these “trust” -demands have a cultural manifestation. The study
also acknowledge that algorithmic systems can build and maintain a negative trust
towards the marginalized (Bibri  et al., 2024), whilst price sensitivity and other
economically-based limitations can overcome even the most trusted AI nudges (Rai et
al., 2023).

The findings shift the question from whether AI can close the green divide to how it
can be designed with ethics to be multifunctional across disparate cultures. AI systems
must be transparent, trustworthy, and culturally adaptable in their interaction des ign.
This requires algorithms that flexibly adjust message framing based on the user’s
cultural background particularly important for pluralistic societies such as India.

Limitations

First, the model operates on a cross-sectional basis, meaning all described relationships
are correlational. This model serves as a baseline for longitudinal research that can
describe the time -related impacts of the algorithmic nudges toward sustainable
behavior.

Second, the model is spatially limited to India. While tracing the nuances of the model
within a single context is important, the model needs to gain traction in cultural contexts
that are more broad and spatially diverse to assess the model’s circumstantiality.

Third, using a unidimensional continuum to represent cultural values as
individualism/collectivism balances constraining the research framework and testing
parsimony and power trade -offs for moderational  studies, but it also lacks a more
precise approach to diversification. Considering power distance, uncertainty avoidance
and long term (Hofstede, 2001), India’s cultural diversity and complexity cannot be
understood via a single bipolar continuum. Thus, t he study argue that future studies
distinguish cultural mechanisms and should consider multi -item cultural frameworks
(e.g., Singelis et al., 1995) to unpack more diversified frameworks. Geotagged sampling
and tests for intraregional diversification are also warranted.

Fourth, the study would like to move beyond the paradigm of retail and consumption
to the areas of health, finance and public services, where Ai -led personalization has
significant potential for deeper ethical issues.

Fifth, concerns of the Global South on algorithmic bias and social injustices have
received little attention. Future research should consider how bias in AI systems could
deepen existing inequalities and reduce scarce trust for the most marginalised people.

Practical Implications for Asian Business
The results provide three key recommendations for Asian businesses that want to close

the green divide with AI -enabled personalization.  First, trust is the most important
factor (β = 0.396, f² = 0.244). Managers are encouraged to go beyond accepting trust as
an outcome and to develop trust purposefully. Special efforts need to be directed to the
“trust by design” concept with explainable AI (XAI) systems. XAI systems explain the
“who” and “the why” of the recommendations, enabling the transformation of the AI
from a black box to a trusted advisory partner. Regular checks of algorithms by As ian
companies and the removal of bias through data audits are recommended, as well as the
adoption of data sovereignty in their data protection efforts, methodologies and
practices. The systems will be less supportive of ethical personalization that is unwilling
to pay to avoid structural bias.

Second, cultural moderation is appreciated not as a casual effect but as an integral
design choice (interaction effect β = 0.371, f² = 0.194). Merchants and Traders in the e-
commerce and face -to-face marketplace need to be more considerate of the personal
characteristics and values of their plural, heterogeneous Indian customer base and not
implement a personalization of service strategy purely based on market and customer
behavioral activities. Since the value of personalization that is balanced by FBS
(Framing, Bias, and Salient effect) is significant, which is given by medium effect size
(f² = 0.194), it is more important to frame design personalization in the logic of value
systems, especially the personalization that is ethical in nature. This type of
personalization goes beyond value and purpose of matching product offers to the
personalization service recipients themselves and, ultimately, to the value systems.

Third, the digital and retail variability in Asia requires an omnichannel nudge
architecture. AI personalization ought to be integrated within the various channels
including social media as well as updated branded apps, in -store kiosks and touch -
points based on QR codes. A customer data platform (CDP) merges these channels to
provide consistent and culturally accessible nudges at every decision moment from
product discovery to checkout. Lastly, the trust deficit can of course not be resolved by
the acti ons of a single company. Asian business leaders should develop multi -
stakeholder coalitions with regulators, digital literacy NGOs and educators to jointly
develop SDG -aligned ethical AI frameworks. Educating consumers, in a respectful
manner, on how the d igital green economy and AI can empower sustainable healthy
choices, will be the best way to build trust. Ultimately, integrating transparency of
technology, cultural intelligence and collaborative governance will lead to success.
