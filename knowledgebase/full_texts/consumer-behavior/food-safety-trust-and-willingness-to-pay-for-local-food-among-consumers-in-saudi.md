Abstract
Food systems represent more than sources of nutrition; they embody cultural identity, heritage, and
sustainability. As global interest in locally produced food grows, the drivers of consumers’ willingness to pay
(WTP) a premium remain underexplored in emerging markets such as Saudi Arabia. This study investigates how
consumer conﬁdence, perceived proximity, and past purchasing behavior shape WTP for local food, with
particular attention to the moderating role of price transparency. Drawing on Social Identity Theory and the
Theory of Planned Behavior, a quantitative survey of 317 Saudi consumers was analyzed using structural
equation modeling. Results show that relational and process proximities signiﬁcantly enhance consumer
conﬁdence, which in turn increases WTP, while identity and access proximities exert weaker effects. Price
transparency and past purchasing behavior further strengthen these relationships. The ﬁndings extend the
literature on consumer behavior, food culture, and sustainability by highlighting how fostering consumer trust
and transparent practices can support ethical consumption, sustainable local food systems, and cultural
heritage within the framework of Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030.
1. Introduction
Food is much more than nutrition; it embodies sociocultural identities, territorial heritage, and community
belonging. It reﬂects historical traditions, contemporary lifestyles, and the interplay between production,
consumption, and sustainability (Fonte, 2010; Bessière, 2013). Local food systems contribute to cultural
preservation, agricultural and livestock biodiversity, and resilient food networks, while simultaneously supporting
ethical consumption and sustainable development. Globally, local food is valued not only for quality and
environmental beneﬁts but also as a marker of identity, heritage, and place-based authenticity (Everett &
Aitchison, 2008; Sims, 2009).
In Saudi Arabia, local food carries dual signiﬁcance: it preserves cultural and gastronomic heritage while
fostering sustainable agricultural practices and food security. Traditional products, such as dates, dairy, and
region-speciﬁc culinary practices, are intertwined with local customs and tourism experiences, supporting
regenerative tourism initiatives that position gastronomy as both a cultural and economic asset (AlHussaini et
al., 2023). Understanding consumer motivations to pay a premium for local products is therefore crucial for
reinforcing these systems and ensuring their sustainability.
Consumers’ willingness to pay (WTP) a premium for local food reﬂects economic, ethical, and cultural
motivations. Trust, perceived quality, community attachment, and perceived proximity to producers strongly
inﬂuence WTP (Zepeda & Deal, 2009; Thilmany & Watson, 2020; Aschemann-Witzel et al., 2021). Proximity
encompasses multiple dimensions: relational (social ties with producers), process (familiarity with production
methods), identity (cultural or regional aﬃliation), and access (ease of obtaining the product). Each dimension
shapes perceptions of authenticity, quality, and ethical engagement, ultimately inﬂuencing purchasing decisions.
Despite growing attention to local food systems, gaps remain in understanding how consumer conﬁdence
mediates proximity’s effects on WTP, and how past purchasing behavior and price transparency moderate these
dynamics, particularly in non-Western contexts such as Saudi Arabia (Liao et al., 2023). Investigating these
mechanisms is essential for developing policies and marketing strategies that support local producers while
fostering ethical consumption.

This study draws on two complementary perspectives. Social Identity Theory (SIT) (Tajfel & Turner, 1979) posits
that individuals’ identiﬁcation with social or cultural groups inﬂuences behaviors, attitudes, and decision-making.
Applied to food, SIT suggests that consumers support local food as an in-group choice, reinforcing cultural
identity and solidarity with producers.
The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1985) emphasizes attitudes, perceived control, and behavioral
intentions in shaping actions. In local food contexts, TPB helps explain how consumer conﬁdence mediates the
inﬂuence of perceived proximity on WTP. Consumers who feel informed, conﬁdent, and capable of accessing
local products are more likely to act on their preferences, translating into higher WTP.
Local food is a tangible expression of heritage, linking consumption to historical traditions and cultural
narratives (Bessière, 2013; Everett & Aitchison, 2008). In Saudi Arabia, traditional foods are increasingly
integrated into tourism, supporting regenerative tourism that promotes cultural preservation and local economic
development. Gastronomy tourism not only generates revenue for producers but also enhances awareness of
food origins, production practices, and sustainability, reinforcing ethical consumption patterns.
Examining relational, process, identity, and access proximities contributes to understanding how food systems,
consumer behavior, and cultural heritage interact to shape sustainable consumption. These dynamics have
implications for agricultural and livestock biodiversity, as consumer demand can incentivize the preservation of
traditional crops and breeds. This aligns with broader sustainability objectives and the UN Sustainable
Development Goals (SDG 2: Zero Hunger; SDG 3: Good Health and Well-Being; SDG 12: Responsible
Consumption and Production; SDG 17: Partnerships for the Goals).
Price transparency moderates the relationship between consumer conﬁdence and WTP. Transparent pricing
assures consumers of fairness, quality, and ethical practices, strengthening trust and encouraging premium
purchases. Past purchasing behavior also moderates this relationship; habitual buyers tend to show stronger
conﬁdence and WTP due to familiarity, trust, and positive experiences with local producers. These factors are
particularly signiﬁcant in markets like Saudi Arabia, where cultural traditions and community ties inﬂuence
consumption.
While proximity, trust, and consumer conﬁdence have been studied, few investigations integrate these factors in
the Saudi context. Prior research often examines Western markets or individual proximity elements, overlooking
combined effects of relational, process, identity, and access proximities. Additionally, moderating effects of price
transparency and past purchasing behavior remain underexplored, despite their importance in shaping trust and
ethical engagement.
This study addresses these gaps by examining: (1) How consumer conﬁdence mediates the relationship
between perceived proximity and WTP. (2) How past purchasing behavior moderates this relationship? (3) How
price transparency strengthens consumer conﬁdence and WTP.
By answering these questions, the study contributes theoretically to understanding consumer behavior and
locally embedded food systems, and practically by guiding policymakers, producers, and marketers. It
demonstrates how trust, transparency, and engagement with cultural and gastronomic heritage can enhance
sustainable consumption, cultural preservation, and regenerative tourism in Saudi Arabia.

Ultimately, local food is both an economic commodity and a vehicle for cultural preservation, biodiversity, ethical
consumption, and sustainable development. Understanding the interplay of consumer conﬁdence, proximity, and
transparency provides insights for designing policies and strategies that reinforce local food systems, support
livelihoods, and preserve gastronomic heritage for future generations.
2. Literature Review
2.1. Perceived Proximity and Local Food Consumption
Perceived proximity encompasses physical, emotional, and cultural nearness between consumers and products.
In Saudi Arabia, proximity is reinforced by national initiatives such as Vision 2030, which promotes economic
diversiﬁcation, self-suﬃciency, and support for local industries (Alrobaish, et al., 2021). Recent studies highlight
that Saudi consumers increasingly perceive supporting local products as an extension of their national identity
(Feldmann, & Hamm, 2015). This cultural and emotional attachment reﬂects the “country-of-origin effect,” where
consumers exhibit stronger trust and preference for products associated with their own nation due to shared
identity and pride (Ahmed et al., 2020).
The relationship between proximity and consumer conﬁdence is central in shaping purchasing intentions,
especially when moderated by consumer expertise and price transparency (Toukabri, 2021). Proximity not only
reﬂects geographical accessibility but also relational closeness, familiarity, and cultural resonance, all of which
reduce perceived risk and strengthen trust (Dubuisson-Quellier, 2009). Consumers with greater expertise are
better positioned to assess authenticity and quality, thereby enhancing their conﬁdence in local food choices (De
Magistris & Gracia, 2016). Empirical research conﬁrms that the positive impact of proximity on consumer
conﬁdence and purchase intention is ampliﬁed when both consumer expertise and transparent pricing are
present (Jin & Shin, 2020). In the Saudi context, where national pride and consumer expectations for clear
communication are reinforced under Vision 2030, both cultural proximity and price transparency are critical
levers for encouraging willingness to pay (WTP) premiums for local food (Feldmann, & Hamm, 2015).
2.1. Dimensions of Proximity
Proximity can be conceptualized as a multi-dimensional construct that captures the various ways consumers
relate to local products and producers. Drawing on Aaker’s (1991) framework, four complementary dimensions
of proximity are particularly relevant for understanding consumer behavior in local food markets: Relational
Proximity, this dimension reﬂects the emotional bonds and personal connections consumers develop with local
producers, brands, or vendors. Strong relational proximity fosters trust, loyalty, and a sense of belonging, which
in turn inﬂuences ethical consumption and willingness to pay a premium for local products (Fournier, 1998;
Feldmann & Hamm, 2015). Relational proximity emphasizes the importance of interpersonal relationships, direct
communication, and community engagement as mechanisms that strengthen consumer conﬁdence. The
process proximity captures the extent to which consumers are informed about production methods, including
safety standards, sustainability practices, and ethical considerations (Manning et al., 2016). When consumers
have visibility into the production process, they perceive greater transparency and control, which enhances trust
and the perceived value of local products. This dimension underscores the role of educational initiatives,
labeling, farm tours, and digital content in conveying production practices to consumers. The identity proximity
pertains to the alignment between local products and cultural, regional, or national identity. Products that reﬂect
local heritage, traditions, or national pride can evoke emotional resonance and reinforce consumer conﬁdence
Page 4/21

(Thompson & Arsel, 2004). Identity proximity emphasizes symbolic consumption, where purchasing local food is
not only a utilitarian choice but also a reﬂection of cultural values and social identity. However, as empirical
studies suggest, the strength of identity proximity may vary depending on the consumer’s personal identiﬁcation
with the local or national culture. The access proximity reﬂects the ease of obtaining local products, whether
through physical markets, retail stores, or digital platforms (Kumar et al., 2020). Greater accessibility empowers
consumers to make informed purchasing decisions, reinforces conﬁdence, and reduces barriers to engagement
with local food systems. Access proximity also includes factors such as geographic availability, distribution
channels, and e-commerce solutions, which are increasingly important in digitally mediated marketplaces.
Collectively, these four dimensions provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how consumers’
perceptions of closeness emotional, informational, symbolic, and practical shape their conﬁdence in local food
products and their willingness to pay a premium. Recognizing the multi-faceted nature of proximity allows
researchers and practitioners to design targeted interventions that enhance consumer engagement, trust, and
sustainable purchasing behaviors in local food markets.
Together, these four dimensions explain how local food systems build trust and conﬁdence among consumers,
thereby inﬂuencing WTP for local products.
2.1.1. Proximity of Access: Ease of Access to the Point of Sale
Access to local products via physical stores, farmers’ markets, or online platforms has signiﬁcantly shaped
consumer conﬁdence in Saudi Arabia (Toukabri & Ghali, 2020). With rapid urbanization and investments in
logistics, access to local food has expanded, particularly through e-commerce platforms promoted under Vision
2030 (Alshammari, 2020). Empirical evidence conﬁrms that easier access positively inﬂuences purchase
decisions and strengthens consumer trust (Innes & Hobbs, 2011).
Hypothesis H1. The ease of access to local products positively inﬂuences consumer conﬁdence in local
products.
2.1.2. Relational Proximity: Interaction with the Producer
Relational proximity emphasizes trust-based connections between producers and consumers (Toukabri, 2023).
Historically, traditional marketplaces (souks) in Saudi Arabia enabled strong relationships that built conﬁdence in
local goods (Al-Otaibi & Al-Rashid, 2022). Today, digital platforms and social media extend this relational
proximity, allowing producers to engage directly with consumers, enhancing transparency and authenticity
(Lenglet & Muller, 2016). Such interactions are particularly valued by younger generations who seek proof of
ethical and cultural authenticity.
Hypothesis H2. Strong relational proximity between producers and consumers positively inﬂuences consumer
conﬁdence in local products.
2.1.3. Proximity of Identity: Shared Values and Local Identity
Identity proximity refers to the alignment between local products and consumers’ cultural, religious, and national
values. In Saudi Arabia, Vision 2030 promotes local production as a symbol of national pride and cultural
continuity (Al-Tayyar, 2021). Purchasing local goods is also linked to Islamic values such as sustainability,
fairness, and community solidarity (Ahmed et al., 2020). According to self-congruity theory (Sirgy, 1982),
Page 5/21

consumers prefer products that resonate with their self-image and cultural identity, which in turn enhances
conﬁdence.
Hypothesis H3. The alignment of local products with cultural and national identity positively inﬂuences
consumer conﬁdence in local products.
2.1.4. Proximity of Process: Understanding the Production
Process
Process proximity highlights transparency regarding production practices. Rising concerns over food safety,
traceability, and sustainability have made Saudi consumers more attentive to production details (Alshammari,
2020). Knowledge of ethical sourcing, safety standards, and sustainable farming practices reinforces consumer
trust and strengthens their support for local producers (Herault-Fournier & Pouzenc, 2014).
Hypothesis H4. Greater transparency in the production process positively inﬂuences consumer conﬁdence in
local products.
2.2. The Role of Consumer Conﬁdence in Willingness to Pay
Consumer conﬁdence is a pivotal driver of WTP for local food. Dimensions of proximity access, relation, identity,
and process shape trust and perceived authenticity, which directly increase consumers’ readiness to pay a
premium (Toukabri et al., 2019, 2020, 2021). In Saudi Arabia, consumer conﬁdence is reinforced by policies that
promote local food security and authenticity under Vision 2030.
Studies conﬁrm that trust in local producers (relational proximity), ease of access (access proximity), and
cultural alignment (identity proximity) signiﬁcantly increase WTP (El-Feki & Abu-Zayed, 2023). Research also
shows that communication about product origin and production methods enhances transparency and thus
strengthens conﬁdence, leading to greater WTP (Alrobaish, et al., 2021).
Hypothesis H5. Consumer conﬁdence positively inﬂuences WTP a premium for local food.
2.3. Mediating Role of Consumer Conﬁdence
Consumer conﬁdence mediates the effect of proximity dimensions on WTP. Relational trust, process
transparency, cultural identity, and access shape consumers’ perceptions, which translate into higher WTP
through increased conﬁdence (Toukabri et al., 2019, 2020, 2021). Evidence suggests that stronger proximity
enhances emotional bonds, authenticity perceptions, and sustainability recognition, all of which build consumer
conﬁdence and drive WTP (Burgess et al., 2022; Brennan et al., 2022; Ginieis et al., 2023).
Conﬁdence also varies across contexts: for some consumers, identity proximity is most salient; for others,
process transparency dominates. Hence, understanding conﬁdence as a mediator offers actionable insights for
designing effective marketing and policy interventions (Sgroi et al., 2023).
Hypothesis H6. Consumer conﬁdence mediates the relationship between proximity dimensions (relation,
process, identity, and access) and WTP for local food.
2.4. Moderating Roles of Past Behavior and Price Transparency
Past behavior and price transparency further shape the proximity–conﬁdence–WTP pathway.

Past behavior strengthens the proximity–conﬁdence link. Consumers with positive prior experiences of
freshness, authenticity, and ethical practices develop trust, which encourages repeat purchases and willingness
to pay more (Johnson & Browne, 2023; Schmidt et al., 2022). Negative experiences, however, undermine
conﬁdence and reduce WTP.
Price transparency provides clarity and fairness in pricing, reinforcing conﬁdence in the value of local food.
Transparent pricing reduces uncertainty, validates quality claims, and strengthens consumers’ willingness to pay
(Wang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). The effect of transparency is especially strong when combined with positive
past behavior, as it validates prior decisions and enhances trust.
Hypothesis H7. Past behavior moderates the relationship between consumer conﬁdence and proximity.
Hypothesis H8. Price transparency moderates the relationship between consumer conﬁdence and WTP for local
food.
2.5. Conceptual Model and Research Hypotheses
Figure 1 illustrates the proposed conceptual model. Proximity dimensions (relation, process, identity, and
access) are expected to enhance consumer conﬁdence, which mediates their effect on WTP for local food. Past
behavior and price transparency are introduced as moderators that shape the strength of these relationships.
This model provides a framework for understanding consumer decision-making in Saudi Arabia’s local food
market, emphasizing the interplay between proximity, conﬁdence, and transparency. It also highlights pathways
through which marketers and policymakers can promote local food consumption, strengthen consumer–
producer ties, and encourage WTP premiums.
3. Methodology
3.1. Constructs’ Operationalization
To evaluate perceived proximity, this study employed the four-dimensional scale initially developed by Bergadaà
and Del Bucchia (2009) and subsequently validated by Hérault-Fournier et al. (2010). The functional dimension
was removed following empirical testing, given its overlap with utility value. The resulting framework captures
relational, process, identity, and access proximities, and has been applied in several studies on local and direct
sales outlets, demonstrating high reliability and construct validity (Dufeu & Ferrandi, 2013; Mazières & Gautier,
2015). This scale was deemed particularly appropriate for the Saudi local food context as it has been validated
in similar consumer settings.
Consumer conﬁdence was measured using a unidimensional six-item scale developed by Lacoeuilhe and Belaid
(2015) and adapted from Amine (1993). This scale has been widely applied across consumer behavior research
and is recognized for its internal consistency and robustness in capturing consumer trust and conﬁdence
(Delgado-Ballester, 2004).
Price transparency was measured using items adapted from prior research to assess consumers’ perceptions of
the availability and transparency of pricing information. This operationalization is consistent with the approach
adopted by Minbashrazgah et al. (2017), who examined price transparency as a factor inﬂuencing consumers’
Page 7/21

green purchase behavior. The inclusion of price transparency is also theoretically justiﬁed by the broader
literature on information transparency, which suggests that accessible and credible information can reduce
consumer uncertainty and facilitate more informed purchasing decisions. This scale aligns with the growing
emphasis on transparency as a determinant of consumer trust in sustainable and ethical consumption contexts
(Schniederjans et al., 2016).
Finally, willingness to pay (WTP) a premium for local food was operationalized with a two-item scale developed
by Shin et al. (2023), focusing on consumers’ readiness to pay more for locally produced options. Similar short
scales have been effectively used in consumer behavior research to capture intention toward ethical and
sustainable purchases (Lusk & Briggeman, 2009).
Table 1 summarizes the constructs, their dimensions, and measurement items. This combination of validated
and widely adopted scales ensures strong construct validity and reliability, providing a robust framework for
analyzing consumer conﬁdence, proximity, and their inﬂuence on WTP for local food.

3.2. Data Collection and Sampling
Data were collected in two phases to ensure robustness. A convenience sampling strategy was adopted to
reach Saudi consumers, leveraging online distribution through email invitations, social media platforms, and
professional networks. The use of an online survey was justiﬁed for two reasons: (1) it allowed eﬃcient access
to a geographically dispersed population across Saudi Arabia, thereby capturing diverse consumer proﬁles, and
(2) it aligned with the increasing digitalization of both food marketing and consumer purchasing behaviors
(Wright, 2005; Evans & Mathur, 2018).
In Phase 1 (Exploratory), 200 responses were collected to reﬁne the measurement scales, identify problematic
items, and conduct preliminary reliability and validity testing. In Phase 2 (Conﬁrmatory), a larger sample of 367
valid responses was obtained to validate the ﬁnal measurement model and test the proposed hypotheses
(Table 3). This two-stage approach is consistent with recommendations for measurement development and
model validation (Hair et al., 2019).

4. Results
4.1. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)
The analysis began with an Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) using SPSS 27.0 to explore the latent structure of
the measurement scales and assess their psychometric robustness. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of
sampling adequacy exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70, and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was
signiﬁcant at p < 0.001, conﬁrming that the data were suitable for factor analysis (Kaiser, 1974; Hair et al., 2019).
Eigenvalues greater than 1 and factor loadings above 0.60 conﬁrmed the appropriateness of item retention, with
Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values exceeding 0.50 across all constructs. These results suggest that the
indicators adequately represented their intended latent variables.
Reliability was conﬁrmed through Cronbach’s alpha and Jöreskog’s rho, with all constructs surpassing the 0.70
benchmark (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994; Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) further
validated the unidimensionality of constructs, with inertia values remaining within acceptable ranges. Together,
these results establish strong evidence of construct reliability and validity, offering a solid basis for conﬁrmatory
factor analysis.

4.2. Conﬁrmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)
Conﬁrmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS to validate the measurement model and assess
both convergent and discriminant validity. Convergent validity was supported, as all standardized factor loadings
exceeded 0.70, AVE values surpassed 0.50, and Composite Reliability (CR) values were above the 0.70 threshold
(Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hair et al., 2019). This indicates that items effectively converged on their respective
constructs.
Discriminant validity was evaluated by comparing the square roots of AVE values with inter-construct
correlations. In all cases, the square roots of AVE were higher than the corresponding correlations, supporting
the distinctiveness of each construct (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Henseler et al., 2015). These ﬁndings conﬁrm that
the measurement model was statistically sound, providing a robust framework for hypothesis testing (Table 4).

4.5. Mediation Analysis
The mediating role of consumer conﬁdence was tested using the causal steps approach proposed by Baron and

proximity and WTP (H6.3), and no mediation for relational proximity (H6.1).

4.7. Summary of Findings
Taken together, these results highlight that relational, process, and access proximities are key drivers of
consumer conﬁdence and willingness to pay for local food products, while identity proximity appears less
relevant in this context. Consumer conﬁdence partially mediates these relationships and is further strengthened
by past consumer experience. Importantly, price transparency signiﬁcantly enhances the effect of conﬁdence on
Page 14/21

WTP, suggesting that policies and marketing strategies aimed at fostering openness and clarity in pricing can
strengthen consumer trust and boost support for local food markets.
These ﬁndings contribute to the growing body of research on consumer behavior in local food systems by
demonstrating how proximity, conﬁdence, and transparency interact to shape purchasing intentions. They also
provide practical implications for local producers, policymakers, and marketers seeking to encourage consumer
engagement with local foods in Saudi Arabia and comparable contexts.
5. Discussion and Conclusions
The ﬁndings of this study underscore the pivotal role of consumer conﬁdence in shaping purchasing behaviors
related to local food, particularly through the dimensions of relational and process proximities. Consumers who
perceive a strong connection with local producers through interpersonal interactions, process familiarity, and
access to production methods are more inclined to pay a premium for locally sourced products. These results
align with prior research emphasizing the importance of trust and community attachment in promoting ethical
consumption (Feldmann & Hamm, 2015; McFadden, 2016). Notably, the non-signiﬁcant effects of identity and
access proximities suggest that proximity does not exert a uniform inﬂuence across all dimensions of
consumers’ willingness to pay (WTP) for local products. This ﬁnding is consistent with research indicating that
local food consumption is shaped by multiple, context-dependent values and motivations rather than by a single
determinant (Zhang et al., 2020). In the Saudi context, the results suggest that relational and process-based
proximities may be more inﬂuential in fostering trust and engagement, whereas symbolic identiﬁcation with local
producers and perceived accessibility may play a comparatively limited role in determining consumers’ WTP for
local products.
A key contribution of this research lies in demonstrating the moderating role of price transparency in the
relationship between consumer conﬁdence and WTP. The results show that when consumers perceive pricing as
fair, transparent, and easily accessible, their conﬁdence in local food products strengthens, ultimately enhancing
their readiness to pay a premium. This ﬁnding is consistent with recent studies emphasizing transparent pricing
as a trust-building mechanism in sustainable and ethical food markets (Sweeney et al., 2022; Molnár et al.,
2023). By elucidating the interplay between conﬁdence and perceived pricing clarity, this study extends the
literature on local food systems, highlighting an actionable mechanism through which producers and
policymakers can enhance ethical consumption behaviors. Moreover, these insights are particularly relevant to
Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 initiatives, which aim to bolster domestic agricultural products and strengthen local
food systems (Alrobaish, et al., 2021).
The practical implications of these ﬁndings are substantial. For policymakers, strategies that enhance consumer
conﬁdence through community engagement, transparency, and trust-building can signiﬁcantly increase
participation in local food markets. Public campaigns that emphasize the nutritional, economic, and
environmental beneﬁts of local food systems may further stimulate consumer interest and ethical consumption
(Rieﬂer, 2020). For local producers, cultivating strong consumer relationships through effective communication,
storytelling, and trust-building practices is essential. This aligns with ﬁndings from Dubé et al. (2022), who
underscore that consumer trust is a direct predictor of engagement with local food systems. In practice, this
could include initiatives such as farm visits, interactive online content, and consistent messaging about
production practices and local impact.
Page 15/21

This study also opens avenues for future research. Investigating additional psychological and behavioral factors
such as perceived value, risk perception, social inﬂuence, and ethical beliefs could provide a more
comprehensive understanding of local food purchasing behaviors. Longitudinal studies would be particularly
valuable in tracking the evolution of consumer attitudes over time, especially in response to changing market
dynamics, cultural perceptions, or economic conditions (Lal et al., 2023). Expanding the research to different
cultural contexts, particularly in developing nations with distinctive challenges in local food systems, could yield
comparative insights and enhance generalizability.
Moreover, the role of digital platforms and social media in shaping consumer conﬁdence and WTP warrants
further attention. With the increasing digitization of food marketing, online engagement can foster trust, provide
transparent information, and facilitate stronger connections between producers and consumers (Verhoef et al.,
2021). Recent studies highlight that digital storytelling and interactive content can signiﬁcantly enhance
consumer engagement and support for local food initiatives (Bresciani & Eppler, 2022).
In conclusion, this study advances our understanding of the determinants of WTP for local food by highlighting
the critical roles of consumer conﬁdence, relational and process proximities, price transparency, and past
consumer behavior. By addressing existing gaps in the literature and providing actionable insights for both
policymakers and local producers, the research contributes to both theory and practice. Ultimately, fostering
conﬁdence, trust, and transparency can strengthen sustainable and resilient local food systems, supporting the
needs of both consumers and producers while promoting ethical consumption patterns in the Saudi context and
beyond.
