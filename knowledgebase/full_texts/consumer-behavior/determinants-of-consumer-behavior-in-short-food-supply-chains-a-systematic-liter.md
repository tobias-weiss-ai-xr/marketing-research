Abstract
Short food supply chains (SFSCs) are increasingly regarded as promising alterna-
tives to industrialized food distribution systems. They aim to create geographical,
logistical, and social proximity between food producers and consumers. Despite
extensive research on SFSCs in recent years, our understanding of how they can best
be promoted is hindered by the lack of a unified approach in extant studies of con-
sumer perspectives on SFSCs. The aim of this systematic literature review is thus
to provide a comprehensive overview of the key determinants shaping consumer
behavior around SFSCs. Following PRISMA guidelines, 30 peer-reviewed articles
based on empirical research of SFSC consumers in the European Union were selected
for analysis, applying Alphabet Theory as a theoretical framework. The findings empha-
size the influence on consumer behavior of contextual factors, information availability,
and trust, as well as the relevance of other commonly studied factors such as attitudes
and sociodemographics. The review further highlights the importance of understand-
ing how these factors interact to shape consumers’ perceptions of the costs and ben-
efits of SFSCs. Based on these findings, the study gives recommendations to address
the challenges identified and suggests directions for future research.

Marketing, Short food supply chains, Trust
Introduction
The expansion of complex global food supply chains in recent decades has facilitated
the availability of an unprecedented abundance of diverse foods at low prices for con -
sumers in most of the world. While optimizing efficiency and multiplying trade volumes
through enhanced logistics, however, the prevailing industrialized and globalized sys -
tem of food production and distribution has negative consequences for the environment
(FAO 2023) and health (Willett et  al. 2019). The adverse effects of global food supply
chains also exacerbate social inequalities and rural poverty (FAO 2021), as well as dis -
rupting local food traditions (UNEP 2021). In light of these growing challenges, short
food supply chains (SFSCs) are increasingly promoted as an alternative approach to food
distribution.

Proponents of SFSCs argue that reducing intermediaries and thus creating closer
proximity between food producers and consumers can facilitate greater transparency
and foster local economic growth (Hüttl-Maack et al. 2021; Marsden et al. 2000; Petru -
zzelli et al. 2023). It is further argued that SFSCs can serve as “a means to restructure
food chains in order to support sustainable and healthy farming methods, generate resil-
ient farm-based livelihoods (in rural, peri-urban and urban areas) and re-localize control
of food economies” (Kneafsey et  al. 2015, p. 5). These features might be beneficial for
society, culture, and local economy and have generated growing interest in SFSCs across
many member-states of the European Union (Augère-Garnier 2016).
Increasing interest in harnessing the potential of SFSCs is reflected in a number of
adjustments made to EU legislation over the past decade. Already in 2013, the European
Parliament introduced Regulation No. 1305/2013 to support SFSCs, officially defining
SFSCs as “involving a limited number of economic operators, committed to co-opera -
tion, local economic development, and close geographical and social relations between
food producers, processors and consumers” (European Parliament 2013 Article 2 1(m)
p. 499). With this growing recognition of the relevance of SFSCs, there has emerged a
growing body of research investigating multiple aspects of their development (Chiffo -
leau and Dourian 2020; Evola et al. 2022; Paciarotti and Torregiani 2021; Petruzzelli et al.
2023).
Extant research explains how SFSCs create proximity between consumers and pro -
ducers at three levels: logistical, i.e., minimizing the number of intermediaries (Augère-
Garnier 2016; Bayir et al. 2022; Benos et al. 2022; Galli and Brunori 2013; Paciarotti and
Torregiani 2021); social, i.e., enabling information exchange and transparency between
producers and consumers between production and consumption (Galli and Brunori
2013; Marsden et al. 2000; Venn et al. 2006); and geographical, i.e., minimizing physi -
cal distance in the supply chain (Chiffoleau and Dourian 2020; Petruzzelli et al. 2023).
Regarding geographical proximity, some studies of SFSCs have extended the scope of
research to include inter-regional and international channels of distribution where inter-
mediaries are absent and direct communication is maintained, e.g., in the case of direct
online marketing (Bayir et al. 2022; Marsden et al. 2000).
SFSCs encompass a wide range of sales channels, including farmers’ markets, vege -
table-box schemes, community-supported agriculture (CSA), consumer co-operatives,
and solidarity purchase groups (SPGs). The products most frequently purchased through
these channels include fruit and vegetables, dairy products, honey, and eggs, while meat,
pasta, processed foods, and specialty products like wine are sold less (Cicatiello 2020;
Tiganis et al. 2023). Besides offering consumers access to fresh, local, and often organic
food, a core common characteristic of these diverse forms of SFSCs is that information
about production is much more readily available to consumers than conventional sales
channels. Also, the elimination of intermediaries and the higher amount of control along
the value chain enable farmers to obtain higher prices for their products (Bayir et  al.
2022). In addition, SFSCs typically share the aims of supporting more sustainable pro -
duction, enhancing food security, fostering rural development, and often also the wider
aim of promoting social justice. Yet, the effectiveness and impact of these attributes
depends on many factors, such as the actual implementation of environmentally friendly
production methods or consumer participation.

Notwithstanding these common characteristics and typically shared aims and values,
SFSCs vary significantly in terms of producer–consumer relationships, the economic
agreements made among participants, the degree of commitment involved or required,
and in the extent of success in achieving these aims (Kneafsey et al. 2015). Reflecting this
variation, classifications of SFSC channels in the literature differentiate channels accord-
ing to whether the research focus is on geographical proximity (Marsden et  al. 2000),
logistics (Renting et al. 2003), the types of producer–consumer relationships, e.g., part -
nerships, direct relationships, and indirect relationships (Venn et al. 2006), or the level of
commitment of consumers and producers (Galli and Brunori 2013).
Increasing scholarly interest in the potential benefits of SFSCs has not only inspired
plenty of studies in recent years but also numerous reviews of the extant literature aimed
at providing overviews of SFSC-related topics. (A list of relevant reviews is provided in
Table 3 in “ Appendix” .) However, most research has focused primarily on the sustain -
ability aspects of SFSCs and/or on the perspective of producers, while studies exploring
consumers’ perspectives remain limited (Chiffoleau and Dourian 2020; Evola et al. 2022;
Gonzalez-Azcárate et al. 2021; Jia et al. 2024; Venn et al. 2006). This is a significant gap
in research since the viability of SFSCs depends on consumer participation, making it
essential to identify the factors most likely to determine higher levels of acceptance and
adaptation. Without a stronger understanding of consumer preferences and motivations,
as well as the main barriers they face (or perceive) to purchasing via SFSCs, the possi -
bility of supporting and expanding these alternative distribution and sales channels will
remain limited.
Despite growing consumer awareness of problems associated with the conventional
agri-food system, SFSCs remain a niche phenomenon. Extant research on consumer
behavior and barriers to consumption via SFSCs indicates that buying through these
channels is widely perceived as rather inconvenient, above all due to accessibility, prod -
uct range, and price (Chang et al. 2022). The study results on consumer psychographics
are somewhat inconsistent, likely due to a lack of contextualization of the case studies
(Venn et al. 2006). This may explain why some studies identify price and convenience as
major barriers for consumers, while others highlight environmental concerns can out -
weigh them. Although these findings are not necessarily contradictory, they are often
not framed within a situational context or a broader framework that acknowledges the
multitude of behavioral influences. This heterogeneity of research outcomes has recently
been highlighted in a review by Csordás et  al. (2022) of studies connecting consumer
characteristics and attitudes in SFSCs. Their findings thus underscore the need for a
systematic review that applies a unified theoretical framework appropriate for attain -
ing a better understanding of consumer behavior. To address this gap, the aim of this
study is to identify determinants of consumer behavior in the context of SFSCs from a
multilevel perspective. This will be done through a systematic literature review, draw -
ing on Alphabet Theory (2009) as a theoretical framework to provide a comprehensive
and structured approach to extant research and knowledge. Unlike previous studies that
often focus on fragmented aspects of consumer behavior, this review offers a systemic
and theory-driven analysis that integrates sociodemographics, psychographics, behav -
ioral, and contextual factors to answer the central research question of Which factors
determine consumer behavior in the context of SFSCs?

the Alphabet Theory developed by Zepeda and Deal (2009). This is followed by a section

(A list of all the studies reviewed in this paper is provided in Table 4 in “ Appendix” .)
The final sections discuss the study results, acknowledge limitations, and offer possible
directions for future research before summarizing the key conclusions.
Theoretical framework
This study adopts a systemic approach to analyze extant empirical research on SFSCs
and specifically to identify factors influencing consumer choices for SFSC purchases.
Consumer research in general encompasses a wide spectrum of studies aimed at under -
standing how and why individuals behave in certain ways to fulfill their needs and
desires (Solomon 2019). Food choices are shaped by social, environmental, and cogni -
tive factors, with attitudes often highlighted as key drivers of behavior (Vermeir et  al.
2020). However, no consensus exists on how consumer attitudes are formed and how
they translate into behavior. Instead, various explanatory frameworks have been devel -
oped, with predominant models including the Theory of Planned Behavior (Ajzen 1991),
Norm Activation Theory (Schwartz 1977), and Value Theory (Schwartz 1994). While
these theoretical models offer valuable insights, all have certain limitations. In particular,
extant frameworks struggle to explain the intention-behavior gap and fail to address the
role of cultural and contextual dimensions (Lamarque et al. 2023).
Alphabet Theory (Zepeda and Deal 2009) was selected as a theoretical framework for
this systematic review on account of its inclusion of multiple different components rel -
evant to food choices and its capacity to illustrate the interrelationships between behav -
ioral determinants. This approach is particularly appropriate for a literature review
because it integrates multiple dimensions of behavior, highlighting the complexity of
interconnections among psychographic, social and contextual influences, which are
often studied separately. By adopting a multilevel approach, it facilitates a systemic and
structured examination to consumer behavior. Previous studies have made effective use
of Alphabet Theory to organize findings on consumer behavior and to identify gaps in
research (Feldmann and Hamm 2015; Hartmann et al. 2021; Li and Jaharuddin 2020).
Alphabet Theory combines several popular models previously developed to explain
attitude formation and how attitudes translate into behavior. To explain attitude forma -
tion, Alphabet Theory draws on Value-Belief-Norm (VBN) theory (Stern et  al. 1999),
which in turn combines Value Theory (Schwartz 1994), the New Ecological Paradigm
(Dunlap et al. 2000), and Norm-Activation Theory (Schwartz 1977). As shown in Fig.  1,
Zepeda and Deal (2009) extend VBN theory by incorporating sociodemographic aspects,
information-seeking, and knowledge as additional components influencing attitudes.
According to Alphabet Theory, sociodemographic aspects influence values, beliefs and
norms, which in turn shape attitudes and ultimately behavior. Information-seeking, as
an active process driven by VBN, enhances knowledge, which again reinforces attitudes.

To explain how attitudes interact with behavior, Alphabet Theory incorporates the
Attitude-Behavior-Context model (Guagnano et al. 1995). This model explains the inter-
action between attitudes, context and behavior. Contextual factors, such as price or
product availability influence attitudes and can act as purchase barriers. Context thus
significantly influences behavior by acting as a mediator between attitudes and behavior.
Habits are also included as determinants of behavior, comprising routines such as food
purchase patterns, cooking practices and dietary habits.
Methodological approach
As shown in Fig. 2, this systematic literature review followed the PRISMA guidelines for
selecting relevant studies (Liberati et al. 2009). Thus, after formulating the study objec -
tives, a systematic literature search was conducted in the online databases of Web of
Science and ScienceDirect, with results restricted to peer-reviewed scientific articles
published in English up to and including 2024. The final search term was “SFSC” OR
“Short Food Supply Chain*” OR “Short Supply Chain*” AND “consumer*” . The search
was confined to titles, abstracts, and keywords, since most articles on SFSCs mention
“consumer” in their body texts even when they do not include a focus on consumer per -
spectives. The final literature search was conducted on 25 September 2024. The deci -
sion not to include specific distribution channels namely in the search terms was made
to ensure conceptual consistency and to avoid overrepresentation of well-researched
models. By focusing on studies that conceptualize their cases within SFSCs, the review
remains theoretically grounded in the overarching concept. Studies on individual chan -
nels often emphasize their unique features and specific research questions that may not
be representative for the overarching concept of SFSCs.
The following eligibility criteria were set to ensure a standardized and comprehen -
sive selection of studies and to minimize bias: (1) studies presenting empirical evi -
dence from qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-method research; (2) studies focused
on SFSCs in general or at least one type of SFSC distribution channel; and (3) stud -
ies adopting a consumer research approach, offering insights into sociodemograph -
ics, psychographics (such as attitudes, values, and motivations), behavior, and/or

The selection was further confined to studies related to SFSCs in the EU. The reason

as SFSCs is an approach that has been especially brought into focus in academia and

SFSCs. Finally, studies focusing on a single product were excluded to ensure that the
analysis remained centered on SFSCs as a concept and the related consumer behav -

specific aspects could possibly deviate the intended focus of this study.

Overview of selected studies
The results of this review confirm that consumer behavior in the context of SFSCs is a
recent topic. All 30 articles selected were published after 2015, with 24 published since
2019, highlighting the growing relevance of this topic over the past five years.
As shown in Table  2, 20 studies used quantitative methods, eight used qualitative
methods (8), and two applied mixed-methods. Regarding geographic distribution, Italy
(8) and Spain (7) were the countries most represented in the studies, followed by Ger -
many (4), and Hungary (3). Only four studies offer cross-country comparisons within
the EU. Over one-third of the articles (12) studied SFSCs in general, five focused on mul-
tiple SFSCs, while others were case studies of specific channels, with farmer’s markets
being the most popular (7).
Most studies involved SFSC consumers. Four included both participants and non-par -
ticipants/potential participants (Bakos and Khademi-Vidra 2019; Cicia et al. 2021; Gon -
zalez-Azcárate et al. 2021; Lombardi et al. 2015), while one included former participants
of SFSCs (Delibas 2021).
Determinants of consumer behavior
Sociodemographic factors
Sociodemographic factors are very frequently assessed in consumer studies, though not
always as their main focus. Common indicators are education, income, gender, and age.
Whereas Alphabet Theory suggests a link between sociodemographic components and
attitudes rather than directly with behavior, this connection is often overlooked in the
literature, with many studies instead emphasizing the direct impact of these variables on
SFSC consumption behavior. The findings of the reviewed studies are heterogeneous due
to variations in study settings and contexts and the diverse realities of SFSC channels on
the ground. As elaborated below, however, several tendencies are evident in SFSC con -
sumer profiles.
Most studies indicate that SFSC consumers tend to have a higher level of education
and higher incomes than the general population (e.g., Bakos and Khademi-Vidra 2019;
Baldi et  al. 2019; Escobar-Lopez et  al. 2021; Migliore et  al. 2015). Income is found to
influence behavior (Benos et al. 2022; Migliore et al. 2015) and attitudes, with some stud-
ies highlighting its role in shaping perceptions of food affordability and price (Menéndez
i Molist et al. 2024).

Most studies find that more women than men purchase food from SFSCs and thus
conclude that gender has an impact on SFSC shopping behavior (e.g., Giampietri et al.
2016; Peña Rodríguez et al. 2024) due to women having more positive attitudes toward
the environment (e.g., Gonzalez-Azcarate et  al. 2021). Neumann and Mehlkop (2023)
contextualize this finding, suggesting that traditional gender norms related to food shop-
ping routines may play a role.
Findings regarding age vary according to different SFSC channels. Studies of farm -
ers’ markets identify people aged 30–50 as the most likely consumers (Cicia et al. 2021;
Gonzalez-Azcarate et al. 2021; Stanco et al. 2019), while studies of Solidarity purchase
groups find those aged 40–60 are most prevalent among SFSC consumers (Baldi et al.
2019; Lombardi et al. 2015). Studies of SFSCs in general report heterogenous findings
regarding age (e.g., Escobar-Lopez et al. 2021; Tiganis et al. 2023), with some acknowl -
edging the bias of online studies that usually exclude elderly consumers (Peña Rodríguez
et al. 2024).
Household situation is another relevant factor, with most SFSC consumers found to
be members of medium-to-large families or households with children (e.g., Baldi et al.
2019; Hvitsand and Leikvoll 2023), though shoppers at farmers’ markets tend to live in
smaller households without children (Cicia et al. 2021; Stanco et al. 2019). Otherwise,
the presence of children in particular is found to impact attitudes toward health (Delibas
2021).
Regarding area of residence, rural residency correlates positively with SFSC purchases
(Giampietri et al. 2016). Consumers in rural areas are more aware of offline SFSC chan -
nels, while consumers in larger cities are more likely to use online channels (Aguado-
Gragera et al. 2024). Residents of urban and industrialized areas do not associate their
territory with quality agriculture, negatively impacting their attitudes to local products
(Baldi et al. 2019).
Attitudes
In Alphabet Theory, the category of attitudes incorporates VBN theory, thus emphasiz -
ing how values, beliefs, and norms shape the formation of attitudes and drive consumer
motivations. Since the reviewed studies vary in theoretical frameworks, their use of ter -
minology when referring to psychographic factors is inconsistent, complicating compar-
ative analysis. Accordingly, this review de-emphasizes conceptual terminology, instead
organizing study results by inductive subcategories. Key topics associated with attitudes
and motivations include food quality, environment/sustainability, and social responsibil-
ity. Additional themes such as organic food, local food, direct contact with producers,
community, tradition, and animal welfare also emerge as significant motivators.
Some studies cluster or group consumers according to motivations, distinguishing
between people with egoistic, hedonic, and/or instrumentalist motives (health, taste,
etc.) and those with altruistic or community-oriented reasons (local economy, environ -
ment, social reasons, etc.). Overall it is found that most consumers choose SFSCs for
instrumentalist or egoistic reasons (Baldi et al. 2019; Oñederra-Aramendi et al. 2018).
Food quality The most frequently cited motivation for consumer participation in
SFSCs is food quality (e.g., Cruz et al. 2021; Delibas 2021; Hyland et al. 2024). Consum -

ers commonly associate food quality with attributes such as freshness, taste, healthi -
ness, and food safety (e.g., Bakos and Khademi-Vidra 2019; Gonzalez-Azcárate et al.
2021; Lombardi et al. 2015; Tiganis et al. 2023). In this regard, health consciousness is
debated as a key factor (Benos et al. 2022; Neumann and Mehlkop 2023). Negative per -
ceptions of the quality of food produced by the conventional food industry, as well as
of this industry per se, are found to further reinforce positive attitudes toward SFSCs
(e.g., Cicia et al. 2021; Cruz et al. 2021; Zoll et al. 2021).
In SFSCs, greater transparency and readily available knowledge about food origins
provide an alternative or complement to traditional control mechanisms like qual -
ity labels and institutional oversight (Migliore et al. 2015), offering consumers addi -
tional assurance of quality (e.g., Neulinger et al. 2020; Vittersø et al. 2019; Zoll et al.
2021). Some studies report that consumers claim to be able to discern quality directly
through taste (Delibas 2021; Neulinger et al. 2020).
However, food quality can also act as a barrier to SFSC participation. Indeed some
studies identify it as the second most significant obstacle (Gonzalez-Azcárate et  al.
2021). Beliefs or assumptions about inferior food quality in SFSCs usually revolve
around differences compared to standardized supermarket food such as the size of
products and their shelf life (Neulinger et al. 2020).
Environment/sustainability Many studies highlight the influence of consumer atti -
tudes toward the environment (or sustainability) on SFSC purchase behavior (e.g.,
Cicia et  al. 2021; Giampietri et  al. 2016; Stanco et  al. 2019). In the context of pro-
environmental attitudes, some studies highlight the importance of the perceived envi -
ronmental benefits of seasonal and local food (e.g., Baldi et al. 2019; Menéndez i Molist
et al. 2024), the benefits for biodiversity (Vittersø et al. 2019), and the rejection of pes -
ticide use (Baldi et al. 2019) and/or monoculture (Zoll et al. 2021). While SFSCs have
the potential to address certain environmental concerns, their overall environmental
sustainability remains an ongoing debate in the literature. Still, the consumers percep -
tion of SFSCs as more environmentally friendly has a strong impact on their behavior
and is even identified as the primary motivation to participate in SFSCs in some stud -
ies (e.g., Benos et al. 2022).
Social responsibility In the same way, consumers associate SFSCs with social respon -
sibility and attitudes related to social responsibility are frequently mentioned in the
studies reviewed. Positive attitudes toward ethical consumption correlate with higher
SFSC purchases (Bernardi et al. 2021; Giampietri et al. 2018). Key motivations related
to social responsibility are fairness, support for local farmers and businesses (includ -
ing fair remuneration and labor rights), community, development, and the local econ -
omy more generally (e.g., Bakos and Khademi-Vidra 2019; Hvitsand and Leikvoll 2023;
Peña Rodríguez et al. 2024). Interestingly, some consumers even perceive consumption
constraints such as the seasonally limited availability of products as positive, taking
pride in adapting to these limitations (Zoll et al. 2021).

toward organic food on SFSC purchase behavior (e.g., Hvitsand and Leikvoll 2023;

(Stanco et al. 2019), and local food (e.g., Neumann and Mehlkop 2023; Vittersø et al.
2019). Many studies relate local production to sustainability (see above) or freshness.
Direct contact with producers is another important driver for SFSC consumers.
By creating an engaging shopping experience, including through visiting production
sites and tastings (e.g., Gonzalez-Azcárate et al. 2021), such contact generates loyalty
(Giampietri et  al. 2016), thereby positively impacting purchase behavior (Aguado-
Gragera et al. 2024; Vittersø et al. 2019). Participating in SFSCs also fulfills a desire for
community and connection with like-minded individuals (Schmutz et  al. 2017; Vit -
tersø et al. 2019). This applies especially to more community-oriented channels like
CSA, food co-ops, and community gardens (Neulinger et al. 2020; Zoll et al. 2021).
Other influential attitudes include positive attitudes toward tradition (e.g., the con -
servation of traditional landscapes, old varieties, or cooking with fresh ingredients)
(Cicia et al. 2021; Giampietri et al. 2018; Lombardi et al. 2015), as well as concerns for
animal welfare (Hvitsand and Leikvoll 2023; Vittersø et al. 2019).
Interaction between attitudes and behavior Many studies confirm that values, atti -
tudes and motivations influence SFSC consumption behavior (e.g., Baldi et al. 2019;
Lombardi et al. 2015). However, frameworks like the Theory of Planned Behavior high -
light how attitudes shape intentions but do not necessarily result in actions (Giampie -
tri et al. 2016, 2018; Neumann and Mehlkop 2023). By contrast, some studies point out
the direct interaction between attitudes and behavior. For instance, pro-environmental
attitudes as primary purchase motivations often lead to more frequent SFSC purchases
(Cicatiello 2020; Cicia et al. 2021).
Other research applies frameworks like Schwartz’s Value Theory to examine how
values influence behavior (Cicia et al. 2021; Lombardi et al. 2015). Studies find that
the value of self-transcendence and the feeling of belonging to a community enhance
pro-environmental attitudes (Bernardi et al. 2021; Cicia et al. 2021). Benevolence and
universalism are thus found to be greater among SFSC participants than non-partici -
pants, while achievement and power are less important (Lombardi et al. 2015). Stud -
ies also point out that there is a noticeable influence on behavior of other people’s
opinions or social norms—also referred to as subjective norms or injunctive norms
(Giampietri et al. 2018; Neumann and Mehlkop 2023).
Context
The context component is added to Alphabet Theory by incorporating the Attitude-
Behavior-Context Model, with context understood to influence attitudes and to mod -
erate their translation into behavior. Context encompasses external factors, with
relevant topics in the reviewed articles including price, accessibility, convenience,
variety and/or availability of products, and the process of sale. Numerous studies
affirm that contextual factors strongly influence consumer behavior (e.g., Cruz et al.
2021; Giampietri et al. 2016; Maró et al. 2022) and can even override strong attitudes
(Neumann and Mehlkop 2023).

Perceived barriers like long distances, limited opening hours, and/or low product
availability all constitute obstacles to SFSC. A hindering context leads to a lower will -
ingness to buy at SFSCs (Neumann and Mehlkop 2023). Conversely, favorable con -
texts such as accessible points of sale can facilitate SFSC purchases (Zepeda and Deal
2009). While highly motivated consumers may perceive contextual barriers as less rel -
evant, low barriers can also encourage purchases among less motivated individuals
(Hyland et al. 2024; Menéndez i Molist et al. 2024). Nevertheless, low barriers alone
do not guarantee purchases.
The context varies significantly across SFSC channels, as the different channels entail
different attributes, leading to different consumer groups according to the channels.
Cruz et al. (2021) classify SFSC with the help of a four-axis model, based on direct con -
tact vs. intermediaries and shared values vs. certification, that supposedly shapes SFSC
channel features. According to Cruz et  al., the resulting quadrants correspond to one
predominant consumer type, that values and prioritizes different contextual attributes.
Price Price has a strong influence on SFSC behavior, especially for consumers with
lower income (e.g., Aguado-Gragera et al. 2024; Menéndez i Molist et al. 2024; Vittersø
et al. 2019). High prices, or consumers’ perceptions of prices as high, can act as a major
barrier to purchasing through SFSCs (Hvitsand and Leikvoll 2023; Maró et al. 2022; Vit -
tersø et al. 2019). However, price perceptions vary by country (Vittersø et al. 2019); and
while often seen as a disadvantage, price is not always the primary barrier (e.g., Gonzalez-
Azcárate et al. 2021; Tiganis et al. 2023). Many SFSC consumers accept higher prices due
to perceived higher quality (e.g., better taste or freshness) or alignment with their values
(Aguado-Gragera et al. 2024; Neulinger et al. 2020; Vittersø et al. 2019). This indicates that
perceived value for money is more important for consumers’ choice than actual prices.
Accessibility and convenience Accessibility usually refers to attributes of the place
of purchase (e.g., opening hours) that strongly impact SFSC participation (e.g.,
Cruz et al. 2021; Hvitsand and Leikvoll 2023; Peña Rodríguez et al. 2024). Indeed
some studies even find accessibility to be the most influential aspect (Neumann and
Mehlkop 2023), with distance and limited opening hours being major obstacles,
especially for non-regular customers (Maró et al. 2022). Accessibility can also refer
to levels of consumer commitment required for participation in SFSCs. For exam -
ple, channels with lower commitment like farmers’ markets are perceived as more
accessible than CSA groups (Neulinger et al. 2020). This dimension of accessibility
is similar to what other authors describe as “convenience” aspects.
Most studies use the concept of convenience to refer to food acquisition and prepara -
tion, the amount of time and effort required, and the logistics involved (e.g., Aguado-
Gragera et al. 2024; Cruz et al. 2021; Giampietri et al. 2016). The convenience of SFSCs
varies according to channel. For example, online SFSC channels offer easier access,
greater variety, and more overall convenience than offline channels (Gonzalez-Azcárate
et  al. 2021). Conversely, the effort required to shop at farmer’s markets or to manage
membership of CSA can act as a barrier (Maró et  al. 2022; Menéndez i Molist et  al.
2024). Convenience can also refer to the extent that food is processed, where ready-to-
eat food is obviously more convenient than fresh unprocessed food (Stanco et al. 2019).

Inconvenience or high effort are identified as barriers for consumers of all types of SFSC
in numerous studies (e.g., Hyland et al. 2024; Maró et al. 2022; Menéndez i Molist et al.
2024).
Additional topics Limited product variety in SFSCs, especially compared to supermar -
kets, is a barrier for both regular and potential consumers (Hvitsand and Leikvoll 2023;
Neumann and Mehlkop 2023). Many studies emphasize that variety influences consumer
satisfaction and willingness to participate (e.g., Aguado-Gragera et al. 2024; Gonzalez-
Azcárate et al. 2021; Tiganis et al. 2023). The sales process, including service quality, wait-
ing times, and delivery options, impacts consumer satisfaction and choice (Tiganis et al.
2023). Consumers especially value personalized service and the experience of purchasing
food directly from its source (Aguado-Gragera et  al. 2024). Clean and appealing sales
environments also contribute positively to consumer perceptions (Tiganis et al. 2023).
Information and knowledge
Alphabet Theory highlights the circular interrelationship between information-
seeking, knowledge, and attitudes, whereby existing values drive information-seek -
ing and knowledge acquisition that in turn reinforce values and attitudes (Zepeda
and Deal 2009).
Information‑seeking and availability The literature on SFSCs concurs with Alpha -
bet Theory in showing that consumers are usually more interested in receiving
information about the products they buy than average consumers and actively seek
such information (e.g., Hvitsand and Leikvoll 2023; Peña Rodríguez et  al. 2024).
Especially participants in channels like Solidarity Purchase Groups are looking for
detailed information about the products and farmers (Lombardi et al. 2015). First-
time shoppers in particular are interested in product origin and production methods
(Gonzalez-Azcárate et al. 2021).
The reviewed studies thus frequently highlight the extent that information avail -
ability and information flow are crucial aspects of SFSCs (Galli and Brunori 2013;
Marsden et al. 2000; Venn et al. 2006). On this basis, the original expression “infor -
mation seeking” used in Alphabet Theory was extended for this review to “Infor -
mation-seeking and availability” . Information availability has a direct impact on
levels of consumer trust (e.g., Cruz et al. 2021; Delibas 2021; Zoll et al. 2021, 2023),
attitudes around food quality perception (Cruz et al. 2021; Neulinger et al. 2020),
as well as perceptions of price (Vittersø et  al. 2019). As such, information avail -
ability may positively influence consumers toward choosing SFSCs (e.g., Aguado-
Gragera et al. 2024; Lombardi et al. 2015). The study conducted by Bernardi et al.
(2021) finds a direct relation between the flow of information and more sustainable
behavior. While some studies report general satisfaction among consumers with
the information available in SFSCs (Schmutz et  al. 2017; Zoll et  al. 2023), others
indicate that consumers do not feel sufficiently informed about the products (Cruz
et al. 2021; Gonzalez-Azcarate et al. 2021).
Information can be made accessible in various ways, varying significantly across
SFSCs according to the characteristics of the corresponding channel. The study by

Burgess et al. (2024) specifically focuses on communication and information tools
in Alternative Food Networks, including on-package (e.g., ingredient lists, labels)
and off-package (e.g., websites, word-of-mouth) information. On-package infor -
mation is especially important to consumers in supply chains that do not feature
face-to-face interactions (Burgess et al. 2024; Cruz et al. 2021). Face-to-face inter -
actions hold the opportunity to provide personalized information. This direct form
of exchange in channels that work face-to-face offers an advantage over conven -
tional food distribution (Migliore et al. 2015; Schmutz et al. 2017), which is espe -
cially interesting for consumers who do not trust labels (Burgess et al. 2024). Some
studies contradict these findings, however, suggesting that receiving information
during the purchase process is less important for consumers (Tiganis et al. 2023).
The advertisement of SFSC channels and/or products also plays a role in conveying
information to (potential) customers (Hvitsand and Leikvoll 2023), though other
studies have found SFSC consumers less responsive to advertisements (Lombardi
et al. 2015).
Knowledge While many studies examine consumer attitudes toward topics such as
ethical consumption, organic food, or animal welfare and their relation to behavior,
they do not necessarily assess the extent of consumer knowledge on these topics or
how knowledge influences attitudes and behavior. Nevertheless, some studies find
that active SFSC participation is associated with gaining new knowledge and practi -
cal skills (Zoll et al. 2021), which can alter food behavior and habits (Vittersø et al.
2019). For example, Vittersø et al. (2019) emphasize that transparency and proxim -
ity in SFSCs afford opportunities for consumers to gain knowledge directly through
information exchange, observation, and dialogue. These interactions often lead to
a deeper understanding of food production. Notably, lack of awareness or knowl -
edge about SFSC options is identified as a significant barrier to SFSC consumption
(Aguado-Gragera et al. 2024; Peña Rodríguez et al. 2024).
Habit
In Alphabet Theory, cooking, shopping, and dietary habits are understood as being
influential for food purchase behavior. However, this aspect is only rarely explored
in the reviewed studies, with only a few studies briefly mentioning personal habits
(i.e., practices embedded in daily life) as an influence on SFSC shopping behavior
(Oñederra-Aramendi et  al. 2018; Vittersø et  al. 2019). What is important to note
here is that repeating SFSC purchase behavior can become habitual over time,
becoming self-reinforcing through consistent action and thereby lowering per -
ceived barriers (Hyland et al. 2024). Some channels, such as vegetable-boxes sub -
scriptions, make it easier for consumers to integrate SFSC purchases into their
routines, whereas the infrequent occurrence of monthly farmers markets can act as
a contextual barrier for regular purchases (Peña Rodríguez et al. 2024).
Trust
Although trust was not included in the original Alphabet Theory, many stud -
ies emphasize its critical role in SFSC consumer behavior (e.g., Bakos and

Khademi-Vidra 2019; Baldi et al. 2019; Cruz et al. 2021; Zoll et al. 2023). Since dis -
trust in conventional food systems is often what motivates consumers to seek alter -
natives in the first place, trust in alternative food sources is especially relevant (Zoll
et  al. 2021). Trust not only influences purchasing decisions but also the amount
of money spent (Migliore et  al. 2015) and can even substitute for knowledge and
information (Cruz et al. 2021).
SFSC consumers generally trust producers and retailers, while non-participants
often lack trust and have quality concerns (Gonzalez-Azcárate et  al. 2021; Hvit -
sand and Leikvoll 2023). Differences in levels of consumer trust across several
EU countries are found in a study by Vittersø et al. (2019). Key factors for build -
ing trust in SFSCs include availability of information, transparency, direct contact
with producers, and social exchange among consumers.  It is important to acknowl -
edge that trust formation differs depending on the type of SFSC. In channels with
face-to-face interaction, trust is often built through personal relationships with
producers or other consumers. Direct contact with producers is often mentioned
as a crucial component for building trust in SFSCs (e.g., Gonzalez-Azcárate et al.
2021; Hvitsand and Leikvoll 2023; Neulinger et  al. 2020). Additionally, exchange
and reputation within consumer networks play a significant role (Bernardi et  al.
2021; Escobar-Lopez et al. 2021), with reputation highlighted as the strongest fac -
tor in trust-building (Zoll et al. 2023). This is why channels with higher consumer
involvement, such as CSA groups, tend to foster stronger trust compared to others.
In contrast, other channels that do not include face-to-face encounters rely on dif -
ferent trust building mechanisms, like certification or non-direct ways of providing
information. Some studies argue that in the absence of direct contact, reputation
and information availability can have an equally strong effect on consumer trust
(Zoll et al. 2023). Since consumers can find it difficult to assess certain attributes
of products (Delibas 2021), information availability and transparency are central
aspects for building trust (e.g., Cruz et al. 2021; Zoll et al. 2021, 2023). Often the
willingness of farmers to be transparent and let consumers visit their farms sig -
nals trustworthiness (Zoll et al. 2023). Potential SFSC consumers in particular rely
more on on-package information and labels for trust than on direct interactions
with producers (Cruz et  al. 2021). Certification such as organic and quality labe -
ling also contributes to trust, though its importance varies. While some consumers
value certification as proof of quality (Migliore et al. 2015), others either doubt its
validity or find it unnecessary when trust stems from other factors (Delibas 2021;
Gonzalez-Azcárate et al. 2021).
Trust can also arise from personal control and/or direct experiences such as visit -
ing farms, tasting products, or noticing differences compared to similar supermar -
ket goods (Bakos and Khademi-Vidra 2019; Delibas 2021).
Behavior
While all of the components described above can help explain which factors determine
consumer behavior in the context of SFSCs, the complex interplay between these differ -
ent components still makes it difficult to fully understand such behavior. Alphabet The -
ory and other models, as well as findings from various of the reviewed studies, indicate

Page 16 of 40Herzig and Zander  Agricultural and Food Economics           (2025) 13:21
that attitudes do not necessarily translate into behavior. Studies that compare SFSC con-
sumers to non-consumers behavior confirm this attitude-behavior gap. For example,
Gonzalez-Azcárate et al. (2021) found that potential and actual SFSC consumers have
similar attitudes toward key aspects of SFSCs.
A measurable indicator of consumer behavior is purchase frequency, though stud -
ies of SFSCs find important variations in this regard. Thus, Cicia et al. (2021) found
that only 10% of shoppers at farmers’ markets in Germany bought from SFSCs
once a week or more, whereas Stanco et al. (2019) found that 41% of such shoppers
in Italy were regular shoppers. In a study of general SFSC shoppers, meanwhile,
Giampietri et al. (2016) found 53% were regular shoppers. Hyland et al. (2024) note
regional differences in motivations and purchase frequency across Europe.
Purchase frequency also varies among different consumer groups (Hyland et al.
2024). Thus, some studies indicate that higher frequency and quantity of SFSC pur -
chases are related to positive attitudes toward organic food shopping (Menéndez i
Molist et al. 2024; Stanco et al. 2019) and pro-environmental attitudes (Cicatiello
2020), while others find no direct correlation (e.g., Hyland et al. 2024). Frequency
and quantity of purchase are further related to the type of SFSC (Cicatiello 2020).
For instance, vegetable-box schemes imply regular deliveries and often serve as a
main food source, while, e.g., buying from farm shops and farmers’ markets does
not imply such regularity.
Discussion
Although short food supply chains are increasingly regarded as a significant alter -
native form of food distribution, research on consumer behavior in SFSC contexts
remains limited. This systematic literature review was thus undertaken to identify
key determinants of consumer behavior in the context of SFSCs.
Alphabet Theory proved to be valuable for systematically analyzing the selected
studies and organizing them into a relational framework. All of the proposed deter -
minants that Alphabet Theory suggests were identified in the reviewed studies. In
addition, the review findings underscore the critical role of trust as a determinant
of behavior, though trust was not included in Zepeda and Deal’s (2009) original ver -
sion of Alphabet Theory. Based on these findings, “trust” should be incorporated in
the model when studying food-related decision making. As already described in the
corresponding section, from a consumer behavior perspective, the model has some
conceptual weaknesses in its use of the term attitudes. Yet, the framework helped
to organize the broad range of study findings and to provide new insights into the
topic.
While sociodemographic characteristics such as income, education and age can
affect consumers’ willingness or ability to engage with SFSCs, the findings of this
review indicate that such characteristics are not reliable indicators of SFSC pur -
chase behavior insofar as their effects on behavior are always interrelated with other
factors. Also, sociodemographic aspects need to be contextualized in a wider socio -
cultural context. Many studies fail to do so, associating higher-income female con -
sumers with SFSC participation, forgetting to acknowledge how gender roles may
explain higher representation of women among SFSC shoppers since women are

traditionally responsible for food shopping (Neumann and Mehlkop 2023; Zepeda
and Li 2006). Nevertheless, sociodemographic aspects are often used to segment
consumers, which can certainly be useful for marketing purposes. The reviewed
studies indicate that although, e.g., factors such as age and household situation do
not consistently correlate with SFSC purchase behavior in general, patterns can still
be found, especially when looking at channels separately. These outcomes suggest
that variations in the channel-specific settings attract different consumer groups
(Hyland et al. 2024; Vittersø et al. 2019). On the basis of this finding, while target -
ing in general still remains a valuable strategy, it is recommended to compile strate -
gies that strive to create more inclusive contextual settings within the channels to
attract a wider consumer spectrum.
Contextual factors emerge as key determinants of consumer behavior in SFSCs,
influencing decisions and creating both barriers and opportunities. Common bar -
riers include specific accessibility issues, inconvenient operating hours, and a gen -
eral perceived lack of convenience. As the literature suggests that limited access
due to distance and opening hours is a frequent barrier (e.g., Tiganis et  al. 2023;
Neumann and Mehlkop 2023), one approach to addressing that could be extending
farmer’s market hours to accommodate after-work shoppers. A more flexible SFSC
model could combine face-to-face settings with online pre-ordering and local pick-
up points or home delivery, to address the consumers demand for close and easy
pickup options (Hvitsand and Leikvoll 2023). Product variety, another frequently
mentioned barrier, could be improved by producer collaborations, allowing for a
broader product range within a single channel. Implementing these measures could
reduce barriers and enhance a wider availability of SFSCs, fostering consumer par -
ticipation (Giampietri et al. 2018). Marketing efforts should emphasize messages of
convenience and accessibility to align with consumer preferences.
Price is often cited as a contextual barrier, but some studies suggest that it is not
necessarily the most critical factor influencing behavior, which aligns with findings
from other consumer studies (Padel and Foster 2005). Interestingly, the reviewed
studies highlight that the perceived cost or benefit of behavior frequently outweighs
the actual context (or price) in determining consumer behavior. For instance, con -
sumers with limited economic resources may still be willing to pay a premium for
SFSC products if they perceive these to be of superior quality or closely aligned
with their values (Aguado-Gragera et al. 2024; Menéndez i Molist et al. 2024). This
shows how consumers’ perceptions are shaped by the interplay between contex -
tual factors, information and knowledge, attitudes, and sociodemographic factors.
The understanding of how consumers interpret contexts and available information
should guide marketing measures, which should focus on communicating the addi -
tional value of SFSCs, such as supporting local farmers and ensuring fair pricing,
rather than competing solely on price.
Findings regarding the role of attitudes in SFSC consumption are similar to find -
ings about local food (Feldmann and Hamm 2015; Zepeda and Li 2006) and organic
food (Brümmer and Zander 2020; Padel and Foster 2005; Zanoli et al. 2004). Both
altruistic motives (e.g., environmental protection and sustainability) and egoistic
motives (e.g., food quality aspects like healthiness, freshness, and taste) are found

to be central. In addition, attitudes related to social responsibility play a key role
in SFSC consumption. Based on these findings, communication strategies should
combine appeals to both egoistic and altruistic motives. Contrary to the assump -
tion that these motives compete, studies on organic food consumption suggest they
are compatible and resonate strongly with consumers (Kareklas et al. 2014). Effec -
tive communication should emphasize the compatibility of positive implications for
individuals, society and the environment.
The availability of reliable information is another critical factor in this process. In
food shopping, consumers often lack the immediate knowledge to evaluate prod -
uct provenance and value. SFSCs have a clear advantage over conventional chains,
as they imply the availability of information and facilitate communication chan -
nels between consumers and producers. The reviewed studies indicate that trans -
parent and reliable information about the origin of food, production methods, and
additional value increase the consumers’ willingness to buy through SFSCs. This
is closely related to building trust in SFSCs, which is achieved more effectively
through transparency and direct interactions between producers and consumers
than via quality labels or certification. This finding is especially interesting when
compared to studies on trust-building around organic food, usually traded in
long chains, which find logos to be central to consumer trust (Zanoli et al. 2015).
Accordingly, providing transparent information, signaling openness for further
exchange, and offering the possibility of on-site visits to farms are all strongly rec -
ommended for SFSCs. While this is easier in face-to-face channels, making infor -
mation available is an inherit attribute of SFSCs, which makes this relevant for all
types of channels. Non-face-to-face channels can provide information through
websites and videos, or offer monthly newsletters.
Non-SFSC consumers also face different barriers to purchasing compared to reg -
ular SFSC consumers (Gonzalez-Azcárate et  al. 2021). A lack of awareness about
available SFSC options and their potential benefits is frequently cited as a barrier
to engagement (Chang et  al. 2022). As a result, potential consumers may require
more detailed product information than those already familiar with a product or
shop (Hamm 1991), which can also be applied for SFSCs. Finding ways to encour -
age SFSC purchases for potential consumers is crucial to support the development
and expansion of SFSCs. To address this, a communication policy, aimed at inform -
ing and motivating the consumers, has to be developed. It can include advertise -
ment (e.g., brochures, social media), sales promotions (such as discount trial offers
or complementary events like seasonal vegetable cooking classes), and public rela -
tions efforts (e.g., open farm days or participation in community events) (Hamm
1991). Additionally, word-of-mouth advertisement can be encouraged by “custom -
ers acquire customers” initiatives, offering promotions to regular consumers who
successfully refer new buyers (Harth 2022).
Although habits are acknowledged as influential, they remain underexplored in
the reviewed literature about SFSCs. Indeed, this same observation has been made by
Bayir et al. (2022). From a consumer behavior perspective, habitual decision-making is
a relevant determinant of behavior. Consumers automatize everyday decisions in order
to minimize the effort they invest in activities like food shopping (Solomon 2019).

Conventional distribution systems have adjusted to this and are designed to be time-
efficient and low-effort for consumers, typically offering “one-stop-shopping” opportu -
nities (Aguado-Gragera et al. 2024; Bayir et al. 2022). Changing habitual behavior is thus
seen as a major challenge from a marketing perspective (Solomon 2019). Nevertheless,
the reviewed studies suggest a bidirectional relationship between habits and behavior;
for while habitual shopping practices can act as barriers, repeated SFSC purchases can
also gradually transform into habits, thereby reducing perceived obstacles (Vittersø
et al. 2019). To help disrupt the shopping habits of consumers accustomed to conven -
tional sales channels, marketing measures should focus on lowering entry barriers (e.g.,
by offering try-out offers) and raising awareness of SFSCs and their benefits. Targeted
advertisements should center around food quality and taste, since these aspects moti -
vate non-SFSC-consumers in their food choices (Gonzalez-Azcárate et  al. 2021). For
non-regular consumers, marketing strategies should aim to encourage repeated behav -
ior by addressing practical concerns, including improving accessibility, extending oper -
ating hours, and offering reliable delivery services.
This review shows that while many determinants directly affect consumer behavior,
the interplay between them is rather complex and multidimensional. While certain soci-
odemographic characteristics may stand out, they do not reliably predict actual SFSC
engagement. Similarly, motivational profiles can help explain certain behavioral pat -
terns, but positive attitudes toward, e.g., local food, do not necessarily translate into pur-
chase behavior. Knowledge about food production in general, or explicit information on
certain SFSC channels or products in particular, strongly influences attitudes. The effect
of the information again is related to sociodemographic aspects. However, even if con -
sumers are well informed and motivated to purchase SFSC products, their intention to
purchase can be hindered if the context does not provide purchasing opportunities or
only options that are not accessible for them. From a marketing perspective, the focus
should be on tailored information about SFSCs and creating more accessible and con -
venient settings.
Limitations
This study has analyzed the behavior of SFSC consumers by selecting and reviewing
only those studies that explicitly conceptualize their case studies within the context
of SFSCs. This approach presented two primary corresponding limitations. First,
excluding channel-specific studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria poten -
tially limited the findings, insofar as excluded studies might have provided fur -
ther valuable insights into consumer behavior within particular contexts. Second,
drawing comparisons across channels and contexts is challenging yet also intrigu -
ing, since the specific type of SFSC channel and the sociocultural context explored
in case studies affect consumer behavior. Future research could delve deeper into
understanding the distinct implications of various specific SFSC channels for con -
sumer behavior.
Another limitation relates to conceptual accuracy when analyzing attitudes. Atti -
tudes, values, and motivations are terms inconsistently defined and applied across
the reviewed literature. This lack of uniformity introduces conceptual ambiguity,
complicating direct comparisons. While prior studies focused on organic food

consumption have offered more detailed insights into interactions between differ -
ent psychographic components of behavior (Aertsens et al. 2009), this review has
focused on the multiple determinants and their influence on behavior. Despite
these challenges, the analysis provided meaningful and actionable conclusions.
Future research
To strengthen  the understanding of SFSCs and support their development and
expansion, future research should address various fields identified in the study.
First, research should address contextual barriers. In particular, studies could focus
on innovative solutions for creating more accessible SFSC shopping experiences,
e.g., through app-based ordering systems, food-hub-inspired pickup locations, and
novel delivery methods. Evaluating the impacts of government policies or subsi -
dies on improving SFSC logistics and infrastructure could also provide insights to
inform strategies for enhancing accessibility.
Given the finding that price perception frequently acts as a barrier to consump -
tion, future studies should explore this factor in greater depth. Conducting will -
ingness-to-pay studies specific to SFSCs and comparing them to price expectations
could yield valuable insights. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have yet
compared average prices of supermarket-bought organic food with food bought
through various SFSC channels. The findings of such research could be useful for
comparing actual prices with expected prices of SFSCs and later be used to com -
municate the outcomes to consumers. In addition, it would be useful to study how
price perception changes when combined with other factors such as communica -
tion about added value or information about product quality and sustainability.
The effectiveness of different marketing measures should also be further
explored. Here, the focus should be on the effectiveness of communication strate -
gies and technologies for engaging both existing and potential SFSC consumers.
Future research should consider differentiating consumer clusters by comparing differ-
ent SFSC types and examining these in various sociocultural settings in order to attain a
better understanding of consumer behavior and develop targeted marketing strategies.
Given the importance of trust highlighted in this review, future studies could
explore differences in how various SFSC channels foster trust and how trust
impacts long-term consumer loyalty.
The limited focus on habits in the reviewed studies indicates a significant research
gap and an opportunity for further exploration. In particular, such research should
aim at understanding how initial motivations for SFSC participation evolve into
habitual behavior over time.
In the past years, new food trends (like, e.g., veganism or other diets) have gained
importance (Cicia et al. 2021). These diets are often related to values like health,
sustainability or ethical consumption, similar to the values associated with SFSCs.
As new food trends hold potential for growth, future research should explore how
marketers can use this as a synergy to increase interest in SFSCs.
Finally, comparative studies examining differences between consumers and non-
consumers could provide valuable insights to inform efforts aimed at addressing
barriers to SFSC consumption and advancing their development.

Conclusions
As alternatives to conventional food distribution systems, short food supply chains
(SFSCs) afford numerous benefits for producers and consumers. However, their
broader adoption is often hindered by challenges. From the perspective of consum -
ers in particular, barriers such as perceived and actual lack of accessibility and con -
venience inhibit the expansion of SFSCs. In offering a systematic overview of the
factors that determine consumer behavior in SFSCs, and by related recommenda -
tions, this systematic literature review provides valuable insights for researchers
and stakeholders to inform efforts to address existing challenges.
The findings reveal that consumer behavior in SFSCs is shaped by a complex
interplay of factors. Whereas traditional models of consumer behavior focus on
attitudes and sociodemographic factors, this review suggests these cannot explain
SFSC purchasing behavior by themselves but only gain explanatory power when
analyzed in combination with other factors.  The analysis thus emphasizes the
importance of the interplay of these determinants in shaping consumer percep -
tions of the costs and benefits of SFSCs. Notably, the study finds that consumer
perceptions of factors such as price and the value of SFSC products are often more
influential than objective factors such as actual prices. This finding highlights the
critical need for effective communication that focuses on the added value of SFSC
products.
Since the contextual setting was found to be highly relevant to consumer behav -
ior, efforts to support SFSCs should focus on creating more inclusive shopping
experiences and more convenient settings, e.g., by offering home delivery and
extending opening hours. In addition, building trust through direct producer–con -
sumer interactions and transparency was found to be central to fostering long-term
relationships in SFCS channels.
Overall, the findings and analysis of this study underscore the need for further
research on effective communication and marketing strategies for SFSCs. Emphasis
should be placed on improving information availability and transparency as well as
on creating more accessible contexts.
