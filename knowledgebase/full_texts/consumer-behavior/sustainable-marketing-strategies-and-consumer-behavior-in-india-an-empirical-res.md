Abstract

experiencing an unprecedented economic growth and increased environment consciousness.

constructs, Ahimsa (non-violence) and Dharma (duty towards nature) as moderators of the
Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [1] and Value-Belief-Norm (VBN) models [2] instead of
regarding them as peripheral variables. The convergent mixed-methods design was used:

1. Introduction
In line with the theme of ICSISE-2025, which is innovation towards
sustainable ecosystems, this paper focuses on one of the most important
issues at a nexus point between economic growth and environmental
protection. The population of India which is more than 1.4 billion and
growing middle class and government policies, India’s flagship climate
policy initiative, the NAPCC and Swachh Bharat Mission, provide a policy
environment favorable to Sustainable Marketing [3], [4]. However, policy
intent to consumer behavior change is not simple: Sustainable Marketing
is implementing ecological and social responsibility into the core value
proposition of an organization [5] but living in harmony with convenience
and low costs desired by consumers.
The extant constructs, TPB [1] and VBN Theory [2], were confirmed
in high-income and individualistic western markets. They are not well
applied to collectivist emerging markets that are price-sensitive, which
has not been extensively assessed [6], which presents a consequential gap
since the Attitude-Behavior Gap is shown to be significantly broader in a
cost-constrained market [7]. The proposed research closes these gaps by:
(a) applying Ahimsa and Dharma to TPB/VBN as moderating variables;
(b) quantifying the mediating role of Price Sensitivity; (c) determining
culturally based consumer segments; and (d) comparing Green Purchase
Intentions in metropolitan and Tier-2 cities as a proxy of urban and rural,
respectively.

2. Literature Review
2.1 Theoretical Framework: Cultural Integration
Sustainable Marketing, which was initially a niche strategy in the
1970s became a strategic imperative with social and economic aspects, as
well as environmental issues [5], [8]. TPB [1] assumes that attitudes,
subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control are all influential
factors in deciding behavioral intentions. VBN Theory [2] extends a
sequential process between values and beliefs through to personal moral
norms. These two frameworks have their roots in a Western individualistic
setting and need to be applied to collectivist, multi-cultural markets [6],
[9]. The most crucial innovation of the research is that Ahimsa (non-
violence) and Dharma (environmental stewardship) [10], [11] are viewed
as the moderators of the attitude-intention connection as part of the
integrated TPB-VBN framework- not as equivalent. The cultural value
orientation enhances the subjective norm aspect of TPB as it is in the
collectivist society of India where duty-based motivations are significantly
involved in the bridging the relationship between environmental attitudes
and Green Purchase Behavior [12], [13].
2.2 Price Sensitivity, Trust and Urban-Rural Dynamics
The consumer decisions of the developing market are dominated by
price sensitivity. Indian consumers do not focus on sustainable products
and products that provide a long-term environmental benefit like affluent
consumers [14], instead, they emphasize on instantaneous usefulness [15],
[16], which complicates premium-price strategies and demands other
propositions based on cost-reduction, efficiency and durability [17].
Greenwashing has undermined trust in the world [18], in India, where
certification systems are still in their early stages, Brand Trust stands out
as an influential intermediary between communication and purchase
intent [19], [20]. Urban people are more vigilant as compared to rural
counterparts who conserve resources due to economic necessity and not
due to environmental ideology [21], [22]. This urban-rural imbalance has
some serious segmentation implication [23], [24] which are enhanced by
the influence of collectivist family and community in the decision to
purchase a product or service [25], [26].
3. Research Methodology
3.1 Design
A convergent mixed-methodology was used, and it was triangulated
by using survey data, focus groups and content analysis to embrace the

social, cultural and economic nuances of sustainable consumption in

3.2 Quantitative Survey
Questionnaires were given to 1,623 respondents in six cities,
metropolitan (Delhi, Mumbai, Bangalore) and Tier-2 (Pune, Jaipur,
Coimbatore). The stratified random sampling was used to balance gender,
age, income, and education. The modules that were used in the survey
included: (i) Environmental Consciousness (15 items, culturally adapted
using Ahimsa/Dharma-referenced indicators); (ii) Green Brand
Perception; (iii) Purchase Intentions; (iv) Cultural Value Orientation; (v)
Demographics. Reliability and validity: 0.87; CR = 0.91; A VE = 0.64,
Discriminant Validity verified in Fornell-Larcker criterion (Table 1).

3.3 Qualitative Focus Groups
Cultural motivations, attitudinal barriers, and purchase decision
process were studied in 16 focus groups (n = 134; 10 Groups- 8 participants
per group, 6 Groups- 9 participants per group). Sessions were held in
regional languages, and they were professionally translated and analyzed
thematically using NVivo 12.
3.4 Content Analysis
Over six months the coding of sustainability communications of 45
Indian brands in FMCG, Personal Care, Electronics, and Fashion was

conducted in traditional (TV , print) and digital channels. These were claim
specificity, cultural symbolism, pricing strategy, certification visibility and
authenticity.
3.5 Quantitative Analysis
Descriptive statistics, correlations, regression, and hierarchical cluster
analysis [27] were performed with the help of SPSS 26.0. Confirmatory
factor analysis, multiple regression, and Baron-Kenny bootstrapped
mediation were used to test the hypothesized relationships. Baron-Kenny
was used to assess mediation using bootstrapped CIs (5,000 iterations).
4. Results and Analysis
4.1 Environmental Consciousness
The general Environmental Consciousness was moderate (M = 3.68,
SD = 0.84). Age (r = 0.28, p < 0.01) and education (r = 0.34, p < 0.001)
correlated positively with awareness. Metro city users scored significantly
higher than Tier-2 counterparts (3.89 vs. 3.47; t (1621) = 8.73, p < 0.001).
There was a non-linear relationship between income and Environmental
Consciousness, as the levels of Environmental Consciousness increased
with income up to the middle bracket; however, it failed to provide
proportional Green Purchase Behavior at the highest levels of income.

4.7 Urban–Rural Differences and Communication Effectiveness
Tier-2 consumers had more unfamiliarity with sustainability terms,
branded Green Products but more drawn to conventional conservation
practices (composting, water conservation, repair/reuse). Met-shoppers
were more inclined towards packaged sustainable product with
certifications and Tier-2 consumers were more inclined towards unbranded
natural products and traditional preparation- implying that rural
consumers may already be practicing sustainability through the wisdom
of their indigenous and need to do so due to economic pressure. In terms
of communication: 38 percent of examined green campaigns made
verifiable claims; third party certifications of a brand contributed to a 47
percent greater degree of trust; educational messages also produced 34
percent more attention than promotional messages; and using cultural
narratives increased the Authenticity Perception by 29 percent among
consumers with Traditional Value-oriented attitudes.
5. Discussion
Price sensitivity, information asymmetry, low availability of products
and low institutional trust increase the Attitude-Behavior Gap in India.
The prevailing Perceived Authenticity confirms the role of VBN Theory
about the importance of moral norms: authenticity is the gap between the
professed values and the actual purchase behavior. This 67 percent
credibility difference between word-of-mouth and brand communications
is an indicator of the subjective norm element of TPB that is working at a
more powerful level in the collectivist environment of India. Price
Sensitivity ( = 0.41) is not only a demographic phenomenon but a structural
market characteristic that the sustainability strategies have to directly

respond to. The big Price-sensitive  Adopter market (29%) is an opportunity:
the cost-neutral or cost-saving sustainability propositions can be delivered
to this segment. The cultural paradox of high Dharma/Ahimsa orientation
being the most sustainable behavior and the distrust in commercialized
Green Marketing, in turn, highlights that the communication with an
indigenous Indian-based cultural set is the most effective in terms of
reaching the greatest segment (35% Traditional Value-driven).
6. Contributions
6.1 Theoretical Contributions
To begin with, this paper formally incorporates Ahimsa and Dharma
as moderators on the attitude-intention relationship on the single TPB-
VBN model- an empirical novelty in the Indian Green Marketing context.
Second, it shows that the Attitude Behavior Gap is a situational element,
and it is much broader in price-sensitive, collectivist markets, which
require market-specific theoretical extensions. Third, the cultural paradox
(indigenous values facilitating and hindering Green Marketing) brings in
a subtle theoretical layer that is not present in the previous literature.
6.2 Managerial Contributions
The sustainable marketing strategies should be grounded on
authenticity by investing in the supply chain, third party certification and
delivering on promises on a regular basis. Indian realities have to be
considered in the pricing: cost-neutral sustainability (process efficiency,
durability, smaller packs) has a larger audience than premium positioning.
Cultural integration, the association of environmental action with Dharma,
Ahimsa, and the traditional ecological wisdom, is an appropriate factor
that boosts Authenticity Perception, particularly in the 35% Traditional
Value-oriented segment. The promotional tools of the community-based
marketing which involve word-of-mouth, community influencers and
social proofs are always more effective than the traditional advertising
techniques in the Indian relationship-oriented market.
7. Conclusions
Sustainable Marketing in India cannot be done in a fundamentally
different approach as the one that was used in the Western markets. There
is also environmental awareness, which is transformed to Green Purchase
Behavior conditioned by price sensitivity, with the perceived authenticity,

mediated by cultural value orientation, and validated by community
network social. The combination of Ahimsa and Dharma into the set
behavioral patterns indicates an Indian way to sustainable usage pattern
that has been largely ignored by commercial strategies. Brands which
build sustainability stories based on these values, provide verifiable
impact on the environment, offer products affordable to the masses and
involve community trust systems are poised to dominate such a changing
market.
10. Future Research and Limitations.
The sample is also urban-based restricting the ability to generalize
the findings to the rest of India (65% of the population). The sustainable
consumption of rural areas and the traditional ecological practices should
be researched in the future. The cross-sectional design does not allow
causal conclusions; longitudinal research would provide a clearer
understanding of the development of behavior with time. Further insights
into sustainable marketing viability in India would be gained through
category specific research and supply-side approaches (organizational
capabilities, supply chains, policy environments).
