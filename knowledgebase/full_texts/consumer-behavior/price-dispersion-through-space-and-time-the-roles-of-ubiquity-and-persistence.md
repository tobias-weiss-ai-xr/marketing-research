Abstract
A considerable portion of the variability in price dispersion remains inadequately explained in marketing research. Exist -
ing theoretical perspectives that emphasize information asymmetry, market competition, and consumer behavior capture
only limited aspects of this multifaceted phenomenon. Drawing on microeconomic and behavioral pricing theory, we pro -
pose ubiquity and persistence as higher-order contextual moderators that shape how the determinants of price dispersion,
namely micro-market conditions, seller characteristics, product characteristics, and client characteristics, influence price
variability across markets. Ubiquity captures the spatial coexistence of heterogeneous prices, while persistence reflects
their temporal continuity. Rather than constituting types or dimensions of price dispersion, these constructs define the
contextual conditions under which its drivers exert their effects. Building on this foundation, we advance two theoretical
propositions and develop a conceptual framework that clarifies when and where each micro-level mechanism operates
most strongly. The present study contributes to marketing theory by integrating spatial and temporal contingencies into a
unified framework that links pricing behavior to enduring market outcomes and opens new avenues for empirical research.

Introduction
The persistence of price differences across online and
offline markets, as well as the inconclusive empirical evi -
dence regarding their magnitude, challenges the “law of
one price,” which posits that identical goods should trade at
similar prices under competitive conditions (Böheim et al.,
2021). Instead, evidence suggests that deeper market and
behavioral mechanisms drive price variability (Oliveira et
al., 2024). Existing explanations focusing on information
asymmetry (Walter et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2020) address
only a fraction of the intricacies involved. While differences
in access to market information and the presence of search
costs (Pereira, 2005) contribute to price variation, they do
not fully explain why dispersion remains prevalent, even
in digital contexts where transparency should be higher.
Prior research points to several additional drivers, including
market competition (Kim et al., 2023), consumer decision-
making (André et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2020; Rossi & Chin-
tagunta, 2016), entry barriers (Cachon et al., 2008), and
market power among sellers (Gensler et al., 2017; Morgan
et al., 2006; Wang & Li, 2020). Yet the collective influence

Price dispersion refers to the variation in prices for iden-
tical or comparable products across sellers, locations, or
transactions, typically captured through differences in sta -
tistical measures such as price ranges or standard deviations
(Pan et al., 2004). This phenomenon reflects the coexistence
of heterogeneous prices within a market, even under con -
ditions of competition and information availability. Rather
than representing a temporary deviation from equilibrium,
price dispersion is widely recognized as a persistent and
context-dependent outcome shaped by multiple structural
and behavioral factors.
To advance beyond these isolated components, we argue
that understanding price dispersion requires examining
the contextual contingencies under which it manifests and
endures. We introduce ubiquity and persistence as higher-
order contextual moderators that shape how market, seller,
product, and consumer factors influence price variability.
Ubiquity, a term derived from mobile commerce research
(Balasubraman et al., 2002), captures the spatial coexis -
tence of price differences, occurring when multiple sellers
simultaneously offer distinct prices for comparable products
across interconnected markets (Okazaki & Mendez, 2013).
This phenomenon signifies the pervasive nature of pricing
dynamics, which transcend specific locations and manifest
in both digital and physical domains.
Persistence, drawn from econometric time-series analysis
(Dekimpe & Hanssens, 2024), captures the temporal con -
tinuity of price disparities, referring to situations in which
price differences endure or reappear over time despite chang-
ing market conditions (Scholten & Smith, 2002; Kaplan et
al., 2019; Moen et al., 2020). On online retail platforms such
as Amazon, for example, well-known sellers consistently
command price premiums over lesser-known competitors
across extended periods, even as overall market prices fluc-
tuate. A similar pattern is observed in airline markets, where
price variations across carriers for identical routes endure
across booking windows, reflecting stable strategic posi -
tioning rather than temporary deviations. Importantly, these
constructs do not constitute dimensions or types of price
dispersion itself. Rather, they represent higher-order con -
textual conditions that shape how price dispersion emerges
and is sustained across markets. This distinction echoes Var-
ian’s (1980) classical characterization of price dispersion as
manifesting in spatial and temporal forms, which we extend
by conceptualizing ubiquity and persistence as contextual
moderators of these dynamics.
Despite their conceptual distinction, both moderators
may coexist within the same market context, with each
independently shaping the drivers of price dispersion. This
means that the influence of factors such as market compe -
tition, consumer heterogeneity, or information asymmetry
varies depending on the level of spatial ubiquity or temporal
persistence present in a given market. We argue that ubiq -
uity and persistence conditions, understood as the variation
in prices for identical products across sellers, operate as a
phenomenon that unfolds under two independent contex -
tual conditions—spatial and temporal—under which price
dispersion emerges. This perspective challenges the under -
standing of price dispersion as a single continuum or static
deviation from equilibrium. A clear illustration of this is
found in omnichannel retail environments, where the same
product is offered at varying prices across online platforms,
physical stores, and third-party marketplaces, allowing con-
sumers to observe heterogeneous prices in real time across
channels and formats.
Integration of ubiquity and persistence into the theoretical
foundations of price dispersion results in the development
of a framework grounded in a literature-based categoriza -
tion of its key determinants, including micro-market con -
ditions, seller characteristics, product characteristics, and
client characteristics. This framework integrates microeco -
nomic and behavioral perspectives on pricing with market
outcomes, elucidating how spatial and temporal contingen -
cies shape the emergence and persistence of price variability
across contexts.
We employ a deductive objectivist approach to develop a
theoretical framework that identifies price dispersion driv -
ers drawn from the literature. This study is situated within
the enduring divide in pricing research between economics-
based and management-oriented traditions (Monroe & Bitta,
1978), a contrast that provides a foundation for organizing
the fragmented literature into an integrated framework that
reflects both the structural mechanisms of markets and the
behavioral nature of pricing decisions.
This study builds on the integration of previously frag -
mented explanations into a unified micro-level framework
that systematically organizes the drivers of price dispersion
into four dimensions. Rather than introducing new vari -
ables, existing determinants are synthesized and structured
to clarify how they operate jointly and, more importantly,
how their effects depend on contextual conditions. In doing
so, our goal is to shift the focus from identifying isolated
drivers of price dispersion toward understanding the condi-
tions under which these drivers exert their influence.

begin by presenting the drivers of price dispersion, followed
by an analysis of online and offline price dispersion. The
subsequent section discusses persistence as a contingency of
price dispersion, which serves as the basis for two theoreti -
cal propositions on ubiquity and persistence as higher-order
moderators of price dispersion drivers. We then integrate

Drivers of price dispersion
Having introduced price dispersion in the introduction as
changes in the statistical properties of the price distribution,
we now turn to its underlying drivers. Price dispersion occurs
when there are significant differences in the prices charged
by different sellers or at different points of sale (Pan et al.,
2004). Consumers actively construct their own price refer -
ence systems while the products in the market form a price
dispersion range (Li et al., 2023). This price differentiation
ultimately becomes a barrier to the accurate representation of
a product’s price and can even impede the process of com -
paring it with products from other sellers who also employ
means to differentiate their prices in the market (Hagberg &
Kjellberg, 2015). Current literature endeavors to elucidate
the underlying motives of price dispersion by means of a list
of explanatory variables from disparate groups or categories,
including product cost, price elasticity, transaction quantity,
and others (Ghose & Yao, 2011; Ratchford, 2009).
Markets comprising a greater number of smaller firms
tend to exhibit more price dispersion than those dominated
by a handful of large competitors, a pattern attributable to
disparities in production costs and pricing strategies (Boren-
stein & Rose, 1994). Product differentiation also contributes
to varied levels of price dispersion (Clemons et al., 2002),
as does product availability, which can intensify price varia-
tion in competitive markets where consumer preference
drives pricing decisions (Wang et al., 2021). Conversely,
denser markets with numerous competitors may foster more
intense competition and, consequently, lower price disper -
sion (Wang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2015). Empirical studies
frequently examine store format and product characteris -
tics, such as online or offline retailing and product differen-
tiation, as additional sources of variability (Clemons et al.,
2002; Zhuang et al., 2018). For electronic retailers specifi -
cally, Pan et al. (2002) argue that price dispersion correlates
with retailer heterogeneity and service quality attributes,
including product assortment, convenience, reliability, and
accessibility to information. Consumer preferences further
compound this variability: some buyers prioritize expedited
deliveries in online environments, while others accept lon -
ger wait times in exchange for reduced shipping fees (Moen
et al., 2020). Cost differences among sellers in marketing,
distribution, and other operational expenses also contribute
to price dispersion (Nishida & Remer, 2018).
Demand elasticity is another element that may influence
price dispersion levels, as firms set varied prices across
regions based on consumer price sensitivity (Levy et al.,
2004; Nishida & Remer, 2018; Rathjen, 2015). Conse -
quently, consumer evaluations and perceptions of the level
of trustworthiness influence their price judgments (Zhao et
al., 2015). In this context, the alignment between a seller’s
reputation and their products or services becomes crucial.
Heterogeneity in consumer perceptions concerning the con-
gruence between seller descriptions, products, and pricing
policies can exert a positive or negative influence on price
dispersion (Wang et al., 2021).
Information asymmetry contributes directly to pricing
decisions (Grewal et al., 2003). Sellers who possess supe -
rior knowledge regarding prices or product quality can stra-
tegically establish varied prices across consumer segments
(Tellis & Wernerfelt, 1987). By leveraging this informa -
tional advantage, sellers adopt pricing strategies and tactics
that maximize their profits (Levy et al., 2004), an effect that
becomes particularly pronounced for products with intricate
attributes (Bakos, 1997). On the consumer side, buyers also
incur their own costs, including time, money, and effort, to
acquire optimal price and quality information (Jiang & Zou,
2020; Walter et al., 2006). Sorensen ( 2000) demonstrates
that even in markets where firms share similar attributes,
high search costs induce price variations. Consumers face
varying degrees of these costs, and their search behavior
may produce two distinct market equilibria: a competing
equilibrium, in which high-cost search firms serve a broader
consumer base, and a segmentation equilibrium, in which
firms cater exclusively to consumers with high search costs.
When search cost dynamics shift market structure from the
former to the latter, both nominal prices and price dispersion
tend to increase (Pereira, 2005). Consequently, information
costs shape the final transaction price, with buyers seeking
to reduce search expenditures and narrow price variations
by consulting sources such as price trend graphics and con-
sumer reviews (Wang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2015).

We begin by examining the online context, as digital mar -
kets provide a distinctive empirical setting where price
dispersion is more observable, dynamic, and measurable,
offering a clear illustration of the mechanisms that underpin
our theoretical argument.
In the context of e-commerce, consumer searches play a
pivotal role in the purchasing process, thereby reducing the
traditional information disparity that favors sellers (Christo-
doulides, 2009). Online consumers demonstrate a propen -
sity to engage in bargaining behavior, seeking out lower

prices, a behavior that is amplified at elevated levels of price
dispersion (Li et al., 2023). Despite the democratization of
information, perfect market conditions characterized by
complete information and perfect price competition remain
elusive due to enduring search costs and requirements of
information filtering. This dynamic ultimately results in a
state of monopolistic competition, which, in turn, gives rise
to a pronounced increase in price variability for compara -
ble products (Salop & Stiglitz, 1977). Walter et al. ( 2006)
identify two primary online search strategies: cross-site and
in-site searches. Despite the existence of online platforms
that facilitate informed price comparisons, these platforms
do not address the issue of price dispersion, which has the
potential to intensify over time (Böheim et al., 2021). Not-
withstanding the variables that attempt to explain price
discrimination, we posit that price dispersion manifests
across diverse markets, such as traditional (physical) and
electronic marketplaces, as an inherent phenomenon that
unfolds under conditions of both ubiquity and persistence.

As applied to price dispersion, ubiquity captures the spa-
tial coexistence of price differences across sellers (Okazaki
& Mendez, 2013). Perceived ubiquity is shaped by four
experiential dimensions: continuity and simultaneity, imme-
diacy and speed, portability and mobility, and searchability
and reachability (Okazaki & Mendez, 2013). The concept
gained prominence with the rise of smartphone technology,
as researchers recognized that the traditional marketing mix
was ill-equipped to account for the dynamic, networked, and
open systems that ubiquitous consumption entails, marked by
shifting alliances and loosely defined boundaries (Cox, 2004).
A comprehensive review of the studies presented in
Table 1 reveals that price dispersion remains consider -
able, and in numerous instances elevated, in online con -
texts, despite advances in price transparency and reductions
in search costs. This empirical pattern suggests that price
dispersion is not confined to specific market settings but
instead emerges across different contexts, reinforcing its
spatial pervasiveness.
Although ubiquity gained prominence in the context
of the proliferation of e-commerce and mobile services
(Okazaki & Mendez, 2013), traditional markets also offer
examples of omnipresent goods and services, such as elec -
tric power. Recent trends in retail environments, including
the rise of multichannel and omnichannel retailing, have
created conditions for ubiquitous price dispersion (Zhuang
et al., 2018). Electronic markets exhibit significant price
variations among retailers (Baylis & Perloff, 2002), and in
many cases these variations exceed those observed in tra -
ditional markets (Pan et al., 2004), underscoring the spatial
coexistence of heterogeneous prices across sellers and chan-
nels, which we conceptualize as ubiquity. Despite the initial
assumption that electronic markets, being more transparent
and easily accessible, would present lower price dispersion
than traditional markets, existing research has shown that
this is not always the case (Ghose & Yao, 2011; Kiczma-
chowska et al., 2023; Zhuang et al., 2018).
One of the earliest significant contributions to this field
came from Brynjolfsson and Smith ( 2000), who demon -
strated that electronic markets exhibited higher price disper-
sion among retailers, with variations of up to 47%, a pattern
they attributed to the greater incentive online retailers face to
adjust prices more frequently than traditional brick-and-mor-
tar stores. Extending this argument, Clay et al. (2002) argued
that price dispersion in electronic markets extends beyond
the base price, as factoring in additional costs such as ship -
ping tends to amplify dispersion further. Their findings sug-
gested that elevated online price dispersion may also stem
from discrepancies in consumer trust, brand recognition, and
overall awareness, making pricing dynamics in digital mar-
kets more complex than in physical retail. Taken together,
these findings suggest that price variations do not dissipate
with enhanced transparency but may endure over time,
underscoring the temporal condition of price dispersion.
While the prevailing assumption was that price transpar-
ency in digital markets would lead to a single point of conver-
gence, Clay et al. (2002) found that this was not necessarily
the case. Despite the enhancement of comparison mecha -
nisms, digital markets exhibited persistent price differences,
challenging the assumption that greater transparency auto -
matically leads to price uniformity. Lee and Gosain ( 2002)
advanced this discourse by analyzing product-specific price
dispersion, emphasizing the role of product characteristics in
shaping price variability across different markets. Ancarani
and Shankar’s (2004) findings align with the prevailing evi-
dence on the subject. Their research demonstrated that ship-
ping costs have a substantial impact on price dispersion among
online sellers in the Italian market. This finding suggests that
shipping costs may serve as a significant factor contributing
to price variations among online sellers in the Italian market.

the temporal dynamics of price dispersion patterns. Bryn -
jolfsson and Smith ( 2000) and Lee and Gosain ( 2002) had

In the context of market maturity, Li and Tang ( 2011)
examined price dynamics and concluded that online retail

that price dispersion was more pronounced among multi -

Adding to this debate, Kiczmachowska et al. ( 2023)
investigated price differentiation strategies in multichannel

exclusivity. In low-exclusivity categories, such as movies

substantial portion of the empirical evidence pertains to
digital markets, our theoretical framework is designed to
encompass a broader spectrum of contexts, recognizing the
heterogeneity of market frictions and institutional arrange -
ments across offline environments.
Overall, ubiquitous price dispersion coexists across both
online and offline markets, reflecting its spatial nature as
observed in cross-sectional analyses. This price variabil -
ity, even for identical or similar products, is influenced by
key factors. A comprehensive review of the extant literature
identifies four determinants: The following factors must be
considered when analyzing pricing strategies employed by
online retailers: (1) aggressive pricing strategies; (2) con -
sumer awareness and brand trust; (3) additional costs (e.g.,
shipping and handling); and, (4) product characteristics. In
essence, the ubiquitous function serves as a moderating fac-
tor, thereby shaping the relationship between these determi-
nants and the price variability observed across these markets.
Persistent aspect of price dispersion
The temporal aspect is another element outlined in our theo-
retical framework. Consistent with our earlier definition,
persistent price dispersion occurs when “sellers vary their
prices randomly over time so that consumers cannot fully
learn which store is selling at the lowest price” (Lach, 2002,
p. 3). This persistent characteristic is rooted in long-term
patterns and the effects of marketing variables (Dekimpe
& Hanssens, 2024), enduring over time, even when mar -
ket frictions are reduced by other motives (Dinerstein et al.,
2018; Xing et al., 2006).
As shown in Table 2, the temporal persistence of price
dispersion varies across empirical settings (11 to 48 months)
but remains consistently observable over extended periods.
For instance, Lach ( 2002) documents persistent price dis -
persion over a 48-month period, while Baye et al. ( 2006)
observe dispersion dynamics over 18 months, with lev -
els declining from 75% to 30%. Similarly, Wang and Li
(2020) report dispersion levels ranging from 30% to 62%
over a 13-month period. These examples demonstrate that
although price levels may fluctuate, dispersion itself does
not disappear, reinforcing its temporal continuity.
The temporal continuity of price differences underscores
the persistence of such variability, thereby reflecting the
extent to which it endures despite changes in market con -
ditions. Lach’s (2002) analysis of pricing data from Israeli
retailers revealed that, despite variations in store charac -
teristics, price dispersion remained persistent. This finding
indicates that store-specific factors, such as location and
retailer type, alone cannot fully account for price disper -
sion. In accordance with this perspective, strategic product
positioning exerts considerable influence on this dynamic
(Varian, 1980). Retailers who proactively adjust their pric -
ing strategies in response to these factors can enhance their
competitive differentiation, enabling them to charge higher
prices and secure economic gains in the nascent stages of
a product’s lifecycle. Building upon this framework, Xing
et al. ( 2006) observed that price dispersion tends to be
higher at a product’s market entry and gradually decreases
over time. These findings are further reinforced by the sub -
sequent research of Baye et al. ( 2006), who demonstrated
that, even after accounting for retailer heterogeneity, a siz -
able portion (28%) of price dispersion remains unexplained.
This indicates the presence of additional underlying market
forces.
In contrast to the pattern of decreasing price dispersion
over time, Li and Tang ( 2011) documented an increasing
trend among online retailers, attributing it to the heterogene-
ity of online retailers as more brands enter the market, allow-
ing dominant retailers to practice higher prices and reflecting
broader market dynamism and competition. Supporting this
view, Li et al. ( 2023) highlight the role of heterogeneity in
consumer bargaining power, showing that high-experienced
consumers hold stronger bargaining power at high disper -
sion levels, while low-experienced consumers hold stronger
bargaining power at low dispersion levels. Broadening this

Intertemporal price dispersion is typically indicative of
the interplay between supply and demand dynamics, as
well as the pricing strategies employed by sellers over time

sensitivity, and reduce information costs, they do not elimi-

In consideration of transaction prices, Wang and Li ( 2020)
determined that, due to risk aversion in online purchases,
store reputation and product quality are imperative factors
for price dispersion. Therefore, despite the existence of
strategic pricing behavior, sellers exhibit variations in their
capacity to commercialize products. Consequently, experi -

Research propositions on ubiquity and
persistence: Integration of price dispersion
drivers and contextual moderators
The variables employed in the studies presented in Tables 1
and 2 only partially explain price dispersion, with a con -
siderable portion remaining unexplained across both tradi -
tional and electronic markets. Kaplan et al. (2019) show that
decomposition analyses of price variability attribute only
15% of the variation to the location of product sale, leaving
the remaining 85% subject to ongoing scrutiny by economic
and marketing researchers. Economic analyses further iden-
tify three primary sources of price variability: the store, the
transaction, and a combination of both (Kaplan & Menzio,
2015). The framework proposed in Fig. 1 extends beyond
these sources by positioning ubiquity and persistence as
higher-order moderators that condition how each of these
sources affects price variability.
Utilizing established perspectives on theory construction
(e.g., Corley & Gioia, 2011; Whetten, 1989), a framework
is developed that integrates the key constructs and relation-
ships underlying price dispersion. The framework delineates
the determinants of price dispersion (the “What?” condi -
tion) and the boundary conditions under which it evolves
(the “When?” condition) by positioning ubiquity and per -
sistence as distinct contextual moderators. While ubiquity
captures the spatial dimension of price variability (Varian,
1980), persistence reflects its temporal dynamics (Dekimpe
& Hanssens, 2024). Consequently, these moderators collec-
tively influence the way price dispersion manifests across
diverse market contexts.
The interaction between the four dimensions of price
dispersion drivers, namely micro-market conditions, seller,
product, and client characteristics, and the contextual mod -
erators of ubiquity and persistence becomes particularly
evident when considering that price dispersion remains a
persistent and spatially widespread phenomenon despite
advances in transparency, algorithmic pricing, and con -
sumer access to information (Ancarani & Shankar, 2004;
Brynjolfsson & Smith, 2000; Gorodnichenko et al., 2018).
At the micro-market level, ubiquity amplifies heteroge -
neity by exposing consumers to multiple price points for
identical products, even in environments characterized by
high transparency. When search and switching costs remain
uneven, spatial ubiquity emerges naturally from disparate
access to information and consumer inertia (Pan et al., 2004;
Wang et al., 2020). Over time, persistence takes shape as
competitive dynamics stabilize into enduring price dis -
crepancies that reflect structural inertia, asymmetric capa -
bilities, and strategic responses among firms (Dekimpe &
Hanssens, 2024; Kaplan & Menzio, 2015). Together, spatial
distribution and temporal rigidity contribute independently
to the emergence of stable yet uneven price landscapes
across competing retailers.
From a seller perspective, ubiquity accentuates the spa -
tial dispersion produced by diverse pricing and promotional
strategies, such as dynamic pricing, promotional intensity,
and varying degrees of information disclosure (Gensler
et al., 2017), generating visible cross-sectional variability
in consumer price perceptions. In turn, firm-level reputa -
tions, brand trust, and long-term pricing policies reinforce
these disparities over time, transforming short-term fluc -
tuations into structured inter-firm hierarchies (Chellappa et
al., 2011). As a result, sellers’ strategic behaviors are not
isolated events but rather components of enduring pricing
architectures that sustain dispersion across time.
At the level of individual products, ubiquity emerges
when similar or replaceable products exist across various
platforms or channels. This results in simultaneous disper -
sion due to differences in presentation, bundling, or per -
ceived value (Kim et al., 2023). Temporal persistence is
characterized by the sustained presence of these pricing
differences throughout the product lifecycle. These differ -
ences are perpetuated by factors such as technological evo -
lution, product maturity, and quality perceptions (Rossi &
Chintagunta, 2016). In digital marketplaces, these dynam -
ics demonstrate that even systems optimized by algorithms
maintain price diversity rather than eliminating it.
From a client perspective, ubiquity reflects cross-sectional
behavioral heterogeneity, as consumers vary in knowledge,
involvement, and search effort, leading to the simultaneous
observation of diverse prices across markets (André et al.,
2022). Persistence, in turn, is rooted in temporal patterns
in consumer behavior, such as habitual purchasing, price
memory, and fairness perceptions, that sustain differences
in responsiveness to price changes across time and seg -
ments (Kim et al., 2020). As a result, consumer learning and
behavioral inertia underpin long-term dispersion effects that
complement the spatial variability observed across markets.
Ubiquity and persistence independently govern the spa -
tial and temporal boundaries of price dispersion. Ubiquity
captures the instantaneous breadth of dispersion across
actors and channels, while persistence encapsulates its
durability through repeated interactions and organizational
routines. Jointly, their effects determine whether price dis -
persion is primarily transient and competitive, as in ubiqui-
tous contexts, or systematic and structural, as in persistent
ones, embodying stable patterns within evolving markets.
Given their capacity to influence market outcomes, these
constructs provide a robust theoretical foundation for
understanding price variability in omnichannel and digital
contexts.

Final remarks: Contributions and limitations
of the theoretical framework and
implications for marketing managers
This conceptual article examines the drivers of price dis -
persion in both online and offline markets, offering a sys -
tematic classification of the key factors that influence price
variability and assisting firms in addressing both immediate
and long-term pricing challenges. By establishing a theo -
retical framework organized around four categories of vari-
ables drawn from the literature, the study aims to deepen the
understanding of this phenomenon. Price variation remains
closely associated with the costs of obtaining pricing and
product information, and despite reductions in these costs
due to advances in transparency and improved search meth-
ods (Grewal et al., 2003), retailers continue to pursue alter-
native pricing strategies to differentiate themselves from
competitors (Ellison & Ellison, 2009).
We reviewed price dispersion in terms of its ubiquity
(spatial price variation) and persistence (temporal price
variation), building upon the models of geographical and
temporal price dispersion developed by Varian ( 1980).
A comprehensive review of the extant literature revealed
several sources of dispersion, including pricing strategies
(Shankar & Bolton, 2004), market immaturity, or instabil -
ity (Brynjolfsson & Smith, 2000; Lee & Gosain, 2002),
store characteristics (Zhuang et al., 2018), sales taxes, and
shipping costs (Ancarani & Shankar, 2004). Integrating the
ubiquity and persistence dimensions into practical strate -
gies enables marketing managers to design dynamic pricing
mechanisms that reflect both cross-sectional market differ -
ences and temporal price fluctuations.
This article contributes to the literature by systemati -
cally integrating four micro-level dimensions, namely com-
petitive context, seller, product, and client characteristics,
under two higher-order moderators: ubiquity, capturing the
spatial dimension, and persistence, capturing the temporal
dimension. By conceptualizing ubiquity and persistence as
contextual moderators that shape the associations between
micro-level variables and price dispersion, we shift the
focus from isolated explanations, such as consumer search
or seller strategy alone, toward a structure that clarifies
when and where each mechanism exerts its strongest influ -
ence. This integration reconciles conflicting findings from
previous pricing studies and establishes testable contingen -
cies that enrich the existing conceptual framework.
This framework extends pricing theory by providing a
comprehensive perspective on the origins of price disper -
sion. It is widely recognized that the law of one price is not
universally applicable, as many retail markets exhibit signif-
icant price dispersion (Varian, 1980). Price is a multifaceted
concept influenced by various participants in the marketing
value chain, including buyers, sellers, regulators, interme -
diaries, and non-governmental organizations (Hagberg &
Kjellberg, 2015), presenting both challenges and opportuni-
ties for marketing managers.
The proposed framework enables managers to imple -
ment targeted pricing strategies aligned with the dynamic
characteristics of price formation. In practice, effective
price variability management requires a thorough examina-
tion of market dynamics, encompassing competitor pricing,
consumer behavior, and channel-specific trends (André et
al., 2022; Kim et al., 2023). Understanding how micro-
market conditions, seller, product, and client characteristics
influence price dispersion, as conceptualized herein, allows
managers to refine their pricing models based on empiri -
cal evidence rather than arbitrary adjustments. In markets
characterized by high ubiquity, where multiple price points
coexist, visible differentiation tactics such as bundling, prod-
uct versioning, or complementary service offerings tend to
reduce purely price-based competition. In highly persistent
markets, consistent pricing policies, such as loyalty pro -
grams or reputational anchoring, become more effective in
capturing and sustaining margins over time. This diagnostic
perspective allows firms to strategically manage either the
spatial or the temporal dimension of price dispersion.
This article also demonstrates that institutional differ -
ences among sellers, such as dynamic pricing capabilities,

reputation, and consumer trust, are not merely competitive
anomalies. In contexts of high persistence, these character -
istics evolve into strategic sources of sustainable advantage.
Investments in brand trust, post-sale service quality, and
deliberate price transparency function not merely as short-
term costs but as intangible assets that mitigate margin ero-
sion over time.
Conversely, the hypothesis that the electronic market
would be devoid of friction and characterized by perfect
competition has not been substantiated, as price dispersion
appears to be pervasive and persistent, even with minimal
search costs and the ease of price comparisons. This theo -
retical framework underscores the notion that price dis -
persion is driven by factors that extend beyond the scope
of basic transaction costs. These additional factors encom -
pass elements such as search knowledge, consumer risk
perception, the product life cycle, and retailer pricing poli -
cies. In this sense, both online and offline contexts pose
difficulties for researchers and marketers aiming to fully
comprehend and effectively manage this crucial market -
ing mix variable (Bonfrer et al., 2022; Nouri-Harzvili &
Hosseini-Motlagh, 2023).
Given that a considerable portion of the empirical evi -
dence supporting this framework originates from online
and multichannel environments, it is imperative that these
managerial implications be interpreted within the context of
these environments. Nevertheless, the distinction between
online and physical markets is not merely descriptive but
conceptual, as both contexts reveal complementary expres -
sions of the same moderating forces. Digital environments
exemplify the extremes of spatial ubiquity, where prices
adjust rapidly and are visible across multiple channels. By
contrast, physical markets embody temporal persistence,
where pricing rigidities, search costs, and localized com -
petition sustain long-term variability. Consequently, the
moderating logic of ubiquity (spatial variation) and persis -
tence (temporal variation) provides actionable guidance for
managers across both domains, highlighting how pricing
strategies could adapt to levels of transparency, frequency
of adjustment, and market structure.
Subsequent research endeavors may entail the examina -
tion of how managers in online, physical, and multichannel
retail settings formulate pricing strategies that are adapted
to their operational realities. In the digital and multichan -
nel contexts, the presence of real-time pricing flexibility
and cross-channel uniformity has been demonstrated to
exert major influence on the competitive outcomes of busi -
nesses (Thaichon et al., 2022). The integration of artificial
intelligence within pricing strategies enables enterprises to
meticulously monitor market competitors and dynamically
adjust prices (Nouri-Harzvili & Hosseini-Motlagh, 2023). In
traditional retail settings, pricing consistency and perceived
fairness have been identified as key factors influencing con-
sumer responses, particularly in contexts characterized by
reduced transparency and protracted adjustment cycles (Pan
et al., 2004). Integrated pricing systems that align online and
offline channels have been shown to affect consumer trust
and perceived value across contexts (Ghose & Yao, 2011).
Empirical investigation of price dispersion across elec -
tronic and offline markets, including multichannel and
omnichannel environments, remains an important avenue
for future research (Thaichon et al., 2022). Such investiga-
tions would facilitate the testing of the proposed framework
and provide a systematic assessment of price dispersion that
extends beyond prior findings (Chellappa et al., 2011; Zhao
et al., 2015). The availability and consistency of pricing data
continue to constrain empirical analysis (Pan et al., 2004),
and emerging technologies such as AI-driven pricing and
comparison agents offer new opportunities to examine how
price dispersion evolves across markets (Nouri-Harzvili &
Hosseini-Motlagh, 2023). In sum, these avenues position
price dispersion not as a residual market outcome, but as a
structured and evolving phenomenon shaped by technologi-
cal, behavioral, and institutional dynamics.
