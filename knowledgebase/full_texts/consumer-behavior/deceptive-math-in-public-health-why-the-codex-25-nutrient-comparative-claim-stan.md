Abstract
Background: Nutrient comparative claims are globally governed by the Codex Alimentarius standard
(CAC/GL 23-1997), which mandates a minimum 25% relative reduction in macronutrients or energy to
qualify for descriptors such as 'less sugar.' However, this purely relative mathematical metric creates a
critical public health disconnect when applied to baseline products with extreme nutrient densities.
Methods: This perspective paper utilizes a deterministic mathematical modeling approach using a
standard 100 mL carbonated soft drink matrix containing 10.6 g of free sugar as a baseline case study.
We model a legally compliant 25% reduction to 7.95 g/100 mL and evaluate the resulting metabolic
exposure against the absolute daily thresholds established by the World Health Organization (WHO). We
map out the socio-technical, corporate, and structural trade dynamics that complicate global policy
harmonization.
Results: A 25% reduction achieves technical compliance but leaves 19.9 g of free sugar per 250 mL
standard serving. This single exposure exhausts 79.5% of the WHO conditional daily recommendation
(25 g) and 39.7% of the strong daily recommendation (50 g). Consumer behavior literature indicates this
triggers an intense 'healthy halo' effect, biasing consumer choice toward highly dense sugar vehicles.
Corporate pushback focuses heavily on organoleptic degradation, cost recovery freezes, and commercial
speech litigation. Simultaneously, unilateral national deviations from the 25% Codex baseline invite
Technical Barriers to Trade (TBT) challenges under WTO Article 2.2, fragmenting supply chains.
Conclusions & Policy Recommendations: To solve this structural regulatory failure, we propose an
international adoption of a Dual-Gated Claim Framework. To qualify for a comparative label, a product
must clear both a relative gate (25% reduction) AND an absolute cap gate (5 g sugar per 100 mL for
liquids). This must be accompanied by font-size parity rules to eradicate the healthy halo effect and
insulate regulatory frameworks from WTO disputes through transparent public health evidence.
1.0 Background
Non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular pathologies, and dental
caries, represent the leading causes of global mortality. A primary vector driving these epidemics is the
overconsumption of free sugars, predominantly delivered through ultra-processed foods and sugar-
sweetened beverages (SSBs). To mitigate this risk, the World Health Organization (WHO) issued clear
guidelines recommending strongly that free sugars comprise less than 10%, and conditionally less than
5%, of total daily energy intake. This translates to an absolute ceiling of approximately 50 grams and 25
grams of sugar per day, respectively, for an average adult [1].
To empower consumers to make healthier dietary choices, front-of-pack labeling (FOPL) and nutrient
claims have expanded dramatically. The international benchmark governing these labeling parameters
Page 2/8

is the Codex Alimentarius Guidelines for Use of Nutrition and Health Claims (CAC/GL 23-1997) [2]. Under
these global guidelines, nutrient comparative claims are deﬁned as statements that compare the nutrient
levels or energy values of two or more similar foods. The standard speciﬁes that a relative difference of
at least 25% in energy or macronutrient content is mathematically suﬃcient to permit the use of
comparative descriptors such as 'reduced,' 'less,' or 'fewer.'
While this relative mathematical threshold operates cleanly under laboratory conditions and corporate
formulations, it creates a dangerous regulatory loophole when applied to highly nutrient-dense matrices
[3]. When a baseline product contains an excessively high amount of a harmful nutrient—such as a
carbonated soft drink containing over 10 grams of sugar per 100 mL—a 25% relative reduction satisﬁes
the legal standard while continuing to deliver a highly concentrated dose of sugar to the consumer. This
paper provides a rigorous analysis of this loophole, modeling a representative carbonated soft drink
case study, mapping out corporate and legal friction points, and presenting an actionable, legally resilient
structural alternative.
2.0 Method
This paper applies a deterministic, quantitative exposure modeling methodology coupled with a
comparative legal and policy analysis. The empirical core of the study models a standard carbonated
soft drink matrix under the current parameters of Codex CAC/GL 23-1997.
2.1 Mathematical Formulas and Modeling Parameters
Let Cbase represent the baseline concentration of free sugar in a standard commercial formulation,
expressed in grams per 100 mL (g/100 mL). According to global marketplace averages, a typical non-
diet carbonated soft drink contains approximately 10.6 g of sugar per 100 mL [4]. The maximum
permissible concentration of sugar in a product claiming a 'reduced sugar' status (Creduced) under
Codex rules is governed by the following relative formula:
Creduced = Cbase × (1 - Δminimum)
Where Δminimum is the minimum relative reduction threshold deﬁned by Codex, which equals 0.25
(25%) [2]. Substituting our baseline value:
Creduced = 10.6 g × (1–0.25) = 7.95 g / 100 mL
To determine actual biological exposure and public health relevance, we calculate the absolute sugar
mass delivered per standard dietary exposure event. While the regulatory metric is standardized to a
nominal 100 mL, standard single-serving containers (cans or cups) distributed across global markets
average 250 mL (Vserving). The absolute mass of free sugar delivered per consumption event (Msugar)
is calculated via:
Msugar = Creduced × (Vserving / 100)

Msugar = 7.95 g × (250 mL / 100) = 19.875 g (rounded to 19.9 g)
2.2 Policy and Legal Evaluation Framework
We evaluated the output of this exposure model against two core public health and trade baselines: (1)
The WHO conditional and strong dietary thresholds for free sugars, calculating the exact percentage of
daily allowances exhausted by a single serving of the reduced product. (2) The World Trade Organization
(WTO) Technical Barriers to Trade (TBT) Agreement, speciﬁcally examining the jurisprudence of Article
2.2, which regulates how national technical regulations interact with international baselines to prevent
hidden protectionism [5].

3.1 The Consumer Psychology Loophole: The Healthy Halo Effect
The core failure of the 25% relative threshold lies in consumer cognitive processing. In peer-reviewed
behavioral literature, front-of-pack comparative claims frequently trigger a psychological phenomenon
known as the 'healthy halo' effect [3]. When consumers see a prominent label reading '25% Less Sugar'
or 'Reduced Sugar,' their perception of the product's overall healthfulness is biased upward [8]. This
creates an implicit, incorrect assumption that the product is inherently safe or nutritious.
Our model demonstrates that this cognitive bias directly endangers public health. A single 250 mL
serving of the reformulated beverage delivers 39.8% and 79.6% of the World Health Organization’s
strong (50 g) and conditional daily free sugar allowance (25 g) respectively. Because the label signals a
reduction, consumers are highly likely to increase their portion sizes or frequency of consumption,
overcompensating for the modest calorie drop and completely neutralizing the intended public health

beneﬁt [3]. The relative claim, in effect, functions as an ongoing marketing optimization tool for a highly
toxic, nutrient-poor matrix.
3.2 Food Industry Pushback: Corporate Concerns and Structural Arguments
Any international or domestic effort to overhaul the 25% comparative claim standard faces aggressive,
sophisticated pushback from the multinational food and beverage sector. This pushback manifests
across three primary arguments:
1. Organoleptic Barriers and Technological Constraints: Industry technical groups argue that sugar is not
merely a sweetener but an essential structural component in carbonated liquids. It modulates viscosity,
mouthfeel, ﬂavor release, and carbonation stability [8]. A reduction beyond 25% without heavy reliance on
synthetic or artiﬁcial non-nutritive sweeteners (NNS) drastically degrades product palatability, driving
consumers back to un-reformulated baseline variants. Furthermore, the long-term metabolic impacts of
massive population-wide exposure to non-nutritive artiﬁcial sweeteners remain highly controversial
among epidemiological researchers.
2. R&D; Capital Investment and Innovation Freezes: Reformulating a core product line requires millions of
dollars in corporate research, supply chain modiﬁcations, and consumer testing [9]. Industry actors state
that front-of-pack claims are the primary economic incentive driving these investments. If regulators
eliminate the ability to proudly market a 25% or 30% reduction because the absolute sugar value is still
too high, corporate boards will simply freeze reformulation budgets. They argue that without a
marketable commercial incentive, it is economically rational to leave the baseline high-sugar formulation
unchanged, thereby stagnating nutritional advancement.
3. Infringement on Commercial Speech Rights: In many highly developed legal jurisdictions, product
labeling is protected under commercial free speech doctrines (such as the First Amendment in the
United States) [10]. Corporate legal counsels argue that if a product does, in fact, contain 25% less sugar
than its predecessor, a regulatory ban on printing that exact statement constitutes an unconstitutional
suppression of truthful, scientiﬁcally veriﬁable factual information. This threatens protracted
constitutional litigation.
3.2 Implications for International Trade: WTO TBT and Border Friction
The friction generated by the Codex standard extends far beyond domestic consumer safety, impacting
the mechanics of international trade. Under the World Trade Organization's (WTO) Technical Barriers to
Trade (TBT) Agreement, international standards—speciﬁcally Codex Alimentarius regulations—are
presumed to be baseline requirements that do not create unnecessary obstacles to commerce [11].
If an individual member nation recognizes the dangerous loophole identiﬁed in our model and unilaterally
enacts laws banning 'reduced sugar' claims on items exceeding an absolute limit (e.g., 5g/100mL), that
nation creates an immediate technical barrier. Exporter countries whose manufacturers rely strictly on
the 25% Codex rule will challenge the domestic restriction under WTO TBT Article 2.2 [5]. They will argue

that the sovereign country's law is 'more trade-restrictive than necessary' to achieve its legitimate public
health objective, asserting that Codex compliance should serve as safe harbor.
This legal friction results in severe supply chain segregation. Multinational corporations are forced to
abandon integrated economies of scale, splitting their production lines to print highly customized
packaging and labels tailored to the legal quirks of speciﬁc borders [12]. For developing countries
dependent on importing affordable goods, this regulatory fragmentation drives up domestic food prices,
triggers border rejections, and causes localized supply shocks.
4.0 Conclusion and Policy Recommendation
The current Codex Alimentarius 25% relative reduction paradigm for nutrient comparative claims is
fundamentally obsolete. It operates in an isolated mathematical vacuum, completely detached from
global biological realities and established WHO physiological limits. Allowing a carbonated soft drink to
boast a front-of-pack wellness claim while transmitting 19.9 grams of sugar per serving actively
undermines global NCD prevention policies. To resolve this structural failure, we outline a clear
regulatory roadmap below:
4.1 The Dual-Gated Claim Framework
National regulatory agencies, coordinated through future revisions of Codex CAC/GL 23-1997, must
transition to a Dual-Gated Claim Framework. Under this structural model, a food product is prohibited
from displaying any relative comparative claim unless it successfully passes two independent regulatory
checkpoints:
Gate 1 (The Relative Metric)
The product must achieve a minimum relative reduction of 25% against its true market baseline,
preserving an incentive for marginal formulation improvements.
Gate 2 (The Absolute Ceiling Metric)
The post-reduction product must possess an absolute nutrient concentration below a scientiﬁcally
determined public health ceiling. For liquid matrices, we recommend an absolute cap of ≤ 5.0 grams of
free sugar per 100 mL.
If a product passes Gate 1 but fails Gate 2—as demonstrated by our 7.95 g/100 mL soft drink case study
—the product is legally barred from using any competitive words such as 'reduced,' 'less,' 'lower,' or 'light.'
It must maintain standard labeling without any preferential marketing incentives.
4.2 Font-Size Parity and Explicit Baseline Disclosures
To neutralize the healthy halo effect completely, any product that successfully clears the Dual-Gated
Framework must be subjected to absolute font-size parity mandates. The promotional claim text (e.g.,
Page 6/8

'REDUCED SUGAR') must not exceed the font size, weight, or visual prominence of the exact
mathematical values of the transition. The label must explicitly state: 'Reduced from 10.6 g to 7.95 g per
100 mL' immediately adjacent to the claim, in identical typography. This ensures the consumer's
cognitive anchoring is rooted in objective mass exposure rather than qualitative promotional
abstractions.
4.3 WTO and Codex Strategy for Sovereign Nations
To insulate these vital domestic health policies from destructive WTO litigation, countries must build
robust, peer-reviewed evidence repositories linking the dual-gated restrictions directly to consumer
behavioral science. Under WTO jurisprudence, trade restrictions are validated if a state proves that less
trade-restrictive measures (like simple education campaigns) are completely ineffective at countering
the healthy halo effect. Simultaneously, public health coalitions must aggressively submit formal
discussion papers to the Codex Committee on Food Labelling (CCFL) to update CAC/GL 23-1997
internationally, converting a localized defense into a global standard.
