Abstract
Research on  solar PV (SPV) water pumping systems has been co nducted across several fields,
including engineering, agricultural economics, consumer behavior, and policy research. This makes it
possible for the same factors affecting adoptions to be described differently and investigated using
various methods. This review aims to collate 119 studies published from 1990 to 2026, synthesize the
factors associated with SWP adoption, review how SWP adoption has been investigated , and pinpoint
existing gaps. A theoretical integrative  review with thematic analysis was used.  The studies were
analyzed at the factor level: 595 factor observations were collapsed into 46 factors, grouped into nine
themes. Fifty studies were selected that were directly related to behavioral outcomes, such as
adoption, adoption intention, purchase intention, and willingness to pay for a solar pumping system .
The remaining studies, which examined the adoption and behavioral outcomes of other solar
photovoltaic (SPV) and renewable energy technologies, were used as supporting evidence to
understand the  broader technical, economic, institutional, and contextual conditions shaping  solar
pumping adoption. The synthesis indicates  that the literature places the greatest emphasis on
economic considerations. The initial cost is seen as a barrier  to adoption, w hereas government
subsidies are viewed as an enabler. However, the evidence is weaker for other funding methods, such
as credit and loans. Another key difference in the review is that awareness research generally focuses
on solar technology, whereas there is much less work on awareness of specific schemes, subsidies ,
and application processes. Even after considering their relevance to the utilization and ongoing
functionality of solar pumps, the market and service conditions, such as availability of spare p arts,
repair services , and after -sales services,  remain comparatively unexplored. The methodological
synthesis also confirms that in the majority of studies , there is no theoretical approach, and that in the
relatively few studies with explicit theory, the  theories vary widely. This paper introduces a more
precise categorization of the economic dimension into cost burden, investment return , and financial
access/affordability, based on these results. The review results will create a coherent evidence base for
future empirical studies and a more structured base for the conceptual models to be developed for
research into solar water pump adoption.

1. Introduction
In India, groundwater irrigation has relied on cheap electricity, and in many places, it uses diesel -
driven pumps. This places a double burden on the cost of agricultural electricity, with some of this
cost borne by state distribution utilities, and the continued pumping of groundwater at the same time
adds to the stress on already -strained groundwater supplies. Therefore, solar PV (SPV) water
pumping systems have been advocated as an alternative  to eliminate reliance on grid electricity and
diesel and to minimize irrigation costs. In March 2019, the Government of India launched the PM -
KUSUM scheme , which aims to deploy 1 .75 million standalone solar agricultural  pumps. But the
growth of solar pumping has not been as rapid as expected. Solar pump usage remains limited even in
most states with a common central subsidy framework, as reported by Narayanmoorthy et al. (202 6),
who examined the adoption of solar pumps in 17  major states. Results from sepa rate research also
demonstrate that financial assistance cannot eliminate all adoption barriers. However, in three talukas
of Nanded, More et al. (2024) observed that 60% of PM -KUSUM beneficiaries experienced delays in
application processing, and 35.83% desired an increase in the PM -KUSUM subsidy even after
receiving it. Srivastava et al. (2025) reported that awareness of the application process was associated
with adoption, and that post-installation challenges still existed in maintaining the panels. Meanwhile,
Nandhini et al. (2023) , in their study of the Villupuram district with 90 farmers, concluded that the
availability of free electricity for pump motors was the primary reason farmers continued to use
electric pumps. This study highlights an important  question: Solar pump adoption depends on more
than just technology and subsidy. Farmers' willingness to adopt a technology will depend on the
economic, institutional, informational, and local factors in which they operate . This is especially
significant in states where electricity for agriculture is delivered to farmers at or near subsidized rates.
Even in cases of high groundwater stress, the financial benefit of a solar pump might not be clear .
Meanwhile, farmers may have other issues with the investment , subsidy processes, technical
maintenance, reliability, awareness, institutional support, and repair and service facilities. The
literature thus shows that solar pump uptake is not a purely “financial incentive” problem: it is a
multi-dimensional problem.  It is not easy to capture this multidimensionality in a summary of the
findings of separate studies. A systematic search of the literature can be useful for identifying
constructs and dimensions that recur consistently across studies, even when they are r eferred to by
different terms or conceptualized differently . It can also serve as a basis for conceptual frameworks ,
variable selection, and instrument design for larger empirical studies. For instance,
A literature review can  be used to develop and defin e dimensions before questionnaire development
and empirical testing take place.  An initial literature review, often integrated with preliminary
stakeholder perspectives, can be used to develop a conceptual model of key determinants of adoption,
as demonstrated by Agrawal & Jain (2019). Following the same reasoning, this review paper aims to
analyze the adoption factors influencing solar water pump installations, as the current evidence and
perspectives on these factors are  dispersed across disciplines and r esearch traditions. The literature
pertaining to solar technology is indeed extensive and disparate. Technical performance, life -cycle
costs, and economic feasibility under specific subsidy conditions are often emphasized in engineering
and energy studies.  Studies in agricultural economics typically analyze observed adoption using
econometric models such as logit or probit, whereas those in the fields of behavior or management
may focus on  adoption intention, perceived usefulness, social influence, or attit ude. Qualitative
studies, on the other hand, help to understand farmers’ experiences, institutional obstacles, and
implementation issues. These methods provide a range of perspectives, and results from one tradition
often do not align with those  from another. In their Q -sorting with 22 Indian stakeholders, Srinivasa
and Mithöfer (2024) discovered that there were two viewpoints, one economic/financial and the other

One challenge is that of terminology. Many of the same factors are referred to in different ways in
different studies. Examples of government finan cial support can include “subsidy”, “government
assistance”, “capital subsidy”, “financial incentives”, “government initiatives”, “beneficial product
scheme,” or “investment incentives”. Also, the economic advantage due to the reduction in running
costs ca n be called “diesel saving”, “electricity saving”, “running cost saving”, “operational cost
saving”, “cost-effectiveness”, or “energy bill saving”.  If these terms are simply counted as separate
factors, the resulting picture may reflect differences in the authors’ vocabulary rather than genuine
differences in the underlying determinants of adoption.  This makes factor synthesis an important part
of understanding the literature. This review , therefore, treats the 119 studies as a body of qualitative
evidence rather than as 119 separate summaries. The analysis proceeds by coding the studies at the
factor level, standardising different terms that refer to similar concepts, grouping the resulting factors
into broader themes and sub -themes. It also considers how t hese factors have been investigated,
which research methods and adoption measures have been used, and which theories and conceptual
models have guided previous research. Particular attention is given to financial incentives, social
influence, policy , and i nstitutional support because these areas are closely connected with the
practical decision to adopt solar water pumping systems. Accordingly, this review is organized around
three broad areas: the factors examined in previous research, the methods used to study adoption, and
the theoretical perspectives used to explain it. The review seeks to answer the following questions:
RQ1. What factors influence the adoption of solar water pumping systems in agriculture?
RQ2. How can factors reported under different n ames be synthesized into common themes and sub -
themes?
RQ3. What research designs, samples, sampling approaches, methods, analytical tools , and adoption
measures have been used in previous studies?
RQ4. What theories and conceptual models have been used to explain solar water pump adoption?
RQ5. What is the direction and consistency of evidence regarding the major factors influencing
adoption?
2. Review Approach and Analytical Procedure
2.1 Review approach
The present study adopts an integrative literature review with thematic synthesis. The purpose is not
to provide a paper -by-paper summary, but to bring together the existing evidence on solar water
pumping and related research on renewable -energy adoption, and to develop a consolidated
understanding of the  factors, research approaches , and theoretical perspectives that have emerged
from the literature. An integrative review is appropriate for this purpose because the available studies
differ considerably in their research designs, methods, outcomes, context s, and conceptual
approaches. The review is based on a corpus of 119 studies published between 1990 and 2026.
The corpus was compiled with a systematic literature search and some relevant literature on solar
irrigation adoption, farmers' technology adopti on, and acceptance of renewable energy. It is not the
aim of the review to cover all the published studies. Instead, the 119 studies comprise a wide range of
similar and dissimilar studies, enabling commonalities to be identified, research methods to be
compared, and the evidence to be synthesized. Of the 119 studies, 50 are directly related to solar

2.2 Analytical procedure
Qualitative coding, text analysis, and thematic synthesis were performed, and manual reading and
verification were performed. The research context, the technology or adoption problem, the sample,
the research design, the data collection method, the analytical method, the theoretical perspective, the
adoption outcome, and the factors analyzed were examined for each study. Financial incentive,
awareness, social influence, institutional support, policy, technological characteristics, economi c
benefits, and other factors affecting farmers' decisions on solar water pumping were given particular
attention.
The analysis was performed at the factor level rather than at the study level. This was necessary
because the same underlying factor can be used in a number of alternate ways across different studies.
For example, the terms subsidy, government assistance, capital subsidy, financial incentives, and
government support may refer to the same or similar forms of financial and institutional support. The
economic advantages of diesel and electricity savings can be the same, as can the advantage of
reduced running and operational costs. The terms were therefore analyzed and grouped together under
common concepts. During coding, the authors' own terms we re preserved for each synthesized factor
to allow tracking of each to its specific studies of origin. Themes and sub -themes were derived from
similar factors after coding the initial codes. The process made it possible to move beyond finding
results and fo cus on a more integrated picture of the factors and determinants of solar water pump
adoption. Synthesis also identified whether factors were cited as positive, negative, insignificant,
mixed, or descriptive observations. If the study did not examine the r elationship between the factor
and adoption, the factor did not have a significant influence. This review not only covered factor
synthesis but also literature search on the research methods and theories. Research design, sampling
method, sample size, data collection, and analysis methods were compared to identify patterns across
methods. Theories and conceptual models used to explain adoption were also analyzed to understand
the underlying theories of existing studies and to assess the under -researched are a of adoption
research.
2.3 Use of AI-assisted analytical tools
This review was facilitated by two artificial intelligence tools, Google Notebook LM  and Claude
(Anthropic), as well as manual reading, coding , and verification. The division of work between t he
tools and the researcher  was continued to maximize efficiency and to preserve the researcher's
judgment. Notebook LM was used to search the collected literature, identify themes within clusters of
studies, compare recurring themes, and locate passages i n the texts that support specific constructs.

This method is part of the general shift toward the human -in-the-loop paradigm in the methodological
literature on AI (Wagner et al., 2026; Malik & Terzidis, 2026). AI-supported literature analysis is also
supported by e mpirical assessments. For instance, in the study by Khraisha et al. (2024), GPT -4's
screening and extraction accuracy, after adjusting for chance agreement and dataset imbalance,
dropped to moderate or even very small levels. When comparing NotebookLM, Gem ini, and a related
tool with a fully processed manual review  (Lucio et al., 2025),  these tools only identified some
themes identified by hand and generated themes that were inaccurate or unsupported by the content of
the source documents. Likewise, Shor et  al. (2025) stated that the value of NotebookLM for literature
review relies greatly on the quality of the uploaded corpus and the researcher's skill in crafting
suitable prompts. Similarly, in a review of 21 tools developed to assist with literature revie ws,
Bolaños et al. (2024) found that these tools are better viewed as semi -automated rather than fully
automated. Accordingly, AI tools in the present study were used primarily to support organization,
comparison, and preliminary thematic identification, while the final decisions regarding coding, factor
consolidation, interpretation, and reporting remained with the researcher.
2.4 Synthesis of factors
The factor synthesis followed a three -stage process. Firstly, the factors reported in each study were
noted as the term used by the original authors. Secondly, terms referring to the same conceptually
related factors were compared and grouped under standardized factor labels. Third, the factors were
classified into themes and sub -themes in terms of their conce ptual meaning and the influence they
have in the adoption decision. For example, some terms considered related to government financial
support were analyzed, including subsidy, government assistance, capital subsidy, and financial
incentive. Likewise, the savings in diesel, electricity, and running and operational costs were explored
as manifestations of the economic value of lower operating costs. This way, differences in
terminology are not conflated with underlying determinants of adoption. The synthesis  also separated
factors that were tested from those described or discussed by the authors but not tested. Any factor
cited frequently in the literature was not automatically taken to indicate that it was a factor in the
study. The evidence was interpreted in the context of the reported relationship, the manner in which it
was investigated, and the first study's context.
2.5 Developing research methods and theories
 In addition to factor synthesis, the review examined studies on the adoption of solar water pumps.
The methodological analysis included the research design, sample characteristics, sampling method,
data collection method, and analysis method. The reviewed studies include quantitative, qualitative,
and mixed-methods studies, as well as analytical methods such as descriptive analysis, econometric
analysis, regression analysis, structural equation modeling, factor analysis, Q methodology, technical
and economic feasibility analysis, a case -based approach, and qualitative analysis. The theoretical
analysis focused on the theories and conceptual models pertaining to adoption. They include
behavioral, economic, institutional, and technology -acceptance viewpoints. The aim was not only to
identify the theories that have been applied, but also to establish which parts of the adoption process

2.6 Interpretation of the evidence
The review is mainly a qualitative synthesis, rather than a statistical pooling of effect sizes. So, the
number of studies that have investigated a factor does not reveal the magnitude of the effect, but the
amount of attenti on that the factor receives in the research literature. Likewise, results from various
studies using different samples, research designs, and geographic settings are not considered directly
comparable. Where studies report a factor as an enabler of adoptio n, it is interpreted as positive
evidence; where a factor is identified as a barrier, it is interpreted as negative evidence. These factors
were evaluated but not found to be significantly related to the study; hence, they are not included
separately with those factors that were not examined. In this case, the evidence is not presented as one
if there is a divergence across studies or contexts. This helps the review go beyond simply listing the
number of studies, and consider the factors studied, how they w ere defined and measured, what the
evidence indicates, and areas of uncertainty.
 2.7 Scope and limitations
The 119 studies refer to various geographical and research contexts , with a significant number of
studies from India and other South Asian countries . This distribution is based on the geographical
distribution of the selected literature and contexts included in the review. The conclusions are thus a
synthesis of the literature selected and should not be interpreted as representing all global studies o n
the adoption of solar WPs. Additionally, the review does not give a formal quality score or statistical
weight to individual studies. Instead, methodological characteristics, sample sizes, research designs ,
and reported findings are presented to enable interpretation of the evidence in context.

4. Factor Synthesis: Process and Coding Procedure
The first step in the current review was to standardize the terminology before counting and comparin g
the factors reported across the different studies. This is necessary because various researchers employ
different words to mean the same thing. For instance, 31 studies were found for the category defined
in this review as Government Subsidy Policies, bu t these studies used 27 different terms, such as
“subsidies,” “capital subsidy,” “government subsidy,” “financial incentives,” “government
initiatives,” “government incentives,” “beneficial product scheme,” “investment incentives,” “subsidy
reform,” “incen tive-based pricing,” “subsidy threshold,” “subsidy rate,” “lack of incentives” and
“demand for increased subsidy.” These terms, if treated on a case -by-case basis, would give the
impression that government subsidies are less important than they actually ar e, since differences in
terms would be interpreted as differences in the underlying factor. After standardization, government
subsidy policies emerge as the second-most-frequently examined factor in the reviewed literature. The
same problem arises in resea rch papers discussing the savings to be enjoyed from solar pumping.
Researchers have employed a variety of terms , including ‘Diesel saving', ‘Reduced running cost',
‘Operational cost saving', ‘Lower lifecycle cost than diesel', ‘Reduction in energy bill', ‘Cost-
effectiveness', and ‘Unit cost below diesel & grid. While there are various versions of these terms,
their common denominator is the economic return from the irrigation water that can be reused for the
same purpose. Therefore, they were both included in the factor synthesis.
Some concepts, however, were deliberately kept separate because combining them would remove an
important distinction. Technology Awareness was distinguished from Scheme and Subsidy
Awareness. Knowing that solar pumps are available  is different from knowing about a particular
government scheme, its subsidy provisions , or the procedure for obtaining financial support. These
forms of knowledge may also come from different sources and have different implications for
adoption. This was reflected in the literature, with 24 studies focusing on technology awareness and 6
studies on scheme or subsidy awareness. Likewise, Credit and Loan Availability were not included in
Government Subsidy Policies. Both can alleviate farmers' financial problems, but in different ways. A
subsidy directly lowers the amount the farmer has to invest, while a loan gives him access to money

4.1 Themes and sub-themes
The 46 synthesized factors that emerged from the reviewed studies were grouped into nine broad
themes. For most themes, it was possib le to put their factors forward under their sub -themes because
these factors were conceptually the same. The Economic and Financial theme, however, needed a
second-order grouping, as the factors in the theme reflect various aspects of the farmer's economic
decision. The economic factors do not all refer to the same type of consideration. Some are associated
with the expenses of acquiring and operating the system, some with the economic benefits or returns
to be gained from the system, and others with financ ing the investment. The differences between
these are important and could be lost if they are just recorded as a list. This distinction is especially
important in the context of the larger research, because money is at the heart of the business.
Accordingly, the Economic and Financial theme was divided into three groups:
 Cost Burden includes the economic expense for the farmer in purchasing the system and how
much their financial situation impacts the purchase. This will contain the Initial Investment Cost,
Price, and Cost Sensitivity.
 Economic and financial benefits include economic benefits realized from the utilization of the
system. It includes Operational Cost Savings, Investment Return, Profitability, and Income Benefit.
Operational cost savings are pr imarily about diesel or electricity costs, and investment returns and
profitability are the financial benefits that can be realized over time. Financial access and affordability
relate to the technologies' financing mechanisms and their affordability for f armers. It contains
Government Subsidy Policies, Credit and Loan Availability, Affordability, and Income Capacity.
 This set-up enables the review to separate the cost of the technology itself from the returns
that can be harvested as a result of its applic ation, as well as the mechanisms that make the initial
investment financially available. The other eight themes are followed by their sub -themes, which are
not further grouped.

6.1 Overall Pattern of the Synthesised Factors
The factor synthesis reveals that the literature is focused primarily on economic, technological and
institutional explanations of the adoption of solar water pumps. Most of the studies are about

environmental concern, reliability and performance, technical suitability a nd system design, and
perceived usefulness and relative advantage (20 studies each). The major topics of interest are
identified in the literature. The economic factors reveal a fairly obvious trend. Initial Investment Cost
is typically classified as a bar rier while Operational Cost Saving, Investment Return and Profitability
and Income Benefit are mostly classified as positive influences in adoption. Government Subsidy
Policies indicate a predominantly positive trend. Taken together, these findings imply t hat farmers'
cost-benefit analysis of solar pumping considers not only the costs of the pump system but also the
potential income gains  from incorporating the technology. This pattern is further explained through

6.3 Direction and Consistency of the Evidence
The frequency with which a factor is studied does not necessarily indicate w hether the evidence for
that factor is consistent. Table 11 thus accounts for the incidence of tests and the trend in  the results.
This makes it easier to identify factors that are consistently supported by studies from those where
there is some variation between studies or contexts. One of the most evident patterns in the literature
is indicated in the Initial Investment Cost. 29 out of 35 studies focused on this issue conclude that
high initial cost is a barrier to adoption. The finding is echoed in other  areas and research methods,
showing that the initial cost is a constant need for potential adopters. Patterns for Government
Subsidy Policies are also very positive . 29/31 studies indicate a positive relationship with adoption or
adoption-related outcome.  However, Tate et al. (2012) showed that government renewable -energy
initiatives were not a major driver of adoption among farmers in the West Midlands, where cognitive
and educational factors were  predominant. This implies that the role of subsidy might b e partly
context-dependent, given economic and institutional circumstances. Another relatively consistent
pattern is Grid and Infrastructure Policy. All 12 studies investigating this link found a positive
correlation between infrastructure and solar energy  uptake, often  because poor grid electricity

association between electricity tariff rates and solar pump adoption for 17 big Indian states.

consistent evidence base. The positive result is reported by 19 studies, the non -significant result is

The dominance of cross -sectional survey research has provided useful evidence on associations
among factors such as cost, perceived usefulness, attitude, aw areness, and intention to adopt .
However, most of these studies observe respondents at a single point in time. Only a small number of
studies use designs that are better suited to examining effects, including Ali et al. (2016), Sunny et al.
(2022), Negera et al. (2025) , and Peer et al. (2026). Thus, findings reported as influences or effects
should generally be interpreted as associations unless the study design provides stronger evidence.
A further limitation is the lack of longitudinal evidence. The revie wed studies generally examine
adoption, intention , or post -adoption conditions at one point in time rather than following farmers
through the stages of awareness, application, approval, installation,  and subsequent use. This limit
understanding of how fact ors may change during the adoption process. For example, administrative
delays reported by More et al. (2024) and repair delays reported by Varshney et al. (2025) could have
different implications at different stages of the farmer's experience with the technology.
The literature also contains relatively few studies examining mediation or moderation. Only five
studies, Rezaei and Ghofranfarid (2018), Irfan et al. (2020), Khalid et al. (2021), Ariffin et al. (2022),
and Lussanje et al. (2025), test such conditional relationships, and none examines a financial incentive
as a moderator. This is relevant because the effect of a subsidy may reasonably vary depending on
farmers' awareness, affordability, access to credit, or ability to complete the application process.
Another useful methodological development is the use of stated -preference and discrete -choice
approaches, although these remain uncommon. Korekallu Srinivasa et al. (2025) and Gbodji et al.
(2026) asked farmers to evaluate alternative policy and tech nology attributes. Such approaches allow
researchers to examine trade -offs among different forms of financial and institutional support rather
than treating each factor in isolation.
Methodological reporting also varies across the literature. As noted ear lier, 3 2 of the 87 primary
studies do not clearly report how respondents were selected. The methodological evidence , therefore,
suggests a need for greater transparency in sampling, measurement , and analytical procedures,
particularly in behavioral adoption studies.
The methodological literature provides a useful foundation  however remains largely cross -sectional
and quantitative. Greater use of longitudinal designs, mixed methods, qualitative inquiry, stated -
preference approaches , and models examining medi ation or moderation could provide a more
complete understanding of how farmers move from awareness to adoption and continued use.

8. Theoretical and Conceptual Gaps
The methodological findings are closely connected with the theoretical gaps in the literatu re.
Although several behavioral theories have been applied, existing frameworks do not fully capture the
specific conditions under which farmers adopt a subsidized solar water-pumping system.
The first gap concerns the limited use of explicit theoretical f rameworks. This is understandable for
many technical and economic studies, where behavioral theory may not be necessary. However, for
survey-based adoption research, an explicit theoretical basis can help explain why particular
constructs are selected and how they are expected to relate to adoption.  Among the studies using
theory, the Theory of Planned Behavior, Technology Acceptance Model, Diffusion of Innovations,
and UTAUT are the most frequently used. These frameworks provide useful explanations of atti tude,
perceived usefulness, social influence, behavioral control, and facilitating conditions. However, they
provide less direct explanations of government subsidies and other financial incentives, which are
particularly important in the context of solar irrigation.
A second gap is therefore the limited conceptualization of financial incentives. Thirty-one studies are
considered and generally find that government subsidy policies have a positive effect on adoption
outcomes. But financial incentives are ofte n considered in isolation rather than assessed through the
different mechanisms by which they could reduce initial investment costs, make investment more
affordable, boost returns, or affect access to credit. The techno -economic literature also suggests th at
the subsidy effect can vary with the amount of subsidy. For instance, Paudel et al. (2025) determined
the economic viability threshold for subsidies, while Powell et al. (2021) examined the deployment
and subsidies for solar irrigation . Yet another conc eptual disconnect is the need for awareness and
information. There are different types of awareness technology and scheme awareness, but they are
treated as part of a larger awareness construct. Fourteen of the 24 studies reviewed discussed
technology awareness, while 6 of the 44 studies reviewed discussed scheme and subsidy awareness.
Less attention has been given to the financial -benefit awareness and knowledge of application
procedures. This is significant, as a farmer might know about a solar pump but m ay not be aware of
the details of a government scheme. Limited integration of institutional conditions in behavioral
adoption models is also found in the literature. Administrative efficiency, scheme implementation and
extension support, and service delive ry are mentioned in the empirical literature, but institutional
perspectives are seldom included in the main adoption frameworks. There is no representation of
institutional theory or the technology -organization environment in the reviewed corpus, and
facilitating conditions in UTAUT offer only part of an explanation of the institutional environment.
This is remarkable as procedural and administrative efficiency is cited as an impediment in most
studies focused on it, and institutional and governance issues  were also identified in the stakeholder
analysis by Srinivasa  & Mithöfer (2024). Another area of weakness is the limited link between
adoption and post -adoption experience. Most adoption studies focus on whether farmers will adopt,
while other studies exa mine satisfaction, maintenance, service support, and system performance after
adoption. The gap in post-adoption service delivery in solar irrigation—typified by the 110-day repair
delays in Nepal (Varshney et al., 2025) and panel malfunctions in Maharasht ra (More et al., 2024) —
highlights the need for a more robust conceptualization of service quality and satisfaction in green
technologies. As established by Tamanna (2023), service quality and customer satisfaction are
multidimensional constructs that can b e conceptualized in terms of  end-user expectations and
qualitative experiences. Applying such integrated qualitative -empirical frameworks to the solar pump
value chain is essential to transition from mere technology installation to long -term user satisfact ion
and sustainability. Peer information and demonstration effects also play a role in how existing users'
experiences influence prospective adopters, as evidenced by Varshney et al. (2025), Korekallu

9. Conclusion
This review compiled 119 studies to summarize knowledge about solar wa ter pump adoption and
focused its analysis on the factors contributing to adoption, how they have been examined, and the
theories explaining adoption. The literature was not viewed as a series of stand -alone studies but
rather was coded at the factor level , and terms that share similar underlying concepts were grouped
together. This process led to 595 observations of factors, which were captured and codified into 46
factors grouped under nine thematic headings. The economic, technological, and institutional  factors
associated with the adoption of solar water pumps are well and consistently treated in the literature.
Cost burden, investment return, and financing and affordability are the most cited constraints and
benefits for adoption; these are grouped toge ther so that these factors are easier to read in the pattern
than if they were presented in an undifferentiated list, as initial investment cost is mostly cited as a
constraint, while the other factors are mostly cited as benefits. Other well -represented a reas of
investigation are technological performance, perceived usefulness, government support, and
awareness. In contrast, the issues of service infrastructure, administrative procedures, alternative
financing strategies (e.g., credit), and certain social issues have been relatively under -researched,
despite post-adoption evidence indicating their importance. The methodological review reveals that
most of the literature is cross -sectional and quantitative, employing structural equation modeling,
regression, and techno-economic analysis. Likewise, 3 2 of the 87 primary studies report sampling in
an unclear manner. Longitudinal designs, qualitative approaches, stated -preference methods , and
studies examining conditional relationships remain limited; only four s tudies use a design capable of
supporting a causal claim, and only five test mediation or moderation.
The theoretical review similarly indicates that existing adoption frameworks explain attitudes,
perceived usefulness, social influence , and behavioral con trol reasonably well, but do not fully
capture the role of financial incentives and institutional processes in the adoption of subsidized
agricultural technology. Sixty -eight per cent of studies use no explicit theoretical framework.
Awareness, too, is com monly treated as a single broad construct even though technology awareness,
scheme awareness , and procedural knowledge appear to carry different implications for adoption.
Taken together, these findings point towards a need for a more context -specific and more conditional
understanding of solar water pump adoption: not simply whether a factor matters, but for whom,
under what institutional conditions, and at what stage of the awareness -to-adoption process. Future
research can build on the well -established e conomic and behavioral evidence while paying greater
attention to how financial incentives, awareness, institutional support, and service delivery interact .
The findings of this review provide a conceptual and methodological foundation for future empirical
research on solar water pump adoption. The identified gaps, in particular the limited disaggregation of
awareness and the largely untested assumption that financial incentives operate independently of it,
support the development of a framework that treats  these dimensions as related rather than separate,
and provides a clearer basis for constructing measurable variables for empirical testing in the context
of solar water pump adoption.
