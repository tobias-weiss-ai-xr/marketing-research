Abstract
As an anti -consumption or counter movement against influencer driven consumption, d e-influencing aims to
discourage consumers from over-consuming, making unnecessary purchases, or making misaligned choices. The
contents of de-influencing started to become more visible across digital platforms, and understanding consumers’
responses in terms of purchasing intention after being exposed to any type of d e-influencing content became an
important topic. In this perspective, research focuses on determining whether d e-influenced purchase intention
aligns with consumers’ demographic similarities and the platforms on which they encounter de -influencing
content. The research employed quantitative design and used surveys to collect data, utilizing purposive sampling
that targeted only the consumers who had previously been exposed to de-influencing contents. The survey included
demographics of participants, platform exposure of d e-influencing, influencer and brand recall, and purchase
intention scale. After the cleaning process, a total of 283 data were used in further analysis. To assess alignment
of purchase intention according to demographics and platforms, the Multiple Regression Qua dratic Assignment
Procedure (MRQAP) is tested with UCINET. Research data were transformed into square matrices representing
similarities, and findings indicated that specifically platform similarities significantly predict similarity in d e-
influenced purchase intention and have a higher coefficient value compared to demographics. Additionally, at
group level, mean differences revealed that younger participants, the lowest-income group, students, and users of
Instagram or TikTok showed stronger an d similar de -influenced purchase intention. Finally, this study offers a
unique perspective to consumer behavior research by framing d e-influenced purchase intention as a relational
pattern rather than isolated outcome.

INTRODUCTION
The u sual digital content streamed by influencers for any type of consumer -brand interaction
generally stays on the side of prompting brand and might be seen as PR work rather than users’
experience. Therefore, this general communication has become a tradition in influencer marketing, and
to encourage consumption, influencers highlight mostly benefits for products or brands. As a result, they
have succeeded with vigorous interaction and general consumption inclined over the potential. However,
from an anti-consumption perspective an approach called de-influencing challenged this trend by giving
certain signals to consumers for reconsidering product/brands for risks, unnecessities, over-consuming,
misaligned purchasing. D e-influencing is becoming clearly visible across digital platforms and open
path for consumers to navigate marketplace by not consuming or changing the way they consume. Still,
unconsciously consumption continues to shape lives, and not all consumers uniformly re act according
to counter messages delivered by de-influencing. At this point, this study critically questions according
to purchasing intention which type of consumers similarly respond to these counter messages.
De-influencing has attracted attention among academics in various disciplines that intersect with
consumer behavior, including marketing, economics, and psychology. Geçit et al. ( 2024)’s work

1. CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK OF DE-INFLUENCING
Growing consuming trends caused by influencers recently sought attention from individuals to
the opposite side of consumption. D e-influencing one of the approach of not consuming or reflecting
the critical side of consuming is defined as resistance to, distaste of, or even resentment or rejection of
consumption (Michaelidou et al., 2026). Additionally, besides being significant counter tr end of
consumer, different explanations are made in various works. Apostol and Asiminei (2025, p.2) alleged
that early manifestation of de-influencing occurred at the start of 2023 at TikTok to discourage purchase
of popular products and described d e-influencing as critical response to popular consumerist culture,
overconsumption, and influencer marketing. Kılıç and Polat (2025) states it is still early to set exact
definition and explains de-influencing as a conscious effort of influencers to discourage followers from
buying or engaging consumption. Wiratha and Kemalasari (2025) states that in response to promotional

of anti‑consumption messaging aimed at encouraging to reduce consumption (Elhajjar & Itani, 2025),
or transparency‑driven movement toward commercial influences (Sheng & Yesiloglu, 2025). Sortly,

On digital platforms, de-influencing has increasingly been observed in various forms of content,
including influencers sharing negative consumption experiences, recommending lower -cost
alternatives, and raising ethical concerns  (Michaelidou et al., 2026) , and is not solely on rejecting
specific brands or products but signifying disaffection with influencer economy and emotional labor it
imposes (Fitriani, 2025, p. 316). This dynamic positioning for d e-influencing also causes a broader set
of variables to be related to. For instance, authenticity, empowered consumption, effects on the self,
moral responsibility (Michaelidou et al., 2026), brand image (Harff & Le, 2026) source credibility
(Wiratha & Kemalasari , 2025), willing to pay more, engagement intention (Tran et al., 2025) service
failure (results in negative electronic word -of-mouth), clout (Chamberlain et al., 2025), information
quality, attitude and usefulness (Lim et al., 2025) purchase intention (Harff & Le, 2026; Elhajjar & Itani,
2025; Tran et al., 2025; Gennette, 2024; Wallbaum, 2023). In essence, there are studies that
acknowledge various variables working with d e-influencing, however with the shortage of empirical
evidence connecting d e-influencing with behavioral outcomes, variables might be considered as
insufficient.
To better understand de-influencing and its outcomes, it is useful to state the phenomenon within
theoretical frameworks. As Michaelidou et al. (2016, p. 2) explains “moral responsibility theory” which
that individuals feel a sense of obligation toward others and society is considered as an underlying
theory. The theory helps explain why both content creators and consumers en gage with anti -
consumption messages in the context of d e-influencing. Influencers who create de-influencing content
may feel a sense of moral obligation to promote responsible and ethical consumption. Consumers may
respond positively because the content aligns with their own sense of moral responsibility in relation to
overconsumption and its societal or environmental consequences. Second, as Elhajjar and Itani (2025,
p.2) explain d e-influencing can be understood via “social influence theory” . In perspective of d e-
influencing, this theory explains how receiving anti -consumption messages from social media can
change purchase intentions, particularly when consumers identify with the influencer or their
community. These theoretical perspectives contribute to the conceptual background of this research by

In this modern era, digital trends are still effective on consumer behavior and as explained above,
de-influencing is started to strongly resonate in its way. To illustrate it further some real-world examples

highest frequencies among all and second was on Instagram, and the rest were below 15 percent in total.
Finally, to fully understand the concept of d e-influencing, the phenomenon needs to be
contextualized in the larger transformation of influencer culture and digital consumerism. Influencer
marketing was successful at the start because of its authenticity, relatability, and trust, but the rise of
promotional content has led to influencer fatigue, decreasing audiences’ trust in influencers and setting
the stage for the development of de-influencing (Fitriani, 2025, p. 317-319). In addition, the rise of de-
influencing can also be tied to larger socioeconomic crises, as the increasing cost of living and economic
insecurity made audiences more critical of excessive consumerism, influencer promotions, and
unnecessary consumption (Moir, 2025, p. 915 -916). Lastly, d e-influencing encourages consumers to
consume less, choose alternatives or think critically about their consumption behavior is part of broader
discourses around responsible and sustainable consumption (Arman Zengi & Ören, 2025). Together,
these explanations indicate that de-influencing is not simply a trend, but rather a fundamental reaction
to the accumulated tensions in the culture of influencer marketing, developing alongside both economic
realities and changing consumer values.
2. HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT
De-influencing as a persuasive counter -message within digital platforms changing consumers’
perspectives however previous research indicates that responses to d e-influencing content are not
uniform and depending on contextual factors (Tran et al., 2025; Wiratha & Kemalasari, 2025). The
existing literature on de-influencing remains limited, and insufficient attention has been paid to whether
consumers with similar characteristics respond in systematically comparable manner. Therefore, before
explaining hypothesis development process, it is important to note that this research is grounded in the
intersection between consumer decision‑making and homophily. In the principles of homophily,
individuals tend to form social bonds with others who share similar demographic or psychological

characteristics, resulting homogeneity in networks characterized by frequent interaction and mutual
influence (McPherson et al., 2001). Therefore, peer contexts based on networks might foster attitudinal
and behavioral convergence and demographic similarit y might increase similarity in purchase
intentions. From the perspective of social similarity theory, the effects of de -influencing messages on
consumers' decision-making processes may vary systematically across demographic groups, such that
individuals with similar demographic characteristics are likely to  interpret these messages in aligned
ways. In short, consumers whose demographics are alike might eventually be similarly de-influenced in
purchasing intention.
De-influencing is conceptualized as a practice that emphasizes authenticity, societal benefit, and
moral responsibility in the work of Michaelidou et al. (2026), and Tran et al. (2025) argue that exploring
individual-level differences beyond values and attitudes could further deepen our understanding of how
this practice operates. Accordingly, Gennette (2024) found that purchase intention is lower when
participants showed a de-influencing content compared to a positive message valance and mention that
older age groups (beyond Gen Z) might not get affected by d e-influencing contents cause their
purchasing habits might not be affected by an influencer.
Drawing deductions from Kılıç and Polat (2025) examining some digital platforms are more prone
to de-influencing contents than others, Apostol and Asiminei (2025, p. 2) mentioning platforms using
algorithmic visibility to disseminate anti-consumption message, Chamberlain et al. (2025) notifying in
review platforms negative electronic word-of-mouth triggered by service failures causes de-influencing,
Dzreke and Dzreke (2025) showing the consequence of influencer betrayal (posting a d e-influencing
content) in different platforms ending in various negative outcomes. Following these evidence and an
inference in accordance with digital platforms reflecting differential perspectives and not homogeneous
(Cennamo, 2021), the importance of platform specific findings surfaces and platforms used by
consumers might shape how users encounter d e-influencing messages. Given these background,
following hypotheses are formed.
• H1: Demographic similarities of consumers significantly predict similarities of de-influenced
purchase intention.
• H2: Similarities in platforms exposed to de-influencing significantly predict similarities of de-
influenced purchase intention.
3. RESEARCH DESIGN
In the last decade, one of the topics which sought attention very much, influencer marketing
continues to shape consumers decision process. Normally, social media influencers put forth the good
sides of promoted brands or products, however in d e-influencing perspective with negativity bias
consumers tend to pay more attention to avoid harmful consequences of consumption (Harff & Le,
2026). Within this context d e-influencing discourages product/brand purchase by emphasizing
overconsumption, moral consumption (Michaelidou et al., 2026), transparency and ethical consumption

To reveal an answer to the question mentioned, this research is designed quantitatively and used
a structured survey as a data collection tool. To align the sampling with the question study employed
purposive sampling. In this type of sampling participants are chosen with specific criterion (Palinkas et
al. 2015). With this perspective in this research the selected participants were the only ones who were
exposed to de-influencing content. Research data were collected via online survey and terminated at 308
(The necessary ethics committee permission was obtained. Date and number: 27.03.2025:897556). After
data cleaning procedure (in this case, four participants did not want to participate, 21 participants did
not expose to d e-influencing contents) analysis is conducted with 283 clean data. Hence this study is
using multiple regression quadratic assignment procedures (MRQAP), Dekker et al. (2007, p. 566)
explains this method is a permutation -based nonparametric regression for analyzing relationships
between square matrices, therefore allowing inference for this research without relying on usual sample
size limitations.
Online surveys required voluntary participation and started with a brief explanation of d e-
influencing. After participants volunteered for the survey, they needed to state whether they have been
exposed to any type of de-influencing (This question was like voluntary question if answered no survey
got terminated). Participants were then asked which platform they encountered d e-influencing content
(multiple choice), which influencer (open ended, not necessary to fill) and which brand (open ended,
not necessary to fill). Following participants who stated they have been exposed to d e-influencing
answered 5 item purchasing intention scale questionnaires (5 items were taken from Spears and Singh
(2004) and adopted for post-exposure). Finally, five questions were addressed for demographics.

approaches have been emerged to draw attention to issues such as risks, overconsumption, unnecessary

use etc. When d e-influencing is considered as a tool for anti -consumption, with help of influencers, it
contributes this process by encouraging a wide range of consumers to reflect more critics for purchasing
decisions. Accordingly, this study is designed in conjunction with the question of whether de-influenced
purchase intention is associated with similarity between individuals and sought a detailed explanation.
Findings of this study contribute directly to consumer behavior, especially in demographic factors
explaining consumer behavior. In addition, when examined superficially, findings insightfully indicate
that consumers’ responses to d e-influencing are not homogeneous but instead a systematic pattern.
Before interpreting the findings, it is proper to acknowledge limitations to better contextualize the results
regarding external validity. First limitation comes from research question itself and creates a causal
inference. Findings reflect only associations rather than any effects for de-influenced purchase intention.
Therefore, interpreting, and generalizing findings as only these factors affect purchasing intention is
considered both a limited and biased interpretation. Second, on one side use of purposive sampling gives
advantages to better comprehend subjects’ nature but constrains generalizability to a broader population.
General descriptive results in the study reflect that exposure to d e-influencing content is highly
linked with negative purchase intention. Most of the participants are seen scoring below neutral midpoint
therefore it can be said that in general de-influencing is discouraging intention to purchase. This pattern
is similar with a work comparing types of influencer message according to d e-influencing vs
influencing, de-influenced purchase intention score is seen on the negative side of axis and significantly
lower than influenced purchase intention (Wallbaum, 2023). Similar results also can be drawn from the
work of Gennette (2024) in which groups exposed to a de-influencing content scored lower than neutral
and positive influences according to purchase intention. In addition, within general descriptives
Instagram is seen as most frequently used platform among participants and has the highest de-influenced
purchase intention score. Like in the work of Kılıç and Polat (2025) frequently posted d e-influencing
contents might increase exposure and can be conclude with as in their statement of “monkey see, monkey
do”. In short, if socially and visually active platforms frequently convey anti -consumption messages,
consumers are likely to form behavioral intentions not to use.

noticeable thing is the model’s explanatory power being modest. This is as outcome for the nature of

underexplored intersection for de-influencing, social similarities and consumers’ purchasing intention.

Implications & Research Agenda: In perspective of practical implications, findings contribute to
digital platforms by unveiling that exposure to d e-influencing does not operate with uniformity across
both consumers and platforms. Therefore, marketers and especially brand managers should
acknowledge demographic and platform-based classifying may be effective for de-influencing content.
In addition, for digital marketers, platforms like Instagram might be a priority check for controlling risks
such as loss of reputation, reduced sales, dissatisfaction etc. One last thing for policy makers, as d e-
influencing is seen as more real than any sponsored influencing contents (Sheng & Yesiloglu, 2025),
instead of apprising contents policy makers should transparently introduce both negative and positive
sides of their products or brand and discourage over consumption.
From theoretical standpoint, this study contributes mostly to consumer behavior, especially
demographic factors affecting behavior. Findings extend literature of influencer marketing by
positioning exposure to a d e-influencing content with a meaningful form of social influence. Proving
similarities of demographics is bonded with de-influenced purchase intention captures a pattern in which
traditional individual-level models might overlook. In addition, findings contribute to Theory of Planned
Behavior by prevailing purchase intention is indeed not only shaped by demographics but mostly might
be shaped by attitudes, beliefs, psychological and social alignments etc.
With given limitations and in -process lacks are considered for future research designs, with a
cognitive dissonance perspective to make sure whether exposure to a de-influencing content eventually
becomes an actual buying (conflict) or not, de-influenced effect for love brands or products (to create a
conflict) is highly recommended. Another agenda, influencer-based content examination or a sentiment
analysis for d e-influencing content should be preferred to determine and enlighten whether feeling of
distrust comes from which (influencers, brands or products, comments etc.). Lastly, psychological
predictors such as skepticism, need for recognition, attachment, and trust may be examined in smaller
but diverse samples to construct and compare visual network models of the relationships associated with
exposure to de-influencing.
