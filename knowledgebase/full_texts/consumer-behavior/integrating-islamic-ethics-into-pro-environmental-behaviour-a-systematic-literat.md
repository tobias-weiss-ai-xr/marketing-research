Abstract
The integration of Islamic ethics into pro-environmental consumer behavior has emerged as a rapidly
expanding research domain; however, extant frameworks remain fragmented. This study aims to
synthesize global literature on green consumption within an Islamic context through a Systematic Literature
Review (SLR). Utilizing the PRISMA 2020 protocol, 78 Scopus-indexed articles published between 2015
and 2025 were analyzed to identify theoretical antecedents and behavioral barriers. The findings propose
the Integrated Islamic Green Behavior Model (IIGBM), which incorporates three primary structural
pathways: the Stewardship Pathway (Khalifah), the Balance Pathway (Mizan), and the Wholesomeness
Pathway (Thayyib). The analysis reveals a significant "green gap," in which high spiritual intentions are
frequently constrained by moderating factors such as premium pricing, limited product availability, and
institutional policy constraints. This study concludes that transitioning from a "Halal" compliance
orientation toward "Thayyib" ethical values is a critical strategy for converting transcendental accountability
into tangible market actions. The theoretical contribution of this research adds a novel dimension to Norm
Activation Theory by incorporating a divine ethical calculus into the discourse of sustainable consumerism.
The findings further demonstrate that Islamic ethical principles provide explanatory mechanisms beyond
conventional environmental behavior theories by embedding divine accountability into sustainable
consumption decisions. The theoretical contribution of this research extends the Theory of Planned
Behavior (TPB), the Value-Belief-Norm (VBN) theory, and the Norm Activation Theory (NAT) by
integrating Khalifah (stewardship), Mizan (balance), and Thayyib (wholesomeness) as distinct pathways
linking spiritual responsibility to pro-environmental behavior.

A. INTRODUCTION
Global environmental degradation, characterized by waste mismanagement and resource depletion,
necessitates a shift from profit-centric utility models toward ethical consumption frameworks (Al Hadi,
2025). Traditional consumer behaviour theories often overlook the spiritual and normative drivers that
dictate decision-making in Muslim-majority markets. In these contexts, consumption is not merely an
economic transaction but a manifestation of moral responsibility, where individuals align their purchasing
habits with broader ecological balance (Iqbal & Khan, 2020). The transition toward sustainable practices—
such as reducing household waste and reusing goods—reflects a move away from linear consumption
toward a circular economy grounded in religious values (Filimonau et al., 2022).
Empirical evidence suggests that religiosity significantly influences ecologically conscious behaviour
(ECCB) and purchase intentions across various Islamic contexts (Omar et al., 2015). For instance, in the

focusing on isolated variables without a unified theoretical framework.
This systematic literature review (SLR) synthesizes 78 peer-reviewed articles from 2015 to 2025 to map
the evolution of Islamic green consumption. By evaluating religiosity as both a mediator and a moderator,
this review addresses existing theoretical inconsistencies. The analysis moves beyond descriptive trends to
propose a structural model that integrates Islamic ethics—specifically the concepts of khalifah (stewardship)
and mizan (balance)—into models of pro-environmental behaviour. The following sections detail the
bibliometric analysis, thematic clusters, and the resulting conceptual framework.

B. LITERATURE REVIEW
The Intersection of Islamic Ethics and Environmental Stewardship
Islamic green consumption diverges from secular eco-centrism by grounding environmental actions in
the ontological concept of khalifah (stewardship). This framework conceptualizes humans as trustees rather
than autonomous owners of the earth, a distinction that shifts the motivation for conservation from
pragmatic utility to transcendental accountability (Al Hadi, 2025; Davis et al., 1997). Within this normative
structure, individuals are tasked with maintaining mizan—the divinely ordained equilibrium of the natural
world. Consequently, pro-environmental behaviour transcends voluntary altruism and functions as a
mandatory expression of faith, in which any disruption to the ecological balance signifies a breach of
spiritual trust (Khalid, 2002; Nasr, 1996).
The operationalization of these ethics in consumer markets manifests as a tension between israf
(wastefulness) and thayyib (wholesomeness). While conventional models like the Theory of Consumption
Values focus on functional and economic trade-offs (Majeed et al., 2022), the Islamic paradigm mandates
that products meet the dual criteria of being halal (permissible) and thayyib. This latter construct integrates
ethical production, ecological safety, and social justice, forcing a broader evaluation of a product's lifecycle

not merely lifestyle choices but fundamental components of spiritual and social identity (M. N. Khan &
Kirmani, 2018; Omar et al., 2015).

Dominant Theoretical Lenses in Islamic Green Research
Scholarship in this domain relies on a triad of theoretical frameworks to explain the antecedents of
sustainable consumption among Muslim cohorts. These lenses categorize behaviour through rational
intention, moral obligation, and multidimensional value evaluation. The Theory of Planned Behaviour
(Ajzen, 1991) remains the most prevalent framework in the analyzed literature. It posits that behavior is a
direct result of intention, which is shaped by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control.
(Naalchi Kashi, 2019) demonstrates that, in the Iranian context, these three components significantly
determine consumers' green purchase intentions. Within Islamic research, scholars frequently
conceptualize religiosity as a distal antecedent that reconfigures these components. Specifically, (M. N.
Khan & Kirmani, 2018) argue that religiosity reinforces subjective norms through community expectations,
where the social pressure of the ummah (community) compels individuals to adopt eco-friendly habits as a
visible manifestation of their faith.
While TPB focuses on the rational calculus of intention, the Value-Belief-Norm (VBN) and Norm
Activation Theory (NAT) address the internal moral drivers of behaviour. (Schwartz, 1977) conceptualizes
NAT as a mechanism in which awareness of consequences and the ascription of responsibility trigger a
personal norm. (Stern, 2000) extends this into the VBN framework by linking stable values to environmental
beliefs. (Al Hadi, 2025) demonstrates that, for Muslim consumers, green knowledge activates personal
norms most effectively when they align with Islamic ethical values, such as stewardship (khalifah). This
moral activation is fundamental for high-involvement behaviours that require individual sacrifice, such as
recycling or paying a premium price for sustainable goods (Md Isa et al., 2017).
Complementing these psychological models, the Theory of Consumption Values (Sheth et al., 1991)
provides a market-oriented perspective on how consumers evaluate green products. This framework posits
that consumer choice is a function of multiple value dimensions: functional, social, emotional, conditional,
and epistemic. (Majeed et al., 2022) apply this to the Pakistani market, evaluating how these
multidimensional values influence the choice of green products. In Muslim-majority markets, these

sustainability-oriented consumption in Islamic contexts. Consequently, the proposed Integrated Islamic
Green Behavior Model (IIGBM) extends these theoretical perspectives by positioning Khalifah
(stewardship), Mizan (balance), and Thayyib (wholesomeness) as distinct ethical pathways that connect
spiritual accountability with pro-environmental intentions and behaviors.

The Role of Religiosity as a Multidimensional Construct
Religiosity represents a composite psychological and sociological construct that transcends mere ritual
adherence, functioning instead as a primary regulator of consumer ethics. Scholarly consensus distinguishes
between intrinsic religiosity—where faith serves as a central, internalized motive—and extrinsic religiosity,
where religion is used as a means to achieve social or personal goals (Allport & Ross, 1967). Within the
Islamic consumption framework, these dimensions dictate the intensity and consistency  of pro -
environmental commitment (Omar et al., 2015). While conventional marketing models often treat religion
as a binary demographic variable, contemporary research conceptualizes it as a multidimensional influence
across ideological, ritualistic, and consequential domains (Robbins et al., 1966).
Empirical evidence demonstrates that religiosity operates as both a direct antecedent and an intervening
variable in sustainable decision-making. For instance, (Susminingsih et al., 2024) demonstrate that
religiosity significantly enhances green purchase intentions for traditional eco-friendly products, such as
natural-dye batik, by fostering a sense of spiritual duty. Furthermore, (Iqbal & Khan, 2020) argue that the
spiritual dimension of religiosity predicts psychological well-being, which, in turn, facilitates the adoption
of reusing behaviors. This suggests that the drive toward sustainability is not merely an external response to
environmental crises but an internal pursuit of spiritual coherence and balance.
The influence of religiosity also extends to emerging market structures, notably the sharing economy
and collaborative consumption. In these sectors, faith-based values foster the interpersonal trust and
communal responsibility required for resource sharing (Iqbal & Khan, 2020). (M. I. Khan et al., 2024)
demonstrate that, among university students, religious values significantly influence the intention to use
green ride-hailing services, reflecting a transition from ownership-centric consumption to lifestyle-oriented
sustainability. However, the relationship between faith and action remains non-linear. (Sarabdeen Dr &
Sarabdeen, 2024) challenges the assumption of a universal positive correlation, conceptualizing religiosity
as a moderating factor that can either mitigate or exacerbate the "green gap"—the discrepancy between
stated environmental values and actual market behavior.

barriers. While religiosity strengthens personal norms, (Al Hadi, 2025) posits that it can be negatively
associated with green behavior when consumers face structural constraints, such as prohibitive costs or

C. METHODS
This study employs a systematic literature review (SLR) to synthesize the current body of knowledge
regarding the intersection of Islamic ethics and pro-environmental consumer behavior. To ensure structural
integrity, transparency, and replicability, the methodology adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for
Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement (Page et al., 2021). This systematic
approach minimizes researcher bias by utilizing a predefined search strategy and explicit eligibility criteria,
distinguishing it from traditional narrative reviews.
The Scopus database served as the primary data source, selected for its comprehensive coverage of high-
impact journals in management, environmental, and social sciences. The search was executed in March
2026 using a Boolean string designed to capture three thematic pillars: (1) Green Consumption, (2)
Islamic/Religious Context, and (3) Consumer Behavior. The finalized search string was: TITLE-ABS-
KEY ( ( "green consumption" OR "green product*" OR "green purchas*" OR "sustainable consumption"
OR "eco-friendly" OR "pro-environmental" OR "green marketing" ) AND ( "Islam*" OR "Muslim*" OR
"halal" OR "religiosity" OR "sharia*" ) AND ( "consumer behavior" OR "purchase intention" OR "buying
behavior" OR "consumer attitude" OR "consumer" ) ).
The screening process followed a four-stage deductive approach. Initial identification yielded 115
records. To maintain contemporary relevance and academic quality, the study applied the specific inclusion
and exclusion criteria detailed in Table 1.

particularly regarding the "green gap" between intention and actual behavior in Muslim-majority markets.

Thematic Clustering of Theoretical Frameworks
The synthesis of the 78 articles identifies three primary theoretical clusters that define the current
landscape of Islamic green consumption research. These clusters reflect distinct psychological and
sociological mechanisms through which religious ethics influence pro-environmental intentions.

Cluster 1: Rational-Choice Models (TPB/TRA Focus)
The majority of the reviewed studies (n=52) utilize the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) or the Theory
of Reasoned Action (TRA) as their primary lens. These studies demonstrate that Islamic religiosity
functions as a significant antecedent that reconfigures the standard predictors of intention. For instance,
(Naalchi Kashi, 2019) indicates that attitudes and subjective norms serve as the strongest predictors of green
purchase intention in Muslim contexts. Within this cluster, religiosity is frequently conceptualized as a force
that strengthens subjective norms, as the community expectations of the ummah create a collective pressure
toward sustainable habits (M. N. Khan & Kirmani, 2018; Susminingsih et al., 2024).

Cluster 2: Moral-Normative Models (VBN/NAT Focus)
The second cluster (n=38) applies the Value-Belief-Norm (VBN) theory and Norm Activation Theory
(NAT) to explain behavior through the lens of moral obligation. These studies highlight a chain of activation
where the awareness of ecological consequences, filtered through Islamic stewardship (khalifah), triggers a
personal norm. (Al Hadi, 2025) demonstrates that this moral activation is essential for behaviors requiring
individual sacrifice, such as recycling or reducing plastic consumption. This research posits that religious
accountability is a more stable predictor of long-term, sustainable behavior than secular environmental
concern alone, as it links ecological action to spiritual consequences (Md Isa et al., 2017; Omar et al., 2015).

Cluster 3: Value-Attitudinal Models (TCV/Ethical Focus)
The final cluster focuses on the Theory of Consumption Values (TCV) and ethical consumption
frameworks. Research in this category evaluates how consumers perceive the multidimensional values of
products beyond simple utility. (Majeed et al., 2022) identify that functional, social, and epistemic values
drive green product choice in the Pakistani market. A distinct contribution in this cluster is conceptualizing
the halalan-thayyiban paradigm as a market differentiator. (Hassan & Sengupta, 2019) argue that for
Muslim consumers, product value includes its wholesomeness (thayyib), encompassing ethical production

Analysis of Core Constructs and Variables
The synthesis of the 78 articles identifies a consistent array of operational variables that define the
structural relationship between Islamic ethics and environmental action. These constructs are categorized
into three functional groups: primary antecedents, mediating mechanisms, and moderating boundaries.
Independent Variables: Religiosity and Islamic Ethical Knowledge
Religiosity and spirituality function as the primary exogenous constructs in the analyzed models. The
literature demonstrates that religiosity is not a monolithic driver; rather, its impact depends on whether the
belief system is internalized or socially driven (Solekah et al., 2020). (Nguyen et al., 2024) argue that
religious beliefs are a significant predictor of intentions, shaping consumers' cognitive evaluations of green
alternatives. Beyond ritualistic adherence, spirituality serves as an explanatory mechanism for sustainable
consumption, fostering the psychological well-being necessary for consumers to adopt long-term habits such
as clothing reuse (Iqbal & Khan, 2020). Furthermore, contemporary research highlights the emergence of
ethical self-identity as a foundational driver, particularly among younger demographics who view green
vehicles and sustainable technologies as an extension of their moral values (Bhutto et al., 2022).
Mediating Mechanisms: Bridging the Faith-Action Gap
The path from religious values to actual pro -environmental behavior is predominantly indirect,
mediated by cognitive and affective evaluations. Two primary mediating pathways emerge:  First,
Environmental Concern and Knowledge: Consumers' affective evaluation of ecological issues mediates the
relationship between faith and green purchase intentions (Nguyen et al., 2024). (Majeed et al., 2022)
demonstrate that environmental concern partially mediates the influence of multidimensional consumption
values on choice behavior. Additionally, the specific knowledge of eco-labels serves as a bridge, translating
general sustainability paradigms into actionable consumer choices (M A Qureshi et al., 2023). Second,
Personal Norms and Attitudes: Following the logic of Norm Activation Theory, individual moral
obligations act as internal regulators of behaviors that require sacrifice, such as waste reduction (Al Hadi,
2025). (Solekah et al., 2020) find that environmental attitudes significantly mediate the relationship between
religious orientation and green marketing effectiveness, suggesting that faith must first cultivate a pro-
environmental mindset before behavioral change occurs.

Moderating Factors: Identifying Structural and Institutional Barriers
The synthesis identifies several boundary conditions that interfere with the strength of the relationship
between religious motivation and actual behavior, accounting for the persistent "green gap."  First,
Economic and Value Barriers: Functional values, specifically price sensitivity, often moderate the impact
of religious ethics. High premium costs for eco-friendly goods can negate the influence of environmental
concerns, particularly in markets where price-value trade-offs are dominant (Majeed et al., 2022). Second,
Policy and Institutional Context: External pressures, such as plastic ban policies, interact with personal
religiosity to shape behavior. While institutional mandates strengthen social norms, they can occasionally
reduce individual responsibility if consumers perceive the action as a forced regulation rather than a moral
choice (Al Hadi, 2025). Third, Socio-Demographic and Political Covariates: The effectiveness of religious
values in driving sustainability is also moderated by socio-economic status, education levels, and political
orientations. (Yang & Arhonditsis, 2022) argue that these factors reliably predict whether environmental
skepticism can be overcome through value-based appeals.

Synthesis: The Integrated Islamic Green Behavior Model
The synthesis of the 78 articles culminates in the proposed Integrated Islamic Green Behavior Model

structural pathways that integrate Islamic ontological principles into the psychological mechanisms of
sustainable consumption. The model posits that pro-environmental outcomes result from three distinct yet
interlocking pathways: Stewardship, Balance, and Wholesomeness.
Path A: The Stewardship Pathway (Khalifah → Moral Norms)

perception enhances the multidimensional consumption value of green goods, which directly drives green
purchase intention. In contexts with high religiosity, this pathway becomes a dominant predictor of actual
behavior, as consumers seek products that provide both spiritual coherence and functional utility (Solekah
et al., 2020). This pathway is particularly influential in categories such as organic food and natural-dye
textiles, where the "purity" of the product is central to the consumer's self-identity (Bhutto et al., 2022).

Mapping the "Green Gap" in Muslim-Majority Markets
The synthesis of the 78 articles demonstrates a consistent discrepancy between high spiritual intentions
and actual green behavior, a phenomenon conceptualized as the "green gap." While Islamic ethics provide
a robust normative foundation for sustainability, the transition from intention to market action is frequently
hindered by external variables. This indicates that religious orientation alone is an insufficient predictor of
pro-environmental behavior when unsupported by a conducive market infrastructure.
Research across Indonesia, Pakistan, and Malaysia identifies that Muslim consumers often exhibit high
levels of environmental concern driven by their faith, yet these sentiments do not reliably translate into
purchase behavior (Al Hadi, 2025). (Yang & Arhonditsis, 2022) describe this as a failure to "walk the talk,"
in which established commitments to resource conservation remain unfulfilled. In the energy sector and
green products context, consumers may acknowledge the religious obligation of stewardship (khalifah) but
continue to prioritize non-green alternatives due to immediate situational pressures (Majeed et al., 2022; M
A Qureshi et al., 2023).
The analysis suggests that functional and economic barriers primarily widen the green gap. (Majeed et
al., 2022) demonstrate that functional value-price has a negative impact on choice behavior in Pakistan,
suggesting that price sensitivity can override religious altruism. Even when consumers are highly religious,
they often face a "trade-off" in which the higher costs of eco-friendly products serve as a deterrent (Al Hadi,
2025). Furthermore, the limited availability of green products in conventional retail spaces acts as a physical
barrier, making sustainable choices less convenient for the average consumer (Iqbal & Khan, 2020).
The results highlight that religiosity requires a supportive institutional environment to be effective. (Al
Hadi, 2025) posits that while plastic ban policies strengthen social norms, the absence of a green
infrastructure—such as accessible recycling facilities or subsidized eco-labels—prevents consumers from
actualizing their moral norms. The discrepancy is most evident in urban settings, where the pace of
consumption is high, and the availability of sustainable alternatives is low (M. I. Khan et al., 2024).
Consequently, the literature argues that closing the green gap in Muslim-majority markets requires a dual
approach: reinforcing the spiritual mandate of mizan while simultaneously reducing the structural costs of
green consumption.

Discussion
The results of this systematic review demonstrate that integrating Islamic ethics into pro-environmental
behavior provides a robust normative framework that extends beyond the boundaries of secular consumer
theories. By synthesizing 78 articles, this study  identifies a shift from rational -choice utility to a
transcendental accountability model. The following subsections interpret the theoretical and practical
implications of the Integrated Islamic Green Behavior Model (IIGBM) and its capacity to address the
persistent "green gap" in Muslim-majority markets. The proposed IIGBM model challenges the sufficiency
of traditional models such as the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and the Value-Belief-Norm (VBN)
theory when applied to religious cohorts. While TPB focuses on the rational calculus of intention (Ajzen,
1991), this review posits that Islamic ethics—specifically through the Stewardship (Khalifah) and Balance
(Mizan) pathways—reconfigure the very nature of "subjective norms" and "personal obligations." For
Muslim consumers, environmental action is not an optional altruistic choice but a mandatory expression
of faith  (Al Hadi, 2025).
This finding highlights a fundamental distinction between the proposed IIGBM and dominant
environmental behavior theories. While TPB explains environmentally responsible actions through
attitudinal evaluations, social pressures, and perceived control, it does not explicitly explain why individuals
perceive environmental protection as a moral obligation beyond personal or social expectations. Similarly,
VBN and NAT successfully describe the activation of personal norms through environmental values and
awareness of consequences; however, these frameworks remain largely secular and do not identify a
transcendental source of accountability. In contrast, the IIGBM proposes that environmental responsibility
is rooted in divine stewardship (Khalifah), ecological ba lance (Mizan), and ethical wholesomeness
(Thayyib), thereby extending existing theories by incorporating spiritual accountability as a distinct
motivational mechanism. This theoretical extension is particularly relevant in Muslim-majority societies,
where consumption decisions are frequently evaluated not only according to economic utility but also
according to religious and ethical obligations.
Building on the findings of (Iqbal & Khan, 2020), this study argues that the spiritual dimension of
religiosity serves as a primary driver of psychological well-being, which, in turn, facilitates sustainable habits
such as resource sharing and reuse. This demonstrates that pro-environmental behavior in Islamic contexts

translating spiritual values into marketplace actions. This finding reinforces the argument that sustainable
consumption requires both normative motivation and institutional support, highlighting the
complementary roles of religious ethics, market accessibility, and public policy in achieving long-term
behavioral change.

as a primary marketing differentiator. Moving from a focus on legal permissibility (halal) toward ethical
wholesomeness (thayyib) allows brands to integrate environmental safety and social justice into their core
value proposition (Hassan & Sengupta, 2019).

communicate broader sustainability attributes throughout the product lifecycle. While halal certification
primarily assures religious permissibility, thayyib encompasses environmental integrity, ethical sourcing,
responsible labor practices, resource efficiency, and consumer well-being. Consequently, organizations
should integrate sustainability indicators into product labeling, marketing communications, and corporate
reporting systems. Such an approach enables consumers to evaluate products not only according to
compliance requirements but also according to their ecological and social impact, thereby strengthening the
ethical value proposition of green products in Muslim markets.

E. CONCLUSION
This systematic synthesis of 78 articles establishes that integrating Islamic ethics into pro-environmental
consumer behavior creates a normative framework that transcends conventional secular consumption
theories. The results indicate a fundamental shift in sustainability motivation among Muslim consumers,
moving from rational utility toward a model of transcendental accountability. The proposed Integrated
Islamic Green Behavior Model (IIGBM) demonstrates that ontological principles—specifically Khalifah
(stewardship), Mizan (balance), and Thayyib (wholesomeness)—function as the primary drivers of green
intentions. Consequently, pro-environmental behavior is conceptualized not as an optional lifestyle choice
but as a mandatory manifestation of faith and muamalah (social ethics).
The primary theoretical contribution of this review lies in delineating three structural pathways—
Stewardship, Balance, and Wholesomeness—that elucidate how Islamic values activate personal norms.
This study extends existing literature by demonstrating that in religious contexts, subjective norms are
driven by both communal expectations (ummah) and divine mandates. Furthermore, the analysis of the
"green gap" provides a theoretical explanation for the discrepancy between spiritual values and market
actions, identifying structural barriers as key moderators. Beyond this contribution, the proposed IIGBM
extends dominant behavioral frameworks, including the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), Value-Belief-
Norm (VBN) theory, and Norm Activation Theory (NAT), by introducing transcendental accountability
as an additional explanatory mechanism for pro-environmental behavior. Unlike conventional models that
primarily emphasize rational evaluation, environmental beliefs, or personal moral obligations, the IIGBM
incorporates Khalifah, Mizan, and Thayyib as distinct Islamic ethical pathways linking spiritual
responsibility to sustainable consumption decisions. By aligning Norm Activation Theory (NAT) with
transcendental ethics, this research offers a novel dimension to consumer behavior theory in emerging
markets.
In practice, these findings necessitate a paradigm shift for marketers, moving from a narrow focus on
halal compliance to an emphasis on thayyib (comprehensive wholesomeness). The findings suggest that
halal certification alone may be insufficient to satis fy the evolving expectations of environmentally
conscious Muslim consumers. Instead, the integration of thayyib principles —including ecological
responsibility, ethical production, resource efficiency, and social justice—provides a more comprehensive
framework for sustainable market positioning. Branding strategies that integrate ecological safety, ethical
production, and social justice will likely resonate more with ethical Muslim consumer segments. For
policymakers, framing environmental regulations—such a s plastic reduction initiatives—within the
narrative of mizan (ecological balance) can improve voluntary compliance. This approach transforms the
