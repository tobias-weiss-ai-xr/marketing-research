Abstract
Purpose : This study examines how perceived green marketing inﬂuences consumer perceptions and
behavior in thrift-store consumption, with particular attention to perceived environmental value,
perceived market disruption, consumer switching behavior, and perceived impact on MSME
sustainability.
Design/methodology/approach: A quantitative survey design was employed using data from 500
consumers across ﬁve major Indonesian cities: Jakarta, Bandung, Surabaya, Yogyakarta, and Makassar.
Respondents were selected using purposive sampling and had prior experience purchasing thrift
products. Data were analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) with
SmartPLS.
Findings/results: The results demonstrate that perceived green marketing positively inﬂuences
perceived environmental value (β = 0.771, p < 0.001). Perceived environmental value signiﬁcantly
inﬂuences perceived market disruption (β = 0.782, p < 0.001), which subsequently inﬂuences consumer
switching behavior (β = 0.738, p < 0.001). Consumer switching behavior signiﬁcantly affects perceived
impact on MSME sustainability (β = 0.912, p < 0.001). The ﬁndings further reveal signiﬁcant sequential
indirect effects from perceived green marketing to perceived MSME sustainability through environmental
value, market disruption, and switching behavior (β = 0.406, p < 0.001).
Practical implications and Originality/value: The ﬁndings suggest that MSMEs should respond to
sustainability-driven market disruption through green branding, circular practices, product differentiation,
and digital engagement. The study contributes by integrating green marketing, consumer perceptions,
market disruption, and perceived MSME sustainability into a sequential consumer-based framework.

1. INTRODUCTION
The rapid growth of the fashion industry has signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced consumer lifestyles, particularly in
urban areas. In Indonesia, increasing digital exposure and evolving consumption patterns have made
consumers more responsive to trends, including those related to sustainability and environmental
awareness (Kotler et al., 2019). As a result, consumption decisions are no longer driven solely by
functional needs but also by perceived values and symbolic meanings attached to products.
Recent studies suggest that sustainable consumption has evolved beyond environmentally conscious
purchasing into a broader lifestyle orientation closely associated with the principles of the circular
economy. In the textile and apparel industry, consumers increasingly evaluate products not only based
on functional and economic beneﬁts but also on environmental responsibility, product life extension, and
resource eﬃciency. Consequently, green marketing has become a strategic approach that enables ﬁrms
Page 2/25

to communicate sustainability values while encouraging consumers to participate in circular
consumption practices, including the growing acceptance of second-hand and thrift products.
Furthermore, recent evidence indicates that consumers' environmental knowledge and perceptions of
green branding have become critical determinants of the effectiveness of green marketing strategies in
promoting sustainable purchasing behaviour (Cui et al., 2025).
In recent years, the concept of green consumption has gained considerable attention, referring to
consumer behavior that incorporates environmental considerations into purchasing decisions (Joshi &
Rahman, 2015). One emerging phenomenon within this trend is the growing popularity of thrift stores,
which consumers often perceive as environmentally friendly alternatives to new fashion products.
Through various marketing messages emphasizing sustainability, reuse, and waste reduction, thrift
stores have positioned themselves as part of a sustainable lifestyle.
From a consumer perspective, these marketing messages play a crucial role in shaping perceptions.
Green marketing is not only about what ﬁrms communicate but also about how consumers interpret and
internalize these messages (Peattie, 2001). Recent studies further argue that the effectiveness of green
marketing increasingly depends on the authenticity, transparency, and credibility of environmental
claims. As consumers become more environmentally conscious, they are also more capable of
distinguishing genuine sustainability initiatives from symbolic or misleading environmental messages.
Consequently, consumers' perceptions of green marketing are formed not merely by exposure to
environmental communication but also by the degree to which they perceive such communication as
trustworthy and consistent with ﬁrms' actual sustainability practices (Fang, 2024; Cui et al., 2025). In the
context of thrift stores, consumers may perceive secondhand products as contributing to environmental
sustainability, regardless of the actual environmental impact. This perception becomes a key driver in
inﬂuencing consumer attitudes and behavior.
Perceived environmental value is one of the most important factors explaining this process. It refers to
consumers' evaluation of a product's environmental beneﬁts (Chen & Chang, 2012). When consumers
perceive that purchasing thrift products beneﬁts the environment, they are more likely to develop
favorable attitudes and engage in pro-environmental consumption behavior (Wang et al., 2021). This
perceived value is often reinforced by moral satisfaction, where consumers feel ethically responsible for
making environmentally friendly choices (Hartmann & Apaolaza-Ibáñez, 2012).
These perceptions can lead to changes in consumer behavior, particularly in the form of switching
behavior. Consumer switching behavior refers to consumers' tendency to shift their purchasing
preferences from one product type to another (Ajzen, 1991). In this case, consumers may shift from
purchasing newly produced fashion items to thrift products, which are perceived as both environmentally
beneﬁcial and economically attractive. Previous studies have shown that environmental perceptions
signiﬁcantly inﬂuence switching behavior and sustainable consumption patterns (Joshi & Rahman, 2021;
Wang et al., 2021).

As consumer switching behavior becomes more widespread, it may inﬂuence broader market dynamics.
From a perception-based perspective, consumers may begin to observe changes in the fashion market,
such as lower price expectations, shifting demand patterns, and increased competition. This
phenomenon can be understood as a perceived market disruption, in which consumers recognize
changes in market structure driven by evolving consumption trends (Christensen, 1997; Porter, 1980).
Importantly, these perceived changes in the market may also extend to how consumers view the
condition of local businesses. In Indonesia, Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) play a vital
role in the fashion industry. However, as consumers increasingly engage in thrift consumption, they may
perceive that local MSMEs face greater challenges in maintaining competitiveness and long-term
sustainability. These perceptions, whether fully accurate or not, are important because they reﬂect how
consumers interpret the broader impact of their consumption choices.
Despite the growing interest in green marketing and sustainable consumption, most existing studies
have primarily focused on the direct relationship between green marketing and consumer behavior,
particularly purchase intention, loyalty, and green buying decisions (Joshi & Rahman, 2015; Wang et al.,
2021). More recent literature has broadened this perspective by highlighting the roles of sustainable
branding, consumer trust, and circular economy practices in shaping environmentally responsible
consumption. However, these studies generally examine green marketing, circular economy, consumer
trust, or sustainable branding as separate research streams rather than as an integrated behavioural
process.
Therefore, a signiﬁcant research gap remains regarding how consumers' perceptions of green marketing
generate behavioural changes that subsequently reshape market dynamics and perceptions of MSME
sustainability. Although recent studies have acknowledged the importance of circular economy practices
and sustainability-oriented marketing, limited attention has been devoted to understanding the
sequential mechanism linking perceived green marketing, perceived environmental value, consumer
switching behavior, perceived market disruption, and perceived MSME sustainability within a single serial
mediation framework. Existing research continues to investigate these constructs largely in isolation,
resulting in a fragmented understanding of how sustainability-oriented marketing inﬂuences broader
economic and market outcomes.
Moreover, recent systematic evidence emphasizes that future research should move beyond examining
green purchase intention alone and investigate how sustainability-oriented marketing inﬂuences broader
market outcomes, including competitive dynamics, business resilience, and organizational sustainability
under the circular economy paradigm. Responding to this recommendation, the present study integrates
green marketing, environmental value, consumer switching behavior, market disruption, and MSME
sustainability into a comprehensive perception-based framework that captures the broader
consequences of sustainability-oriented consumer behaviour.
To address this gap, this study adopts a consumer-based perspective. It proposes an integrated
framework that links perceived green marketing, perceived environmental value, consumer switching
Page 4/25

behavior, perceived market disruption, and perceived impact on MSME sustainability. This approach
emphasizes that market changes are not only driven by objective conditions but also by how consumers
perceive and respond to sustainability-related messages.
The novelty of this study lies in its integration of green consumption and market disruption within a
single perception-based framework. Additionally, this research introduces the concept of a perceived
sustainability trade-off, in which consumers recognize that environmentally driven consumption may
also affect local economic actors. By focusing on perception rather than objective performance, this
study provides a new perspective on understanding the broader consequences of green marketing
practices.
Although previous studies have examined the relationship between green marketing, sustainable
consumption, and business sustainability, many of them implicitly interpret consumer evaluations as
evidence of actual business performance. However, consumer perception does not necessarily reﬂect
objective organizational outcomes. Consumers form subjective judgments regarding how their
purchasing behaviour may inﬂuence local MSMEs, even though they typically do not possess complete
information about ﬁrms' ﬁnancial or operational conditions. Therefore, this study deliberately
conceptualises MSME sustainability as consumers' perceived impact on MSME sustainability rather than
actual MSME performance. This distinction provides a more theoretically appropriate framework for
analysing behavioural responses within the context of thrift-store consumption.
From a practical perspective, this study offers important implications for policymakers in Indonesia.
Understanding how consumers perceive the impact of thrift consumption on local businesses can help
policymakers design more balanced strategies that consider both environmental and economic
sustainability. Moreover, the ﬁndings can provide insights for local MSMEs in adapting to changing
consumer perceptions and market trends.
2. LITERATURE REVIEW AND HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT
2.1. Perceived Green Marketing and Perceived Environmental Value
Green marketing refers to marketing activities that incorporate environmental considerations into the
development, communication, and promotion of products and services (Peattie, 2001). In the context of
sustainable consumption, green marketing enables ﬁrms to communicate environmental attributes and
encourage consumers to recognize the environmental consequences of their consumption choices.
However, the effectiveness of green marketing is not determined solely by the environmental practices
implemented by ﬁrms. Rather, it also depends on how consumers perceive, interpret, and evaluate
environmental messages (Testa et al., 2020).
In the context of thrift stores, green marketing can be communicated through messages emphasizing
reuse, waste reduction, product life extension, and sustainable lifestyles. Such communication may lead
consumers to perceive thrift products as environmentally preferable alternatives to newly produced
Page 5/25

fashion products. Recent research also suggests that green marketing has increasingly become an
important mechanism for promoting sustainable consumption in the textile and apparel industry by
strengthening consumers' environmental awareness and perceptions of environmental value (Cui et al.,
2025).
Perceived environmental value refers to consumers' subjective evaluation of the environmental beneﬁts
associated with a product or consumption choice (Chen & Chang, 2012). According to value perception
theory, consumers evaluate products based on the beneﬁts they perceive relative to the attributes and
consequences associated with consumption (Zeithaml, 1988). Therefore, when consumers receive
credible and convincing environmental information through green marketing, they may attach greater
environmental value to the promoted products.
For thrift consumption, environmental messages concerning the reduction of textile waste, reuse of
existing clothing, and sustainable consumption may strengthen consumers' beliefs that purchasing
secondhand products contributes to environmental protection. Previous research has demonstrated that
green marketing and environmental communication can enhance consumers' perceptions of the
environmental value of products and subsequently encourage more sustainable consumption
evaluations (Wang et al., 2021). Furthermore, consumers increasingly assess environmental claims
according to their authenticity, credibility, and consistency with sustainability practices (Fang, 2024).
Therefore, consumers who perceive thrift stores as actively communicating environmental beneﬁts are
expected to assign greater environmental value to thrift consumption. Accordingly, the following
hypothesis is proposed:
Perceived green marketing positively inﬂuences perceived environmental value.
2.2. Perceived Environmental Value and Perceived Market Disruption
Perceived environmental value represents consumers' evaluation of the environmental beneﬁts
associated with a consumption alternative. While previous studies have primarily examined
environmental value in relation to green purchase intention and sustainable consumption behavior,
environmental evaluations may also inﬂuence consumers' perceptions of broader changes occurring
within a market.
In the fashion industry, the increasing acceptance of secondhand and thrift products represents a shift in
how consumers evaluate fashion products. Consumers may perceive that environmental beneﬁts
associated with thrift consumption provide an alternative value proposition to conventional fashion
products. Consequently, environmental value may extend beyond an individual product evaluation and
inﬂuence consumers' perceptions of changes in market demand, competition, pricing, and consumption
patterns.
From a market perspective, disruption occurs when established market structures, competitive
relationships, or consumption patterns are challenged by alternative products or business models
Page 6/25

(Christensen, 1997). The expansion of circular economy practices has created alternative consumption
systems that challenge conventional linear production and consumption models. In fashion, secondhand
consumption can represent such an alternative because it extends product life and reduces dependence
on newly produced products (Bocken et al., 2016).
From the consumer perspective adopted in this study, perceived market disruption does not represent an
objective measurement of market performance. Instead, it reﬂects consumers' perceptions that changes
in consumption values are altering market dynamics. Consumers who strongly recognize the
environmental value of thrift products may therefore become more aware of changes in the fashion
market, including increasing competition from thrift stores, changes in consumer demand, and shifts in
fashion consumption patterns.
This argument is consistent with research emphasizing that sustainability-oriented consumption and
circular economy practices can inﬂuence competitive dynamics and encourage changes in market
structures (Dangelico et al., 2020; Bocken et al., 2021). Thus, stronger perceptions of environmental
value are expected to increase consumers' perceptions that the fashion market is undergoing disruption.
Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:
Perceived environmental value positively inﬂuences perceived market disruption.
2.3. Perceived Market Disruption and Consumer Switching Behavior
Market disruption can alter consumers' perceptions of available alternatives and encourage changes in
established consumption patterns. Disruption is not necessarily limited to technological innovation;
changes in consumer preferences and the emergence of alternative consumption models can also
contribute to transformations in market dynamics (Christensen, 1997).
In the fashion industry, the growth of thrift consumption provides consumers with an alternative to newly
produced fashion products. The increasing availability and visibility of thrift products may change
consumers' perceptions of price, product value, sustainability, and consumption alternatives. When
consumers perceive that the fashion market is changing because of the growing presence of thrift
stores, they may become more willing to reconsider their established purchasing behavior.
Consumer switching behavior refers to a change in consumers' purchasing preferences from one
product or provider to another. In this study, switching behavior speciﬁcally refers to consumers'
tendency to move from newly produced fashion products toward thrifted or secondhand alternatives.
Such switching can reﬂect changes in consumers' evaluations of product value and the attractiveness of
alternative consumption choices.
The Theory of Planned Behavior suggests that consumers' perceptions and evaluations can inﬂuence
behavioral tendencies and subsequent actions (Ajzen, 1991). In the context of sustainable consumption,
consumers may respond to perceived changes in the market by adapting their purchasing behavior
Page 7/25

toward alternatives that they consider more attractive or consistent with their environmental values.
Previous research has similarly demonstrated that changes in consumer preferences and sustainability-
oriented market conditions can inﬂuence green consumption and purchasing behavior (Joshi & Rahman,
2021; Wang et al., 2021).
Accordingly, when consumers perceive stronger disruption in the fashion market caused by the growing
presence of thrift stores, they are expected to be more likely to switch from newly produced fashion
products to thrift alternatives. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:
Perceived market disruption positively inﬂuences consumer switching behavior.
2.4. Consumer Switching Behavior and Perceived Impact on MSME Sustainability
Consumer switching behavior can have consequences beyond individual purchasing decisions because
aggregate changes in consumer preferences may inﬂuence demand and competitive conditions faced
by businesses. When consumers increasingly switch from newly produced fashion products to thrift
alternatives, local fashion MSMEs may be perceived as facing changes in demand, pricing pressure, and
competitive intensity.
MSMEs are particularly relevant in this context because their limited resources and capabilities may
make them more vulnerable to changes in competitive environments. The resource-based view argues
that sustained competitive advantage depends on ﬁrms' ability to possess and deploy valuable
resources and capabilities (Barney, 1991). When market conditions change rapidly, ﬁrms with limited
resources may experience greater diﬃculty in maintaining their competitive position.
In this study, perceived impact on MSME sustainability refers speciﬁcally to consumers' perceptions
regarding how the expansion of thrift consumption may affect the revenue, growth, competitiveness, and
long-term sustainability of local MSMEs. Importantly, this construct does not represent an objective
measure of MSME ﬁnancial or operational performance. Rather, it captures consumers' subjective
assessments of the potential consequences of changing consumption behavior for local businesses.
Consumer switching from newly produced fashion products to thrift alternatives may lead consumers to
perceive that local fashion MSMEs face greater competitive pressure. Thrift products may provide
consumers with relatively affordable alternatives while simultaneously being associated with
environmental and sustainability beneﬁts. Consequently, increasing switching behavior may be perceived
as reducing the attractiveness of conventional fashion products and creating challenges for MSMEs that
primarily depend on newly produced fashion goods.
Previous research has emphasized that changing market conditions, competitive pressures, and external
disruptions can create signiﬁcant challenges for SMEs (Eggers, 2020; Kraus et al., 2020). At the same
time, MSMEs may respond to such changes by developing new capabilities, differentiating their
products, and adopting sustainability-oriented strategies.
Page 8/25

Therefore, greater consumer switching toward thrift products is expected to increase consumers'
perceptions that local MSMEs face challenges to their sustainability. Accordingly, the following
hypothesis is proposed:
Consumer switching behavior positively inﬂuences perceived impact on MSME sustainability.
2.5. Mediation Mechanisms
The relationships among perceived green marketing, environmental value, market disruption, switching
behavior, and perceived MSME sustainability may not occur solely through direct relationships. Instead,
consumer perceptions may develop through a sequence in which environmental marketing
communication shapes environmental value perceptions, environmental value shapes perceptions of
market change, and perceptions of market change inﬂuence behavioral responses.
The mediation framework is therefore developed based on the empirical sequence:
Perceived Green Marketing →  Perceived Environmental Value →  Perceived Market Disruption →
Consumer Switching Behavior →  Perceived Impact on MSME Sustainability
This sequence is consistent with the broader argument that sustainability-oriented marketing can
inﬂuence consumers through cognitive evaluations before producing behavioral and market-level
consequences.
2.5.1. The Mediating Role of Perceived Environmental Value
Green marketing communication provides environmental information that consumers use to evaluate
the beneﬁts of a product or consumption practice. When consumers perceive green marketing positively,
they are more likely to assign greater environmental value to the promoted alternative (Chen & Chang,
2012; Wang et al., 2021).
In the present context, perceived environmental value serves as the cognitive mechanism through which
perceived green marketing may inﬂuence consumers' perceptions of market changes. The signiﬁcant
indirect effect reported in the study for PG →  PE →  PM (β = 0.603, t = 16.103, p < 0.001) supports this
mediated relationship. 
Thus:
Perceived environmental value mediates the relationship between perceived green marketing and
perceived market disruption.
2.5.2. The Mediating Role of Perceived Market Disruption
Perceived environmental value may subsequently inﬂuence consumer switching behavior through
consumers' perceptions that the market is changing. When consumers recognize strong environmental
Page 9/25

value in thrift consumption, they may perceive increasing disruption in conventional fashion markets,
which can encourage them to reconsider their existing purchasing patterns.
Therefore:
Perceived market disruption mediates the relationship between perceived environmental value and
consumer switching behavior.
2.5.3. The Mediating Role of Consumer Switching Behavior
Perceived market disruption may ultimately affect consumers' perceptions of MSME sustainability
through changes in their own purchasing behavior. When consumers switch from newly produced
fashion products toward thrift alternatives, they may perceive that local fashion MSMEs face greater
competitive pressure and challenges in maintaining their market position.
Accordingly:
Consumer switching behavior mediates the relationship between perceived market disruption and
perceived impact on MSME sustainability.
2.6. Sequential Mediation Mechanism
Beyond the individual mediation relationships, the ﬁndings indicate a broader sequential mechanism
connecting perceived green marketing with perceived impact on MSME sustainability. Speciﬁcally, the
indirect effect follows the sequence:
PG →  PE →  PM →  CS →  PI
Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:
Perceived green marketing of thrift stores positively inﬂuences perceived environmental value.

The conceptual framework of this study illustrates the sequential relationship between perceived green
marketing of thrift stores and the perceived impact on MSME sustainability from a consumer
perspective. Speciﬁcally, perceived green marketing inﬂuences perceived environmental value, which
subsequently drives consumer switching behavior. This behavioral shift then leads to perceived market
disruption, ultimately shaping consumers' perceptions of the impact on MSME sustainability. The model
emphasizes a fully mediated relationship, indicating that the effect of green marketing on MSMEs does
not occur directly but through a chain of cognitive and behavioral processes. This framework highlights
the importance of consumer perception as a key mechanism in understanding how sustainability-
oriented marketing practices can inﬂuence broader market dynamics and the perceived condition of
local businesses.
3. METHODS
This study adopts a quantitative research approach using a survey method to examine the relationships
among perceived green marketing, perceived environmental value, consumer switching behavior,
perceived market disruption, and perceived impact on MSME sustainability (Sekaran & Bougie, 2016).
The unit of analysis is individual consumers, as the study focuses on consumer perception. Data were
collected through a structured questionnaire using a ﬁve-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly
disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The respondents were selected using purposive sampling, with the
criteria that participants must have prior experience purchasing thrift products and be at least 17 years
old. The study involved 500 respondents distributed across ﬁve major cities in Indonesia—Jakarta,
Bandung, Surabaya, Yogyakarta, and Makassar—with 100 respondents from each city to ensure broader
representation of urban consumer behavior.
The collected data were analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM)
with SmartPLS software. The analysis consists of two main stages: the measurement model (outer
model) and the structural model (inner model). The measurement model was assessed through validity
and reliability tests, including indicator loadings, composite reliability, Cronbach's alpha, and average
variance extracted (AVE). Meanwhile, the structural model was evaluated by examining path coeﬃcients,
coeﬃcient of determination (R²), and hypothesis testing using bootstrapping procedures. This approach
allows examination of both direct and indirect effects, particularly sequential mediation among variables,
to provide a comprehensive understanding of how perceived green marketing inﬂuences consumer
behavior and perceived market outcomes (Hair et al., 2019; Henseler et al., 2009).

5. DISCUSSION
5.1. The Effect of Perceived Green Marketing on Perceived
Environmental Value (H1)
The ﬁndings show that perceived green marketing positively and signiﬁcantly inﬂuences perceived
environmental value (β = 0.771, t = 26.486, p < 0.001). This result indicates that consumers who perceive
thrift stores as communicating strong environmental messages are more likely to recognize the
Page 18/25

environmental beneﬁts of thrift consumption. Messages concerning reuse, waste reduction, sustainable
fashion, and product life extension can therefore strengthen consumers' environmental evaluations.
This ﬁnding is consistent with Peattie (2001) and Testa et al. (2020), who emphasize the importance of
environmental communication and consumers' interpretation of green marketing. It also supports
perceived value theory (Zeithaml, 1988), which suggests that consumers evaluate products according to
the beneﬁts they perceive. In the thrift context, environmental beneﬁts become an important component
of perceived product value. The ﬁnding is further consistent with Chen and Chang (2012), Wang et al.
(2021), and recent evidence that green marketing can strengthen environmental awareness in
sustainable fashion consumption (Cui et al., 2025). Thus, H1 is supported.
5.2. The Effect of Perceived Environmental Value on
Perceived Market Disruption (H2)
Perceived environmental value has a positive and signiﬁcant effect on perceived market disruption (β = 
0.782, t = 34.325, p < 0.001). This ﬁnding suggests that consumers who perceive greater environmental
value in thrift products are also more likely to perceive changes in fashion-market dynamics, including
changes in demand, competition, pricing, and consumption patterns.
This result extends the conventional green-consumption literature, which commonly focuses on
environmental value as a determinant of purchase intention or sustainable behavior. The present ﬁnding
indicates that environmental value may also shape consumers' perceptions of broader market
transformation. From a circular economy perspective, secondhand consumption represents an
alternative to conventional linear fashion consumption by extending product life and encouraging reuse
(Bocken et al., 2016). Accordingly, consumers who recognize these environmental beneﬁts may
increasingly perceive thrift consumption as a force changing the conventional fashion market. H2 is
therefore supported.
5.3. The Effect of Perceived Market Disruption on
Consumer Switching Behavior (H3)
The results demonstrate that perceived market disruption positively and signiﬁcantly inﬂuences
consumer switching behavior (β = 0.738, t = 26.240, p < 0.001). This ﬁnding indicates that consumers
who perceive greater changes in the fashion market are more likely to shift from newly produced fashion
products toward thrift alternatives.
The result can be understood through the Theory of Planned Behavior (Ajzen, 1991), which emphasizes
the role of perceptions and evaluations in shaping behavioral responses. As consumers observe the
increasing visibility and acceptance of thrift products, they may reconsider established consumption
patterns and perceive secondhand products as increasingly attractive alternatives. The ﬁnding also
complements research showing that changing consumer preferences and sustainability considerations
can encourage sustainable purchasing behavior (Joshi & Rahman, 2021; Wang et al., 2021).
Page 19/25

Importantly, the ﬁnding positions consumers as active participants in market transformation. Market
disruption is therefore not only an external phenomenon but can also be reﬂected in consumers'
recognition of changing consumption alternatives. H3 is supported.
5.4. The Effect of Consumer Switching Behavior on
Perceived Impact on MSME Sustainability (H4)
Consumer switching behavior has a strong positive and signiﬁcant effect on perceived impact on MSME
sustainability (β = 0.912, t = 133.973, p < 0.001). This is the strongest direct relationship in the model,
indicating that consumers' switching behavior is strongly associated with their perceptions of
consequences for local MSMEs.
The result suggests that when consumers increasingly move from newly produced fashion products to
thrift alternatives, they are more likely to perceive that local MSMEs experience greater competitive
pressure, diﬃculties in growth, and potential effects on revenue and long-term sustainability. This
interpretation is consistent with the resource-based view, which emphasizes that ﬁrms with limited
resources may be more vulnerable to changes in competitive environments (Barney, 1991). It is also
consistent with research highlighting the challenges faced by SMEs under changing and disruptive
market conditions (Eggers, 2020; Kraus et al., 2020).
However, the construct represents perceived impact, rather than objectively measured MSME
performance. Therefore, the ﬁnding should be interpreted as consumers' subjective assessment of the
consequences of changing consumption behavior, rather than evidence that thrift consumption has
objectively reduced MSME performance. H4 is supported.
5.5. Mediation and Sequential Mechanism
The indirect-effect results provide further support for the proposed mechanism. Perceived environmental
value signiﬁcantly mediates the relationship between perceived green marketing and perceived market
disruption (β = 0.603, t = 16.103, p < 0.001). This indicates that green marketing is associated with
market perceptions through consumers' recognition of environmental value.
Furthermore, perceived market disruption signiﬁcantly mediates the relationship between perceived
environmental value and consumer switching behavior (β = 0.577, t = 16.096, p < 0.001). This suggests
that environmental value becomes behaviorally relevant through consumers' perceptions that the
fashion market is changing.
Consumer switching behavior also signiﬁcantly mediates the relationship between perceived market
disruption and perceived impact on MSME sustainability (β = 0.673, t = 23.244, p < 0.001). Thus,
perceived market changes are associated with perceived MSME consequences through actual changes
in consumers' purchasing preferences.
Page 20/25

Most importantly, the complete sequential indirect effect from perceived green marketing →  perceived
environmental value →  perceived market disruption →  consumer switching behavior →  perceived
impact on MSME sustainability is signiﬁcant (β = 0.406, t = 10.137, p < 0.001). This represents the central
contribution of the study. It demonstrates that the perceived consequences of green marketing extend
beyond environmental evaluations and individual consumption decisions to perceptions of market
transformation and MSME sustainability.
Overall, the ﬁndings reveal a potential sustainability trade-off. Thrift consumption may be perceived as
environmentally beneﬁcial while simultaneously being associated with competitive challenges for local
MSMEs. However, this does not necessarily mean that thrift consumption objectively harms MSMEs.
Instead, the ﬁndings highlight the importance of MSMEs adapting through green branding, circular
practices, product differentiation, and digital engagement. Such strategies can help local businesses
respond to changing sustainability expectations while maintaining competitiveness.
.
6. CONCLUSION
This study concludes that perceived green marketing plays an important role in shaping consumers'
perceptions and behavioral responses in the context of thrift-store consumption. The ﬁndings
demonstrate that perceived green marketing signiﬁcantly increases perceived environmental value,
which subsequently strengthens perceived market disruption and encourages consumer switching
behavior from newly produced fashion products toward thrift alternatives. Consumer switching behavior,
in turn, signiﬁcantly inﬂuences consumers' perceived impact on MSME sustainability. The signiﬁcant
sequential indirect effect further conﬁrms that the relationship between perceived green marketing and
perceived MSME sustainability operates through a chain of environmental value, market disruption, and
consumer switching behavior (β = 0.406, t = 10.137, p < 0.001). These ﬁndings highlight a potential
sustainability trade-off in which environmentally oriented consumption may be perceived as beneﬁcial
for environmental objectives while simultaneously creating competitive pressures for local MSMEs.
However, such market disruption should not necessarily be viewed solely as a threat, as MSMEs can
respond through sustainable product development, circular business practices, green branding, product
differentiation, and digital engagement. The study therefore contributes a consumer-based perspective
by demonstrating how sustainability-oriented marketing can extend beyond individual consumption
decisions to shape perceptions of broader market transformation and MSME sustainability.
