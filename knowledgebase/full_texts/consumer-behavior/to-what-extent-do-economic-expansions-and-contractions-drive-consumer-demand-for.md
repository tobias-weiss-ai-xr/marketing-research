# To what extent do economic expansions and contractions drive consumer demand for different categories of luxuries, including high-end, accessible, and quiet luxuries?

ISSN 2959-6130
Dean&Francis
Abstract:
The purpose of this paper is to investigate how different
categories of luxury goods respond to changes in economic
conditions. This study builds a three-tiered framework
to evaluate how macroeconomic drivers shape consumer
behavior for high-end, accessible, and quiet luxuries during
economic transitions. This study focuses on secondary
research by first conducting a Pearson correlation test to
analyze the relationship between macroeconomic indicators
and luxury sales, then reviewing academic journals,
financial reports, and news articles to use the Chinese
market as a case study. The data and correlation test reveal
that the GDP growth rate shows weak and statistically
insignificant correlations, while inflation volatility is
strongly and negatively correlated with luxury demand
across almost all categories, and the opposite is observed
for CPI. The behavior of different luxury tiers varies
with the characteristics of their customer segment. The
resilience of high-end and quiet luxury amid contractions
lies in the income stability of their targeted customers,
which includes high net-worth individuals. More evident
growth is observed in quiet luxury despite downturns as
preferences shift towards risk-averse investment in quality
and resonance with the brand’s cultural value. Moreover,
accessible luxuries experience volatility because the
middle class’ discretionary spending is highly sensitive to
fluctuations in the job market and inflation. Finally, this
research concludes that luxury consumption is not directly
driven by general economic growth but by conditions that
affect real consumer purchasing power.
Keywords: consumer behavior, economic cycles, luxury
categories, luxury consumption

1. Introduction
Having existed since the beginning of civilization, luxury
goods have dynamically evolved into different categories,
each targeting a specific customer segment [1]. Luxury
fashions serve a symbolic significance for the expression
of the ego and an outward representation of membership
in some group to which distinction is ascribed [2]. How -
ever, new and previously established segments of luxury
goods emerged during the decade, and cultivated customer
groups and marketing channels that act as key drivers re-
flecting the changes in consumer demand amid economic
recessions [3].
There are various factors affecting luxury demand, includ-
ing but not limited to psychological drivers treating luxury
consumption as a form of escape from reality [4], eco -
nomic growth [5], a brand’s global reputation [6], sustain-
ability and timelessness [3], and digital consumption [5].
Among the various drivers, the impact of macroeconomic
factors on luxury consumption has always been under dis-
cussion in academia. Namely, Linda Ni discussed luxury
consumption during an economic crisis [7], and Davari et
al. examined the determinants of personal luxury purchase
intentions in a recessionary environment [4]. While past
researchers often evaluated luxury as a whole or differen-
tiated luxury based on sectors, existing discussions over -
look the emerging categorical differences in the dynamic
luxury market.
Therefore, this paper integrates consumer taste and behav-
ior with luxury firms’ marketing strategies and revenue to
ultimately analyze their correlation with economic con -
ditions in China. This paper discusses three categories of
luxuries, including high-end luxuries with exclusive qual -
ity and global brand reputation [8], accessible luxuries
that are more affordable to those with limited purchasing
power, and quiet luxuries, which are culturally-rooted and
timeless pieces [9].
2. literature Review
2.1 Luxury goods classification
Although there is no single definition of luxury in aca -
demia [10], luxuries are often associated with qualities of
exclusivity, craftsmanship, and personalization [11]. In
this paper, luxury refers to goods with rarity, uniqueness,
and sophisticated craftsmanship that serve a personalized
meaning for consumers.
Historically, luxury goods were linked with wealth and
power and confined to the consumption of elites [1]. How-
ever, as luxury evolved over time, some luxuries became
more accessible to the emerging middle class [12], while
other brands strive to create products or services that ex -
emplify uniqueness and reputation [11], and still others
bring products of exceptional craftsmanship that tell dis -
tinct socio-cultural narratives [12]. Through examining
the major characteristics of luxury goods in the recent
decade, this paper categorizes luxuries into three groups:
high-end luxuries, accessible luxuries, and quiet luxuries.
High-end Luxuries
High-end luxuries are highly priced and globally rec -
ognized, many of which feature recognizable logos and
brand symbols that serve as a status symbol for their
consumer [13]. Characteristics of exclusivity and crafts -
manship are especially key to high-end luxuries, where
offers like limited editions sold only in specific seasons at
restrained amounts evoke consumers’ desire for consump-
tion [14]. Representative examples of high-end luxuries
include Gucci and Prada. The target customer group of
high- end luxuries is predominantly high net-worth in -
dividuals (HNWIs) who have a high willingness to pay
for premium due to desire for exclusivity and status, and
whose demand is relatively inelastic to prices due to their
substantial disposable income.
Accessible Luxuries
Accessible luxuries are those that still serve emotional
and symbolic benefits but are more affordable [1]. The
phenomenon of the globalization of luxuries follows the
digitalization of luxury markets in recent years [15]. Dig -
ital media platforms allow consumers to access luxuries
via the e-commerce applications Tmall Pavilion and JD,
and Tmall had been expanding to encompass more than
200 renowned luxury brands by 2021 [8]. Representative
brands include Coach and subsidiary brands under Tap -
estry, and common goods that fall into this category are
perfumes, accessories, and cosmetics [12]. The intended
consumers are the white-collar group and the emerging
middle-class from the second-and third-tier cities in Chi-
na.
Quiet Luxuries
Quiet luxuries are non-conspicuous and emphasize a min-
imalist style [10] that is refined through quality materials
and craftsmanship, creating narratives that resonate with
consumers [16]. To craft a brand story or unique cultural
narrative, quiet luxury brands adopt different branding
strategies. For instance, domestic luxury brands in Chi -
na, such as Shang Xia and Icicle, blend traditional Chi -
nese craftsmanship with modern design and sustainable
practices [17]. Rational consumers with discretionary
income and more cultural capital in a particular domain
who prefer subtle signals are consumers of quiet luxuries
[18]. Consumers of quiet luxuries do not pursue the status
symbol typically brought by luxuries, but rather treat the
luxury as a piece of timeless, sustainable form of art.

Economic patterns are often described by the business
cycle, where a country experiences alternating periods of
expansion, peak, contraction, and trough. Expansionary
periods are typically marked by rising output, consumer
confidence, employment, and inflation, whereas contrac -
tionary phases are associated with the opposite [19]. The
macroeconomic indicators of growth in real GDP, CPI,
inflation rate, and unemployment are key indicators of
China’s transition between the cyclical phases, as they
were used to evaluate the macroeconomic cycles China
has experienced since the onset of reform in the late 1970s
by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) [20].
Existing research analyses China’s striking patterns of
trend and cycle of macroeconomic time series through
extensive micro and macroeconomic databases [21].
Research about China’s financial cycle illustrated that it
exhibits fewer contractions but longer expansions, which
distinguishes it from mature market economies [22]. The
characteristic of high policy dependence in the expansion
stage is connected with the weakness of the built-in stabil-
ity mechanism [22]. Meanwhile, China’s investment- driv-
en growth model, with the average investment-to-GDP
ratio amounting to 45 percent [23], constitutes shorter
contractions as the government intervenes to stabilize in -
vestment [21].
Referencing the patterns above that scholars identify,
China’s macroeconomy from 2015 to 2025 can be clas -
sified into periods of expansion and contraction. China
experienced a period of stable expansion following an
economic boom from 2015 to 2019, where GDP rose, and
inflation showed a decreasing trend (Figure 2 and Figure
3). COVID-19 cast a significant recessionary shock on
the country from 2019 to 2020, when China experienced
stagflation (Figure 1 and Figure 2). In 2021, expansionary
fiscal and monetary policies drove the economy and stim-
ulated consumption, leading to a tremendous bounce back
in the GDP growth rate. However, this was only a tem -
porary rebound, and China’s GDP growth soon dropped
back, and inflation rose again in 2022, marking another
period of contraction and stabilization efforts that lasted
through 2025 [24].

2.3 Economic conditions’ effect on Chinese lux-
uries
Macroeconomic conditions influence luxury consumption
primarily through their impact on consumers’ purchas -
ing habits, disposable income, and market expectations.
During periods of economic expansion, rising wages and
improved employment increase households’ disposable
income. Since luxury goods are generally income elastic,
spending capacity on luxury markets grows more than
proportionately, especially for middle-market consumers
[1]. Dahm points out that this income effect facilitated
seven years of global luxury market growth, where in
2015, the market surpassed the €1 trillion mark, reaching
€1.2 trillion in 2017. The world has seen a growing mid-
dle-class population and, accordingly, an expansion of
entry-level priced luxury goods offered by luxury brands
[15]. However, the COVID-19 pandemic cast a consider -
able shock on the luxury market worldwide.
Salah Hassan’s research demonstrated that luxury demand
does not utterly collapse under economic recession. Rath-
er, consumption declines unevenly for different brands [6].
High-end segments and investment-oriented purchases
are observed to be more resilient to economic recessions.
This observation is supported by the 2008 global financial
crisis, in which Louis Vuitton and Gucci maintained a
relatively stable performance due to their perceived brand
globalness and superiority from 2008 to 2010 [7]. Mean-
while, Cartier jewelry has continuously experienced up
to 10% appreciation per year [28], revealing inelasticity
in consumers with high disposable income and optimism
who have lower sensitivity to short-term income fluctua -
tions.
In contrast, accessible luxury brands whose consumer
base is more closely tied to middle-class income growth
are more vulnerable to macroeconomic slowdowns. Low-
er-income consumers changed their consumption patterns
of luxuries more dramatically than high-income con -
sumers because they have less job security and are more
susceptible to price changes [7]. Granted, more luxury
brands have been expanding into non-first-tier cities from
2022 to 2024 [29], reflecting a growing market potential
in second-or third-tier cities dominated by middle-class
consumers. Therefore, although economic downturns
prompt consumers to reassess non-essential expenditures,
existing research consistently finds that the impact is un -
even across brands and product categories due to product
characteristics and the socioeconomic profile of target
consumers.
2.4 Theories’ application in the luxury market
Economic fluctuations exert varying impacts on the Chi -
nese luxury market, reflecting the distinct behaviors of
luxury goods across different price tiers and the respons -
es of different consumer groups. From the most classic
Veblen, snob, and bandwagon effects to the foundational
Keynesian and neo-classical consumption theories, and
then to price level theories supported by modern luxury
market data, there are numerous theories that, together,
explain the root causes of the heterogeneous economic
impacts observed in the Chinese luxury market.
Thorstein Veblen’s theory of conspicuous consumption
depicts how the consumption of a good increases because
it bears a higher rather than lower price [30]. Conspicuous
consumption refers to the purchase of expensive goods to
display wealth wastefully rather than to attempt to satisfy
more utilitarian needs of the consumer [31]. The theo -
ry applies to the high-end and accessible luxury sector,
where, despite the price of the luxury, the motive for pur -
chasing is to gain or maintain a higher social status.
The snob effect illustrates how consumers employ con -
sumption to gain uniqueness, and their consumption
decreases when more people are also consuming the
commodity, contrary to the bandwagon effect [30]. Quiet
luxury consumption can be seen as partially driven by the
snob effect, where consumers seek individual exclusivity
instead of affiliation to a social group.
The Keynesian theory of consumption illustrates that the
marginal propensity to consume (MPC) decreases when
income expectations are uncertain, but it is likely to aver -
age out for the community as a whole [32]. On the other
hand, the neoclassical consumption function illustrates an
alternative: income uncertainty might loosen an agent's
budget constraint and encourage extra consumption [33].
Nevertheless, the rate of luxury purchases is tightly con -
nected with income. A greater proportion of income is
spent on luxury goods as a result of an increase in income,
and high-income individuals tend to be heavy luxury con -
sumers [34].
3. Methodology
With the aim of systematically assessing the impact of
economic downturns on different categories of luxury
demand, this study focuses on secondary research by first
conducting a Pearson correlation test to analyze the rela -
tionship between macroeconomic indicators and luxury
sales, then using the Chinese market as a case study to
ultimately evaluate how the drivers shape tiered consumer
behavior during economic transitions. Sources are eval -
uated for the academic credentials of the authors, priori -
tizing peer-reviewed scholarly research and professional
data from global consulting firms and official brand annu-
al reports, ensuring that the analysis balances theoretical
frameworks with real-time financial market data.

3.2 Statistical Analysis
To address the research question, this study employs a
combination of macroeconomic data and industry-specific
data from 2015 onward. By conducting a correlation anal -
ysis between representative macroeconomic indicators
and luxury market performance, the varied responsiveness
of luxury demand across categories and economic phases
is analyzed.
The selected indicators are the GDP growth rate, year-on-
year change in the inflation rate, unemployment rate, and
CPI (base year=2010), and global luxury market indica -
tors, including the size of the global personal luxury mar -
ket and annual revenues of representative brands: Hermès
(high-end luxury), Coach (accessible luxury), and Brunel-
lo Cucinelli (quiet luxury). The selected indicators could
represent the stages of the business cycle because they
are widely referenced in macroeconomic studies and have
been applied in IMF research on China’s macroeconomic
fluctuations.
The impact of economic conditions on the luxury goods
sector is revealed through brand- specific regional revenue
data and global luxury market performance. Secondary
data are retrieved from reputable consulting firms such
as PwC, McKinsey, Bain, and Savills, ensuring their ac -
curacy. Empirical data about the change in global luxury
market size provides an overview of the market, while
regional sales data of companies reveal luxury purchase
amounts in Asia, or more specifically, Greater China. Sta-
tistics from Bain's annual reports document that China has
been playing a dominant role in world luxury consump -
tion since 2015 [35], suggesting that global or regional
sales revenue data of brands can be used as a proxy for
Chinese demand.
The individual brands’ annual revenue by region and rev-
enue by sector originate from the companies’ published
financial reports, a primary source, safeguarding their
credibility. The wide range of sources sets the basis for
assessing how different luxury categories respond uneven-
ly to macroeconomic changes. Overall, the validity of the
data is ensured through reliance on internationally recog-
nized consulting firms and luxury brands with established
methodologies and transparent reporting standards. By
referencing figures across multiple sources where possi -
ble, data consistency is maintained.
4. Analysis and Discussion
China continued to serve as a key driving force for global
luxury consumption from 2015, when Chinese consump -
tion accounted for 31% of the global luxury market, the
largest portion among all countries [35]. Bain reports
that China’s share of the global personal luxury market
has been expanding at a rate of 18%, exceeding all other
countries in 2017. Meanwhile, in 2022, China’s share
of the Asia-Pacific region of the global personal luxury
goods market reached 44.74% [36]. These figures under -
score that global and Asia-Pacific luxury goods sales data
can signal Chinese luxury sales because its market con -
tributes a substantial share of total sales. Thus, this section
first investigates a global relationship between economic
conditions and luxury sales, then dives into how shifts in
the aggregate revenue reflect trends in the dominant Chi-
nese market.

Results demonstrated that the GDP growth rate and unem-

of changes in luxury demand, as their correlations are
weak and insignificant, with coefficients generally below
|0.35| (Figure 4), indicating a highly random relationship
between the datasets. Globally, 47% of luxury sales are
from top spenders [43], and luxury goods have high price
elasticity because they are not necessities, coinciding
with the discovery of Alghanim and Ndubisi [34]. Their
perceived value depends on factors like exclusivity, crafts-
manship, and status symbol, as illustrated by Sanz-Lopez
[11]. The core limitation of GDP growth lies in its nature
as an aggregate measure of economic output and activity
that inadequately captures the purchasing power of afflu -
ent consumers who fuel almost half of the global luxury
demand.
Conversely, CPI and inflation are more significant drivers.
Inflation affects all consumers unevenly, with high-income
households being better positioned with higher disposable
incomes and access to financial instruments [44]. As infla-
tion erodes the real value of money, consumers may pri -
oritize immediate consumption of pieces that retain value
because savings lose purchasing power. LVMH displays
the highest significant correlation with CPI (0.900). Since
it manages a diverse portfolio of brands across all tiers, it
acts as a microcosm for the global luxury market, mirror -
ing how inflation and CPI directly reflect changes in the
cost of living and the real value of money. Thus, CPI and
inflation are more effective indicators of luxury consump-
tion because they readily affect consumers’ choices for
luxury, such as preference towards timeless investment
pieces or unique seasonal products.
4.1.1 category-level Responses to Macroeconomic
Fluctuations
Luxury categories are tiers that define consumer prefer -
ences and seek value. Among all categories of luxuries,
China’s CPI and the year-on-year change in the inflation
rate are proven to show a strong and significant correla -
tion with global luxury demand. This suggests that lux -
ury consumption can be driven by asset inflation but is
also highly sensitive to the economic uncertainty that is
brought along.
Sustained increases in price levels, which are often asso -
ciated with asset inflation and eroding purchasing power,
are accompanied by rising luxury demand. The quiet
luxury sector represented by Brunello Cucinelli appeared
to have the strongest positive relation with CPI and the
second largest negative relation with inflation among
representatives of the categories. The accessible sector
represented by Coach exhibited a moderately strong pos -
itive correlation with CPI of 0.768, and high-end luxuries
represented by Prada showed a 0.742 correlation, which
is still evident but the least among the three categories.
However, this trend reverses when examining the velocity
of inflation. Prada’s global revenue is the most negatively
associated with the change in the inflation rate, followed
by Brunello Cucinelli and finally Hermes.
While high-end luxury can withstand high price levels, it
is uniquely sensitive to macroeconomic uncertainty rep -
resented by rapid inflationary shifts. For the wealthy, their
spending on luxury depends on their portfolio’s perfor -
mance, which is connected to the volatility of the econom-
ic context. High price levels don’t affect their purchasing
power to a great extent, yet the high speed at which prices
are rising indicates instability and unfavorable conditions
for purchasing discretionary luxury goods because they
are prone to lose value amid economic uncertainties. As
Aït-Sahalia et al. propose, the wealthy’s consumption
of basic necessities is saturated, and wealth shocks are
reflected in the consumption of luxury goods [45]. Their
choice of luxuries as a form of investment becomes less
secure under rapidly transforming market conditions,
and the high-end spenders choose to protect their broader

The sectors of luxury represent functional categories that
reflect the material preferences of the market. Their be -

investment pieces, representing high-end luxuries with
high prices, limited supply, strong brand heritage, and tan-

subject to accelerating inflationary caution.
As observed in expansionary periods, the growth in
wealth of HNWIs in China since 2019 was the foundation
that allowed HNWIs to remain resilient when the middle
class began to struggle in 2023. By 2022, their wealth in
trillions of RMB rose by 11%, and Chinese millionaires
generate an estimated 42% of domestic luxury purchases,
reflecting their dominating contribution to local demand.
Asset preservation and personalized experience are pri -
oritized for HNWIs, who favor luxuries’ role as stores of
value and symbols of exclusivity, making luxuries sensi -
tive to inflationary uncertainty that may delay discretion -

up to 40% of the market, has contracted slightly, with a
CAGR between -1% and -3% [58]. The geographic shift
of luxury brands opening and the wealth expansion of
consumers explain that high-end luxury consumers may
postpone purchases amid uncertainty, pushing brands to
capture rising consumer pools while maintaining exclusiv-
ity for HNWIs with financial stability.

5. Evaluation
This paper aims to discuss the cyclical economic periods
China experienced in the past decade and their correlation
with luxury consumption patterns. The study adopts a
categorical framework that divides luxury consumption
into high-end, accessible, and quiet segments, allowing
for more nuanced analysis of consumer responses to eco -
nomic expansion and contraction. The analysis is strong
in its incorporation of diverse secondary resources to
weave an interconnected explanation for the varying re -
sponses of different luxury categories to macroeconomic
indicators. In particular, the distinction between economic
responsiveness and resilience enables a more precise in -
terpretation of why different luxury categories may remain
stable yet still exhibit strong correlations with economic
variables. The data analysis is based on multiple luxury
brands’ global sales revenue and four major economic in -
dicators, providing an overview of trends in economic cy -
cles and the luxury market while also visualizing changes
in annual revenue of specific brands.
However, the analysis is subject to several limitations.
Firstly, it is limited by the availability of categorical sales
revenue data and China’s 2025 macroeconomic indicators.
For instance, the change in inflation has not been pub -
lished. Most evidence is aggregate market data, and cate -
gorical analysis is represented by one to two luxury brands
only, which limits the precision in distinguishing different
behaviors of luxury. Similarly, consumer behavior is in -
ferred through store openings, brand strategies, and sales
revenue rather than direct household-level consumption
data, which limits the accuracy of the analysis. Future
investigations could incorporate such information instead
of using a single brand’s sector revenue as a benchmark to
consider the entire Chinese landscape.
Moreover, some useful data cannot be utilized because
they do not cover the entire timeframe for research, such
as the total Chinese sector sales of luxury. Additionally,
the study focuses on a decade in which the economic con-
traction was shaped by the COVID-19 shock and subse -
quent recovery, which may have been driven by economic
rebounds rather than classical contractionary pressures.
This constrains the analysis of long-term structural shifts
in consumer taste.
Future research could address these limitations by em -
ploying firm-level data and large- scale consumer sur -
veys to test the robustness of the identified mechanisms.
Nonetheless, within its scope, the study provides a mech -
anism-driven explanation of how economic conditions
shape luxury demand across categories and consumer seg-
ments.
6. conclusion
In conclusion, the data and correlation test reveal that
luxury consumption is not directly driven by general eco -
nomic growth but by conditions that affect real consumer
purchasing power. Across nearly all luxury indicators, the
GDP growth rate shows weak and statistically insignifi-
cant correlations. This is because GDP reflects a country’s
overall economic situation, but luxury demand is highly
dependent on consumers with relatively high purchasing
power. On the other hand, inflation volatility is strongly
and negatively correlated with luxury demand across al -
most all categories, and vice versa is observed for CPI.
Ultimately, the performance across luxury categories re -
flects how macroeconomic contractions unevenly impact
the assets of different consumer tiers. The resilience of
high- end and quiet luxury lies in the income stability of
HNWIs, VICs, and Gen Z, who are inheriting wealth from
their previous generation. Their substantial assets are
largely independent of cyclical labor income, and these
elite groups may see their net worth increasing during
volatility through investment in value-preserving luxuries.
They view luxury as a store of value and a marker of cul -
tural capital rather than mere status signals, leading to a
