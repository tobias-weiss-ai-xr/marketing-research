Abstract
We look at how household spending changes when labor markets have bigger wage gaps, less stable jobs, and
more flexible work forms; we use the utility maximization framework and inter-temporal budget constraint to
see how these three labor market changes a ffect personal consumption choices. We find two main influence
paths; one works through the budget constraint itself, changing what people can buy now and what they expect
to buy later; the other works through income expectations, where higher uncertainty makes people save more
for safety and spend less right now. These two paths do not work the same for all consumers; our analysis of
group differences shows that employment type, income level, and age group all change how people react.
Formal workers have fairly predictable budget limits, while flexible workers deal with much more changeable
income. Low and middle -income households are more likely to face slow wage growth, while high -income
households may gain from higher pay for skilled work. Young workers usually put off big purchases; middle-
aged workers have fixed financial duties that are hard to change; older workers have less time to make up for
income drops. These differences mean that policies to keep consumption stable should target different groups
separately, instead of only using broad demand management methods. For the research method, this paper
combines existing theoretical and empirical studies to build a complete micro-economic framework; it follows
how labor market shocks move through budget constraints and income expectations, and finally lead to
different consumption results for different groups.

1. Introduction
In recent years, many different factors have changed the global labor market; digital technology
development, industrial restructure after the pandemic, and the shift to low-carbon economy all play a part [1].
These changes are more than just changes in to tal employment numbers; they have changed how wages are
distributed, made jobs less stable, and made flexible work much more common. All these changes directly
affect how households earn money, how much they expect to earn in the future, and finally how th ey spend
their money. Since household consumption is still the main driver of economic growth, understanding how

individual consumers adjust their spending when labor markets change has become an important topic for
studying modern economies.
Current research still has two main gaps. First, most studies look at overall relationships; they do not
carefully track the micro-level influence paths step by step [2]. Second, most research treats all consumers as
the same group; it does not pay enough attention to how employment type, income level, and age make people
respond differently to the same labor market change. To fill these two gaps, this paper tries to answer the
following question:
Through what micro -economic paths do wage polarization, falling job stability, and more flexible
employment change consumer behavior, and how do these effects differ across different groups of people?
To answer this question, we build our analysis on two basic micro-economic theories: utility maximization
and the inter-temporal budget constraint. We find two paths that connect labor market change to consumption
decisions; one path makes the budget const raint tighter or looser itself, the other changes long -term income
expectations and how much people save for precaution. We then divide the analysis along three dimensions:
employment type (formal work vs flexible work), income level, and age group.
This paper makes both theoretical and practical contributions. On the theory side, we expand the use of
classical consumption models — the permanent income hypothesis, the life -cycle hypothesis, and
precautionary saving theory — and add labor market structural change as a reason why different people have
different consumption responses. On the practical side, it gives a micro -economic reason to move past broad
aggregate demand policies, and toward policies that target the specific influence paths tha t affect the most
exposed groups.
2. Literature Review
2.1 Defining Labor Market Change and Consumer Behavior
We define labor market change in this paper as long -term structural shifts in employment structure, wage
distribution, and work forms; it is not the same as short-term cyclical changes. Following the method from [3],
we measure labor market change along th ree dimensions: wage gaps across different skill groups, higher
employment instability and unemployment risk, and the fast growth of flexible, non -standard work. This
framework covers both global trends recorded by the ILO and World Bank, and the specific path of
employment change in China.
For the consumer side, we define consumer behavior within the standard micro -economic framework; it
refers to inter-temporal consumption and saving decisions that people make to get the most utility under budget
limits. Instead of looking at total househol d consumption (the macro approach), this paper focuses on two
micro-level indicators. One is the marginal propensity to consume (MPC), which measures how much of each
extra unit of income goes to consumption; the other is consumption structure, measured by  the share of basic
living, development, and enjoyment spending in total expenditure. These two indicators show the decision -
making logic that labor market shocks actually disturb.
2.2 Theoretical Foundations
The connection between labor markets and consumption has a long history in economic theory. Keynes [4],
in the General Theory, was the first to make the most direct point: current consumption depends on current
disposable income, and the labor market is th e main source of that income. The Absolute Income Hypothesis
is simple, but its meaning is important; when labor markets change wages or employment levels, consumption
changes along with them.
Friedman [5] expanded this idea with the Permanent Income Hypothesis. He argued that consumers base
their spending on long -run expected income, not short -term changes. This is especially relevant for
understanding labor market change; if job stability shap es people’s expectations of permanent income, then
falling job security may lower consumption even when current wages stay the same. Modigliani and Brumberg
[6] further developed the life -cycle view; they showed that consumers spread their consumption even ly over
their whole life, borrowing when young, saving in middle age, and using savings when retired. This life-cycle

view is very important for understanding why labor market shocks affect different age groups differently; we
discuss this point more in Section 4.
Leland [7] introduced the idea of precautionary saving. He showed that when income is uncertain and
people are careful about risk, they save more and spend less right now. Stephens [8] gave early empirical
evidence for this; he found that expectations of j ob loss significantly lower household spending, especially
spending on durable goods. Dynan et al. [9] added a distribution angle: high-income households have a much
lower MPC than low and middle -income groups. This means wage polarization, which makes income gaps
wider, may lower the overall consumption tendency of the whole economy.
Evidence from China is largely in line with these mechanisms. Chamon  and Prasad [10] found that rising
saving rates among urban Chinese households came from growing income uncertainty, caused by labor market
and pension system reforms; spending on development and enjoyment was squeezed the most. Feng et al. [11]
showed tha t wider pension coverage greatly reduces precautionary saving among urban households; this
finding is directly relevant for flexible workers, who often do not have such coverage. Blanchflower and
Machin [3] also noted that flexible workers have a much stro nger motive to save for precaution than formal
employees.
2.3 Research Gaps and Contributions
Even with all this existing research, there are still important gaps. Most of the literature stays at the macro
level; it looks at the total consumption effects of labor market changes, but does not break down the micro -
level influence paths in a systemati c way. There is also not enough attention paid to heterogeneity — how
employment type, income bracket, and age group change the way people actually experience  and respond to
labor market shocks. Without adding heterogeneity into the micro -level utility framework, existing research
cannot provide the detailed theoretical support that targeted consumption policies need.
The methodological innovation of this paper is that it puts heterogeneity across employment type, income
level, and age group into a unified micro -economic utility framework. In this way, it provides a systematic,
broken-down account of the influence  mechanisms that connect structural labor market change to individual
consumption decisions.
3. Impact Mechanism of Labor Market Changes on Consumer Behavior
3.1 Changes in Budget Constraints
The inter-temporal budget constraint is the logical starting point. The constraint states that the present value
of lifetime consumption must equal the present value of lifetime labor income plus initial wealth [6]. Labor
market changes disrupt this constraint in three distinct ways:
The first is the most direct: wage polarization reshapes current disposable income. Automation and
digitalization have widened the skill premium, pushing the income distribution outward for high -skilled
workers while stagnating wages for low - and middle -skilled workers [12, 13]. In Keynesian terms, this
translates immediately into divergent consumption capacities—the budget line expands for some and contracts
for others.
The second is subtler but arguably more consequential: declining employment stability shrinks the present
value of expected future income. Rising unemployment risk and shorter job tenures reduce the discounted
stream of earnings that finances future consum ption, forcing households to reallocate spending and saving
across their remaining working life [14]. This is not just about losing a job today; it is about the erosion of
confidence in what one will earn tomorrow.
The third mechanism is qualitatively different from the first two. The expansion of flexible employment
introduces income volatility that undermines the predictability of the budget constraint itself. Even when
expected income is unchanged, a more volatile  income process raises the variance of feasible consumption
paths, effectively tightening the constraint through liquidity considerations [15]. A gig worker who earns the
same average monthly income as a formal employee faces a fundamentally different—and tighter—budget set
because the variance matters independently of the mean.

Taken together, these three mechanisms ensure that labor market change operates as a structural shock to
the budget constraint underlying every consumption decision. The constraint does not simply shift inward or
outward; its shape and stability change in ways that differ across workers.
3.2 Adjustment of Income Expectations
The second transmission path runs through income expectations. Friedman's Permanent Income Hypothesis
[5] is the anchor here: current consumption is governed by permanent income, not transitory fluctuations. This
means that shifts in long -run income expect ations—not just current wage changes —are what trigger
consumption adjustment. Formally, while the budget constraint channel operates through shifts in the feasible
consumption set, the income expectation channel operates through revisions to the permanent income
component of the utility -maximization problem—the two channels are linked by the fact that any change in
the constraint's shape feeds into the consumer's assessment of long-run earnings.
Wage polarization affects this mechanism by altering the expected lifetime trajectory of earnings. Workers
in shrinking middle-skill occupations are not just earning less today; they are revising their permanent income
downward, and with it their sustainab le consumption path. Workers in expanding high -skill segments do the
opposite [12].
Declining employment stability and rising unemployment risk operate differently. They raise the variance
of expected future income rather than its mean. Leland's framework [7] predicts that under prudent preferences,
income uncertainty strengthens precauti onary saving and crowds out current consumption.Stephens [8]
provides empirical evidence that job-loss expectations significantly depress household spending, with durable
and discretionary goods hit hardest.
Flexible employment adds a third layer. Flexible workers often have weaker access to unemployment
insurance and pension coverage, increasing their exposure to income volatility [3,16]. Greater income
uncertainty may therefore strengthen precautionary saving and shift consumption toward necessities [16].
The budget constraint and income expectation channels do not operate in isolation. They interact: a shock
that tightens the current budget constraint while simultaneously raising income uncertainty produces a
consumption response larger than either channel  alone would predict. The next section examines how this
interaction plays out differently across consumer groups.
4. Heterogeneity Analysis of Consumer Behavior
The two transmission channels identified above —budget constraint adjustment and income expectation
revision—do not operate with uniform intensity across all consumers. Employment type, income level, and
age cohort systematically shape how each channel func tions, producing distinct patterns of consumption
response. This section examines each dimension in turn.
4.1 Employment Type: Formal Versus Flexible Workers
The gap between formal and flexible employment is not just about income levels —it is about the very
nature of the budget constraint each group faces. Formal employees typically have stable contracts, employer-
provided social insurance, and predictable wage trajectories. Flexible workers —gig-economy participants,
temporary contract wo rkers, the self -employed in non -standard arrangements —face pronounced income
volatility, limited access to unemployment insurance, and weaker pension coverage [3].
These structural differences mean that the two channels operate differently across the two groups. For a
formal worker, a negative wage shock shifts the budget line inward, but the shift is predictable. The worker
can re-optimize along a known constraint. For flexible workers, income volatility makes the constraint less
predictable even when expected income is unchanged. Higher income volatility may therefore strengthen
precautionary saving and suppress current spending [3,16].
The practical implication is clear. Facing the same adverse labor market shock, formal workers can partially
buffer consumption through social insurance and stable expectations. Flexible workers typically have weaker
buffers and may reduce current consumption by shifting spending toward necessities and postponing durable-

goods purchases. The two channels reinforce each other for flexible workers, producing a consumption
contraction that income changes alone cannot explain.
4.2 Income Level
For low - and middle -income consumers, the budget constraint channel is likely to be strongest. These
groups are disproportionately concentrated in middle-skill occupations vulnerable to automation-driven wage
stagnation and displacement [12]. When wage polarization compresses their earnings, the budget line tightens
with almost no buffer —savings are minimal, and consumption is already concentrated in necessities. The
income expectation channel may reinforce this effect: workers in shrinking middle -skill occupations face a
structural downward revision of permanent income, with limited prospects of re -employment at comparable
wages. Precautionary saving intensifies, further crowding out current consumption and shifting the spending
mix toward subsistence categories [10].
High-income consumers may experience the two channels differently. The skill premium that drives wage
polarization simultaneously expands the budget line for high -skilled workers, increasing current disposable
income and widening the affordable consumption  set [12]. Through the expectation channel, rising demand
for high -skilled labor lifts the expected lifetime earnings trajectory, reducing the perceived need for
precautionary saving and releasing resources for current spending. This may shift consumption toward
developmental and enjoyment categories, with durable goods and services absorbing a growing share of total
expenditure.
The pattern that emerges is one of consumption polarization mirroring the wage polarization in the labor
market. The same structural forces —automation, digital transformation, and the decline of middle -skill
occupations—simultaneously widen the consumption  possibilities of high -income households and narrow
those of low- and middle-income households. Budget constraints diverge mechanically with income; income
expectations diverge endogenously as workers in different segments of the labor market form fundamen tally
different assessments of their future earnings.
4.3 Age Cohort
Age matters because it determines the time horizon over which consumption adjustment can occur.
Modigliani and Brumberg's life -cycle framework [6] provides the organizing logic: consumers at different
stages face systematically different income profiles, consumption needs, and adjustment windows.
Young consumers—roughly 25 to 35 —are in the early accumulation phase. Their current income is low
relative to expected lifetime earnings, and they face high demand for durables, housing, and human capital
investment. Labor market shocks affect this group m ainly through the expectation channel rather than the
budget constraint: because current income is a small share of lifetime resources, moderate wage changes have
limited direct effect on the feasible consumption set. What matters more is that flexible emp loyment is
prevalent among young workers, introducing substantial uncertainty into permanent income expectations. The
behavioral response takes the form of postponement —delayed home purchases, deferred marriage and
childbearing, and increased investment in education as a hedge against labor market risk.
Middle-aged consumers—roughly 36 to 55—are at the peak of the earnings profile but simultaneously bear
the heaviest consumption burdens. Mortgage payments, children's education, and elderly care obligations make
their consumption basket highly rigid. For t his group, labor market shocks are most consequential because
they strike at the point of maximum financial obligation. Any income loss directly threatens the capacity to
meet fixed commitments. Unemployment risk at this stage inflicts the greatest damage on permanent income
expectations—the remaining working life is still substantial, but the cost of occupational retooling is high.
Stephens [8] documents that elevated job-loss expectations among middle-aged workers significantly depress
household consumpti on, especially of durable goods. The behavioral response is a sharp increase in
precautionary saving and a forced compression of discretionary spending, while subsistence spending is
maintained.
Consumers approaching retirement —55 and above —face the narrowest adjustment window. They have
entered the dis -saving phase, and consumption is increasingly financed by accumulated wealth and pension
income rather than current labor earnings. Labor market s hocks affect this group through a distinctive

mechanism: the choice set  narrows to delayed retirement or premature labor market exit. The pension
replacement rate becomes the decisive parameter. Feng et al. [11] demonstrate that expanded pension coverage
significantly reduces precautionary saving among urban Chinese households, underscoring the critical role of
institutional insurance for this cohort. Where pension coverage is inadequate, older workers facing
employment displacement exhibit the most conservative consumption behavior of all groups—the compressed
time horizon leaves no room for income recovery.
Across all three dimensions, the analysis suggests that labor market shocks need not have uniform
consumption effects. Employment type determines the volatility of the constraint, income level determines the
buffer space available for adjustment, and age d etermines the time horizon over which adjustment can occur.
These dimensions interact in practice—a middle-aged flexible worker with low income faces the most severe
consumption compression—and together they explain why aggregate consumption responds unevenly to labor
market structural change.
5. Conclusion
This paper studies how wage polarization, falling job stability, and the growth of flexible employment
change consumer behavior; it works through two interacting micro -economic channels: budget constraint
adjustment and income expectation revision.
The budget constraint channel changes what consumers can afford now and what they expect to afford later;
the income expectation channel makes precautionary saving stronger when future earnings are more uncertain,
and this crowds out current spending even when current income does not change. The main value of this paper
is showing that these channels work differently across three dimensions. Employment type decides whether
the budget constraint changes in a predictable way or becomes unstable itself; income level decides how much
buffer people have to adjust. Low and middle -income households face tight limits, while high -income
households gain from the skill premium. Age defines how much time people have to recover: young workers
put off big purchases; middle-aged workers cut back on optional spending because they have fixed obligations;
older workers have the smallest window for adjustment.
By adding this heterogeneity into the micro -level utility framework, this paper gives a systematic
explanation of how structural labor market change leads to different consumption outcomes. The analysis also
has policy meanings. Because influence mechanisms differ across groups, targeted measures can work together
with broad aggregate demand management. Expanding social insurance for flexible workers could help
stabilize their budget limits and reduce excessive precautionary saving; retraining and skill -upgrading
programs for middle -skill workers could ease the wage polarization that limits their consumption;
strengthening pension systems could make older workers less vulnerable when they lose jobs. These measures
target the specific ways that labor market change lowers consumption, instead of only relying on broad demand
stimulus.
We should also note several limitations of this study. The analysis is theoretical; it is based on existing
evidence rather than original empirical data. The three heterogeneity dimensions are treated as separate, but in
reality they work together; for example, a middle -aged flexible worker with low income faces combined
pressures that separate analysis can only partly show. In addition, the framework does not consider institutional
differences across countries, which may change how strong the identified influence channels are. Future
empirical research using household panel data is needed to test and measure the channels identified here, and
to estimate the interaction effects that this theoretical framework suggests.
