On the day I started writing this review of Tatiana Konrad’s edited collection, Plastics, Environment,
Culture, and the Politics of Waste, the headline on the evening news was the results just in from
Geneva, where intergovernmental negotiations on a global treaty to end plastic pollution had
broken down. The splintering was attributed to the divisive yet critical issue of plastic production.
Where proponents for waste reduction argued for cuts and the phasing out of toxic materials,
nations with greater economic interests in plastic production favored maintaining those materials
in circulation through increased recycling and reuse.  While the absence of an agreement
represents a setback, it is important to acknowledge that the multinational discourse signals
some progress insofar as strategies to address plastic pollution have, for too long, focused on
localized efforts and individual consumer behaviors rather than coordinated systemic change.
An awareness of the inherently transnational nature of the plastic problem is one of the strengths,
too, of Konrad’s engaging and insightful collection of essays, which variously map the deep
entanglement of plastics with the fossil fuel industries and petrocultures that have come to
dominate everyday life under modern capitalism. The book is arranged in a five-part structure,
bringing together multidisciplinary contributions from established and emerging scholars,
grouped thematically around the topics of plastic lives, plastic proliferation, plastics in art and
literature, and their future. Its publication arrives at a moment of heightened attention to plastics,
as the impact of microplastics on the human body and our oceans, most notably, is of growing
concern to the public, scientists, and scholars. What distinguishes Konrad’s Plastics, Environment,
Culture, and the Politics of Waste is the proposition that culture represents a hitherto neglected
lens for analyzing the root causes of plastic waste. In this way, the volume responds to “the crucial
necessity of shifting focus away from plastic as the frame of reference and bringing the gaze back
to human social relations, which are responsible for transforming and destroying the environment
through multiple means, including through plastic” (Konrad 2023, 3).
A fast-paced foreword by Cymene Howe sketches the book’s terrain with a survey of plastic’s
many contradictions. Plastic is simultaneously a beautiful thing, a terrifying thing, an impervious
thing, a leaky thing, and a regenerative thing. Plastic is plasticity, writes Howe, just as it is
multiple, geomorphological, a great pretender, and ultimately: everywhere (Howe 2023). Konrad’s
introduction, “The Petroproduct: On Plastics, Capitalism, and Oil,” clearly locates the ubiquity of
plastic as a political-economic and social issue, rather than solely an environmental one. As such,
the volume builds on and extends the critical work in the energy humanities more generally and,

more specifically, in the field of petrocultures. Its wide-ranging contributions share a concern for
the culture of plastic consumption and the “material agency of plastic” (Konrad 2023, 3), each
highlighting the role of human actions in shaping the ecological crisis that plastic waste embodies.
Under the rubric of “Plastic Lives,” essays in the first part historicize the development of plastic
into one of the key materials of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. From its initial application
in military technologies to its role in accelerating mass consumerism in the 1950s, plastics
received heightened attention in the 1960s Pop era, then again in the postmodernist 1980s, until
their recent resurgence during the COVID-19 pandemic. In “Plastics: What Are They Good For?”
Louise Dennis is one of the few contributors to argue for the merits of plastics, noting some of
their beneficial applications and suggesting that plastics may be good for many things “as long as
they are thoughtfully used for the best applications, looked after, and disposed of appropriately”
(41). One suspects that the authors of the subsequent chapters might respectfully disagree. The
essays that follow mostly highlight the extent of plastics’ misuse and the inadequacy of current
processes to handle the types of plastic produced and the extraordinary volume of it, which
requires responsible disposal.
Indeed, the failure to properly care for, “look after,” or even notice plastic is part of the “dreamwork”
(89) that it performs in the oil economy (Boetzkes and Feldman 2023). Angela Cope’s short
material history, “How Hula Hoops Changed Hygiene: From Damp-Cloth Utopianism to Chemical
Cleaning,” considers the postwar development of Low- Density Polyethylene (LDPE) and High-
Density Polyethylene (HDPE) and their application firstly in novel consumer items like Hula Hoops
and Tupperware, which set the scene for the feminization and domestication of plastic, then its
eventual widespread use as disposable packaging. Today, this throwaway mindset is entrenched
globally. However, the responsibility for processing plastic waste is largely borne by local
municipal governments, for whom the task is made more difficult by the “weak recycling waste
regime” identified by Lily Baum Pollans, whose contribution, “The Anti-Plastic City,” positions
those municipalities targeting waste reduction over waste recycling as representing “a potentially
transformative move” (69).
While targeting waste reduction over recycling seems logical, the essays in part 2,  “Plastic
Proliferation,” question the feasibility of achieving large-scale reductions when “In materialist
terms, plastic replicates the capitalist logic that brought it into being, and this very logic continues
to reproduce itself as environmental contamination that retroactively affirms the supremacy of
capitalism” (Boetzkes and Feldman 2023, 87). The presence of microplastics in some of the most
remote ecosystems on the planet is a testament to the pervasiveness of plastic and the capitalist
flows that entrench its circulation. For Chantelle Mitchell and Jaxon Waterhouse, the extraordinary
reach of plastic pollution into our waterways warrants extension of the land-based notion of
the Anthropocene to encompass the “anthrop-ocean” (106), conceived as “a wet ontological
framework which moves beyond the terrestrial into a consideration of fluvial and oceanic
consequences of human impacts upon that which covers more than 70 percent of the globe”
(107). Marine life entangled in plastics and microparticles encased in polar ice represent tangible
evidence of this “anthrop-ocean,” although its reach increasingly extends well beyond the limits of
the visible.
Polymers from textiles, furnishings, consumables, and other products, for example, are now
ingested daily, making plastic microparticles detectable in the human bloodstream and organs.

Renée Hoogland is astute in her adoption of the term “eco-intimacies” to describe the increasingly
complex affective relationships forming between plastics and people. Hoogland’s case study is
the mass spillage of plastic toys that occurred when more than 300 shipping containers were lost
from the MSC Zoe during poor weather in 2019, and which elicited feelings of care and attachment
from local communities when the toys washed ashore. Where consumerism and its waste by-
products are often cast as a phenomenon of Western capitalism, Andrija Filipović’s “ Jugopolastika:
Plastics and Postsocialist Realism” reflects growing interest in Red Anthropocene studies, which
map the “environmental impacts of historical socialism and communism on local, regional and
planetary scales, and the effects of these impacts we are living with today” ( Filipović 2023, 126).
Culture has a double meaning in this volume. Some contributions address the habits, behaviors,
and actions that underpin our dependency on plastics. Others are more concerned with the role of
cultural production in mediating our relations with this material. Parts 3 and 4 are most explicitly
concerned with cultural production, focusing on plastics in art (part 3) and in literature (part 4),
respectively. For visual artists, the artificial materiality of plastic has exerted an appeal, insofar
as it is novel, malleable, and even creatively liberating in its diverse applications. Danielle O’Steen
observes that in the 1930s and 1940s, “corporations saw the value in engaging with the arts and
design to reach consumers and grow their emerging industry” (161). However, the intersection of
art with commerce created a fraught space for collaboration.
Seen from a contemporary perspective, these activities occurred in a more innocent time
when the extent of plastic’s harm was not widely known. The work of contemporary artists, by
comparison, is more politically engaged. Dutch artist Maria Roelofsen, for example, reconfigures
salvaged natural materials and ocean plastics into whimsical sculptural objects that surface “the
circuitous nature of global waste” (Beck and Diamanti 2023, 203). For the American film scholar
David LaRocca, the adoption of prosthetics in cinema speaks to the contribution of plastics “to
modern cognitive experience” (219). In the 1980s, China became the largest recipient of global
plastic waste from Western nations. Here, artists have adopted photography and filmmaking to
forge “a visual politics of waste” (Oana Lupascu 2023, 182). Seminal eco-cinema projects like
Wang Jiuliang’s Beijing Besieged by Waste and Plastic China have raised international awareness
of the ecological crisis. At the same time, the artistic process helps work through the complex
social and political inequities associated with the nation’s economic dependence on plastic.
The relationship between plastic and literature explored within part 4, “Plastics in Literature,”
arguably represents the most novel contribution as it is less common to encounter literary
criticism in a field that favors reportage with citable facts and figures over subjective perspectives
and lived experience. In “Polymeric Thinking,” Lynn Keller considers the personal trauma wrought
by the misuse of plastic as detailed by American author and poet Allison Cobb in the novel
Plastic: An Autobiography,1 prompting consideration of the need for environmental discourses to
become more inclusive of those modes of expression that reflect “the multiple levels of harm to
human and nonhuman bodies that have resulted from plastic’s proliferation and its environmental
entanglements” (Keller 2023, 246). For Emily Potter and Kirsten Seale, the “plastic city” that
emerges in Australian writer Vanessa Berry’s non-fictional Mirror Sydney2 ruptures the shiny
veneer of progress and modernity that too often deflects attention from the resource extraction
and exploitation that underpin  the growth of globalized cities like Sydney.

Plastic pollution is a modern problem, but storytelling is one of the oldest tools that we use to
make sense of the world we inhabit. Donna A. Gessell’s “Better Learning through Plastic? The
Moby-Duck Saga and Pedagogy” surveys the stories that were told about the 1992 “rubber duck
spill,” which, like the 2019 MSC Zoe shipping container disaster that came after it, brought the
logistics of the global shipping trade and its navigation of ocean currents into the consciousness
of the general population. Gessell asks whether the cuteness of the rubber ducks, alongside the
unexpected value that scientists gleaned from the ecological disaster in understanding ocean
currents, helped normalize the problem of plastic pollution in the ocean. Inevitably deflecting the
seriousness of the spill in some measure, the affective force of the rubber ducks nonetheless
engaged diverse audiences, including children for whom storytelling and learning are closely
linked. Importantly, “popular imagination has created strong pedagogies for children to connect
plastic in their daily lives to plastic ocean pollution” (Gessell 2023, 281).
As editor, Konrad elected not to contribute a conclusion, meaning the final essays comprising
part 5, “Plastics and the Future,” work together as an endnote of sorts. These essays reflect most
intensely the darkness of the time during which the volume itself was brought to completion,
namely, the waning period of the COVID-19 pandemic. Patrick D. Murphy’s “Disposable: The Dirty
Word in Medical Plastics” charts the rapid rise in the adoption of single-use medical materials and
plastics since the 1980s, despite the lack of suitable recycling and disposal methods for much
medical waste. Writing during the pandemic, Murphy is sympathetic to the concern for human
health that took precedence over environmental concerns at that time. Yet the ever-increasing
reliance on disposable plastics in the health sector has not abated, even as the pandemic set back
efforts to reduce consumer reliance on disposables in other facets of daily life (e.g., shopping and
takeaway food).
Sasha Adkins and Brittany Y. Davis adopt a more polemical stance in “Eco-Fascism and
Alienation: Plastics in a Post-COVID World.” On the one hand, the authors echo other contributors
in their assertion that “[f]rom conception until death, we all live in the shadow of plastics” (325).
On the other hand, the pair extrapolate further on the dehumanizing nature of our relationship to
plastics and the blind spot of racism and xenophobia that can lurk within environmentalism. Given
the extent of social polarization that now grips the world, it is worth considering whether adopting
loaded frameworks like eco-fascism will help move the dialogue forward or further divide.
Authored prior to the Geneva session, Mark Simpson’s “Plastic in the Time of Impasse” is
prescient in its reflections on the “stuckness” of the fossil fuel-reliant petrocultures within which
plastic mires us. The rhetoric of the clean energy transition is ill-founded, argues Simpson, insofar
as history shows that “forms of energy never really disappear: they only and always accrete”
(341). This is not to say that we should abandon the energy transition. Rather, it speaks to the
importance of culture in the study of petrocultures, as any “meaningful transformation in energy
regime must involve not just a shift in the dominant sources of energetic power but also and
crucially a rending and remaking of the social fabric itself” (Simpson 2023, 356). There are no
easy solutions offered to the “impasse” of plastic time in Konrad’s volume, and researchers and
practitioners working in the field to advance alternatives might note this omission. In adopting the
cultural lens, however, the volume analyzes the problem at a macro, global scale. It is effective
in revealing both the extent of our dependence on this deleterious substance and the breadth of
systemic changes needed to halt its accelerating ecological harm.
