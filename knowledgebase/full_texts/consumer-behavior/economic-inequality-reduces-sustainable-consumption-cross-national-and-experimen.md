Abstract
This research investigates the relationship between economic inequality and sustainable consumption, highlighting how
socioeconomic disparities shape consumer behavior. Across three studies—using secondary data from 20 countries, a vignette
experiment, and UK survey data—we provide empirical evidence that individuals report lower levels of sustainable consump-
tion when inequality levels are objectively high, perceived as high, or experimentally manipulated as high. This effect can
be explained through norm activation theory: As inequality rises, consumers feel less responsible for environmental damage
and are less aware of the environmental impact of their consumption choices. These dynamics weaken sustainability-oriented
personal norms and, in turn, reduce sustainable consumption. Our findings suggest that reducing economic inequality can
significantly enhance sustainable consumption.
Keywords Economic inequality · Sustainable consumption · Norm activation theory · Personal norms
Introduction
Environmental crises, reflected, for instance, in climate
shifts, a loss of biodiversity, and the overexploitation of
natural resources, pose considerable challenges for socie-
ties, economies, and the long-term sustainability of both
human and non-human well-being (e.g., Böhm et al., 2022;
Roberts et al., 2022; Shaw et al., 2022; Tindall et al., 2025).
The current model of consumption—a system that pro-
pels resource exploitation, environmental degradation, and
greenhouse gas emissions at rates that far exceed the Earth’s
regenerative capacity—lies at the heart of many of these
environmental crises. As sustainable consumption involves
consumers taking responsibility for the environmental con-
sequences of their purchasing decisions, it has become a
central topic within business ethics research. Thus, as Shaw
et al. ( 2022, p. 847) put it, addressing the consequences
of these phenomena of unsustainability “is a critical issue
for consumer ethics.” Transforming consumption patterns
toward more sustainable practices has therefore emerged as
a vital pathway for addressing mounting environmental chal-
lenges (Carrington et al., 2012; Gordon et al., 2011; Hosta &
Zabkar, 2021; Shaw et al., 2022; White et al., 2019).
However, despite increasing recognition of the impor -
tance of sustainable consumption, the actual level of sustain-
able consumption behavior remains strikingly limited. This
raises the question of why consumers are reluctant to adopt
more sustainable consumption practices (Goor et al., 2025;
White et al., 2019). The present research sought to advance
the understanding of the factors that inhibit sustainable con-
sumption by focusing on a critical variable that has received
little attention in the sustainable consumption literature thus
far: economic inequality.
Economic inequality can be defined as “the magnitude of
the income or/and wealth gap between the poor and wealthy
within a particular society” (Jetten et al., 2021 , p. 242).
Hence, it encompasses two inequality dimensions: income

Beyond their independent relevance for business eth-
ics, the relationship between economic inequality and sus-
tainable consumption raises the fundamentally normative
question as to whether consumers can be expected to act
as ethical agents when the socioeconomic conditions nec-
essary for sustainable consumption are unequally distrib -
uted. Consumer ethics often assumes that individuals can
express moral values through marketplace choices. However,
if economic inequality systematically constrains consumers’
willingness or ability to engage in sustainable consumption,
then sustainable consumption becomes not merely a matter
of individual responsibility but also a question of distribu-
tive justice and social structure. We argue that economic
inequality—which has increased substantially in many
societies over the past three decades (Bourguignon, 2015;
Savage, 2021)—limits the ability and willingness of indi-
viduals to participate in sustainable consumption. There-
fore, we proposed the following research question: How does
economic inequality impact individual private sustainable
consumption?
To explain the effect of inequality on individual sustaina-
ble consumption using a social psychological perspective on
moral action, we draw on norm activation theory (Onwezen
et al., 2013; Schwartz, 1977; Schwartz & Howard, 1981).
This theory posits that individual altruistic behavior, such
as environmental stewardship, is triggered by the activa-
tion of personal norms via the awareness of consequences,
which reflects individuals’ awareness of the potential impact
of their own actions, and the ascription of responsibility,
which refers to the perception of personal responsibility for
contributing to specific problems, such as environmental
degradation (Schwartz, 1977).
We theorize that inequality undermines individuals’
awareness of consequences and diminishes their sense of
responsibility, both of which are crucial for activating per -
sonal norms. As a result, individuals in contexts of inequal-
ity perceive their actions as having minimal impact and feel
less responsible for environmental degradation. This leads to
a reduced propensity to engage in sustainable consumption.
We conduct three studies to demonstrate and explain the
effect of economic inequality on individual sustainable con-
sumption. Study 1 utilizes cross-country data from the Flash
Eurobarometer 367 and the OECD to investigate how differ-
ences in income inequality between countries affect individual
sustainable consumption. We show that people living in more
unequal societies are less willing to pay a higher price for green
products and report less actual sustainable purchasing behav-
ior. To establish causality, Study 2 employs a two-condition
between-subjects experimental design to examine the manip-
ulated effects of high versus low economic inequality. While
keeping the income level constant across both conditions,
we demonstrate that respondents in the manipulated higher
inequality condition are less willing to consume sustainably
than respondents in the manipulated lower inequality condi-
tion. Finally, Study 3 examines the psychological mechanism
by which economic inequality negatively influences individual
sustainable consumption, drawing on self-reported survey data.
Consistent with our hypotheses, the findings indicate that per-
ceived inequality is associated with less sustainable consumption
due to weakened (sustainability-related) personal norms. These
norms are less likely to be activated when individuals perceive
higher levels of economic inequality due to a diminished aware-
ness of consequences and a reduced sense of personal respon-
sibility. Our findings suggest that reducing economic inequality
can significantly enhance sustainable consumption and is thus
likely to decrease those aspects of environmental degradation
that are directly linked to individual consumption.
This article advances the literature in several ways. First, we
demonstrate that a negative relationship exists between two cen-
tral issues in business ethics, i.e. economic inequality (e.g., Beal
& Astakhova, 2017; Ketokivi et al., 2025; Simpson, 2009) and
the individual sustainable consumption of private goods (e.g.,
Elgaaied-Gambier et al., 2026; Hosta & Zabkar, 2021). Prior
research on the effect of inequality has primarily referred to the
context of environmental public goods and not to individual pri-
vate consumption (e.g., Baumgärtner et al., 2017; Drupp et al.,
2018), or relied on aggregated data and broad proxies of sustain-
able outcomes, such as environmental quality or  CO2 emissions
(e.g., Apeti et al., 2025; Baležentis et al., 2020; for a literature
overview, see Table S13 in the Supplemental Online Material
[SOM]). Complementing these earlier studies, our research uses
individual-level measures of private sustainable consumption.
Second, we show that the effect of economic inequality on sus-
tainable consumption is robust across different studies using dif-
ferent forms of economic inequality (i.e., objective, perceived,
and manipulated) and different indicators of private sustainable
consumption. Specifically, sustainable consumption is assessed
using three variables: willingness to pay for environmentally
friendly products, the purchasing of environmentally friendly
products, and environmental consumption. Third, we explain
the negative effect of economic inequality on private sustain-
able consumption by highlighting the underlying psychological
mechanisms among individual consumers. In employing norm
activation theory, we offer a novel, individual-level explana-
tion of why economic inequality undermines sustainable con-
sumption. By demonstrating that economic inequality weak-
ens consumers’ awareness of consequences and ascription of

Literature Review
While research on economic inequality is growing in impor-
tance in the field of business ethics (e.g., Beal & Astakhova,
2017; Chen et al., 2025; Hudson et al., 2022), research on
the relationship between economic inequality and private
sustainable consumption is sparse. Research examining the
relationship between economic inequality and sustainability
has predominantly focused on environmental quality or  CO2
as the outcome variable at the country level (e.g., Chancel
et al., 2023; Duarte et al., 2021; Hubacek et al., 2017; Rid-
zuan, 2019; Uzar & Eyuboglu, 2019; see for an overview,
Table S13 in the SOM).
In one of the first studies on the link between income
inequality and environmental quality, Boyce (1994) theoreti-
cally asserts that in unequal societies, environmental policy
and quality are negatively affected by power imbalances
because wealthier people have more access to power and
can influence environmental policies in their own interest.
Both Baumgärtner et al. (2017) and Drupp et al. (2018)
theorize the effect of economic inequality on societal will-
ingness to pay for environmental public goods in the context
of public policy schemes, such as benefit transfers systems.
They argue that the distribution of income should affect the
value society places on environmental public goods in a pub-
lic policy context, and Baumgärtner et al. (2017) confirm
this rationale by drawing on Swedish survey data.
Problematizing inequality with regard to the emissions
shares of different income groups, several studies exam-
ine which income groups cause particularly large shares of
environmental degradation. These studies document that
wealthier households tend to pollute much more than poorer
households: Hubacek et al. (2017) document that the top
10% of income earners are responsible for 36% of global
carbon emissions, while the poorest 50% of the global pop-
ulation are responsible for only 15% of the global carbon
footprint. Likewise, the Climate Inequality Report (2023)
highlights that the wealthiest 10% of the global population
accounts for nearly half of all carbon emissions, while the
top 1% alone emits more than the entire bottom 50% com-
bined. Importantly, 2/3 of global carbon inequality is driven
by within-country inequality, highlighting the importance
of a country’s inequality level for environmental outcomes.
Further studies examine the relationship between economic
inequality and phenomena of unsustainability on a societal
level. Using scenario analysis, Duarte et al. (2021) assess the
impact of consumption habits in the EU-27 and the UK between
1999 and 2015 on carbon emissions at a societal level. Their
results suggest that reducing wealth inequality through income
redistribution can reduce societal emissions, as high-income
groups consume more carbon-intensive goods. Similarly, Uzar
and Eyuboglu (2019) analyze societal emissions data for Tur-
key from 1984 to 2014 and find a positive association between
income inequality and aggregate  CO2 emissions. Hassan et al.
(2022) report a positive and statistically significant effect of
income inequality on  CO2 emissions in BRICS countries. Che
et al.’s (2023) cross-country study also shows that the positive
impact of income inequality on carbon emissions is significant
for both developed and developing countries but more strongly
for more developed countries. Other studies show that the rela-
tionship between economic inequality and  CO2 emissions can be
conditional on a country’s income level (Baležentis et al., 2020;
Grunewald et al., 2017; Wu & Xie, 2020). Similarly, exploring
the impact of inequality on national  SO2 emissions, Ridzuan
(2019) finds that income inequality increases what is termed
the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) turning point, so that
under conditions of high inequality, it needs a higher level of
income until additional income reduces national  SO2 emissions.
Ridzuan (2019) thus argues that high inequality can damage the
environment: When income inequality is high, people worry
more about unemployment, social welfare, and daily survival,
which may result in fewer demands for more environmental
protection.
In contrast, Sager (2019) points out that the relationship
between income and  CO2 emissions can be illustrated by
environmental Engel curves, as emissions tend to increase
with rising income levels. If lower-income groups gain
greater purchasing power through redistributive measures,
overall pollution levels could increase. At the same time, a
reduction in wealth among the rich may have only a limited
impact on their consumption patterns, given their relatively
inelastic lifestyles. Hence, Sager (2019) concludes that lower
income inequality can increase aggregate emissions. Further
studies similarly report that reduced economic inequality
can be associated with higher  CO2 emissions at a societal
level (Baek & Gweisah, 2013; Grunewald et al., 2017; Wu
& Xie, 2020).1

Overall, this review of the existing literature on inequality
underscores the absence of research examining individual-
level private sustainable consumption as an outcome vari-
able. It is thus not known how inequality actually relates
to individual sustainable consumption of private goods or
how mechanisms work through which inequality may impact
sustainable consumption. However, deeper knowledge of
the relationship between economic inequality and sustain -
able consumption is of central importance for business eth-
ics. Sustainable consumption is commonly understood as
an expression of consumers’ moral responsibility for the
social and environmental consequences of their marketplace
choices (e.g., Shaw et al., 2022). At the same time, economic
inequality has emerged as a major concern in business ethics
because it raises questions of distributive justice and shapes
individuals’ opportunities to participate in economic and
social life (e.g., Beal & Astakhova, 2017; Ketokivi et al.,
2025). Understanding how inequality affects sustainable
consumption is therefore essential for evaluating the condi-
tions under which consumers can be expected to act as ethi-
cal agents. If economic inequality systematically constrains
individuals’ perceived moral obligation to engage in sustain-
able consumption, then sustainable consumption cannot be
understood solely as a matter of individual responsibility,
but must also be considered in light of broader societal struc-
tures. Examining the relationship between inequality and
sustainable consumption therefore contributes to ongoing
business ethics debates concerning consumer responsibility,
distributive justice, and the societal conditions that enable
or hinder ethical behavior in the marketplace.
Norm activation theory, developed by Schwartz (1977)
in the context of altruistic behavior, has been widely used
to explain why individuals engage in pro-environmental
actions, ranging from sustainable consumption and energy
conservation to recycling and re-use behavior to the use of
public transportation (e.g., Joanes, 2019; Matthies et al.,
2012; Saleki et al., 2021). Norm activation theory posits
that individual sustainable action—especially in the context
of altruistic behavior, such as environmental stewardship—
is triggered by the activation of personal norms (Schwartz,
1977). Personal norms reflect what people believe they
should do. It is their perceived moral obligation to act in
a certain way (Dietz, 2023). According to norm activation
theory, altruistic behavior arises from the strength of inter -
nalized personal norms. Schwartz (1977) argues that most
people possess altruistic personal norms to a certain degree.
However, to enact altruistic behavior, these norms need to
be activated. The activation of personal norms requires the
following conditions: awareness of consequences (i.e., the
recognition that one’s actions can have considerable envi -
ronmental or social impact) and ascription of responsibility
(i.e., the belief that one has a moral obligation or personal
responsibility to act to prevent negative consequences, such
as environmental destruction). According to the theory, only
when individuals are aware of the beneficial or harmful con-
sequences of their behavior and have a sense of responsibil-
ity for pro-social and pro-environmental outcomes can they
develop the “moral obligation to perform or refrain from
specific actions” (Schwartz & Howard, 1981, p. 191; see
Schwartz, 1968).
Norm activation theory has been employed diversely in
empirical studies. A prominent approach that we adopt in
our empirical design suggests that both awareness of con-
sequences and ascription of responsibility impact personal
norms as a mediating variable, which in turn affects inten-
tions and behavior (Bamberg & Möser, 2007 ; Han, 2014;
Nordlund & Garvill, 2003; Savari et al., 2023).
Hypotheses
Figure 1 illustrates our theoretical model. We propose a neg-
ative influence of economic inequality on individual sustain-
able consumption. To explain the mechanism behind the link
between inequality and sustainable consumption, we draw
on Schwartz’s norm activation theory (Onwezen et al., 2013;
Schwartz, 1977; Thøgersen, 2006), proposing that the rela-
tionship between inequality and an individual’s sustainable
consumption is mediated by their personal norms, which
are in turn activated by two key factors: (1) ascription of
responsibility and (2) awareness of consequences.

accountability for contributing to certain problems, such
as environmental degradation. Norm activation theory sug-
gests that a sense of responsibility is crucial for activating
personal norms that motivate altruistic behavior, which also
involves sustainable actions (Schwartz, 1977). If individuals
feel that they personally contribute to a problem, they will
take responsibility for the problem and will feel a moral
obligation to address it (Ai & Rosenthal, 2024). In contrast,
if individuals attribute the problem to other actors, they will
also place the responsibility for addressing it on those actors.
We argue that in contexts of high inequality, individuals with
rather low and middle incomes view environmental respon-
sibility as the concern of the wealthy, whom they see as the
primary contributors to and beneficiaries of environmentally
harmful practices. Research shows that, in less egalitarian
societies, the affluent pollute the environment to a signifi-
cantly greater extent than the affluent in more egalitarian
societies (Dorling, 2017). Likewise, Boyce (2007) empha-
sizes that “those who are relatively powerful and wealthy
typically gain disproportionate benefits from the economic
activities that degrade the environment, while those who are
relatively powerless and poor typically bear disproportionate
costs” (p. 267).
Complementarily, under conditions of high inequality,
high-income individuals may ascribe responsibility for
environmental degradation to the low-income segments of
society, as when faced with trade-offs between expensive,
more sustainable products (e.g., organic meat or meat sub-
stitutes and durable fashion) and cheaper, less sustainable
products (e.g., conventional meat from factory farming and
fast fashion), the low-income segments of society tend to
choose the more environmentally harmful products (see
Lerner & Rottman, 2021; Szulc-Obłoza and Żurek, 2024).
Following this logic, poverty is frequently described as a
source of unsustainability (Brundtland et al., 1987). Specifi-
cally, the Brundtland report argues that there is a “downward
spiral of poverty and environmental degradation” (Brundt-
land et al., 1987, p. 14) and that “[p]overty is a major cause
[…] of global environmental problems” (Brundtland et al.,
1987, p. 19). Both argumentations, ascribing responsibility
for environmental degradation primarily to the rich or to
the poor, respectively, show that contexts of high inequality
allow individuals to blame others for environmental degra-
dation and, consequently, to not feel a moral obligation to
act sustainably themselves.
Consequently, in countries with higher levels of economic
inequality, many individuals may be more inclined to deny
responsibility for environmental issues and, therefore, may
not feel a moral obligation to act sustainably. Thus, per -
sonal norms are unlikely to be activated under these con-
ditions. Conversely, in more egalitarian settings, individu-
als across all parts of society may feel a greater sense of
responsibility because they all perceive their contributions to
environmental pollution and problems. This shared respon-
sibility leads to stronger moral obligations to act sustainably
and address environmental issues. Hence, we hypothesize
that an individual’s ascription of responsibility for environ-
mental outcomes would be reduced by higher levels of ine-
quality, resulting in weaker personal norms and subsequent
sustainable consumption behavior.
Awareness of Consequences
Awareness of consequences captures an individual’s recogni-
tion of the potential effects of their actions. This “awareness
activates a personal norm that determines whether he/she
should perform a particular action that prevents a harmful
outcome” (Han, 2014, p. 463). Awareness of consequences
is influenced by both situational and individual factors
(Schwartz, 1968). We argue that inequality, as a situational
condition, limits the awareness of the consequences of indi-
viduals. The challenges linked to inequality lead individuals
to focus on immediate, self-interested goals rather than on
the long-term, sustainable outcomes of their actions. Conse-
quently, they tend to be more conscious of the repercussions
for themselves rather than for others, and they may even sup-
press awareness of the negative consequences of their behav-
iors as they seek personal benefits. Research documents that
individuals act more selfishly and unethically and also show
greater acceptability of self-interested unethical behavior in
conditions of inequality (Côté et al., 2015; To et al., 2023).
This can be partly explained by increased status anxiety and
status seeking under conditions of inequality (Melita et al.,
2021, 2023; Velandia-Morales et al., 2022).
Furthermore, in environments characterized by inequal-
ity, individuals may feel a reduced sense of behavioral con-
trol and perceive themselves as controlled by external forces,
leading them to believe that their actions have a minimal
impact on their environment. Bandura (2001) posited that a
diminished sense of personal agency can lead to passivity
in individual behavior. If individuals perceive the conse-
quences of their behavior as negligible in the face of larger
inequalities, their understanding of the impact of their activi-
ties and their willingness to engage in sustainable practices
may diminish (Antonetti & Maklan, 2014; Ellen et al.,
1991). Consequently, individuals who perceive their own
sustainable consumption choices as practically irrelevant are
likely to experience a weaker sense of moral obligation and
a lower willingness to engage in sustainable consumption.
The limited activation of personal norms will also reduce
their actual sustainable consumption behavior. Hence, we
hypothesize that an individual’s awareness of consequences
is negatively influenced by inequality, resulting in weaker
personal norms and subsequent sustainable consumption
behavior.

Studies
Study 1
Method
Design and  Data Study 1 uses secondary cross-national
survey data to examine whether individuals in societies with
higher levels of inequality report lower levels of sustainable
consumption. It uses individual-level sustainable consump-
tion data from the Flash Eurobarometer 367, titled “Atti-
tudes of Europeans towards building the single market for
green products” (European Commission, 2013). To assess
each country’s level of income inequality, we utilize data
from the 2012 OECD statistics (Teorell et  al., 2020): the
Gini coefficient (a statistical measure of economic inequal-
ity within a population, which ranges from 0 to 1, where
0 represents perfect equality and 1 represents perfect ine-
quality) as well as the P90/P10 and P80/P20 income ratios
(measures of income inequality that compare the income of
someone at the 90th percentile to that of someone at the
10th percentile or 80th to 20th percentile, respectively). For
our control variables, we draw on additional data from the
Quality of Government (QoG) project and the Yale Center
for Environmental Law (Teorell et al., 2020). After match-
ing these secondary data sources with the Flash Eurobarom-
eter data, our final sample includes information on 17,203
individuals from 20 countries. The mean age of the partici-
pants is 52.24 years (SD = 15.64); 58.76% are female, and
49.36% have completed higher education.
Measures Sustainable consumption is measured with two
variables: (1) willingness to pay more for environmentally
friendly products and (2) purchasing of environmentally
friendly products. Willingness to pay more for environ-
mentally friendly products is based on the item “How much
more, if anything, would you be willing to pay for products
if you were confident that they were more environmentally
friendly?” This is coded as 0 when the respondent indicates
no willingness to pay more for environmentally friendly
products and as 1 when the respondent indicates a willing-
ness to pay 5% or more for environmentally friendly prod-
ucts. The second dependent variable, buy environmentally
friendly products, captures the environmental behavior of
the respondents. They were asked to identify if they “often
buy environmentally-friendly products,” “sometimes buy
environmentally-friendly products,” “do not buy environ-
mentally-friendly products,” “do not buy environmentally-
friendly products but you may do so in the future,” “used to
buy environmentally-friendly products but you stopped,” or
“have not bought environmentally-friendly products and do
not intend to do so.” Because the first two responses express
sustainable consumption, we code them as 1, and the other
response categories as 0.
To capture inequality, we use three objective inequality
measures from the 2012 OECD country statistics (OECD,
2024; Teorell et al., 2020): (1) the Gini coefficient (at dis -
posable income, post taxes, and transfers), (2) the P90/P10
disposable income decile ratio, and (3) the P80/P20 dispos-
able income decile ratio.
At the individual level, we control for gender (0 = male,
1 = female), age, tertiary education (0 = no, 1 = yes), and
environmental concern (capturing whether the respondent
believe that concerns about the environment are exagger -
ated; 4-point scale, 1 = strongly agree, 4 = strongly disa-
gree). Although the secondary dataset does not include
direct measures of individual income, it allows for the use
of occupation as a proxy. The occupation variable consists
of 22 categories, covering a range of high-, middle-, and
low-income professions, as well as other groups, such as
the unemployed, students, retirees, and individuals engaged
in unpaid domestic work (see Table S2 in the SOM). We
include the occupation variable as a categorical measure in
the analysis, thereby accounting for systematic differences
across occupational groups, such as variations in income. At
the country level, we control for GDP per capita converted
to constant US dollars (logged, World Bank data from 2012;
Teorell et al., 2020) and the environmental quality of the
country, as measured by the 2012 Environmental Perfor -
mance Index (Block et al., 2024; Teorell et al., 2020). All
variables and items are described in the SOM.
Generative AI tools were not used to obtain, construct,
process, or analyze the data reported in Study 1. After the
study had been completed, ChatGPT was used to support
language editing and a pre-submission consistency review
of the manuscript and Supplemental Online Material. The
authors independently assessed and verified all suggestions
and retained full responsibility for the reported methods,
results, and interpretations.

Method
Design and Data Study 2 employs an experimental vignette
design in which inequality is manipulated to assess its direct
impact on individual sustainable consumption behavior
(cf. Jasinenko et al., 2026; Wahl et al., 2010). The advan-
tages of this procedure are twofold. First, by experimentally
manipulating inequality through a vignette, Study 2 demon-
strates the causality of the effect of inequality on sustainable
consumption behavior. Second, the experimental design of
Study 2 allows income to be held constant across high- and
low-inequality conditions. This enables a more robust exam-
ination of the relationship between inequality and individual
sustainable consumption, independent of income effects.
We employ a two-condition between-subjects design to
examine the manipulated effects of high versus low income
inequality. Participants are randomly assigned to one of two
conditions. Depending on their assigned condition, partici-
pants are informed that Varosia’s citizens generally experi-
ence either high or low levels of inequality. This approach
allows us to manipulate and compare the effects of the level
of inequality on participants’ perceptions and responses
as consumers within a specific societal context. Partici-
pants were asked to imagine living as middle-income citi-
zens in a fictional society called Varosia (cf. Brieger et al.,
2026; Jasinenko et al., 2026; Wahl et al., 2010). The descrip-
tion of middle-income life—encompassing economic secu-
rity, lifestyle, housing, education, and healthcare—in which
the participants were instructed to imagine themselves liv -
ing was held constant across both experimental conditions.
What varied was the portrayal of the low- and high-income
groups. In the low-inequality condition, the lifestyles of
these groups were depicted as relatively similar to those of
the middle-income group. In contrast, in the high-inequality
condition, the disparities between income groups were very
pronounced. Complete vignettes are provided in the SOM.
After reading the vignette, the participants responded to
items measuring their perception of inequality (manipulation
check), sustainable consumption intentions and behaviors,
personal norms, ascription of responsibility, and awareness
of consequences. Finally, the respondents completed the
values and social desirability scales and provided socio-
demographic information. We chose the United Kingdom
(UK) as the geographic context for this study, based on the
evidence from Study 1 (see Fig.  2, panels a and b) and the
recognition that the UK has relatively high levels of income
inequality (for a more detailed discussion of inequality in the
UK, see Dorling, 2023). We recruited 200 UK-based par -
ticipants via the Prolific.com survey platform in June 2025
and assigned 100 participants to each inequality condition.
The participants’ ages were between 20 and 70 years (M  =
44.24; SD = 12.18); 50% were male, and 41.50% had at least
an undergraduate college degree.
Measures Sustainable consumption is measured using
three variables. We use the same exact willingness to pay
for environmentally friendly products and buy environmen-
tally friendly products items  as in Study 1. Additionally,
we implement an environmental consumption scale con-
sisting of five items (α = .92) on purchasing environmen-
tally friendly products from Gupta and Agrawal (2018). An
example item from this scale is “I buy products that are envi-
ronment friendly,” rated on a 5-point scale from 0 (strongly
disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Due to the vignette design,
all items begin with the phrase “In Varosia, I would ….”
As in Study 1, we control for gender (male = 0, female
= 1), age, and tertiary education (no = 0, yes = 1). In addi-
tion, we control for self-transcendence values and social
desirability. Self-transcendence values have consistently
been identified as a key predictor of pro-environmental
attitudes and behaviors at the individual level (e.g., Chan,
2020; Hansla et al., 2008; Jacobs et al., 2018; Schultz, 2001).
Controlling for social desirability is essential because self-
reports of pro-environmental attitudes and behaviors are
often upwardly biased, as individuals tend to present them-
selves in a favorable manner and overreport socially desir -
able views and actions (Kormos & Gifford, 2014; Vesely &
Klöckner, 2020). Self-transcendence values are measured
with five items (e.g., “It’s very important to him/her to help
the people around him/her. He/she wants to care for their
well-being”; 5-point scale, 1 = not at all like me, 5 = very
much like me; α = .81; Bilsky et al., 2011; Welzel, 2010).
Social desirability is measured with three items (e.g., “In an
argument, I always remain objective and stick to the facts”,
5-point scale, 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree;
α = .60; Nießen et al., 2019). All variables and items are
described in the SOM.

Analysis and Results
In the first step of analysis, we verify whether the manipula-
tion was successful as intended. Indeed, Varosia is perceived
as having higher economic inequality in the high-inequality
condition (M = 6.256, SD = .732) than in the low-inequality
condition (M = 2.124, SD = 1.314, t (198) = −27.466, p  <
.001).
Following the manipulation check, we perform inde-
pendent samples t-tests to compare the dependent variables
between the high- and low-inequality conditions and then
utilize regression analysis for further examination. Figure 3
illustrates the notable differences in sustainable consump-
tion between the manipulated high- and low-inequality con-
ditions. Participants in the low-inequality condition report
higher levels of sustainable consumption, measured using
the same dependent variables as in Study 1, along with the
environmental consumption scale. In the low-inequality con-
dition, 92% of respondents are willing to pay at least 5%
more for environmentally friendly products, compared to
only 78% in the high-inequality condition [t(198) = 2.813, p
= .005, SDlow inequality = .273, SDhigh inequality = .416; Fig. 3a].
Similar significant differences are also found for the envi-
ronmental consumption scale [t (198) = 3.491, p  = .001;
Fig. 3c], with participants in the low-inequality condition
scoring higher on environmental consumption (M  = 2.886,
SD = .772) than those in the high-inequality condition (M
= 2.494, SD = .816). However, we do not find a significant
relationship with buying environmentally friendly products
[t(198) = 1.297, p = .196; Fig. 3b]. Participants in the low-
inequality condition are more likely to buy such products (M
= .970, SD = .171) compared to those in the high-inequality
condition (M = .930, SD = .256), but this difference is not
statistically significant.
Table  2 presents the results of the regression analy -
sis, which controls for sociodemographic characteristics,
participants’ values, and social desirability. The first two
models use logistic regression due to the binary nature of the
dependent variables: (1) willingness to pay more for envi -
ronmentally friendly products and (2) the purchasing of such
environmentally friendly products. The third model applied
ordinary least squares (OLS) regression to the continuous
environmental consumption scale. The high-inequality con-

more for environmentally friendly products and the envi-

the high-inequality condition and the purchasing of environ-
mentally friendly products is negative but not statistically
significant (b = −.815, p = .265).

Method
Design and Data Study 3 investigates the underlying psy -
chological mechanism behind the influence of inequality on
sustainable consumption by testing norm activation theory
with self-collected survey data. This study shows how per -
ceived inequality influences sustainable consumption via
personal norms, which are, in turn, activated by the aware-
ness of consequences and ascription of responsibility. We
recruit 321 participants from the UK via Prolific in July
2023 to complete a 10-min survey. We exclude two partici-
pants who did not specify their gender, resulting in a final
sample of 319 respondents providing data for the analysis.
The participants’ mean age is 40.44 years (SD = 12.74);
49.53% are female, and 50.16% have completed tertiary
education.
Measures Sustainable consumption is measured using three
variables. We use the same exact items on willingness to pay
for environmentally friendly products and buy environmen-
tally friendly products as dependent variables as in Study 1.
As an additional variable, we utilize the same environmen-
tal consumption scale by Gupta and Agrawal (2018) as in
Study 2 (α = .88).
Inequality is measured with five perceived inequality
items (α = .77; García-Castro et al., 2020; Sommet et al.,
2018). An example item is “In the UK, differences in income
are too large” (7-point scale, 1 = strongly disagree, 7 =
strongly agree).
Personal norms are measured with five items (α = .81),
taken from Vining and Ebreo (1992). An example item is “I
feel a strong obligation to consume as sustainably as pos-
sible” (5-point scale, 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly
agree).
Ascription of responsibility  is measured with two items
(α = .89), taken from Vining and Ebreo (1992). An example
is “The consumption behavior of households like mine is
responsible for a very large part of environmental pollution”
(5-point scale, 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).
Awareness of consequences is measured with three items
(α = .89), taken from Vining and Ebreo (1992). An example
is “Sustainable consumption is a major way to reduce one’s
own carbon footprint” (5-point scale, 1 = strongly disagree,
5 = strongly agree).
As in Study 1, we control for gender (0 = male, 1 =
female), age, tertiary education (0 = no, 1 = yes), and envi-
ronmental concern (measured as in Study 1, α = .86). In
addition, we control for income, self-transcendence values,
and social desirability. Income is captured with an estab-
lished item from the European Social Survey: “How do you
feel about your present household’s income nowadays?”
(5-point scale, 1 = “Finding it very difficult on present
income,” 5 = “Living comfortably on present income”).
Self-transcendence values are measured with five items (e.g.,
“It’s very important to him/her to help the people around
him/her. He/she wants to care for their well-being”; 5-point
scale, 1 = not at all like me, 5 = very much like me, α =
.76; Bilsky et al., 2011; Welzel, 2010). Social desirability is
measured with three items (e.g., “In an argument, I always
remain objective and stick to the facts”; 5-point scale, 1 =
strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree, α = .66; Nießen et al.,
2019). All variables and items are described in the SOM.

Analysis and Results
To test for mediation, we employ path analysis within the
structural equation modeling (SEM) framework using the
“gsem” command in the Stata 15 software with maximum
likelihood estimation. To compute the conditional indi-
rect effects, we use the “nlcom” command within Stata.
The model combined linear equations for the continuous
outcomes with Bernoulli-logit equations for the binary
outcomes. Indirect effects were calculated as products
of the corresponding path coefficients using the “nlcom”
command.
Figure  4 summarizes the effects, with more detailed
information provided in Table 3. The results reveal a nega-
tive effect of perceived inequality not only on sustainable
consumption—measured as a willingness to pay for green
products (b = −.401, p = .045) or as an environmental con-
sumption scale (b  = −.160, p = .004)—but also on per -
sonal norms (b = −.203, p < .001). The effect of perceived
inequality on the purchase of green products is negative but
not statistically significant (b  = −.193, p  = .383). In addi-
tion, we find that perceived inequality is negatively linked
to the mediating variables ascription of responsibility (b  =
−.194, p = .007) and awareness of consequences (b = −.173,
p = .002), indicating that individuals who perceive greater
inequality tend to feel less responsible for environmental
outcomes and are less convinced that their sustainable con-
sumption has an environmental impact. As predicted, ascrip-
tion of responsibility (b  = .082, p  = .054) and awareness
of consequences (b  = .172, p  = .002) are also positively
related to personal norms. Personal norms are, in turn,
strongly positively associated with all three dependent vari-
ables: willingness to pay (b  = 1.275, p  < .001), buying of
environmentally friendly products (b = 1.281, p < .001), and
the environmental consumption scale (b = .622, p < .001).
Results on the indirect effects further indicate that per -
ceived inequality is negatively related to personal norms via
the two mediating variables: ascription of responsibility (b =
−.016, p = .117) and awareness of consequences (b = −.030,
p = .027). However, this indirect effect is statistically signifi-
cant only for awareness of consequences, whereas the effect
via the ascription of responsibility narrowly fails to reach the
conventional significance threshold. This pattern appeared to
result from the substantial shared variance between the two
mediating variables (residual covariance = .296, p  <.001),
which reduces the unique contribution of each mediator
when both are included simultaneously. For instance, when
the model is estimated without awareness of consequences,
the indirect effect of perceived inequality on personal norms
via the ascription of responsibility is statistically significant
(b = −.027, p = .032), underscoring the important mediating
role of the ascription of responsibility.

Discussion and Conclusions
Across three studies—using secondary data from 20 coun-
tries, a vignette experiment, and a survey conducted in the
UK—this research finds that economic inequality negatively
affects sustainable consumption, even after controlling for
individual income or occupation. We use norm activa-
tion theory (Schwartz, 1977; Schwartz & Howard, 1981)
to explain this effect by showing that inequality reduces
consumers’ (sustainability-related) personal norms, which
in turn are activated by an individual’s awareness of con-
sequences (i.e., people’s beliefs that their sustainable con-
sumption behaviors can have a positive impact) and ascrip-
tion of responsibility (i.e., people’s perceived levels of
responsibility for environmental degradation), ultimately
affecting sustainable consumption.
The findings demonstrate the relevance of inequality
for sustainable consumption. Unlike earlier research that
explained the link between inequality and unsustainability
at the national and collective levels (see Table S13 in the
SOM), this study provides a new perspective by explain-
ing the negative relationship between economic inequality
and private sustainable consumption at the individual level.
All three studies revealed a negative impact of inequal-
ity on private sustainable consumption. This central find-
ing is in line with earlier conceptual, economic modeling
work (Baumgärtner et al., 2017; Ridzuan, 2019), scenario
analyses (Duarte et al., 2021), and empirical findings
(Drupp et al., 2018; Uzar & Eyuboglu, 2019) showing a
link between economic inequality and the phenomenon
of unsustainability (such as with national greenhouse gas
emissions) on the national level. In contrast, our findings
challenge the findings of Sager (2019), who argues that
the overall level of pollution could increase when lower
income groups gain greater purchasing power and con-
clude that lower income inequality can increase aggregate
emissions.
Moreover, the current research is able to clarify the
underlying psychological mechanism of the negative rela-
tionship between inequality and individual sustainable con-
sumption. Drawing on norm activation theory (Schwartz,
1977; Schwartz & Howard, 1981), we demonstrate that
inequality impedes sustainable consumption by weakening
sustainability-related personal norms. This occurs through a
reduced awareness of consequences and a diminished sense
of responsibility among consumers. Thus, the findings con-
firm the key assumptions of norm activation theory and
verify the centrality of personal norms for sustainable con-
sumption (cf. Demarque et al., 2015; Roos & Hahn, 2017).
Going beyond earlier studies, we show that these norms are
shaped not only by personal or situational factors (see Lee
et al., 2023; Sajjad et al., 2024) but also by broader socio -
economic structures, such as economic inequality.
As our results indicate, the indirect effects of inequality
via ascription of responsibility and personal norms become
stronger once awareness of consequences is excluded from
the model. This raises the question of whether a serial media-
tion structure, as for example assumed in value–belief–norm
theory (Stern et al., 1999), might better represent the under-
lying relationships than the parallel mediation model that we
originally theorized. Nevertheless, we sought to reinforce
our theoretical positioning based on norm activation theory
(Schwartz, 1977; Schwartz & Howard, 1981) by arguing
that the two constructs are conceptually distinct rather than
causally sequential. Specifically, awareness of consequences
reflects individuals’ cognitive recognition of the negative
outcomes associated with unsustainable behavior, whereas
an ascription of responsibility represents a moral evalua-
tion of one’s own obligation to mitigate such outcomes.
At the same time, we acknowledge that estimating a serial
mediation model—where inequality affects the ascription
of responsibility, which in turn influences awareness of
consequences, personal norms, and ultimately sustainable
consumption—also yielded significant indirect effects across
all three dependent variables (see Table S12 in the SOM).
This suggests some empirical interdependence between the
two constructs, even if the causal sequence remains theoreti-
cally debatable.

tributions to the debates on sustainable consumption and
inequality. First, it demonstrates that a negative relation-
ship exists between income inequality and the individual
sustainable consumption of private goods. Second, we

Implications for Practice and Business Ethics
Scholarship
Overall, this research highlights that reducing income
inequality can increase sustainable consumption patterns.
This is thus in line with earlier research on the aggregated
national level, which suggests effects of economic inequal-
ity on the phenomenon of unsustainability based on sce-
nario analyses (e.g., Duarte et al., 2021). Based on these
insights, implications for policy and business ethics arise.
First, policymakers on the national level should consider
the negative effects of economic inequality on sustainable
consumption when discussing redistributive taxes and the
potential side effects of tax reforms. Reducing income and
wealth inequality is likely to contribute to reducing aspects
of environmental degradation that are directly affected by
individual consumption, such as carbon emissions (Ivanova
et al., 2016) or the depletion of freshwater resources (Hoek-
stra & Mekonnen, 2012). Based on the findings, govern-
ments could implement more progressive tax systems aimed
at reducing income and wealth inequality. This could not
only address economic inequality but also indirectly pro-
mote more sustainable consumption. For example, countries
with high income inequality could implement taxes that dis-
courage the overconsumption of unsustainable goods, such
as individual carbon taxes or luxury goods taxes (Dietsch,
2024; Oswald et al., 2023). However, there are potential
risks associated with such redistributive measures (Chancel
et al., 2023; Sager, 2019). Lower income groups typically
consume within sustainable limits and are least responsible
for exceeding environmental targets; at the same time, an
additional unit of expenditure is often more carbon inten-
sive for poorer households than for wealthier ones, as higher
income groups spend a smaller proportion of their income
on consumption (Chancel et al., 2023). This raises the
question of how the consumption patterns of lower income
groups might change once they gain greater purchasing
power through redistribution.
Second, recognizing that inequality reduces individuals’
ascription of responsibility, local governments in areas with
higher levels of income inequality could establish demo-
cratic processes (e.g., green participatory budgeting) that
allow citizens to vote on sustainable investments for their
communities. This participatory approach would shift per -
ceptions by involving people directly in sustainability deci-
sions and fostering a sense of responsibility. As a second
implication for policymakers at the local level, governments
(and NGOs) could design geographically targeted educa-
tional campaigns (Rowe, 2007). Such campaigns, tailored
to communities facing high income inequality, would focus
on raising awareness of unsustainable consumption’s envi-
ronmental, social, and economic consequences, specifically
addressing the knowledge gaps in these areas. For example,
drawing on the finding that awareness of consequences and
ascription of responsibility play a key role in regions with
high levels of inequality, showcasing sustainable practices
by local individuals or families from different socioeco-
nomic backgrounds can provide relatable examples. This
reduces the perception that only affluent or powerful actors
are responsible for sustainability, making it clear that small
actions matter, even at the household level.
Third, businesses can draw valuable insights from this
research, particularly regarding differentiated marketing and
pricing strategies. Businesses could consider how income
inequality influences consumer behavior, for example,
by promoting sustainable products through tiered pricing
models. Offering discounted or more affordable versions
of sustainable products to lower income consumers could
encourage wider adoption and raise awareness that sustain-
able consumption can make a difference. In addition, by
promoting low-cost sustainable habits (e.g., reducing food
waste or using less water), businesses could reduce the feel-
ing that sustainability is only the responsibility of those who
can afford expensive, eco-friendly products.
Fourth, given the finding that the awareness of conse-
quences is lower in contexts with higher income inequality,
businesses should acknowledge the importance of clear envi-
ronmental impact labeling when marketing their products in
highly unequal markets. For example, including information
about a product’s resource usage or carbon footprint can
enhance consumer awareness of the environmental con-
sequences of their purchases (Camilleri, 2019; Taufique,
2022), thereby encouraging consumers to make more sus-
tainable choices.
Fifth, with regard to implications for business ethics, this
research picked up and connected two phenomena regarded
to be of central importance for business ethics: grand chal-
lenges connected to sustainability (e.g., Greenwood and
Freeman, 2017; Shaw et al., 2022) and inequality (e.g.,

 S. A. Brieger et al.
Cornelius, 2022; Jain et al., 2022). We show that these phe-
nomena are fundamentally interconnected and that ethical
and sustainable consumption can be regarded as a transmit-
ter between these phenomena. Highlighting this intercon-
nection is an important insight for business ethics, as the
negative link between economic inequality and sustainable
consumption questions whether consumers can be expected
to act as ethical agents when economic inequality is high.
The findings thus challenge the common assumption in
consumer ethics that individuals can express moral values
through marketplace choices (see e.g., Shaw et al., 2022). By
connecting the issues of economic inequality and sustainable
consumption, we also address the call by Jain et al. (2022)
to regard grand ethical challenges as intertwined and to not
address them as separate problems. In line with Jain et al.
(2022, p. 836), we show that an ethical solution to the grand
challenges of unsustainability goes beyond seeking a “silver
bullet (often technological) solution.” Instead, it is essential
to assess the ethical antecedents of these challenges, such as
economic inequality.
Limitations and Avenues for Further
Research
The limitations of this study suggest directions for further
research. A first limitation is that our studies do not capture
the attitudes and decision-making of the highly privileged
and powerful. It is well documented that “political and eco-
nomic elites are not likely to respond to surveys, participate
in experiments, or allow an ethnographer routine access to
their decision-making processes” (Dietz, 2023, p. 15). Thus,
we have only a limited understanding of how privileged and
powerful elites differ in their personal norms and sustainable
consumption behavior across contexts characterized by dif-
ferent levels of inequality.
Second, our study uses indicators of sustainable con-
sumption that require additional spending, such as willing-
ness to pay more for environmentally friendly products and
the actual purchase of environmentally friendly products.
While ensuring that consumers are willing to purchase envi-
ronmentally friendly products is key for sustainability transi-
tions (Sala & Castellani, 2019), future research could expand
this scope by examining other forms of sustainable behavior,
which are more accessible to low-income groups—such as
minimizing food waste, recycling products at the end of the
product lifetime, or conscious consumption for environmen-
tal reasons (cf. Auxtova et al., 2025; Mehmood et al., 2024;
Meng et al., 2025).
Third, it is important to note that this research demon-
strates the negative effect of inequality on sustainable con-
sumption in both an international context and within the
UK—a country characterized by relatively high income
inequality from a Western perspective but moderate levels in
a global context. While income inequality remains a serious
issue in Europe, comparative research indicates that its lev-
els are generally more moderate than those observed in many
other world regions (World Inequality Database, 2025).
This raises the question of how sustainable consumption is
affected in countries with even higher levels of inequality.
Future research could therefore extend the analysis to other
world regions to better understand both between-country
and within-country variations in the relationship between
inequality and sustainable consumption.
Fourth, while norm activation theory offers a useful
lens through which to understand the relationship between
economic inequality and individual sustainable consump -
tion, it has important limitations and cannot fully account
for the complexity of individual behavioral responses. For
instance, norm activation theory assumes a largely linear
process whereby sustainable behavior is driven by personal
norms that must first be consciously activated. However,
research increasingly shows that sustainable (consumption)
behaviors often emerge from automatic, habitual, or situ-
ational processes rather than from deliberate moral reason-
ing or norm activation (Klöckner & Blöbaum, 2010; Ouel-
lette and Wood, 1998; Shove, 2010; Verplanken and Roy,
2016). Moreover, while norm activation theory posits that a
heightened ascription of responsibility strengthens personal
norms and thereby promotes sustainable behavior, research
also shows that a strong sense of responsibility can at times
overwhelm consumers. For instance, Longo et al. ( 2019)
show that consumers with deep knowledge of sustainability
issues may experience emotional distress upon recogniz-
ing the challenges of achieving a fully sustainable lifestyle.
Similarly, individuals who feel personally responsible for
“saving the planet” may experience frustration and moral
conflict when they realize that their individual actions alone
are insufficient, which can ultimately lead to disengagement
from sustainable behaviors. Future research should therefore
apply alternative theoretical lenses to deepen the understand-
ing of individual-level sustainable consumption, particularly
within contexts characterized by higher levels of inequality.
One promising avenue could be investigating status anxi-
ety, which is why individuals in highly unequal societies
may not engage in sustainable consumption (Pybus et al.,
2022). Another interesting perspective could include market
system justification theory (Jasinenko et al., 2020), which
has been found to be associated with economic inequality
(Du & King, 2022; Haack & Sieweke, 2018). As customers
with higher market system justification tend to perceive the
corporate social responsibility (CSR) efforts of low-CSR
companies more favorably, it can be assumed that they may
also perceive the purchase of less sustainable products from
such firms as more legitimate.

between environmental and social aspects of sustainable
