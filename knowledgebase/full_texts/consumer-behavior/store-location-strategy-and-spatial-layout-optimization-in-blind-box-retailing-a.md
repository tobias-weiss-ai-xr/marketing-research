  Abstract
This study examines how store location decisions and in -store spatial design
jointly shape consumer behavior in blind -box retailing, using POP MART as a
representative case. Drawing on central place theory, the Huff gravity model,
third-place theory, the stimulus -organism-response and
pleasure-arousal-dominance frameworks, and the ories of scarcity, collecting and
social identity, it develops an integrated location -space-behavior framework. A
qualitative case -study approach compares landmark flagship stores, standard
shopping-center stores and a high -risk lower -tier location scenari o, drawing on
corporate disclosures, industry reports and publicly available consumer reviews.
The analysis indicates that POP MART expands hierarchically and selectively, and
that location suitability depends on target -consumer concentration, tenant -mix
compatibility, store visibility, accessibility and joint -marketing opportunities
rather than on pedestrian traffic and rental cost alone. At the store level, spatial
design influences approach behavior, browsing duration, product exposure, social
interaction and purchase intention. The study proposes an optimization
framework combining decompression zones, loop -based circulation, distributed
attraction points, IP -themed zones, unboxing areas and data -supported
adjustment, and recommends staged market entry, modular store design and
product recycling.

1. Introduction
Generation Z has substantially reshaped retail demand. These consumers value
personalized expression, emotional satisfaction and identity construction as much as
product function, and their decisions are strongly influenced by social media content,
online communities and peer recommendation. Within this shift, designer toys have
grown from a niche cultural product into a rapidly expanding retail category, and

POP MART has played a leading role in their commercialization in China by
combining artist-created IP with the blind-box format. Blind-box consumption draws
on curiosity, the desire to complete a series, perceived scarcity and the pleasure of
opening an unknown package, so the value of a product extends well beyond its
physical attributes[1][2].
Although e -commerce and automated retail machines broaden market  coverage,
physical stores remain strategically important. They provide sensory stimulation,
immediate product interaction and a social atmosphere that online channels cannot
reproduce, and they operate simultaneously as sales outlets, brand communication
platforms, exhibition spaces and meeting points[3][4]. The performance of an offline
store depends on two closely connected decisions: where it is located and how its
interior space is organized. Location determines the quantity and the composition of
potential customer traffic; spatial layout determines whether that traffic is converted
into engagement and sales.
Existing research has tended to treat these decisions separately. Retail location
studies emphasize accessibility, market potential and economic efficiency, whereas
store-environment studies examine atm ospherics and layout within a given site.
Comparatively little attention has been paid to how external location conditions and
internal spatial design jointly shape consumer behavior in experience -oriented
retailing[5][6].
This study therefore takes POP MART as a representative case and examines the
relationship among store location, spatial layout and consumer behavior. It adopts a
qualitative case -study design drawing on corporate disclosures, industry reports,
media coverage and publicly available consumer reviews, and compares landmark
flagship stores, standard shopping-center stores and a high -risk lower-tier scenario.
The analysis follows a location -space-behavior framework: location conditions are
evaluated first, spatial design is then interpreted in behavioral terms, and the two
levels are finally integra ted into optimization proposals. Section 2 reviews the
theoretical foundations, Section 3 analyzes the location strategy, Section 4 proposes
behavior-based layout optimization, Section 5 discusses challenges and responses,
and Section 6 concludes.
2. Theoretical Framework
2.1. Retail Location Theories
Central place theory explains the hierarchical distribution of commercial functions
through market thresholds and ranges. Frequently purchased necessities have low
thresholds and short ranges, whereas specialt y goods require larger market areas
and higher concentrations of effective demand. Designer toys behave as specialty
goods: their consumption is discretionary, their value depends on symbolic and
aesthetic recognition, and demand is concentrated among youn g urban consumers.

Third-place theory adds a social dimension. Informal gathering places outside the
home and the workpl ace derive value from the relationships and community
experiences they support. Designer toy stores can perform this role, because
blind-box consumption involves collecting, exchanging, unboxing and sharing. A
store that maximizes merchandise density and transaction speed, however, provides
little social space. Location evaluation thus extends from market accessibility to
social embeddedness.
2.2. Consumer Behavior in Blind-Box Retailing
The stimulus -organism-response model holds that environmental stimuli affect
internal psychological states, which in turn produce behavioral responses. In a retail
context, stimuli include lighting, color, music, spatial density and product displays;
internal states include perceived attractiveness, curiosity and emotional arousal;
responses include approach or avoidance, dwell time, purchase and social sharing [9].
These internal states are commonly described through pleasure, arousal and
dominance. Bright colors, focused lighting and large IP installations may raise

pleasure and arousal, whereas excessive product density, confusing circulation and
crowding reduce perceived control and encourage avoidance [6].
Scarcity and uncertainty are central to blind -box value. Limited editions and secret
figures increase perceived uniqueness, while concealed content generates
anticipatory utility: part of the satisfaction is produced before the outcome is
revealed, and the occasional rare f igure operates as an intermittent reward.
Collecting extends this logic, since figures are interconnected members of a series
and incomplete sets remain cognitively salient, sustaining the motivation to continue.
Social identity theory explains the communi ty dimension: rare figures function as
symbolic capital, and displaying, unboxing or exchanging them communicates
membership . Stores that provide space for such interaction generate value beyond
the transaction itself.
3. POP MART's Store Location Strategy
3.1. Evolution of the Store Network
POP MART's offline expansion has been shaped by both city hierarchy and
commercial-district hierarchy. Early stores were used to test demand and refine the
retail model; once the company focused on its own designer toy  IP, stores in major
metropolitan shopping centers assumed a branding role, positioning the brand
alongside fashion, beauty and entertainment tenants. The format was then replicated
in provincial capitals and supported by complementary channels: roboshops,  which
require little space and staffing and are suitable for testing emerging locations, and
pop-up stores, which support launches and collaborative campaigns at low
commitment.
By the end of 2025 the company operated 410 retail stores and 2,350 roboshops  in
the Chinese mainland, and 445 stores including Hong Kong, Macao and Taiwan.
Growth no longer depends primarily on adding outlets; flagship stores in landmark
locations, including Shanghai, Bangkok and Sydney, are increasingly used to
strengthen cultural and experiential positioning. Four characteristics summarize the
strategy: hierarchical expansion from high -tier cities outward; emphasis on
consumer quality rather than pedestrian volume; reliance on tenant -mix
complementarity; and a close connection be tween location and brand
communication.
3.2. Key Location Factors
Location suitability cannot be determined by pedestrian volume or rental cost alone
[7]. Six factors are decisive. First, compatibility with the target consumer profile: age
structure, purchasing power, cultural consumption habits and familiarity with
designer toy IP matter more than absolute visitor numbers [10]. Second, accessibility
and visibility, which are related but distinct: proximity to public transport, position
along major internal circulation routes, and the ability to be noticed from a distance

are separate requirements. Third, economic feasibility: projected sales, gross margin,
rent, staffing, decoration and renewal costs should be assessed under optimistic,
baseline and pessimistic scenarios, because sales per unit area alone understates the
value of exhibition and interactive space.
Fourth, tenant-mix complementarity: cinemas, fashionable food and beverage outlets,
beauty and consumer- electronics retailers attract compatible visitors and generate
customer-flow and image synergies, whereas an excessive concentration of directly
competing designer toy stores divides demand. Fifth, compatibility with the shopping
center's positioning, which shapes consumer expectations before entry. Sixth,
marketing resources: atriums, exterior plazas, digital screens and public exhibition
areas allow IP campaigns that a purely rental negotiation woul d overlook.
Competition and cannibalization within the company's own network should also be
examined, since a new store placed too close to an existing outlet or roboshop may
redistribute rather than expand demand [8].
3.3. Comparison of Store Formats and Location Scenarios
Three cases illustrate that location quality must be judged against the intended
function of the outlet. The global flagship store on East Nanjing Road in Shanghai,
opened in 2022 with approximately 600 square meters, operates as a destination: it
combines tourism flows, landmark visibility and sufficient space for exhibitions and
interactive installations, and a second, larger flagship open ed nearby in 2025.
Standard shopping-center stores form the main body of the network; they are not
expected to become citywide landmarks but must be replicable, operationally
efficient and profitable, with performance assessed through sales per unit area,
conversion, average transaction value and inventory turnover.
A newly opened peripheral shopping center in a lower-tier city represents a high-risk
scenario. Preferential rent cannot offset a thin target market, limited discretionary
purchasing power, an i ncomplete tenant portfolio, few marketing opportunities and
uncertain shopping-center performance. Under such conditions a pop -up store or
roboshop is a lower-cost market-testing instrument than a permanent lease.

4. Consumer Behavior-Based Spatial Layout Optimization
4.1. Behavior Patterns and Spatial Requirements
Consumer behavior in a POP MART store can be understood as a sequence of
attraction, entry, orientation, exploration, product evaluation, purchase and
post-purchase interaction. Motivations are mixed: goal -directed visitors seek a
particular series or new release, while recreational visitors are drawn by exploration,
visual enjoyment and social interaction, and a single visit often shifts between the
two. At the storefront, consumers process competing stimuli from surrounding
tenants, so a recognizable focal point and visual permeability determine entry[11].
Inside the store, positive emotional responses and comfortable exploration increase
product exposure, but dwell time should not be extended through deliberate
confusion or congestion; distributing popular products across several areas reduces
both crowdi ng and fatigue. Collection -related search requires complete- series
displays and legible IP classification, while duplicate figures create demand for
exchange mechanisms. After purchase, unboxing, comparison and photography
frequently occur on site, and whe n they take place in checkout queues or main
circulation paths they obstruct other customers.

4.2. Optimization Strategies
Optimization should follow the customer journey. The entrance requires a
decompression zone, that is, a relatively open transition area in which consumers
adjust from the shopping-center environment, together with a clear visual hierarchy
in which one primary focal po int, such as a full -wall blind -box display, can be
identified immediately. Storefronts should be visually permeable, entrance displays
should be updated with new releases, and promotional information should be
simplified; popular displays should not be pos itioned where stopping customers
block the entrance.
Circulation should be organized as a loop connecting the entrance, major IP zones,
new-product displays, interactive areas and the checkout, defined by flooring,
lighting and shelf orientation rather than by physical barriers. Attraction points
should be distributed along the route in a progressive rhythm, with particular
attention to corners and deep areas that fall outside consumers' initial field of vision.
Shortcuts should remain available for goal -directed visitors, and path width should
be increased where product launches generate queues.
Display and zoning should balance commercial density with consumer comfort. A
visual hierarchy should distinguish new releases and rare editions from routine
merchandise; density can be high for repeated blind-box walls but lower for products
that require individual appreciation . IP zones should be visually distinct yet
consistent with the overall brand identity, and each should carry a concise narrative
rather than a simple product grouping. A dedicated unboxing and social-sharing area,
modest in size but well lit and equipped with a stable surface and waste disposal,
separates post-purchase interaction from circulation and supports user -generated
content.
Digital t echnology should function as an enabling layer rather than a source of
novelty. Augmented reality and in- store screens can extend IP narratives and
preview products; electronic shelf labels can update prices, inventory and purchase
limits during launches; online -offline inventory integration reduces unsuccessful
visits; and anonymous customer -flow analysis, combined with sales records and
employee observation, allows layout adjustments to be evaluated through controlled
comparison. Data collection should re main aggregated and privacy -compliant, and
technological investment should be justified by measurable benefit .
5. Challenges and Strategic Responses
Continued expansion faces four constraints. Extension into lower -tier cities risks

market mismatch, since the model developed in first-tier and major second-tier cities
cannot be transferred directly. Continued dependence on prime commercial districts
raises rental and renovation costs. Competition for suitable retail space is
intensifying as other experience -oriented formats pursue the same units.
International expansion adds differences in consumer preference, shopping -center
structure and cultural interpretation of IP.
Store operations face parallel difficulties: rapid IP iteration requires frequent spatial
updates; uneven customer flows create crowding during launches and idle capacity
at other times; secondary -market resale confirms collectable value but encourages
speculative purchasing; consistency across store formats is difficult to maintain; and
packaging generates environmental pressure.
Five responses follow. The company should enter uncertain markets in stages, using
pop-up stores and roboshops before committing to permanent leases. Store formats
should be differentiated by function, with flagship stores concentrating on
exhibitions and events and standard stores on operational efficiency. Fixtures should
be modular so that displays can be reconfigured without renovation. An official
duplicate-exchange and recycling system would address both collection frustration
and packaging waste. Finally, layout optimization should become a continuous,
data-supported process rather than a one-time design decision.
6. Conclusions
This study examined POP MART's store location strategy and spatial layout from a
consumer behavior perspective. Location decisions extend beyond traffic, distance
and commercial scale: suitable sites require a concentration of target consumers,
tenant-mix compatibility, visibility, accessibility and opportunities for IP -related
marketing. The company applies a differentiated format strategy in which flagship
stores serve brand communication, standard stores provide replicable profitability,
and pop-up stores and roboshops test uncertain markets. Spatial layout operates as a
behavioral guidan ce system: entrance design affects attention and store entry,
circulation affects product exposure and dwell time, zoning affects search efficiency
and social interaction, and post -purchase areas affect sharing and repeat visits. The
value of a physical st ore therefore cannot be assessed through sales per unit area
alone.
The study is conceptual and based on a single company, and it does not assign
empirical weights to the proposed factors. Future research could test the
location-space-behavior framework qu antitatively using consumer surveys,
behavioral observation, transaction data and spatial heatmaps, and could compare
different designer toy brands, city tiers and national markets. Omnichannel
integration, responsible blind -box consumption and packaging r eduction merit
particular attention as experience-oriented retailers expand.
