The development of the household gas network
(jargas) supports the domestic energy transition,
but its success depends on public acceptance, not
just infrastructure. This qualitative research with
grounded theory explores the process of
receiving gas netwo rks through interviews,
observations, and documentation of active
customers. The results showed that acceptance
was formed from the interaction of benefit

strengthen service adoption. These findings
contribute to the study of energy consumer

services.

INTRODUCTION
Why do some communities accept the use of the household gas network,
while others continue to show resistance even though the service offers relatively
better economic and operational benefits?" This question is a fundamental issue
in the development of ho usehold gas networks (jargas) as part of the national
energy transformation in Indonesia. The question is not only related to the
adoption of energy technology, but also touches on the social process that
explains how people build trust, interpret benefits, evaluate risks, and ultimately
decide to accept or reject an energy service innovation.
Attention to public acceptance or social acceptance has been an important
theme in the energy transition literature over the past two decades ( (Claudy et
al., 2011) ; (Huijts et al., 2012) ; (Sovacool & Gross, 2015) ; (Wolsink, 2018) ;
(Wüstenhagen et al., 2007) ). Various studies show that the success of the
implementation of energy innovation is not only determined by technological
readiness, economic feasibility, and regulatory support, but also by the level of
public acceptance as an end user. In fact, many en ergy projects that are
technically feasible and economically feasible experience implementation
obstacles due to low social acceptance of the innovations introduced.
The urgency to understand the process of forming public revenue is
increasing along with the development of the national energy transition agenda.
In the Indonesian context, the development of a household gas network is one of
the strategic programs that a ims to improve the efficiency of domestic energy
consumption while reducing dependence on subsidized LPG. Gas networks offer
various benefits, such as continuity of energy supply, ease of use, long-term cost
efficiency, and improved safety aspects through network-based distribution
systems.
However, the development of the domestic gas network nationally still
shows relatively limited achievements compared to the target set by the
government. Data from the Downstream Oil and Gas Regulatory Agency shows
that by the end of 2024 the number of new  household connections will reach
around 818 thousand connections, while the national target set in the National
Medium-Term Development Plan will reach four million household connections.
The considerable gap between the target and the realization indicat es that the
challenge of developing the gas network is not only related to infrastructure
development, but also concerns public acceptance of grid-based energy services.
The marketing literature, consumer behavior, and innovation show that
acceptance of a service is not solely determined by the functional benefits offered.
(Kotler, n.d.) explains that consumer decisions are influenced by a combination
of psychological, social, and experiential factors that shape the perception of a
product or service. In the context of services, (Zeithaml et al., 2000) emphasizing
that customer experience and quality of service interaction have an important
role in building trust and encouraging customer acceptance. Meanwhile, research
on energy innovation shows that risk perception, social legitimacy, and public
trust are important factors influencing public acceptance of new energy
technologies and services ( (Claudy et al., 2011) ; (Huijts et al., 2012) ;
(Wüstenhagen et al., 2007)).

Various theoretical perspectives have been used to explain the
phenomenon. Diffusion of Innovations Theory (Rogers, 2003) explained that the
level of innovation adoption is influenced by the perception of relative profits,
suitability, complexity, ease of trial, and ease of observation of the benefits of
innovation. Meanwhile, the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis et al., 1989)
emphasizing the importance of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use
in shaping user acceptance of a technology.
Despite making significant contributions, both approaches tend to position
individuals as rational decision-makers and focus on evaluating the benefits and
characteristics of innovation. In practice, the acceptance of domestic energy
services such as the h ousehold gas network is a more complex phenomenon
because it involves user experience, service interaction, risk perception, trust
relationships with service providers, and the influence of the social environment
in which people interact. Therefore, an app roach that only focuses on
technological factors and individual perceptions has not been able to fully
explain the dynamics of public acceptance and rejection of gas network services.
Empirical studies of domestic gas networks in Indonesia also show similar
limitations. Most of the research still focuses on technical, economic, and energy
policy aspects, such as distribution efficiency, investment feasibility and
infrastructure developm ent strategies. Research that examines aspects of user
behavior is still relatively limited and generally uses a quantitative approach
based on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) or other structured behavior
models. As a result, the understanding of how  society builds meaning,
experience, trust, and social legitimacy for the use of household gas networks is
still not sufficiently developed.
Thus, there are important research gaps to overcome. Until now, there is
still a limited understanding of the social processes that explain how the
acceptance and rejection of the household gas network is formed in people's daily
lives. In fact, understand ing the process is crucial to explain why services that
objectively offer better economic and operational benefits do not always gain
high acceptance rates in society.
Based on these gaps, this study aims to develop a theoretical understanding
of the process of forming public acceptance and rejection of the use of household
gas networks. In contrast to previous research which was dominated by
deductive approaches and bas ed on variable testing, this study uses the
Grounded Theory approach to delve deeply into experiences, perceptions, social
interactions, and the process of forming meaning that underlies people's
decisions in accepting or rejecting the use of gas.
Through this approach, this research is expected to produce a conceptual
model that explains the mechanism of forming public acceptance and resistance
to the household gas network in a more comprehensive, contextual, and in
accordance with the social chara cteristics of Indonesian society. In addition to
contributing to the development of literature on social acceptance of energy
innovation, this research is also expected to provide practical implications for
governments, regulators, and energy service providers in designing strategies for

THEORETICAL REVIEW
Factors Determining Public Acceptance of Energy Innovation
Public acceptance of energy innovation is not solely determined by
technical excellence or economic efficiency, but is a multidimensional
phenomenon involving psychological, social, and institutional factors.
Innovation Diffusion Theory (Rogers, 2003) Identify five key characteristics that
influence adoption: relative superiority, suitability, complexity, trial, and
intensity. Meanwhile, the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis et al., 1989)
emphasizing two core constructs, namely the perception of benefits and the
perception of ease of use as the main determinants of technology acceptance. In
the context of the household gas network (jargas), the public evaluates benefits
such as cost efficiency, continuity of supply, and ease of operation compared to
cylinder LPG, but also considers the risks and adaptation efforts required.
In addition to individual factors, the acceptance of energy innovation is also
influenced by the social environment and collective learning mechanisms. Rogers
explained that the adoption process takes place gradually through the stages of
knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, and confirmation. In practice,
people not only make rational decisions based on functional benefits, but are also
influenced by other users' experiences and social recommendations. Therefore,
the successful implementation of  the gas network requires an approach that
focuses not only on the characteristics of the technology, but also on how the
public builds a perception of the benefits, risks, and social legitimacy of the
innovation.

The Role of Customer Experience and Trust in Gas Services
Customer Perceived Value (Parasuraman et al., 1988)  Explains that the
customer shapes the use decision based on a thorough evaluation of the benefits
obtained compared to the sacrifices made. These benefits are not only economic
and functional, but also include emotional, psychological, and social benefits. In
gas network services, the perception of positive value is formed when people feel
cost efficiency, ease of use, security, and convenience that exceed installation
costs, leakage risk, or supply uncertainty. The greater the perceived value, the
higher the acceptance and sustainability of the use of the service.
Trust Theory is an important foundation in public utility services due to the
high uncertainty and inherent risks. (Morgan & Hunt, 1994)  states that trust
reduces the perception of risk and strengthens the long -term relationship
between the customer and the service provider. In the context of the gas network,
trust is formed through positive experiences on supply stability, responsiveness
to handling disruptions, information transparency, and operational safety
assurance. A superior customer experience, covering all touchpoints from
socialization to complaint handling, directly influences the perception of value
and trust, ultimately driving satisfaction, loyalty, and positive recommendations.

Social Legitimacy and Community Dynamics in Domestic Energy Adoption
Social legitimacy (Suchman, 1995)  It is a collective perception that an
innovation is considered appropriate, in accordance with societal norms, values,
and expectations. In domestic energy use, individual decisions are not entirely
autonomous but are influenced by the experiences of neig hbors, community
leaders, and community norms. In a communal society, social legitimacy is an
important mechanism that reduces uncertainty and risk perception. The more
users in a region provide a positive experience, the higher the social legitimacy
of th e network, which in turn accelerates adoption and strengthens the
sustainability of the use of the service.
The process of forming social legitimacy is closely related to observability
and social trialability. When people can observe firsthand the tangible benefits
that other users get, such as ease of cooking, supply stability, and cost savings,
the collective perception of services becomes more positive. Conversely, negative
information spread in social networks can hinder acceptance even if it is
technically a quality service. Therefore, the successful implementation of the gas
network depends not only on the technical quality and management of the
company, but also on the ability to build sustainable social support through
effective communication, community participation, and the proof of benefits that
can be widely observed at the community level.

METHODOLOGY
This study uses a qualitative approach with a Grounded Theory design that
aims to build a substantive theory about public acceptance of the household gas
network (jargas) based on experience, social interaction, and the construction of
meaning by users (Rukin, 2019). This approach was chosen because it is able to
explore the complexity of social phenomena that are not sufficiently explained
through variable measurements alone. The location of the research was
determined purposively in Riau Province, including Pekanba ru City, Perawang
District (Siak Regency), Pelalawan Regency, and Dumai City. These four regions
were chosen because they have active customers with diverse social, economic,
and environmental characteristics, so that they are able to provide information -
rich cases to develop theories rooted in empirical reality.
The main informant is an active customer of the gas network with a
minimum use period of one year, selected by purposive sampling accompanied
by theoretical sampling until it reaches theoretical saturation. Supporting
informants include service officers, t echnicians, and community leaders for
perspective triangulation. Data collection was carried out through three
techniques at once: semi -structured in-depth interviews to explore informants'
experiences and perceptions, field observations to understand the real conditions
of gas network use, and documentation to collect supporting data such as service
reports and socialization materials. These three techniques are carried out
simultaneously and iteratively according to the flow of Grounded Theory.
The data analysis followed the three stages of Strauss and Corbin's model:
open coding (identifying initial concepts such as perceptions of efficiency, safety,
and social influence), axial coding (connecting between categories in a cause-and-

RESULTS
Informant Profile and Stages of the Gas Revenue Process
The informants consist of active customers of the gas network (experience
≥1 year) and supporting informants (service officers, technicians, community
leaders). The majority use gas networks for cooking and domestic activities. The
process of public accept ance takes place through five interrelated stages: initial
perception formation, benefit -risk evaluation, user experience, trust building,
and social legitimacy. These five stages show that acceptance is not an instant
decision, but rather the result of cognitive, empirical, and social interactions.

The perception of the value of benefits has proven to be the fundamental
foundation that drives people's initial acceptance. Functional benefits such as ease
of use, continuity of supply, and efficiency of household activities are key
considerations. However, this study also found an emotional dimension of value
perception, in the form of increased comfort and a reduction in psychological
burden in energy management. This is in line with the expansion of the concept of
value (Holbrook, 1999) which includes experiential and emotional value. Thus, the
perception of value is not only cognitive -rational but also affective, which
strengthens society's transition from the evaluation stage to the actual use stage.

Trust in service emerges as a central factor that determines continued
acceptance. Trust is not formed instantly, but rather through the accumulation of
positive experiences on two dimensions: technical quality (supply stability,
installation safety) and f unctional quality (officer responsiveness, information
transparency). The results of this study confirm the view (Grönroos, 2007) that
service relationship marketing is built through the consistency of the two
dimensions. In addition, trust is also relational: good interaction between
companies and customers, including prompt handling of complaints, is key in
building public confidence that the risks of gas use can be managed properly.
Another dominant factor is customer experience and social legitimacy.
Positive experiences across service touchpoints (from installation to disruption
handling) reinforce value perceptions, trust and drive recommendation behavior.
Conversely, negative expe riences can weaken the entire acceptance structure.
Meanwhile, social legitimacy —which is formed through other users'
recommendations, community collective experiences, and social norms—serves as
an external reinforcer. These findings support the concept of observability of (Orr,
2003) that the easier it is to observe the benefits of innovation in a social
environment, the faster adoption occurs. Overall, the five categories found —
benefit value perception, risk perception, customer experience, trust, and social
legitimacy—are integrated into the core category of "Experience-Based Trust and
Benefit Value," which explains that net revenue is the result of a dynamic
interaction between empirical experience, verified benefits, and social support
within the community.

CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
This study concludes that public acceptance of the household gas network
(jargas) is a complex and multidimensional social process, not just an economic or
technical decision. Perceptions of benefits  such as convenience, efficiency, and
continuity of supply are the starting points that drive positive orientation, but they
are not yet deterministic. Customer experience plays a central role in consolidating
that perception: positive experiences reinforce trust and a sense of security, while
negative experiences can lead to resistance. In addition, social legitimacy  in the
form of environmental recommendations, the experience of other users, and
collective acceptance within communal communities serves as a social validation
that accelerates adoption and strengthens acceptance stability. Overall, the success
of network development depends not only on infrastructure and technology, but
also on the ability of institutions (companies and governments)  to build quality
customer experiences, strengthen trust, and manag e social legitimacy at the
community level. Thus, public acceptance of grid-based domestic energy services
is the result of an integration of functional, experience, relational, and social factors
that work simultaneously in shaping decisions on the adoption and sustainability
of service use.
