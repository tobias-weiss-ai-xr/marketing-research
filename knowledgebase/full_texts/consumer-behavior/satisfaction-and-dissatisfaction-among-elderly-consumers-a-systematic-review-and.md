Abstract
This article synthesizes five and a half decades of work (1970–2025) on satis -
faction and dissatisfaction among elderly consumers through a systematic review
conducted in adherence to PRISMA  2020 with a predefined review protocol, in -
dependent duplicate screening and data extraction, a qualitative risk of bias ap -
praisal, and a GRADE assessment of evidence certainty of studies published in 100
peer-reviewed journals in marketing, retailing, and consumer behavior. We frame
satisfaction (dissatisfaction) as the positive (negative) evaluation that arises when
the perceived gap between expectations and desires and the actual consumption
experience is favorable (unfavorable). Six business-relevant themes emerge. On the
satisfaction side, greater well-being, deeper brand attachment and stronger customer
loyalty characterize older consumers. On the dissatisfaction side, complaint behav -
ior is activated and shaped by individual characteristics and the specific nature of
the grievance. Building on these insights, we propose a research agenda to guide
future theoretical and managerial work in an aging marketplace.

1 Introduction
Population aging is reshaping demand, service delivery, and brand relationships. By
2030, one person in six worldwide will be aged 60 years or older, and by 2050 the
global 60-plus population will approximately double to 2.1 billion, with rapid growth
among those 80-plus, according to the World Health Organization (WHO 2024).
These shifts increase the salience of elderly consumers’ needs and elevate the stakes
of getting satisfaction and dissatisfaction right for firms and policymakers alike.
In consumer research, satisfaction is commonly modeled as an evaluative judg -
ment that follows the confirmation or disconfirmation of expectations and desires
during the consumption process (Guido 2014a). This judgment links to perceived
value, service quality, and encounter-level experiences that shape post-choice atti -
tudes and behaviors. Classic work on expectancy-disconfirmation, perceived value,
and service quality remains foundational for organizing evidence on elderly consum-
ers’ outcomes (Oliver 2010). At the same time, life course theories clarify why older
adults may arrive at distinct evaluative judgments (Moschis 2019). Socioemotional
selectivity theory proposes that as perceived time horizons narrow, goals shift from
knowledge acquisition to emotion regulation, altering how experiences are appraised
(Carstensen and Reynolds 2023). Related work documents a positivity effect in atten-
tion and memory that can color evaluations (Carstensen and DeLiema 2018), while
research on cognitive age shows that how old people feel may be more predictive of
marketplace responses than chronological age (Peluso et al. 2020). Together these
perspectives suggest that satisfaction—and dissatisfaction—among older consumers
reflect motivational priorities, identity processes, and age-related shifts in informa -
tion processing.
Against this backdrop, the literature on elderly consumers is extensive but frag -
mented across domains such as retailing, health services, and media commerce,
and across methods from experiments to qualitative inquiry. Previous reviews have
advanced understanding of specific aspects of elderly consumer behavior. Yoon et al.
(2009) synthesized cognitive and motivational processes in older adults’ decision-
making but did not systematically address satisfaction outcomes. Cameron et al.
(2016) examined complaint behavior in service contexts without integrating satisfac-
tion antecedents or connecting dissatisfaction to broader loyalty dynamics. Sachdeva
(2024) mapped the economic significance of the senior market while focusing pri -
marily on consumption trends rather than evaluative judgments. While these contri -
butions illuminate important facets of elderly consumption, they collectively provide
a fragmented account of consumer evaluation in later life, treating satisfaction and
dissatisfaction as separate phenomena, dispersing findings across domains and meth-
ods, and lacking an integrative perspective capable of reconciling positive and nega-
tive evaluations across the consumption trajectory.
The present review addresses these gaps by offering an integrative synthesis of
satisfaction and dissatisfaction among elderly consumers. By reconnecting positive
and negative evaluative processes within a unified thematic framework and tracing
their development across more than five decades of empirical research (1970–2025),
this study advances the literature toward a process-oriented understanding of con -
sumer evaluation in later life. We treat satisfaction and dissatisfaction as judgments

their antecedents and downstream outcomes. Following the guidance of Paul and
Criado (2020), we adopted a theme-based systematic literature review approach that
emphasizes transparency, replicability, and practical relevance, operationalizing a

By providing the first systematic integration of satisfaction and dissatisfaction
dynamics in later life across five and a half decades of research, this review advances
both theoretical understanding and practical guidance. Specifically, it makes three
contributions. First, it offers conceptual clarity by organizing the evidence into six
business-relevant themes: on the satisfaction side, the pursuit of well-being, brand
attachment, and loyalty; on the dissatisfaction side, complaints, complainant profiles,
and types and management of grievances. Second, it reconciles tensions in prior work
by situating encounter-level drivers of satisfaction alongside recovery and response
dynamics after failures, and by contrasting brand-attachment mechanisms with store-
loyalty patterns. Third, it translates the synthesis into actionable implications, high -
lighting levers that repeatedly matter for older consumers’ satisfaction, including
respectful interpersonal contact, clarity and fairness in responses to problems, and
decision-support formats that reduce search costs without signaling patronization.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The next section describes the
methodology used for the theme-based structured systematic literature review. The
following section presents and discusses the six themes identified in our systematic
review. Based on this framework, we subsequently provide an outline of our sugges-
tions for future research and discuss implications for both theory and practice. We
then illustrate the external validity of these implications through documented real-
world cases. A final section concludes the paper.
2 Methodology
This research provides a systematic analysis of studies examining satisfaction and
dissatisfaction among elderly consumers in marketing contexts, drawing on peer-
reviewed articles published in marketing and consumer journals over more than five
decades (1970–2025). The review encompasses empirical research adopting experi -
mental, descriptive, exploratory, and mixed-method designs, as well as prior reviews
and theoretical contributions relevant to understanding evaluative processes in later-
life consumption. In some respects, this inquiry bears the features of a “qualitative
meta-analysis” (Bal and Nijkamp 2001): by presenting the results of published stud-
ies in a coherent, integrated form, it considers all articles on the subject within a
specific time frame and a defined array of journals. In accordance with the Preferred
Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines
(Page et al. 2021), our research followed the prescribed process in three stages: (1)
identifying the relevant literature; (2) assessing it for screening and eligibility; and (3)

extracting and synthesizing data, with study selection and data extraction performed
independently by two reviewers and discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer.
For identifying the relevant literature, we used a theme-based approach to a struc-
tured systematic literature review (Paul and Criado 2020; Snyder 2019) and focused
on the peer-reviewed articles that constitute the basis of the marketing research
domain, supplementing database searches with backward/forward citation chasing
and targeted checks of grey literature (e.g., policy reports, white papers, theses) to
assess potential publication bias, while restricting eligibility to peer reviewed jour -
nals. Examined studies were taken only from scholarly journals subjected to a peer-
review process. A study was considered a peer-reviewed scholarly paper when: (a)
it was written by university academics for a predominantly academic audience; (b)
it was intended for publication in journals recognized for their scientific objectives;
(c) its publication format was an article; and (d) it underwent an editorial process
involving review and approval by subject-matter experts to ensure quality standards.
Five conference proceedings from established scholarly associations—specifically,
the American Marketing Association and the Association for Consumer Research—
were included as they respect these rules and are widely recognized as core outlets
in marketing and consumer research. The present analysis concerned articles pub -
lished over the last 55 years—from January 1st, 1970 to August 1st, 2025—in lead -
ing Anglo-American academic marketing and consumer-behavior journals, searching
at minimum Business Source Premier, EconLit, and ABI/INFORM, and completing
the final search on 1 August 2025 with citation-chaining updates recorded. Busi -
ness Source Premier is one of the most popular databases for managerial research,
offering full text from more than 2200 business journals with coverage dating back
to 1965; EconLit is the world’s leading economics database, now containing more
than two million bibliographic records from 1886 to the present; and ABI/INFORM
Complete is a key economic-financial database, encompassing roughly 5000 interna-
tional journals with content available from 1971 onward. English-language journals
were selected because English is the field’s lingua franca and most studies originate
in Anglo-American contexts. In addition, three widely recognized French-language
journals were included, as they emerged from our database queries and met our topi-
cal inclusion criteria. These journals are indexed in the databases used for this review
and are established outlets in marketing and consumer research. Their publication of
English versions and abstracts facilitated data extraction and verification.
For assessing the literature for screening and eligibility, we followed a mixed
quantitative–qualitative procedure. First, we defined twenty keywords or strings—
i.e., aged, ageing, baby boomer, baby boomers, elderly, gerontology, grey consumer,
grey consumers , mature consumer , mature consumers , old age , older consumer ,
older consumers, retired, retirement, senior consumer, senior consumers, senior citi-
zen, senior citizens, seniority—on the basis of the most recurring terms in scientific
articles (as mapped in the comprehensive review of elderly consumer literature by
Guido 2014b). We then added the two focal terms of our thematic review: satisfaction
and dissatisfaction, combining terms with Boolean operators across title/abstract/
keywords (and the full text, when available) and documenting full search strings and
date limits for reproducibility (Online Appendix 1). These strings were searched in
the two EBSCOhost databases and in the ABI/INFORM Complete, restricting the

sample, measures, and outcomes using a piloted form; we then conducted a quali -
tative, design-specific risk-of-bias appraisal (selection, measurement, confounding,
reporting), recorded funding sources and author-declared conflicts where available,
and produced a narrative synthesis following SWiM (Synthesis Without Meta-anal -

without quantitative pooling. For both primary and review articles, we explicitly
coded the funding status as reported by the authors (funded vs. unfunded/no funding
statement) and treated this as a study-level characteristic during synthesis. Among
funded studies, all authors declared no conflicts of interest. At the theme level, we
evaluated small-study effects and publication bias (e.g., scarcity of null results, meth-
odological asymmetries). We applied GRADE principles qualitatively to assess the
certainty of evidence, considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, impreci -
sion, and publication bias. Our scan of the grey literature revealed no apparently
suppressed findings, and the substantial representation of negative outcomes across
themes suggests that publication bias was unlikely.
3 Thematic analysis
Themes and subthemes were identified through iterative reading and conceptual clus-
tering, following the principles of thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006) and
interpretive content analysis in consumer research (Spiggle 1994). Given the hetero-
geneity of study designs and contexts spanning five and a half decades, the thematic
presentation adopts a narrative-descriptive approach rather than a meta-analytic one.
Theme development followed an iterative and interpretive process consistent with
narrative and thematic synthesis approaches. Studies were repeatedly read and con -
ceptually clustered based on their primary contribution to satisfaction and dissatisfac-
tion among elderly consumers. Emerging themes were discussed and refined within
the author team until conceptual saturation and internal coherence were reached. The

The experience-evaluation stage of elderly consumers typically concerns their
evaluative judgments of satisfaction or dissatisfaction—defined as a psychological
state arising from the perceived divergence between accumulated expectations and
desires, on the one hand, and actual consumption experiences, on the other. This
state may foster greater well-being, brand attachment, and loyalty toward brands or,
conversely, in the case of dissatisfaction, may trigger complaints shaped by elderly
consumers’ individual characteristics and the nature of their grievances. Appendi -
ces 4–9 illustrate—for the six themes identified in our review—each of the articles
extracted, the type of research conducted (exploratory, descriptive, experimental,
theoretical, or review), the independent variables considered (age and others), the
dependent variables (satisfaction-/dissatisfaction-related variables), and the sample
description (when available). Although several articles addressed multiple themes,
we allocated each article to the theme to which it contributed most and provided

detail the themes identified.

forms about an experience: when these anticipations exceed, at the moment of evalu-

4 Elderly consumer satisfaction
4.1 Well-being
The pursuit of well-being is a multidimensional evaluative process through which
older adults gauge and adjust circumstances to sustain a worthwhile existence.
Typically, it combines life satisfaction, affective-cognitive appraisals of health, and
pragmatic judgments of healthcare quality, reflecting interplay between fulfillment,
functional capability, and value extracted from support services (see Online Appen -
dix 4). In this review, well-being is not treated as an outcome independent of satisfac-
tion, but as a higher-order evaluative frame through which satisfaction judgments are
formed and interpreted in later life: older consumers’ appraisals of specific consump-
tion experiences are embedded within broader assessments of life quality, health sta-
tus, and healthcare adequacy.
4.1.1 Life satisfaction
According to Berg and Liljedal (2022), interest in older consumer behavior expanded
in the 2010s to include areas such as digital marketing and sustainable consumption,
highlighting the need to understand their satisfaction and responses as this segment
grows. More recently, Zheng et al. ( 2024) reviewed over 100 antecedents of older
consumers’ purchase intentions and behaviors, highlighting how these factors con -
tribute to intergenerational gaps in customer satisfaction and subjective well-being.
As life satisfaction is a key lens for assessing market decisions and outcomes, these
insights align with the view that corporate ethics should go beyond profit, avoid -
ing the exploitation of vulnerable consumers, the dissemination of misleading mes -
sages, the abuse of labor, excessive pricing, and inequitable redistribution of income.
Instead, the ultimate aim should be the welfare of the community in which they oper-
ate, so that economic systems can be judged on moral grounds by the well-being they
confer on society (Laczniak 1999). For older adults, life satisfaction stems from com-
paring personal expectations with outcomes achieved (Sherman and Cooper 1988).
This is a subjective metric, because individuals differ in their goals and the ways
they pursue them; yet if companies can offer products and services that help each

person reach those goals, they will enhance their likelihood of success. To identify
the factors that most influence overall life satisfaction, Bearden et al. ( 1979) experi-
mentally tested several possible determinants. Among all aspects considered, health,
social relationships, and quality of life emerged as the primary drivers of well-being,
whereas financial status appeared to exert the least direct influence, thanks to older
consumers’ ability to forgo non-essential spending when emergencies arise.
Social relationships, in particular, shape elderly well-being: Webster (2008) found
that seniors who participate in recreational associations meet their need for belong -
ing—one of their strongest motivational forces—reinforce their sense of autonomy
by escaping daily routine, and enrich their knowledge, developing responsibility and
self-esteem through social roles within a group. Subjective differences, however,
seem to characterize the aging process—and thus social relationships—between men
and women. Sherman and Shiffman (1984) advanced two socio-gerontological theo-
ries to explain changes in subjective well-being by gender. According to the Level-
ing Hypothesis, a man’s living conditions deteriorate more rapidly than a woman’s
as he ages, because she retains existing roles and adapts more easily to new ones.
By contrast, the Jeopardy Hypothesis posits that social inequality between men and
women widens with age to the detriment of women, especially with respect to mental
health and overall satisfaction. According to Ulvoas-Moal ( 2010), “spirituality”—a
transcendent construction of life’s meaning that often reaches its peak in later life—
enables older adults to better cope with aging constraints, mortality awareness, and
intergenerational tensions, thereby enhancing well-being. Spirituality influences
older consumers’ satisfaction by shaping consumption attitudes in relation to their
values and identity, fostering respect in market exchanges, and directing consump -
tion toward intrinsic fulfillment rather than merely extrinsic prestige.
4.1.2 Health satisfaction
Health satisfaction—that is, contentment with one’s health—is often the key to under-
standing the overall well-being of an older person. Rahtz and Sirgy (2000) set out to
study how life satisfaction depends on satisfaction with personal health, the health
services offered by one’s community, work, and family, showing that all these fac -
tors, directly or indirectly linked to the healthcare provided to the elderly, determine
general well-being. This is a “spillover effect” from one domain to another (Sirgy et
al. 1991); thus, satisfaction with medical and health services (when measured on a
scale that discriminates between two opposite poles), with perceived health status,
and a low cognitive age (feeling young) influence life satisfaction; and perceived
health status also, though to a limited extent, moderates the effect of satisfaction with
health services on life satisfaction. A study by Braunsberger and Gates (2002) found
that, considering variables such as health status, age, gender, and education level, the
patients most satisfied with healthcare and related programs are the healthier, older,
male individuals with less education. As for the specific impact of age on satisfac -
tion, older adults are generally more satisfied than younger people because they have
lower expectations of the health system (recalling the limited services of the past),
are usually treated better than the young, and are less inclined to complain. Accord -
ing to Essén and Wikström (2008), both rational evaluations and emotional reactions

faction depends mainly on provider factors and therapy quality; for the latter, on the
convenience and ease of visits, since access to care matters less to healthy patients
than to those with chronic conditions.
4.1.3 Perceived quality of healthcare provision
Consumer satisfaction with health is not necessarily linked to perceived quality of
healthcare services—that is to an overall judgment of excellence or superiority—
because satisfaction can rest on different criteria. It does become connected, however,
when the utility generated by the services is compared, in value terms, with the cost
of obtaining them; in that case, the evaluation includes the attributes, benefits, and
goals sought in the perceived experience. Heras et al. ( 2008), for example, mea -
sured perceived quality in Spanish nursing homes by assessing technical factors (care
requirements and implementation), interpersonal factors (professional involvement
of staff), and environmental factors (sector characteristics); their results indicated
that this assessment model may be ineffective in actually improving residents’ care
and quality of life. The reason becomes clear from a French study on similar institu-
tions by Saint-Paul and Dreyfus (1994): while the quality detection method proposed
by researchers was based on facilities (buildings, decorations, institutionalization of
relationships, etc.), the results of patient preferences gave greater weight to offered
health services, namely the factors linked to residents’ daily needs (in particular, the
quality of served meals). In order to understand the real needs of patients through this
type of study, it is essential to identify their expectations, deploying a wide range of
techniques (also due to elderly’s greater difficulties in understanding standard detec-
tion methods): discussions with staff, residents and their families, inspections, ques -
tionnaires and related explanatory booklets, etc. (Smith 1993).
Perceived service quality turns into user value when quality drivers (physician
behavior, staff behavior, external factors) are weighed against the sacrifices required
to obtain them (physical effort, perceived risk, monetary cost); that value strongly
influences purchase intentions and repeat use of the service (Brand et al. 1997).

Paquet and Dubé ( 2004) have shown that when satisfaction and service quality
for older adults are evaluated, emotions play a decisive role and therefore must be
measured directly in each consumption episode. Their study reveals that positive
emotional responses stem less from tangible or sensory aspects of the service than
from the interpersonal aspects of the relationships formed between providers and
users. Ma and Dubé (2006) have argued that the ultimate judgment of service quality
depends on the alternating, complementary combination of agency—the tendency
to dominance and control—and communion—the inclination to compromise and to
intimate and condescending participation—that emerges in those relationships. In
this regard, age has a positive and significant impact on the service quality perception

are stricter evaluators is thus a misleading stereotype. Older users rate highly those
services featuring direct contact with providers (face-to-face, or remotely), just as
younger users do, and—unlike younger users—also give high ratings to low-contact,
routine services, which therefore must be managed just as carefully when aimed at
seniors.
4.2 Brand attachment
Brand attachment is an enduring connection that anchors older consumers to specific
brands and directs their marketplace behavior. It coalesces around three forces: a
smaller consideration set that eases decision making, a preference for long-estab -
lished brands that signals reliability, and identity-based relationships grounded in
affective commitment and perceived partner quality. Together these dynamics con -
serve cognitive resources, reinforce self-concept, and sustain connections, making
attachment a central lens for understanding consumer satisfaction in later life (see
Online Appendix 5). These attachment mechanisms directly shape satisfaction out -
comes in later life: when older consumers rely on familiar brands, their evaluative
judgments depend less on objective product performance and more on the confir -
mation of identity-based expectations and emotional bonds, resulting in satisfaction
thresholds that differ systematically from those of younger consumers.
4.2.1 Smaller consideration set
Elderly consumers’ satisfaction with a given product or service may more readily
translate into a bond with a specific brand, detached from the rational considerations
that perhaps initially shaped their preference; this essentially emotional attachment
narrows the consideration set  of options and leads them to favor well-established
brands. According to Uncles (2008), today’s consumers have greatly increased their
competence in the practical use of new technologies and their authority, thanks to
information access that reduces bargaining asymmetry vis-à-vis suppliers. Con -
sequently, for firms operating in the Internet era, maintaining stable and enduring
customer relationships is increasingly difficult: while the web provides tools (com -
munities, forums, blogs, etc.) that can strengthen the bond with a brand, it also gives
informed users countless reasons to exit an exclusive relationship with a single brand.

LaBarge and Pyle (2020) revealed that seniors actively balance between “successful
aging” (maximizing life quality and satisfaction) and vulnerability by adapting their
consumption habits. For example, proactive planning and product/service selection
help older consumers preserve autonomy, whereas avoidance or lack of satisfying
options can increase feelings of vulnerability.
Not all consumers, indeed, are equally competent or able to exercise this infor -
mational authority. Older adults, especially those with higher cognitive age, belong
to this group, so it is essential to establish personalized relationships with them and
to reinforce socio-emotional ties in both physical and virtual spaces. The tendency
for older adults to consider fewer brands, models, and retail outlets in their shopping
activities is confirmed by a French study on car repurchase (Lambert-Pandraud et
al. 2005), which shows that older consumers repurchase a brand more frequently
than younger cohorts. The authors attributed this pattern both to biological aging and
cognitive decline, which make decision making for unfamiliar options more difficult,
stressful, and risky, and to socio-emotional selectivity and decision aversion, which
render familiar solutions more attractive because older adults are more likely than
younger ones to recall the positive aspects of previous experiences and to replicate
their choice dynamics. Simcock et al. (2006) showed that, in purchasing a car, older
adults are particularly influenced by the need to limit different types of risk, depend-
ing not only on product characteristics but also on individual and situational fac -
tors. Because cultural values and self-image influence both evaluation and adoption,
purchases occur only when the older consumer believes that the marginal benefits
outweigh the perceived costs.
4.2.2 Preference for long-established brands
The same factors mentioned above lead older adults to favor brands that have long
been on the market (Kim and Srivastava 2024). The rationale is that older consumers
place more importance on consistency-related brand traits (e.g., stability, reliability)
and less on innovativeness and excitement-related brand traits (e.g., dynamic, adven-
turous). According to Anderson and Sharp ( 2010), who studied 230 brands across
several product categories, brands that are older and experiencing declining sales
are purchased more frequently by older consumers; younger consumers, by contrast,
prefer new and fast-growing brands, probably because they have not yet formed a
stable reference set, remain undecided, and are inclined toward novelty in the pursuit
of new experiences. Indeed, older consumers do buy new products: none of the above
findings, although significant, is that extreme. In about 70 percent of new brands, one
in seven purchases is made by older consumers.
To explain why older adults prefer long-established brands, Lambert-Pandraud
and Laurent ( 2010) investigated three mechanisms: nostalgia, which suggests that
consumers develop their preferences when young and keep them for life; innova-
tiveness, whereby younger consumers show a greater propensity than older ones to
adopt new products; and attachment, whereby consumers develop emotional bonds

4.2.3 Emotional and identity-based brand relationships
Attachment towards some brands is representative of a relationship that takes on
emotional and identity significance  for the elderly. Jahn et al. ( 2010) observed how
variations in the control of emotions emerging with age, following the decline of
cognitive abilities and the changed perception of time (now felt as finite), can lead to
differences in brand attachment. Distinguishing between two groups of seniors on the
basis of chronological age (younger seniors, 50 to 65 years, and older seniors, over
65 years), these authors found that younger seniors elaborate brand information more
deeply, identify with it, and, trusting it, develop an affective commitment toward
it; this is not the case for older adults, for whom the emotional bond constituted by
affective commitment depends more on the perceived quality of a brand as the coun-
terpart in a relationship with themselves as consumers (partner quality). Therefore,
if the former group assigns importance to “what a brand is” (how the brand is) in
relation to their self-evaluation, for the latter it is “what a brand does” that matters,
because high brand quality guides their trust and affective commitment. In particular,
Jahn et al. (2012) found that, for older female consumers, this difference systemati -
cally drives their loyalty and satisfaction.
The role of one’s own identity also prevails in attachment to domestic brands,
a concept linked to the Country-of-origin effect. In a study conducted in Mozam -
bique (Lee et al. 2010), it was shown that, for older consumers, loyalty to national
brands reflects their consumer ethnocentric identity, based on the belief that buying
foreign products is wrong because it harms the economy and leads to job losses.
Ethnocentric identity is driven by perceived social-norm pressure rather than older
consumers’ internalization of the norm itself (a result that could be influenced by the
study’s cultural context). In such situations, efficient promotion of domestic prod -
ucts should rely on word-of-mouth communication, which increases social pressure;
endorser-based advertising would be more effective in the opposite case, encourag -
ing identification with a social group. In another study carried out in South Korea,
Bae and Jo (2024) found that older adults felt threatened in their identity by negative
age stereotypes and sought to disassociate themselves from the “old” group, thereby
heightening their desire to purchase “masstige” brands—mid-range prestige labels
offering youthful symbolism. This effect was strongest among seniors with a highly
interdependent self-view.
4.3 Loyalty
Loyalty refers to acting in accordance with what all firms desire—namely, that con -
sumers repeatedly purchase from them. As such, loyalty limits personal freedom by
encouraging consumers to behave as these entities wish, reinforcing habitual purchas-
ing patterns. In the case of older consumers, this force, which can generate satisfac -

tion, is observed taking the form of “brand loyalty” when it leads them to repurchase
the same products or services, or “store loyalty” when it encourages repeated visits to
the same retail outlets. Loyalty programs aim to strengthen either form by motivating
older consumers through trust, familiarity, convenience, or personal interaction (see
Online Appendix 6).
4.3.1 Brand loyalty
A satisfied consumer is likely to repurchase a product or brand perceived as exceeding
expectations, thereby becoming a loyal customer. Evanschitzky and Woisetschläger
(2008) examined the antecedent stages that lead older adults to repeatedly buy and
use products from the same brand. They found a negative association between chron-
ological age, biological age (dependent on health status), cognitive age (on mental
abilities) and sociological age (on number of social contacts) on the one hand, and
both the accuracy of information search and the breadth of the consideration set on
the other—as older consumers are more reluctant to expend effort in order to evaluate
novel options. According to Hlavinka (2011), future marketing operators who want to
retain consumer loyalty will need to account for five major changes, foremost among
them the aging population with its distinctive behaviors, along with the expansion of
the middle class, the concentration of wealth, the increased frequency of alternating
economic cycles, and advances in technology toward what is perfect, instantaneous,
and personal.
Because older adults are less inclined than younger ones to switch brands, D’Amico
(2007) investigated whether advertising can erode this heightened loyalty by promot-
ing brand switching. His study showed no substantial age-based differences: younger
and older consumers change brands at similar rates, despite a slight decline after age
65 and the greater advertising receptivity of younger cohorts, an effect tempered by
their lower purchasing power and their lower probability of exposure to television
advertising. Greater loyalty among older consumers emerged in service contexts.
Patterson (2007) reported a significant correlation between age and the propensity to
patronize the same dental practices, hair salons, and travel agencies. This tendency
stems from the personal relationships that develop through repeated social interaction
between service providers and clients, irrespective of gender. Firms can leverage this
stronger loyalty to attract older consumers, particularly Baby Boomers who possess
higher spending power, longer life prospects and sustained interest in new products.
In service recovery situations, Babin et al. ( 2021) found that perceptions of fairness
(e.g., a clear explanation and adequate compensation) had a stronger impact on older
customers’ forgiveness than emotional factors. This suggests that, after a failure,
senior customers are more willing to continue the relationship when firms provide
transparent, fair remedies that appeal to their desire for honesty and clarity. Ferguson
and Brohaugh (2010) proposed several best-practice marketing tactics: maintaining
consistency and engagement in customer relationships, avoiding stereotypes, empha-
sizing the experience and satisfaction the customer derives rather than the product
itself, and building bonds through shared causes, as well as social or environmental
initiatives that resonate with that generation.

4.3.2 Store loyalty
The purpose of manufacturing firms is to foster customer attachment to the brand,
whereas the purpose of commercial firms is to foster customer attachment to the
point-of-sale. Store loyalty —that is, fidelity to the point-of-sale—underpins con -
sumers’ habitual behaviors and is counted among the determinants of their purchase
intention. When driven by high satisfaction, older adults’ store loyalty exceeds that of
consumers under sixty and is reinforced by the option to return purchased goods and
by the courtesy of the sales staff (Burt and Gabbott 1995). Burnett (1996) observed
that older adults’ independence, defined in terms of their perceived health status, free-
dom from medication, competent shopping, and use of public transport, rather than
mere chronological age, better predicts whether to patronize a wide range of points-
of-sale instead of remaining loyal to a single store. Although lack of mobility is often
thought of as a factor restricting shoppers to one store, Lumpkin and Hunt ( 1989),
in research on clothing stores among older consumers, showed that limited mobility
alone does not affect store choice. Older adults who are not self-sufficient and lack
autonomous mobility display the same degree of store loyalty as other seniors and
rely on the same factors: namely, sales-staff expertise, a comfortable environment,
and readable product labels. Research findings highlight the value of personal, face-
to-face service in satisfying older consumers. Berg et al. ( 2023) found that seniors
who interact directly with friendly, attentive employees report higher store satisfac -
tion than those with minimal staff contact. Positive and supportive service interac -
tions contribute to older consumers’ sense of purpose, independence, relationships,
and self-acceptance, thereby enhancing their satisfaction and overall subjective well-
being in later life (Plaud and Guillemot 2015). Focusing on restaurant experiences,
Lee et al. ( 2012) showed that senior customers report distinct priorities in service
(i.e., courtesy of staff) and, although older patrons are generally satisfied, the link
between service quality perceptions and overall satisfaction is weaker for seniors
than for younger adults. Consequently, when designing services for senior consum -
ers, managers should recognize the existence of significant differences in the levels
of satisfaction and in the perceptions of service quality among customers based on
age, and that the negative relationship between sacrifice and perceived value, and the
positive association of perceived value and satisfaction with behavioral intentions,
are stronger for male and older customers; whereas the positive association of ser -
vice quality with satisfaction and value is stronger for female and younger customers
(Sharma et al. 2012).
Regardless of orientation, older adults are more loyal to points-of-sale than
younger consumers: as Moschis et al. (2004) found in grocery stores, seniors justify
their choice primarily by the ease of locating goods on shelves and, in descending
order of importance, proximity to the store, the availability of brands and family-
oriented items, the presence of express checkout lanes, and low prices. According
to these authors, older women endorse most of these factors and assign even greater
importance to them. Boosting loyalty for these customers can be achieved through
dedicated services, such as parking and help with carrying packages, comfortable
environments and opportunities for socializing, and knowledgeable, kindly staff who
are able to remember individual preferences. Apart from staff friendliness, Paul et

4.3.3 Loyalty programs
In the relationship between manufacturers and retailers, brand loyalty and store loy -
alty constitute inherently conflicting objectives: manufacturers strive for consum -
ers’ allegiance to their brands, while retailers aim to draw shoppers into their outlets
and drive sales of higher-margin private-label products. To determine whether brand
loyalty or store loyalty prevails among older consumers, East et al. ( 1995) studied
the antecedents related to the characteristics of this segment. It emerged that store
loyalty exceeds brand loyalty, which in turn exceeds loyalty to private-label prod -
ucts (for example, supermarket brands). Overall, differences between high and low
loyalty were small but clearly depended on family income, average expenditure,
and the importance attributed to low prices (the lower these factors are, the lower
older consumers’ loyalty). In the special case of TV home shopping, Lim and Kim
(2011) found that networks can improve older customers’ satisfaction by alleviating
loneliness (e.g., through friendly hosts) and emphasizing shopping convenience. In
another paper, Lim and Kim ( 2017) showed that “telepresence”—i.e., the feeling
of being immersed in the TV experience—has a positive effect on older customers’
satisfaction with TV shopping by creating an engaging, immersive environment that
enhances their loyalty.
Loyalty programs based on discounts and savings represent a potential lever to
increase repeated patronage and satisfaction among older consumers (Gillett and
Schneider 1978). However, their effectiveness depends on overcoming barriers that
may reduce participation and satisfaction: older adults often face difficulties in under-
standing program mechanics, using loyalty cards, and evaluating the actual savings
obtained—challenges that can generate frustration and diminish perceived value.

Across these three satisfaction themes, several mechanisms recur: autonomy and
control over consumption choices (evident in well-being pursuits, brand selection,

brand attachment and sustained patronage), and the tension between stability-seek -

emphasizes holistic life evaluations, brand attachment and loyalty research converge
on narrowed choice sets and risk-averse strategies as older consumers prioritize
familiarity and trust over novelty.
5 Elderly consumer dissatisfaction
5.1 Complaints
Complaints are behavioral responses through which consumers communicate dis -
satisfaction with products, services, or purchase experiences, aiming to resolve a
perceived failure or to seek redress. They encompass a range of actions, from direct
confrontation with the seller or provider to indirect behaviors such as switching to
another retailer. Among older consumers, complaints are shaped by multiple factors,
including the nature and intensity of discontent, the propensity to remain passive
rather than take action, and a marked caution in attributing responsibility to the seller
(see Online Appendix 7).
5.1.1 Discontent
Older adults’ condition can often be a source of discontent with purchases, espe -
cially when compared to dissatisfaction in the general population. This may be due
to an underlying weakness linked to their vulnerable status, or to disappointment in
specific consumption experiences, related to their social isolation, which may dis -
proportionately raise expectations. This discontent represents the psychological state
of dissatisfaction before it manifests in overt complaint behavior, making it a criti -
cal but often invisible indicator of evaluative judgments among elderly consumers.
According to various theories reported by Rahtz et al. ( 1988), older adults tend to
become more isolated with age and to be more influenced by mass media, particularly
television. Consequently, they tend to uncritically absorb the content of advertis -
ing messages and, by comparing their own condition with the lifestyle portrayed by
television characters, experience greater dissatisfaction than younger people for not
being able to achieve the level of well-being associated with the material possession
of goods on which most television messages focus. However, Pysarchik (1989) found

1 3
Satisfaction and dissatisfaction among elderly consumers: a systematic…
that, in retirement, older adults’ dissatisfaction does not increase with advancing age,
indicating that the possible causes of discontent with purchased products and ser -
vices may vary widely from one older person to another. For example, Deshpande
and Krishnan (1982), in studying older consumers’ behavior in an information-free
environment, found a correlation between lack of information and unsatisfactory pur-
chase experiences. The combination of social isolation and low self-esteem would
lead older adults to feel unable to access the product information they need, and this
inability would generally make them dissatisfied, at times even producing the feeling
of having been cheated during the purchase experience.
Changes in people’s preferences, attitudes, and behaviors across life stages can
be explained, in addition to being the result of role transitions across these stages, as
a reaction to accumulated stress arising from the onset of new internal and external
demands determined by controllable and uncontrollable events (Lee et al. 2001). The
ability to manage these events, given their number and potential traumatic impact,
determines the extent to which a person’s consumption habits (more than prefer -
ences) may generate discontent (Mathur et al. 2006). Indeed, accumulated stress can
also produce the opposite effect, namely a radical change in consumption habits; this
occurs as a reaction to dissatisfaction with one’s life and, secondarily, with the prod-
ucts and services used. In particular, Lee et al. ( 2007) found that high stress levels
have more pronounced effects on the shift from low to high levels of propensity for
change. This result was obtained by analyzing the personal histories of the elderly
participants in the study and the full series of events in their lives, from cyclical ones
(e.g., chronic illnesses) to those spanning several periods (i.e., common to multiple
age groups).
5.1.2 Inertia
Although the feeling of dissatisfaction appears to be more widespread among older
adults than among younger people, complaint behavior is underrepresented among
consumers over the age of 55: older adults are the least likely consumer segment
to voice their complaints. Andreasen and Manning ( 1990) found that for so-called
“vulnerable consumers”, who are disadvantaged in marketplace exchanges (includ -
ing not only older adults, but also children, the uneducated, the poor, persons with
disabilities, racial minorities, and those with language difficulties), complaint behav-
ior is not only lower than that of other population segments but so are the underly -
ing dissatisfaction rates (contrary to what might have been expected). It therefore
appears that older adults, considered together with other vulnerable consumers, are
not good judges of the reality they face, likely because they are less developed and
attentive, living in circumstances that prevent them from having a clear view of the
market and its inefficiencies; or, conversely, because of the lower needs, aspirations,
and resources available to them, which lead them to make fewer purchases and in a
more cautious manner. Supporting the hypothesis that vulnerable consumers tend
to report higher satisfaction levels, Pathak et al. ( 1993), in analyzing the market for
blood pressure drugs (where consumption is not voluntary but prescribed by a physi-
cian), found that the “imposed choice” made for the older adults by a specialist tends

take any action because they do not believe it will bring about changes.
According to LaForge (1989), this greater tolerance compared to younger people,
which leads to a certain inertia in complaint behavior, can be explained by the con -

become passive and remain so even when contextual changes reduce the likelihood
of failure. For older adults, this occurs because their life experience developed in an

is true that for those older adults living in economic hardship and with expenses
reduced to essentials, the failure of a complaint may be costlier than expected. How-

especially among older adults (particularly widowed and single individuals). More -

agencies that seek to protect their interests from unfair and illegal business prac -
tices is characterized by deep apathy (absence of strong opinions) and unawareness
(Mason and Bearden 1981).
5.1.3 Attribution of responsibility
Older adults typically attempt to resolve problems arising from the purchase of a
product or service indirectly, for example by deciding to use another store in the
future rather than filing a complaint (Belk Smith and Moschis 1989). The main rea-
sons for this non-confrontational attitude are, on the one hand, that they partly blame
themselves, feeling inadequate or incompetent, being unaware of the seller’s obliga-
tions or of complaint procedures, and unable to bear the physical and emotional costs
of complaining; and, on the other hand, that they do not wish to undertake socially
divisive actions, seeking to protect their image (to avoid being identified in undesir -
able ways), to prevent the risk of isolation or market withdrawal, having had previ -
ous negative experiences (Grougiou and Pettigrew 2009). Grougiou and Pettigrew
(2011) demonstrated that older consumers evaluate service encounters primarily in
terms of the social benefits they receive. Seniors’ past experiences, whether as cus -
tomers or employees, shape their service expectations and highlight the need for
frontline staff attributes that facilitate satisfactory service encounters for older cus -
tomers. Roy and Sanyal (2017) identified five dimensions of perceived vulnerability
(physical, financial, social, technological, and service-related) that older consumers
face in the marketplace. These vulnerabilities influence consumption behaviors by
raising search and decision costs and lowering overall satisfaction with purchases.
The authors highlighted that many seniors remain cautious or dissatisfied in transac-
tions due to these vulnerabilities, and called for marketers to show compassion and
for policymakers to protect older consumers’ rights.
This cautious attitude in the attribution of responsibility  for unsatisfactory con -
sumption episodes was examined by Laufer et al. (2005), who sought to identify the
psychological mechanisms that make older adults more careful than younger people
in assigning responsibility to companies rather than to external factors related to the

context in which harmful events occur. They identified three mechanisms. The first is
reliance on available mental schemas, which are more developed in older adults due
to their greater cumulative experience over the years, leading them to refrain from
attributing specific responsibility to a company until the fault for the harm caused
is clearly demonstrated. The second is motivation, such as the desire to control the
external environment or to protect oneself, which influences how older adults react in
a crisis situation, prompting them to adopt a more defensive stance toward a poten -
tially harmful product. The third is the process of information processing about the
harmful product and the causes of the negative events it generates, as older adults
rely more extensively on stereotypes and prior beliefs in analyzing a phenomenon
than younger consumers, not out of prejudice but because of their reduced cogni -
tive resources, which make their processing more sensitive to time constraints, time
of day, and reliance on experience. In general, older adults are better than younger
people at judging responsibility for harm suffered, acting more rationally—though
within the cognitive limits of their condition—less impulsively and less prone to
anger toward the company deemed responsible, and basing their judgment on greater
experience, which enables them to extend the benefit of the doubt to the accused
company until the end.
5.2 Complainant profiles
In the context of consumer dissatisfaction, complainants are those who move beyond
silent discontent to take deliberate action to secure a response from a firm. Among
older consumers, their profile is largely shaped by prior experiences, personal
resources, and perceived obstacles to achieving their goals. Key-factors include the
role of resentment and its potential to trigger rejection, the specific individual traits
associated with the likelihood to complain, and the consequent market segmentation
(see Online Appendix 8).
5.2.1 Resentment and rejection
When a company or brand fails to meet consumer expectations or is perceived neg -
atively, the consumer may develop resentment strong enough to avoid purchasing
products from that company or brand in the future, holding it responsible for a per -
ceived shortcoming. According to Aron et al. ( 2008), the effects of such resentment
are more damaging among older adults than younger ones. While younger consumers
tend to express their discontent indirectly, often through online groups and anon -
ymously, older adults complain openly to store managers or write directly to the
company, causing not only economic loss from the missed sale but also reputational
damage through the negative word-of-mouth they generate by sharing their negative
experiences with friends, relatives, and acquaintances. These authors suggest that
sales staff should be trained to develop specific defensive strategies involving imme-
diate responses (such as formal apologies or remedies like gifts or discounts) to limit
the risk of discontent spreading to other, uninvolved consumers.
Disappointment related to consumption may also arise without a triggering event,
such as damage caused by a product, but rather from a frustrating situation that

prompts rejection among older adults. This was the case studied by Moye and Gid -
dings (2002) in relation to the attributes of a clothing store specializing in apparel
for older customers. They found that physical barriers (lighting, temperature, prod -
uct assortment, etc.), exploratory barriers (credit availability, special offers, product
exchanges, etc.), communication barriers (sales staff friendliness, provision of infor-
mation, assistance, etc.), and the level of satisfaction obtained may, in cases of strong

problems, and unmet expectations of older consumers in retail settings, identifying a
series of physical and psychological barriers to the accessibility of stores and shop -
ping centers that, if not overcome, may lead to a similar rejection response.
5.2.2 Individual differences
Although the frequency of complaint actions among older consumers is lower than
among younger people, older adults are willing to invest considerable resources
when such actions are undertaken (Bernhardt 1981). Over the years, research in
various countries has sought to identify the individual characteristics of potential
complainants, generally reaching the same conclusions, with a few important excep-
tions. V olkov et al. (2002) reviewed the socio-demographic characteristics, personal
values, and personality traits of individuals who regularly voice complaints. The
resulting profile of those who tend to express dissatisfaction is characterized by older
adults with high education levels, high income, active participation in community
life, high self-confidence, and strong self-esteem. Moreover, complainants do not
easily forget the product or service with which they were dissatisfied and recurrently
voice their discontent.
The same socio-demographic characteristics were also found by Grønhaug (1987),
together with a significant correlation between complaint behavior and good health
status, market participation, and previous unsatisfactory experiences, with the sole
important exception of age, whereby complaints came mainly from younger consum-
ers—a result possibly influenced by the data collection method. In an earlier study
on this topic, Liefeld et al. ( 1975) acknowledged that the data could be distorted by
numerous underestimations among both younger and older respondents. Neverthe -
less, their results largely confirmed the average complainant profile, adding the most
frequent attributes of being married, divorced, or separated, and having a medium-
to-high occupational status (a position allowing for greater purchasing activity and
an education level that provides greater awareness of one’s rights and the ability
to write, for example, a complaint letter), while excluding any influence of gender.
In summary, with respect to age, the population of consumers who file complaints
is found mainly among younger and middle-aged individuals (including younger
seniors), with the exclusion of the very old.
5.2.3 Complainant segmentation
Cultural changes in Western societies in recent decades have had varying effects on the
elderly. Today, it is more acceptable to use the term “elder” than “older”. The differ-
ence is not accidental; the adjective “old” is avoided because of its potentially offen-

sive and discriminatory connotations (Guido et al. 2022). In youth-centric settings
(e.g., modern societies), senior customers experience identity threat and a perceived
mismatch with offerings, which usually fosters dissatisfaction; this effect weakens
when young social cues are less desirable or when self-esteem is higher (Amatulli et
al. 2018). If consumers feel treated as old, for example, through patronizing behavior
by sales staff (condescending or over-accommodating), they strongly reject it, lead -
ing to lower satisfaction (Chéron et al. 2021). Older men tolerate mild condescension
slightly more than older women, especially when it comes from a young salesperson.
Overall, seniors of both genders prefer respectful treatment; face-to-face interac -
tions that are respectful and helpful (not patronizing) drive higher satisfaction among
them. According to Berg (2015), older consumers are not inherently more vulnerable
or less satisfied than younger ones. Despite some age-related declines in capabilities,
older persons are less likely to make poor marketplace decisions and do not want to
be treated as less capable (they even exhibit more environmentally friendly consump-
tion patterns than younger ones). Key drivers of consumer vulnerability are poor
economic awareness and lack of time rather than age per se, whereas low numeracy
and limited financial resources increase vulnerability across all ages.
Seeking to distinguish within this population, Keesling et al. ( 1992) proposed a
complainant segmentation based on the type of response older consumers expected
from firms, identifying four groups. The Assentors, representing the smallest seg -
ment, prefer different ways of expressing their dissatisfaction and favor prompt writ-
ten responses that are courteous, personal, and apologetic, even when the validity of
the complaint tends to be questionable. The Credibles, the largest segment, prefer
kind yet firm responses, both written and verbal, particularly when the respondent
holds an important role and adopts a friendly, personal tone. The Suspecters report
low satisfaction levels in any case, even when justifications and apologies are clear,
courteous, and in written form, as they would rather receive a response that offers a
solution or an attempt to solve the problem from someone in a significant position.
The Agnostics show low satisfaction levels and form a highly heterogeneous group,
including individuals who prefer a firm and sincere response, preferably delivered
directly (for example, by telephone), and others who primarily want a courteous and
friendly approach from the other party.
5.3 Grievances
Grievances are specific issues or practices that lead consumers to express dissatis -
faction and seek redress. Among older consumers, they tend to emerge where vul -
nerability intersects with routine market exchanges. Most often, they originate from
unfair market practices, deficiencies in the quality of primary goods and services
essential to their well-being, and the handling of their complaints, which is pivotal to
restoring trust and maintaining relationships (see Online Appendix 9).
5.3.1 Unfair marketing practices
Among the main causes of complaints from older consumers are corporate marketing
practices such as deceptive or aggressive selling techniques, which can harm them or

G. Guido et al.
expose them to outright fraud, as well as unethical or offensive communication strat-
egies, such as advertising campaigns based on negative stereotypes of older adults,
which can generate dissatisfaction and create crisis situations for the firm. Zaltman et
al. (1978) were the first to address the issue of unfair marketing practices in relation
to complaint actions by older adults, as potential victims due to their lower educa -
tion level, income capacity, and wariness. Based on the assumption that dissatisfac -
tion with the consumption experience of a product is not related to the likelihood
of reporting by consumers but rather to their personal characteristics and internal
influences, these authors found that mature and older consumers are less aware than
younger ones of unfair business practices and, moreover, become less likely to report
them as they age. This result is attributed mainly to the lack of social involvement
among older segments, which prevents the transfer and exchange of knowledge.
Another important source of dissatisfaction typically stems from advertising
campaigns that use negative stereotypes of older adults to promote products for the
elderly. These dimensions of fairness and dignity are included insofar as they directly
shape dissatisfaction intensity and the likelihood of complaint, silence, or exit, rather
than as general normative principles of service encounters. Doyle ( 1990) found,
in particular, a critical attitude among older adults toward six false myths wrongly
adopted and promoted by marketers when approaching this segment: viewing older
adults as frugal and unwilling to spend; assuming unshakable brand loyalty; assum -
ing reluctance toward anything new launched on the market; associating them with a
sedentary lifestyle; judging the need for safety as the only truly relevant for them; and
considering new challenges and proposals as alien to their mentality.
5.3.2 Lack of quality
The core content of potential complaints from older consumers lies in the lack of
quality in primary expenditures (food and healthcare), which represent essential
purchases and can significantly affect the household budget of older adults. Mason
and Bearden ( 1979) highlighted the main causes of dissatisfaction related to food
purchases, which, according to Hare et al. ( 1999), can be grouped into three main
categories concerning the food shopping experience: merchandise-related, including
dissatisfaction with price labels, packaging size, and the lack of products specific
to or adaptable for older consumers’ needs; retail practices, including the feeling

5.3.3 Complain response
The complaint response policy, which concerns the possibility of providing dissatis-
fied customers with information, corrections, or compensation upon request, becomes
crucial for managing the firm’s image and potentially recovering lost favor. From the
consumer’s perspective, Ligas and Coulter ( 2000) identified four alternatives avail -
able in the face of an unsatisfactory consumption episode: Remain long-term, that is,
maintaining the trust-based relationship built over time when part of the responsibil-
ity for the negative event is attributed to oneself; Remain short-term, that is, continu-
ing temporarily, reserving judgment until one assesses how the company handles the
complaint and acts accordingly; Good-bye for now, when the relationship is tempo -
rarily interrupted to explore possible market alternatives, while still uncertain about
the final decision; and Good-bye forever, when the abandonment is permanent and
the damage to the company is double, both economic and reputational, due to nega -
tive word-of-mouth.
From the firm’s perspective, the contribution of different complaint response modes
was examined by Sabadie et al. ( 2006) through the lens of “Organizational justice”.
In this framework, key-response elements relate to “distributive justice”, concern -
ing customers’ judgments of whether the firm’s response matches their beliefs about
what they deserve; basic response elements, instead, relate to “interactional justice”,
concerning the interaction between customers and the firm’s frontline staff; while,
more variably, depending on the occasion, either basic or value-added elements are
associated with “procedural justice”, concerning the methods used by the firm to
receive and process customers’ complaints. The weight of key-response elements on
satisfaction remains stable regardless of customer evaluations of the response. The
weight of basic elements is high when the evaluation is negative and low when it is
positive (because their presence is assumed). The weight of value-added elements
follows the opposite logic: when evaluated positively, they strongly influence sat -
isfaction; otherwise, their impact is minimal. It follows that complaint management
is essential not only for regaining dissatisfied consumers but also for ensuring the
efficient functioning of the firm as a whole. Particularly for complaints from older
consumers, the manner in which commitment to customer service is demonstrated is
crucial for the firm’s image (Krentler and Cosenza 1987): specifically, the seriousness
in considering their complaints, the genuine interest in their reasons, and, depending
on the case, either maintaining a position in support of the firm’s products or services
or offering to resolve the problem.
Across these three dissatisfaction themes, the evidence reveals consistent pat -
terns: older consumers’ complaints are often triggered by perceived unfairness rather
than minor service failures, individual characteristics (especially self-efficacy and
social support) shape whether discontent leads to voice or silent exit, and successful
grievance management depends on restoring dignity through respectful interpersonal

6 A future research agenda
Based on the insights from the systematic review, we propose a forward-looking
research agenda focusing on satisfaction and dissatisfaction among elderly consum -

of the review, grouped into two domains: satisfaction-related topics (Sects. 6.1–6.3)
and dissatisfaction-related topics (Sects. 6.4–6.6). In considering the future scenar -

likely to evolve, future research directions are conceptually framed by three major
drivers of change  (Guido 2025) that cut across all themes: global instability fac -
tors (e.g., conflict, pandemics, climate crises); increasing societal fragmentation; and
rapid technological advancements (especially AI). These drivers function as trans-
versal forces  reconfiguring satisfaction and dissatisfaction mechanisms across all
thematic domains. The proposed directions in the future research agenda, therefore,
emerge not only from the gaps identified in the literature but also from these antici -
pated changes in market and societal conditions. An overarching recommendation is
that future studies across all themes should incorporate these macro-environmental
dynamics into their research designs to ensure relevance in an era of uncertainty and
change. The research agenda outlined below is grounded in the six themes identified
through our systematic review. While this structure ensures continuity with existing
evidence, we acknowledge that entirely novel themes (e.g., sustainability, intergen -
erational dynamics) may emerge as the field evolves. Future reviews should revisit
these boundaries as new empirical work accumulates.

questions identified in the review in light of these three drivers of change; the research
topics through which they can be categorized; and a set of future research objectives,

Health and wellness fulfillment  are dominant drivers of satisfaction in later life,
with self-rated health strongly predicting both life satisfaction and consumption
engagement (Jivraj et al. 2014). Yet, well-being is not determined solely by objective
health status; perceptions of wellness, preventive behaviors, and access to supportive
services matter equally (Guido and Mileti 2022). Future research should examine
how social support and community engagement drive well-being outcomes among
culturally diverse older consumers. Societal fragmentation—including the erosion
of traditional family structures—means that for many elders, community organiza -
tions, peer groups, and market-based services substitute for familial support. Cross-
cultural studies show that community participation improves life satisfaction and
perceived health, with variations in the relative importance of family versus peer
networks (Shen et al. 2022). Theoretically, applying social capital frameworks can
illuminate how different forms of support (emotional, instrumental, informational)
influence health-related satisfaction. Managerially, these insights could help design
interventions integrating health promotion with social engagement—such as well -
ness programs in retail environments or loyalty schemes tied to community activi -
ties—that are culturally sensitive and accessible. Moreover, “cognitive age”—the age
individuals feel relative to their chronological age—and the “positivity bias”—the
tendency to focus on positive over negative stimuli—can modulate how health and
care experiences affect satisfaction. Older adults with a younger subjective age often
report higher activity levels and greater openness to wellness innovations (Guido et
al. 2018), while those with a stronger positivity bias may sustain satisfaction even
amid health challenges (Reed and Carstensen 2012). Future research should analyze
how these psychological factors modulate links between health and life satisfaction.
This could involve experimental designs that manipulate message framing (positive
vs. loss-based) in health communications, or longitudinal studies linking cognitive
age trajectories to satisfaction outcomes. Theoretically, this work can refine models
of satisfaction formation by integrating age-related emotional regulation processes.
For practitioners, tailoring health and wellness marketing to align with consumers’
subjective age and positivity orientation could improve engagement and satisfaction.
Perceptions of care quality —whether in healthcare, home assistance, or well -
ness services—are central to older consumers’ satisfaction. The rapid acceleration
of health-related technologies offers both opportunities and risks. Future research
should explore whether digital health innovations enhance older adults’ perceived
care quality and satisfaction. Telemedicine, health apps, and remote monitoring have
shown promise in improving access and continuity of care, especially during COVID-
19 (Biese et al. 2022), but adoption is uneven, with barriers linked to digital literacy,
trust, and usability (Karami et al. 2023). Studies should examine how these innova -
tions interact with cognitive age and prior technology experience to influence satis -
faction. From a theoretical standpoint, extending the technology acceptance model
with aging-related moderators could improve predictive accuracy for elder popula -
tions. Practically, co-designing health technologies with older users and embedding
training or hybrid models (combining digital with in-person care) could ensure inno-
vations translate into perceived quality gains rather than alienation. Considering pol-

6.2 Deeper brand attachment in later life
Brand attachment in later life can act as a stabilizer of choice, a reservoir of psycho-
logical safety, and an identity cue. Against the backdrop of market changes, older
consumers may rely more heavily on brands to satisfy needs for safety, belonging,
and esteem. A priority is to understand why brand repertoires narrow with age in dig-
ital marketplaces. Evidence shows that aging-related limits in working memory and
processing speed encourage heuristic, “good-enough” strategies and the inspection
of fewer alternatives online, shrinking consideration sets and elevating reliance on
diagnostic cues like brand name and price (Guido et al. 2021; Rydzewska et al. 2024).
Future research should isolate the causal role of information overload by experimen-
tally manipulating assortment size, review complexity, and recommender diversity,
and then observing effects on choice quality, satisfaction, and emergent attachment.
Theoretically, such work can integrate age-contingent strategy selection with attach-
ment formation (i.e., when does satisficing around a trusted label evolve into attach-
ment?). Managerially, it motivates testing age-friendly choice architectures—curated
assortments, “trusted brand” filters, human-assisted chat—versus AI curation tuned
to low-novelty settings, to determine when simplification deepens attachment without
foreclosing exploration. Conversely, there is a risk that digital personalization algo -
rithms expose older users to too many novel or unfamiliar brands, leading to decision
avoidance. Research is needed to determine the optimal balance: for example, do AI
recommendation systems that minimize novelty (Zhao and Fu 2024) increase satis -
faction among older shoppers, or can gentle introduction of new brands (with cred -
ible information) expand their repertoire without causing confusion? Given the rapid
technological advancements in retail (e.g. AI, voice assistants), scholars might also
examine how older consumers perceive these tools—as empowering facilitators that
make decisions easier, or as sources of distrust. Integrating a “technological accelera-
tion” macro-driver lens, future studies could explore if interventions like credibility
signaling (e.g. “verified authentic information” badges or humanized online advi -
sors) help seniors confidently consider new products. Ultimately, understanding how
digital information overload curtails brand exploration in later life will inform theo -
ries of bounded rationality in aging consumers and guide marketers to tailor online
experiences that suit older adults’ cognitive styles.

that signal continuity and competence. In later life this may manifest as a flight-to-
familiarity, concentrating purchases on legacy names with strong brand heritage—a
credible cue of reliability over time (Urde et al. 2007). Research should test whether
spikes in macro-uncertainty (inflation shocks, pandemics) measurably tighten rep -
ertoires among older shoppers and strengthen the satisfaction-attachment link rela -
tive to younger cohorts. Design-wise, heritage brands could foreground consistency,
quality stability, and “present through crises” narratives; challenger brands will need
accelerated trust-building (transparent guarantees, third-party endorsements) to over-
come seniors’ elevated perceived switching risk. Theoretically, modeling contextual
moderators (instability × age) can clarify when loyalty reflects satisfaction-based
attachment versus dissatisfaction-driven inertia. It is important for research to map
heterogeneity across older sub-segments—by age band, culture, health/functional
ability, subjective (cognitive) age, and digital literacy. Cross-national work identi -

who privilege consistency (Sudbury-Riley et al. 2015). Reviews of seniors and tech-
nology likewise distinguish capable/seekers from resistant/incapable users (Franco

age moderators and test how micro-communities (local chapters, intergenerational
mentoring) offset weak family ties in fragmented contexts. Methodologically, field
experiments (choice architecture; recommender transparency), longitudinal panels
(attachment trajectories under instability), and mixed-methods (life-story elicitation
plus digital traces) can reveal mechanisms linking satisfaction to deep attachment
in later life. The payoff is theoretical precision—separating attachment from inertia
under real constraints—and actionable guidance for brands to build respectful, resil-
ient relationships with older consumers.
Identity driven brand bonds also warrant close examination, as older consumers’
recall of brands is often deeply intertwined with autobiographical memories, and
brand cues can evoke vivid life episodes that help explain enduring, emotionally
charged attachments (Thoma and Wechsler 2021). Nostalgia can strengthen bonds
and even sustain loyalty through adversity, as people protect brands that symbol -
ize meaningful pasts (e.g., Demirbag-Kaplan et al. 2015). In fragmented societies,
brand communities also supply belonging; among elderly members, a psychological
sense of community correlates with stronger advocacy and loyalty (Swimberghe et

munity ties jointly predict attachment across diverse elder groups (majority/minor -
ity cultures; young-old vs. old-old), and when inclusive nostalgia (multiple cohort
references; culturally plural heritage cues) elevates attachment independent of satis -

scaffolds (offline clubs plus moderated online hubs) to deepen bonds without alienat-
ing subgroups. Determining how new engagement channels (social media, online
communities, AI personalization) can strengthen or weaken emotional attachment in
later life is also closely related to identity issues. Older adults’ digital participation has

6.3 Evolving patterns of loyalty
Loyalty in later life will be reshaped in future scenarios by the combined pressures
of instability drivers, including volatile prices, intermittent supply shocks, and rapid
digitization. For enduring brand allegiance, two forces will likely collide: heightened
value sensitivity, which can trigger switching (e.g., toward retailer brands) when
uncertainty spikes (Mookherjee et al. 2024; Sashittal et al. 2022), and the need for
reliability and reduced decision effort, which can cement attachment to trusted labels
(Gibson et al. 2024). Future work should disentangle these mechanisms by experi -
mentally varying price volatility and perceived stock-out risk in omnichannel con -
texts to observe when seniors “switch to safety” versus “stick with the known”. Such
designs would clarify whether late-life loyalty functions as a short-run coping heu -
ristic or a genuinely durable relationship asset. Evidence that older shoppers are not
uniformly attached to legacy brands—many buy newer brands at rates comparable to
younger cohorts (Phua et al. 2020)—also invites research on how digital merchandis-
ing (filters, badges, scarcity cues) recalibrates the salience of heritage versus novelty
for seniors. Longitudinal field experiments tracking brand repertoires before, during,
and after disruptions (e.g., regional crises or price spikes) could reveal whether brand
allegiance is temporarily suspended or permanently shifts toward private labels and
value brands. These studies would advance theory by integrating risk salience and
bounded search into models of later-life loyalty, while guiding brand managers on
when to emphasize credible consistency (heritage, quality assurances) versus trans -
parent innovation (clear benefits and low learning costs).
Turning to consistent store patronage , trust, habit, and risk aversion plausibly
sustain long-term loyalty to brick-and-mortar formats, yet contemporary evidence is
needed under today’s hybrid retail. Recent work documents both stability and age-
related shifts in elderly shoppers’ behavior relative to younger consumers, underscor-
ing the need to separate resilient routines from malleable practices (Schwendtner et
al. 2024). Researchers should combine passive transaction data with panel measures
of perceived risk and habit to model how routine strength and relational trust jointly
maintain store loyalty under stress. In parallel, service convenience remains a power-
ful mechanism for loyalty and share-of-wallet, operating through perceived service
quality and value—channels managers can manipulate even when macro risks are
uncontrollable (Sen et al. 2023). Societal fragmentation adds a further layer: during

but needs age-specific tests; program introductions and promotion cadence may work
differently for older cohorts if trust and effort costs loom larger. A complementary
stream should examine how online trust formation among older adults conditions
willingness to share data for personalization, and whether “credibility signals” (third-

architecture that converts “passive” affection into “active” participation (Gomez-

6.4 Understanding complaint behavior
Extending the logic from satisfaction and loyalty to the dynamics of dissatisfaction,
latent consumer discontent  should be surfaced before it hardens into exit or quiet
disengagement. Our review shows ample room to move beyond post-hoc complaint
counts and toward detecting unvoiced dissatisfaction as it emerges along the jour -
ney. Methodologically, customer-journey analytics can integrate passive behavioral
traces (e.g., shrinking basket variety, shorter visits, rising help-desk dwell) with peri-
odic micro-surveys to flag early inflection points (Lemon and Verhoef 2016). To link

precursors to subsequent voice, exit, or loyalty, researchers can estimate latent-state
or multi-state (hidden) Markov models that classify seniors into “at-risk” trajecto -
ries and quantify transition hazards under changing conditions; modern variational
Bayesian approaches enable scalable estimation with partial observability (Blei et al.
2017; Singh 1990). Dissatisfaction often appears in language before reaching formal
channels. Automated text analysis of transcripts and messages can reveal “quiet”
frustration typical of conflict-avoidant norms through markers like politeness, miti -
gation, and uncertainty (Humphreys and Wang 2018). Event-study designs around
shocks (price volatility, service reorganizations) can test whether uncertainty accel -
erates transitions from latent discontent to silent attrition versus constructive voice,
with heterogeneous effects by age cohort. Theoretically, this agenda connects sat -
isfaction dynamics to pre-complaint states, clarifying when older consumers’ emo -
tion regulation and risk calculus suppress voice. Practically, it motivates predictive
“early-help” protocols (e.g., proactive human outreach after specific friction signals)
that reduce the cost and stigma of speaking up for seniors who prefer low-conflict
paths.
Building on this detection work, passive response tendencies  require interven -
tions that lower the effort, ambiguity, and perceived social risk of complaining.
The review indicates that many seniors hesitate not because the issue is trivial,
but because channels feel costly, opaque, or depersonalized. Compared to younger
cohorts, seniors often exhibit higher thresholds for complaining, sometimes enduring
subpar experiences without formal protest (Lee and Soberon-Ferrer 1999). Future
studies should therefore test human-centered service designs that combine simple,
legible interfaces with immediate access to empathetic human assistance. Random -
ized field experiments can compare senior-tailored complaint flows—plain-language
prompts, fewer steps, larger targets, and “save-and-return” options—against standard
flows on outcomes such as initiation, completion, and resolution time (Taylor et al.
2023). Because algorithmic touchpoints increasingly mediate first contact, research -
ers should examine whether disclosure, explanations, and easy “escalate to a person”
affordances mitigate algorithm aversion among older users and increase willingness
to engage (Dietvorst et al. 2015). Cross-cultural work can incorporate cultural tight-
ness-looseness to account for norms around deference and conflict avoidance that
shape when complaining is seen as legitimate, and which nudges resonate (Gelfand
et al. 2011). Designs worth testing include stepped-wedge rollouts of senior-friendly
channels in multi-site service networks to capture causal effects at scale, and conjoint
experiments that quantify which features (speed, empathy, accountability, follow-
up) most reduce the “psychological price” of complaining. Theoretical contributions
include a clearer mapping from perceived control and self-efficacy to voice among
older consumers; managerial payoffs include validated playbooks that convert silent
dissatisfaction into constructive feedback without paternalism.
As a further point, shifting the focus from the question of whether to complain to
blame attribution patterns  clarifies how older consumers interpret negative events
and choose coping strategies. Our synthesis suggests that ambiguity around cause
and controllability often shifts seniors toward self-blame or situational attributions,
dampening anger and, with it, voice. For instance, an older shopper facing a confus-
ing new technology might assume “I must not have used it right” instead of viewing

it as a product flaw. This self-blame tendency, reinforced by ageist stereotypes of
declining ability, can reduce negative emotions and thereby suppress the impulse to
complain (Mahapatra and Mishra 2022). Future research should manipulate failure
ambiguity, provider controllability, and explanation content in longitudinal experi -
ments to test how attributions evolve across the complaint episode and how they
channel downstream coping (forgiveness, switching, public versus private word-of-
mouth). Annotated corpora of real complaint narratives—coded for agency, hedging,
pronoun use, and stance—can be analyzed with NLP to infer implicit blame at scale
and to detect when explanations nudge attributions toward fairness without deflection
(Humphreys and Wang 2018). Because resolution justice shapes whether voice leads
to relationship repair, studies should factor distributive, procedural, and interactional
elements to isolate which combinations best restore trust among older customers
when uncertainty is high (Van Vaerenbergh et al. 2014). Where AI triages or pro -

trails improve perceived legitimacy of decisions for seniors who value reliability,
and whether such transparency reduces the need for repeated contact—thereby pre -
serving dignity while increasing compliance. Theoretically, this program integrates
attribution and justice lenses with aging-related motivational priorities; practically, it
enables firms to pre-empt self-blame traps by clarifying responsibility, offering low-
effort remedies, and closing the loop with accountable follow-ups that signal respect.
6.5 Heterogeneity in complainant profiles
In continuity with the focus on the passages between voice, exit and loyalty, resentful
disengagement should be investigated as a silent and persistent outcome of the fric -
tion between social ties and service micro-shocks. According to our review and to
recent psychological evidence (Huang and Li 2023; Lyu et al. 2024), loneliness and
isolation fuel more defensive and less dialogic consumption conduct, with repercus-
sions on the way service slights are interpreted and turned into complaints or into
exits. In contexts of frequent reorganization of channels and supply volatility, the
lack of community supports can favor “held-back” remonstrances that settle into hos-
tility and then into exit, whereas local networks and support spaces mitigate inertia to
voice, even in experiential services where the perception of a “right to complain” is
central (Eastman and Case 2024). Future studies should connect indicators of social
integration (breadth/profitability of networks, proximity of services) with experien -
tial diaries and behavioral traces, modeling latent trajectories that link specific trig -
gers to voice/exit/loyalty through hidden Markov models capable of distinguishing
emotional states and response styles (Colombi et al. 2024). Theoretically, this makes
it possible to integrate the lens of emotion regulation in later life with a dynamic ver-
sion of the voice–exit framework, while on the practical side it suggests “community-
enabled complaint mediation” interventions (proximity desks and peer-assistance) to
intercept resentment before it becomes exit or negative word of mouth.
Moving forward, individual complaint propensities  requires shifting the focus
from chronological age alone to profiles that combine digital competences, cognitive
functioning and cultural norms. Longitudinal studies show that maintaining Internet
use in older age is closely connected to memory and cognitive functioning, factors

that condition the ability to navigate complaint channels and to persist in the process
(Wieczorek et al. 2024). Digital competence, in turn, mediates effective access to
services and can transform latent discontent into voice, reducing complaint inertia
(Heponiemi et al. 2023, 2024). Cultural variability in the ways of expressing dis -
sent remains crucial to calibrate tone and channels (Meiners 2024). Personal dis -
positions and resources further shape whether an older person voices complaints or
remains silent (Dahl and Peltier 2015). Research programs should therefore assess,
with field experiments and A/B tests, to what extent simplified tools (short forms,
voice-notes, co-navigation assisted in branch or via video) anticipate the threshold of
voice compared with standard interfaces; and estimate whether the effect is greater
for segments with low digital self-efficacy or with higher cognitive loads. Theoreti -
cal implications: “trait × context” complaint propensity models that include digital
self-efficacy and memory as moderators of the perceived costs of voice. Practical
implications: adaptive routing that sends older adults with low-competence profiles
to human-guided or hybrid channels, and measures of “effort equity” to guarantee
parity of outcome given equal complaint content.
Finally, moving from propensities to recognizable patterns, complainant arche-
types will have to reflect the growing hybridization between the human front-end
and AI systems. Research with older adults shows a conditional acceptance of AI:
perceived usefulness yes, but the value of the human relationship and of transparency
remains decisive for feeling comfortable in asking for redress (Wong et al. 2025;
Enam et al. 2025). In parallel, the literature on bias and fairness of chatbots indicates
risks of unequal treatment, with potential effects on tone, instructions and probability
of escalation (Xue et al. 2024; Akter et al. 2023).
What follows is an agenda that combines algorithmic discovery and ethnographic
audit: first, identify latent classes of “older complainants” (e.g., automation-skeptical
but assertive; hybrid pragmatists; reserved, mediation-oriented) using multi-coun -
try panels, interaction logs, and NLP annotation of conversations; second, test how
intersectional identities (age × gender × ethnic/sexual minorities) influence comfort in
assertiveness and treatment received, in light of evidence on intersectional stigmati -
zation in service environments (McKee et al. 2024). Methodologically, latent class
models and hidden Markov models are needed for relationship states and response
styles, with fairness-by-design modules (ex-ante evaluations and ex-post monitor -
ing of parity of outcome, tone, resolution time across subgroups). Theoretically, this
makes it possible to update the typology of complainants by incorporating the “algo-
rithmic mediator” as an actor in the relationship; practically, it indicates to firms
and regulators to co-design inclusive channels (human-in-the-loop mandatory for
ambiguous cases), tuned to cultural norms and sensitive to the relational dignity of
the older adult.
6.6 Managing grievances and service recovery
Marketplace fairness challenges  demand closer scrutiny for the older consumer.
Elderly customers are increasingly exposed to unscrupulous practices—from digital
fraud schemes to aggressive sales tactics—that test the limits of their trust. Older
adults may be especially vulnerable to such exploitation, given their tendency to

1 3

trust initial impressions and slower adjustment to betrayal (Horta et al. 2024). Future
research should examine how these unfair marketplace experiences affect seniors’
satisfaction, emotional well-being, and long-term loyalty. Notably, older customers
hold stronger justice expectations and are less easily mollified by superficial gestures
than younger consumers (Roschk et al. 2013). This implies that when service recov-
ery is handled inadequately—through impersonal jargon, lack of follow-up, or over-
reliance on AI chatbots—the resulting loss of trust could be especially pronounced.
For customers over 55, clear and fair remedies (e.g., straightforward explanations
and equitable compensation) have a more powerful impact on forgiveness and loyalty
than emotional appeals (Babin et al. 2021). Theoretically, integrating justice frame-
works with gerontological insights could illuminate why seniors respond strongly
to perceived marketplace injustices, particularly amid rapid technological change
that exacerbates their sense of vulnerability. Practically, firms and regulators should
refine service recovery protocols to be senior-friendly: use plain language, provide
empathetic human support alongside AI tools, and proactively close the loop on com-
plaints to reassure older customers that their grievances are heard and resolved.
Quality shortfalls in essentials —such as healthcare, nutrition, and eldercare ser -
vices—pose another critical avenue for future research. These essential services
directly influence seniors’ health and dignity, so poor quality can erode not only
satisfaction but also fundamental trust in providers. High-quality care demonstrably
improves older adults’ satisfaction and outcomes (Sharkiya 2023), so substandard ser-
vice may undermine their well-being and confidence. Cross-cultural comparisons are
needed to understand how elderly consumers perceive fair treatment and grievance
resolution in different societal contexts. In cultures valuing individual rights, seniors
may be more inclined to voice complaints and expect redress, whereas in collectivist
or high power-distance cultures, they may remain silent to avoid conflict (Meiners
2024). Socioemotional selectivity theory further suggests that many seniors, espe -
cially in harmony-oriented societies, prefer to minimize negative exchanges in later
life, potentially tolerating unsatisfactory essential services without formal complaint.
Future studies should, for instance, compare how elders voice healthcare grievances
across societies with different norms of deference or family involvement. Theoreti -
cally, such work can bridge consumer justice theory with cross-cultural gerontology
to reveal how notions of fairness and entitlement vary among older populations. For
practitioners and policymakers, these insights highlight the need for culturally sensi-
tive grievance handling—ensuring that an older patient from a culture of deference
still feels respected and heard even without voicing a complaint. Training service
providers in cultural competence and instituting independent ombuds offices could
help uphold fairness and trust for seniors across diverse communities.
Evolving complaint resolution strategies  in crisis contexts (such as pandemics
or natural disasters) represent a pivotal research frontier. Global disruptions like
COVID-19 have stress-tested service systems and generated novel complaints from
older adults—from difficulties with abrupt digital transitions to grievances about
safety and access in periods of scarcity. Evidence that crisis conditions alter seniors’
complaining patterns (Meiners et al. 2024) highlights the need to understand how
extraordinary circumstances affect their willingness to voice concerns and the effi -
cacy of resolutions. Future research should test innovative approaches tailored to

older consumers under such strain. One promising direction is personalized support:
for example, dedicated senior help lines or proactive outreach to check on elderly
customers during crises. Another approach is deploying AI-driven solutions (e.g.
chatbots) to handle surges in complaints, while accounting for seniors’ design prefer-
ences. Older adults tend to distrust purely algorithmic responses; notably, an experi-
ment found that AI apologies rebuild much more trust when using a warm, sincere
tone instead of a strictly formal one (Lim et al. 2025). Researchers could also probe
hybrid strategies—such as AI triage followed by human follow-up—to determine
what mix best meets older consumers’ fairness expectations in emergencies. Key
outcomes to assess include perceived justice, emotional comfort, and renewed loy -
alty after a crisis. Theoretically, this work can extend service failure models into
high-uncertainty environments, incorporating crisis management and technology
acceptance insights to explain how seniors cope and regain trust. Practically, the
findings would guide organizations in refining contingency plans—for example, by
integrating empathy-driven AI that transparently explains decisions and by empow -
ering frontline staff to expedite resolutions for at-risk seniors. In an era of frequent
disruption, developing agile, elder-inclusive complaint resolution mechanisms will
be vital for sustaining older consumers’ confidence and well-being.
7 Evidence from practice
To complement the research agenda without restating the practical implications
already embedded in each thematic subsection, this section illustrates the external
validity of the six agenda areas through documented real-world cases and field-based
evidence. These examples show that the issues identified in the review are not only
theoretically relevant but are already emerging in contemporary markets, services,
and consumer-protection systems. They also show that the three transversal drivers
of change identified in the agenda—global instability, societal fragmentation, and
technological acceleration—are already reshaping the conditions under which older
consumers experience satisfaction and dissatisfaction.
7.1 Well-being
The review identifies well-being in later life as a multidimensional construct shaped
by health satisfaction, social connectedness, and access to enabling services. A com-
pelling illustration of how firms can operationalize these insights is provided by the
Dutch supermarket chain Jumbo, which in the summer of 2019 introduced the Klets-
kassa (‘chat checkout’)—a dedicated lane for shoppers who are not in a hurry and
welcome conversation with the cashier. The initiative was part of the Dutch gov -
ernment’s national Eén tegen eenzaamheid (‘One Against Loneliness’) program and
was driven by Statistics Netherlands (CBS) data indicating that approximately one-
third of Dutch citizens aged 75 and over report feeling at least moderately lonely.
Jumbo’s CCO Colette Cloosterman-van Eerd described the supermarket as “a place
at the heart of society” and a vehicle for community-building (Jumbo 2021). Follow-
ing strongly positive customer reactions, the company announced a rollout to 200

stores nationwide in September 2021, prioritizing locations where loneliness is most
prevalent, and by early 2026 the format had exceeded that target. Complementary
‘chat corners’ and a Loneliness Handbook for staff were subsequently introduced.
The initiative has since been replicated across multiple national markets, confirming
its broader resonance. For management, the case demonstrates that service design
oriented toward social well-being can simultaneously generate loyalty and brand dif-
ferentiation, especially as accelerating self-checkout automation risks increasing the
isolation of elderly shoppers.
A complementary institutional illustration is provided by NHS England’s social
prescribing model, which formally connects older and vulnerable individuals to com-
munity groups, local activities, and non-clinical support networks in order to address
loneliness, isolation, and complex social needs (NHS England 2020). Together, the
two cases suggest that well-being among elderly consumers improves not through
digital substitution alone, but through hybrid arrangements that preserve both rela -
tional support and practical autonomy.
7.2 Brand attachment
The review shows that brand attachment in later life deepens when brands acknowl -
edge age as identity rather than stigma, and when they deliver continuity and person-
alized recognition. The L’Oréal Paris Age Perfect—The Perfect Age campaign (UK,
2015–2016) exemplifies this logic in a market context. By 2014, the Age Perfect
skincare line had experienced a 14% decline in value sales, in part because women
aged 55 and over reported feeling invisible to mainstream advertising (IPA 2016).
Working with McCann London, L’Oréal recruited Dame Helen Mirren—then aged
69—as a spokesperson who openly addressed ageing rather than concealing it. In
2016, the sub-range Age Perfect Golden Age  was introduced, engineered specifi -
cally for post-menopausal skin. The IPA Effectiveness Awards 2016 Silver case study
documents the outcomes: the campaign reversed the value-share decline, recruited
78,000 new buyers, and delivered a short-term revenue return on investment of £2
per £1 spent, rising to £4.50 over the longer term (IPA 2016). The driver is societal
fragmentation in its cultural dimension: the historical invisibility of older women in
advertising constitutes an instance of symbolic exclusion whose correction generates
measurable attachment and commercial value. The managerial implication is direct:
brands that design communications and products around the specific needs and biog-
raphies of older consumers—rather than positioning older age as a problem to be
solved—can unlock substantial loyalty and market growth.
7.3 Loyalty
The review identifies loyalty among older consumers as driven by continuity, physical
and cognitive accessibility, and perceived security of the shopping experience, rather
than by promotional incentives alone. Tokushimaru, a mobile supermarket operated
by Oisix ra daichi, illustrates how loyalty can be structurally engineered in response
to the intersection of demographic ageing and global instability. Founded in 2012 to
serve shopping refugees—elderly residents of rural and peri-urban areas with limited

access to retail outlets or public transport—Tokushimaru deploys small trucks that
visit customers at home on a regular schedule, eliminating the physical and logisti -
cal barriers that the review identifies as primary drivers of shopping avoidance and
brand-switching among older consumers. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Oisix
ra daichi reported a year-on-year sales increase of 35.4% in March 2020, as mobil -
ity restrictions made conventional grocery shopping inaccessible for large segments
of the elderly population (Oisix ra daichi Inc., 2020). Today, Tokushimaru operates
nationwide across all Japanese prefectures in collaboration with local governments.
For managers, the case illustrates that building loyalty among older consumers
requires reducing structural barriers—logistical, cognitive, and physical—rather than
relying on price-based or rewards-based schemes. Under conditions of global insta -
bility, the firms that maintain access become the firms that retain loyalty.
A complementary illustration is offered by the Japanese retail group Aeon, which
has developed customer-support initiatives—including in-store care-worker assis -
tance and dementia-friendly shopping environments—aimed specifically at reducing
the cognitive and logistical burden experienced by older consumers (Aeon Co., Ltd.
2010, 2015). The two cases jointly indicate that loyalty in later life is less a matter of
preference and more a function of whether firms actively sustain the conditions under
which older consumers can continue to participate in consumption.
7.4 Complaint behavior
The review documents a pattern of complaint inertia among older consumers, charac-
terized by under-reporting of dissatisfaction, delayed action, and heightened shame-
driven inhibition. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission’s Protecting Older Adults:
2022 Report to Congress  (FTC 2023b) provides large-scale quantitative evidence
for this pattern. The report found that adults aged 60 and over were substantially
less likely to report losing money to fraud than adults aged 18–59, yet when they
did report losses, median individual amounts were markedly higher—USD 1750 for
consumers aged 80 and over and USD 1000 for those in their seventies. Adults aged
60 and over were more than six times as likely as younger adults to report losses
from tech-support scams, and more than twice as likely to report losses from prize
and sweepstakes fraud. These findings are driven by the intersection of technologi -
cal acceleration—digital interfaces, AI-enabled impersonation, and cryptocurrency
payment rails—and societal fragmentation, as social isolation increases both vulner-
ability and the reluctance to seek help. A complementary institutional perspective is
provided by Ofcom’s (2024, 2025) repeated interventions in the UK’s migration of
traditional landline services to digital infrastructure: the regulator documented that
providers were failing to protect elderly customers dependent on telecare devices,
and in 2025 issued a fine of £23.8 million against Virgin Media. Together, these
cases show that silence cannot be treated as satisfaction: the absence of complaint
among older consumers frequently signals harm rather than contentment, and calls
for proactive, multichannel complaint architectures designed explicitly for older and
assisted users.

7.5 Complainant profiles
The review shows that elderly complainants are not a homogeneous group: resent -
ment, inertia, and proactive voice emerge as distinct profiles shaped by individual
differences in digital literacy, relational context, and the nature of the grievance. The
Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s (CFPB) Recovering from Elder Financial
Exploitation: A Framework for Policy and Research (CFPB 2022) provides the most
comprehensive empirical segmentation currently available. Drawing on interviews
with older adults, caregivers, and professionals, the framework identifies key indi -
vidual-difference variables: prior relationship to the perpetrator, digital literacy, care-
giver involvement, and cross-border versus domestic location of the exploiter. The
framework documents that only 5% of reported elder financial exploitation cases
result in full or partial fund recovery, reflecting both the structural barriers to voice
and the heterogeneous capacity of older consumers to navigate complaint systems
alone. Complementary evidence is provided by FTC data ( 2023b) showing that
among consumers aged 80 and over, approximately 15.8% of fraud reports are sub -
mitted by a third party—typically a family member, caregiver, or trusted contact—
with these cases associated with higher median losses of USD 6,000. The CFPB
framework has since been operationalized in the December 2024 CFPB-FinCEN-
Treasury Interagency Statement on Elder Financial Exploitation and in FINRA Rule
4512. For managers and policymakers, the implication is clear: complaint systems
must be designed not only for the autonomous, digitally literate complainant but also
for the assisted voice—the older consumer who can only access redress through a
family member, caregiver, or designated representative.
7.6 Grievances
The review identifies unfair marketing practices, quality shortfalls, and opaque com-
plaint processes as the primary sources of grievance among elderly consumers, and
shows that effective service recovery requires more than financial compensation. Two
enforcement cases illustrate this principle from complementary directions. The FTC’s
action against Publishers Clearing House (PCH), settled in June 2023, documents the
use of ‘dark patterns’—manipulative interface design—to mislead consumers, many
of them older adults with lower incomes, into believing that purchasing products
was necessary to enter its sweepstakes or would improve their odds of winning (FTC
2023a). Additional violations included undisclosed fees averaging over 40% of prod-
uct costs and deceptive email subject lines designed to resemble official documents.
The settlement required PCH to pay USD 18.5 million in consumer redress, dis -
bursed to 281,724 affected consumers in April 2025, and to redesign its user interface
to eliminate deceptive elements (FTC 2025). Critically, the corrective measures went
beyond compensation to mandate structural changes—a particularly credible form
of service recovery when the same firm has repeatedly harmed the same population.
The second case is provided by the corrective measures imposed by the Italian
Autorità Garante della Concorrenza e del Mercato (AGCM) following the Treccani
home-sales investigation (AGCM 2022). The AGCM found that door-to-door sales
created conditions of particular vulnerability for older and frailer consumers. Correc-

for elderly consumers becomes particularly credible when it restores informational

8 Conclusion
This systematic review consolidates five and a half decades of research and identifies
six core findings on satisfaction and dissatisfaction among elderly consumers. On the
satisfaction side, the review shows that well-being in later life is increasingly shaped
by autonomy, health perceptions, and emotional regulation rather than transactional
performance alone. Brand attachment emerges as a stabilizing mechanism that sus -
tains satisfaction and buffers dissatisfaction through familiarity and identity continu-
ity. Loyalty reflects a dual process in which satisfaction-based commitment coexists
with dissatisfaction-driven inertia. On the dissatisfaction side, complaints are trig -
gered less by minor failures than by perceived unfairness, while complainant profiles
reveal substantial heterogeneity in how dissatisfaction translates into voice, silence,
or exit. Grievances underscore the centrality of dignity, fairness, and relational justice
in restoring satisfaction among elderly consumers.

These findings collectively advance both theoretical understanding and mana -
gerial practice in elderly consumer research. Theoretically, they shift the discourse
from age-based generalizations toward process-oriented mechanisms that explain
how cognitive, emotional, and social factors shape evaluative judgments in later
life. The integration of satisfaction and dissatisfaction as complementary processes
reveals that older consumers' marketplace responses reflect not chronological age but
rather resource availability, coping strategies, and contextual factors such as fairness
perceptions and relational quality. Managerially, the findings highlight actionable
levers: fostering well-being through emotionally resonant care experiences, leverag-
ing brand familiarity while avoiding patronization, building loyalty through interper-
sonal relationships rather than transactional incentives, proactively addressing latent
discontent, tailoring complaint management to diverse elderly profiles, and embed -
ding fairness and dignity in service recovery protocols. Together, these insights pro-
vide a foundation for designing elder-inclusive marketplaces that balance efficiency
with empathy.
This review has some limitations. First, restricting our search to English-language
journals may have excluded relevant studies in other languages, though the theo -
retical mechanisms and methodologies reviewed (e.g., socioemotional selectivity
theory, expectancy-disconfirmation) are broadly applicable across cultural contexts
(Taylor 1985). Second, the 50-year timespan (1970–2025) raises concerns about
temporal comparability; while publication dates allow contextualization and theo -
retical patterns appear stable, cohort-sequential designs could better disentangle age,
generation, and period effects. Third, allocating each study to a single theme may
underrepresent multidimensional findings, though cross-references and thematic
appendices help mitigate this constraint.
In an era of unprecedented global instability, societal fragmentation, and techno -
logical acceleration, older consumers’ influence on demand, wealth, and spending
is growing rapidly, reshaping satisfaction dynamics in the coming decades. As con -
sumer markets continue to age, meeting the challenge of older adults’ satisfaction and
dissatisfaction will be essential for fostering inclusive, effective marketplaces. Future
research building on this synthesis can help ensure that aging populations are not
merely accommodated but genuinely valued in both consumer research and practice.
