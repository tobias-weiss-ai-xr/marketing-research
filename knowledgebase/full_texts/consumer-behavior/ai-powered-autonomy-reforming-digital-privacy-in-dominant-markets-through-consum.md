Abstract
This research examines how artificial intelligence and algorithmic systems
deployed by dominant digital platforms, particularly in Indonesia’s e -
commerce, undermine consumer autonomy through extensive data harvesting
and preference manipulation . The study aims to analyze these AI -driven
mechanisms against the consumer protection principles set out in Law No.
8/1999, particularly regarding consumer s’ rights to accurate information and
protection from deceptive practices. The research problem centers on the
interpretive legal analysis of whether AI -powered personalization and opaque
algorithmic decision -making violate consumer protection obligation s,
specifically transparency requirements and honest business practices mandated
by Indonesian Consumer Protection Law. This study employs a doctrinal legal
analysis methodology, incorporating a comparative assessment of Indonesian
consumer law against the European Union’s regulatory framework, examining
consumer protection frameworks , and conducting comparative case studies
from global jurisdictions to develop comprehensive regulatory solutions. The

Introduction

The proliferation of artificial intelligence and algorithmic systems
in digital markets has fundamentally transformed the landscape of
consumer protection, creating unprecedented challenges for traditional
regulatory frameworks. 1 Contemporary digital platforms deploy
sophisticated AI -driven mechanisms that exploit consumer behavioral
biases through extensive data harvesting and preference manipulation,
raising critical concerns about consumer autonomy and the adequacy of
existing legal protections. 2 These developments have prompted scholars
and policymakers worldwide to reconsider the foundational principles of
consumer protection law in the digital age.3
The urgency of addressing AI -powered autonomy violations in
digital markets stems from the pervasive nature of algorithmic decision -
making systems that now mediate virtually all consumer interactions
with digital platforms. 4 Recent empirical research demonstrates that
these AI systems can significantly influence consumer decision -making
through sophisticated choice architectures, dark patterns, and behavioral
exploitation techniques that operate below the threshold of conscio us
awareness. This empirical reality presents a critical challenge to the
Indonesian legal framework, specifically Law No. 8/1999, which relies
on the traditional premise of ‘informed consent ’ and is currently ill -
equipped to address such invisible technological coercion prevalent in

Ideally, the concept of consumer autonomy in digital markets
should shift from a purely philosophical construct to a concrete legal
standard. We define ‘Digital Consumer Autonomy ’ not merely as the
capacity for reflection, but legally as the freedom from ‘material
distortion of economic behavior ’ caused by opaque technological
interference.16 Drawing from Calo ’s theory of digital market
manipulation, autonomy is violated when a system leverages information
asymmetry to exploit consumer cognitive biases, thereby mediating the
consumer’s relationship with the market in a way that the consumer
cannot detect or resist. 17 Under Indonesian Law No. 8/1999, this
constitutes a breach of the ‘objective conditions of choice. ’ Therefore,
personalization crosses the line into illegal manipulation when it creates
a disparity in bargaining power that effectively nullifies the consumer ’s
legal standing as an equal subject in the transaction, violating the
principle of equity mandated by Article 2 of the Consumer Protection
Law.

decision-making and “outward-looking” dimensions that consider “the
relationship of one ’s choices with other individuals ’ and collective
interests.”18 This expanded conception of autonomy is particularly
relevant to AI -driven data processing, where individual choices create
externalities that affect other consumers and broader societal interests.19

It is legally imperative to distinguish ‘Consumer Autonomy’ from
‘Data Privacy,’ as conflating these terms obscures the specific legal harm
caused by AI. While privacy, under the ambit of the Personal Data
Protection Law, primarily concerns the control over unauthorized access
to personal data (secrecy), autonomy pertains to the independence of the
decision-making process itself (agency). As Grafanaki argues, a consumer
may technically consent to data collection —thereby satisfying privacy
standards—yet still be subject to behavioral profiling that manipulates
their choices, effectively eroding their autonomy. 29 Consequently, this
article focuses specifically on the instrumental use of algorithmic
processing to undermine consumer volition, a harm that is distinct from,
and often survives, the procedural compliance with privacy regulations.

designed for human -centered decision-making. Recent analysis of AI ’s
impact on legal professions reveals that algorithmic systems are

mechanisms.30 This trend toward automated decision -making in
commercial contexts raises particular concerns about consumer
autonomy, as it can remove human judgment and discretion from

A. Current AI Practices and Consumer Rights
Violations Under Indonesian Law

The analysis presented in this section diverges significantly from
previous Indonesian legal scholarship, which has predominantly focused
on data privacy violations under Law No. 27/2022 or liability for
algorithmic errors. Unlike prior studies that treat algorithmic
recommendations as neutral market facilitators, this research identifies a
distinct category of legal violation: the substantive erosion of consumer
autonomy through ‘pre-contractual manipulation. ’ The findings
demonstrate that current AI practices do not merely violate privacy, but
fundamentally breach the ‘Right to Correct and Honest Information ’
mandated by Article 4© of Law No. 8/1999, effectively invalidating the
legal element of free consent long before a transaction occurs.

B. Legal Framework Analysis Under Indonesian
Consumer Protection Law

While Law No. 8/1999 serves as the lex generalis  for consumer
rights, addressing AI -driven autonomy requires a systematic
interpretation that integrates modern statutes, specifically the Personal
Data Protection Law (Law No. 27/2022) and the Electronic
Information and Transactions Law (Law No. 11/2008 a s amended). 56
The limitation of Law No. 8/1999 lies in its pre -digital drafting;
therefore, Article 4 ’s right to information must be read in conjunction
with Article 35 of the PDP Law, which mandates data controllers to
ensure the accuracy and transparency of data processing. Furthermore,
the ‘electronic agent ’ provisions in the ITE Law impose liability on
operators for errors in automated systems. A harmonized reading
suggests that ‘consumer protection ’ in the AI era is not a standalone
concept but a composite right derived from the intersection of consumer
law, data privacy, and electronic transaction standards. 57

amendment to Article 7 of Law No. 8/1999 regarding the Obligations of
Business Actors. Specifically, a new subsection (h) should be inserted to
mandate algorithmic disclosure explicitly.81 The proposed draft text for
Article 7(h) would obligate business actors to “disclose the main
parameters of automated decision -making systems that determine

Conclusion

This research demonstrates that current AI -powered practices by
dominant digital platforms do not merely create market inefficiencies
but constitute systematic violations of the Indonesian Consumer
Protection Law (Law No. 8/1999). Specifically, the doctrinal analysis
confirms that algorithmic manipulations and opaque recommendation
systems fundamentally breach Article 4 on the right to correct and honest
information and Article 7(a) on the obligation of good faith. By
redefining these practices as “pre-contractual manipulation,” this study
establishes that consumer autonomy is not merely a philosophical ideal
but a binding legal standard that erodes when digital architectures bypass
rational decision-making processes.
To address this, the study proposes an “AI-Consumer Protection
Framework” that operationalizes the theoretical insights of Dworkin and
Brenncke into practical legal tests. By shifting the regulatory focus from
“market share ” to “Data-Driven Dominance, ” this framework allows
regulators to identify exploitation based on the process of advantage -
taking rather than just the economic outcome. This approach offers a
pathway to interpret existing statutory provisions more dynamically,
ensuring that the law remains effective against novel forms of behavioral
exploitation without waiting for lengthy legislative amendments.
Ultimately, this study calls for a paradigm shift in Indonesian
consumer law—moving beyond a transactional perspective to one that
safeguards the cognitive conditions of choice. While the proposed
framework provides the necessary dogmatic foundation, its s uccess
depends on a dual strategy: judicial willingness to interpret “good faith”
expansively to cover digital manipulation, and a concerted legislative
effort to equip regulators with the technological mandates necessary to
police the algorithmic age.
