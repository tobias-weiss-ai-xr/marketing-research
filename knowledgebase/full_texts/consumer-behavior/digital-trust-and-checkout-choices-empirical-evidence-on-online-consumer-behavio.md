Abstract: This paper examines the role of digital trust, perceived
usefulness, social influence, logistics reliability, and checkout option (cash-on-
delivery and digital payment) in on line consumer behavior across four Asian
markets (Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, and Vietnam) within an emerging
cyber-physical systems (CPS)  environment. Cyber -physical commerce
integrates computational and physical systems —such as smart devices,
embedded sensors, and real -time tracking —into e -commerce platforms,
thereby transforming how users perceive risk, trust, and continuity of use.
Information systems theories (TAM/UTAUT) and consumer trust literature
guided a cross-sectional survey (N = 2,412), analyzed via PLS-SEM with multi-
group analysis. We find that the strongest predictors of purchase intention are
trust and logistics reliability, while perceived usefulness is the major predictor
of continuance intention. Checkout choice has a dual nature: cash-on-delivery
is perceived to reduce payment risk and reinforce the trust –intention
relationship in low card -penetration cohorts, whereas digital payment
strengthens the usefulness –continuance linkage in higher -adoption cohorts.
Social influence has smaller but meaningful effects, especially among newer
online shoppers. Invariance tests indicate that these relationships are broadly
stable across the four markets, with differences in the degree to which
checkout choice moderates key paths. We discuss implicati ons for CPS -
enabled information systems design and platform strategy, emphasizing
investments in transparent logistics, sensor -based tracking, and multi -rail
payments. Limitations include self -reported behavior and cross -sectional
design. We argue that an Asia-specific “trust -first, rails -flexible” CPS
architecture, anchored in smart devices and embedded logistics sensors, can
nudge consumers toward digital payments without eroding perceived safety .

INTRODUCTION
E-commerce markets in Asia are among the fastest
growing globally, yet adoption rates remain uneven across
countries, age cohorts, and payment infrastructures [1, 2].
At the same time, cyber -physical systems (CPS) —defined
here as the integration of computational and physical
systems, including smart devices and embedded sensors—
are becoming central to platform operations. Examples
include smartphone -based order placement, sensor -
enhanced warehouse management, GPS -enabled courier
tracking, and IoT-based delivery lockers. These CPS layers
shape how consumers perceive logistics reliability,
payment risk, and the overall safety of online purchasing
[3-6]. Technology acceptance and continuance have long
been modeled in information systems (IS) research,
primarily through constructs such as perceived usefulness,
effort expectancy, and social influence [7 -9]. However,
logistics frictions and payment rails —both heavily
dependent on CPS infrastructures —are often treated as
contextual rather than structural variables, even though
they are salient in emerging markets [10]. Many Asian
contexts still rely heavily on cash -on-delivery (COD) as a
perceived buffer against payment risk, while mobile
wallets and real -time payment systems (e.g., UPI, QRIS)
reduce friction for repeat purchases and enable CPS-based
innovations like real -time delivery status and embedded
refund tracking [11 -14]. In this environment, cyber -
physical systems connect digital trust (e.g., beliefs about
data security, platform integrity) with physical
performance signals (e.g., on -time delivery w indows

derived from courier GPS, package scanning, and
embedded sensors). It is therefore important for both
theory and practice to understand how trust, perceived
usefulness, social influence, logistics reliability, and
checkout choice operate in a CPS -enabled ecommerce
landscape [10, 15].
Guided by TAM/UTAUT and the trust –risk
literature our research addresses three questions [6, 7, 16-
20]: How are digital trust, perceived usefulness, social
influence, and CPS -enabled logistics reliability directly
related to purchase intention and continuance intention?
How does checkout choice (COD vs. digital payment)
moderate these relationships in CPS-based platforms?
How do these relationships vary across four Asian
markets—Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, and Vietnam —
characterized by different levels of digital payment
penetration and logistics infrastructure [1, 12, 21]
We extend existing work in three ways. First, we
treat logistics reliability —operationalized through CPS -
driven features such as real -time tracking, accurate
delivery windows, and automated refund processing —as a
first-class construct in IS acceptance and continuance
models [22-26]. Second, we conceptualize checkout choice
as a theoretically meaningful moderator, rather than a
mere control va riable, capturing how payment rails
interact with CPS-enabled trust and usefulness. Third, we
provide comparative, multi -country evidence across four
Asian markets with varying levels of CPS maturity and
digital payment adoption [27]. The findings inform t he
design of trust -first, rails -flexible CPS architectures,
enabling platforms to guide consumers from initial, risk -
buffered COD usage toward habitual digital payments
without eroding perceived safety [14, 17, 26].

LITERATURE REVIEW
Theoretical underpinnings of IS and CPS
TAM and UTAUT in CPS -enabled commerce. The
Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified
Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT)
identify perceived usefulness and effort/effort expectancy
as key predic tors of technology adoption and usage [2, 4,
6, 26, 27]. In CPS -enabled e -commerce, usefulness is
manifested in features such as product search,
personalized recommendations, CPS -enabled tracking,
and effortless returns; effort expectancy reflects the ease of
navigating apps on smart devices, completing payments,
and accessing logistics information [28]. Trust –risk
integration. Trust in online platforms encompasses
perceptions of competence, integrity, and benevolence,
with perceived risk shaped by concerns  over privacy,
payment security, and product authenticity [29]. In CPS
contexts, trust extends to physical infrastructures —
warehouses, couriers, sensors, and packaging —because
these elements provide verifiable signals of reliability (e.g.,
GPS tracking, sc anning events, tamper -evident seals).
Trust is therefore distributed across the entire CPS
ecosystem: platform, payment provider, and last -mile
carriers [7, 13, 30]. Continuance and habit. Continuance
intention is linked to confirmation of expectations, ha bit
formation, and switching costs [4, 8, 31]. In CPS -enabled
platforms, habit is reinforced by saved credentials, one-tap
reordering, and reliable, sensor -based delivery
confirmations—all of which reduce cognitive load and
reinforce a perception of seamle ssness [8, 9, 32]. Loyalty
programs, embedded wallets, and CPS -enabled service
recovery (e.g., automatic refund triggers based on sensor
data) further increase switching costs and strengthen
continuance intention [15, 33].

Logistics reliability as a CPS-based driver
Logistics reliability encompasses on-time delivery,
accurate delivery windows, real -time tracking, courier
identity verification, reverse logistics (pick -up and
returns), and prompt refunds [34]. In CPS -enabled
systems, these features are imple mented through sensor
networks, GPS, barcode/RFID scanning, and integrated
warehouse management systems. For consumers in
emerging markets, these physical signals of reliability can
be as important —or more important —than interface
design in shaping purchas e decisions [20, 35]. Real -time
tracking, sensor -driven estimated time of arrival (ETA),
and automatic refund notifications reduce uncertainty and
perceived potential loss. When CPS infrastructures are
weak or fragmented, logistics variance increases and r isk
perceptions rise [36-38].

Boundary conditions: Payments, CPS, and
checkout choice
COD as a risk buffer. COD is often perceived as a
risk-mitigation mechanism, especially where card
penetration is low or chargeback protections are weak [5,
39]. In CPS-enabled delivery, COD can be combined with
tamper-evident packaging and proof -of-delivery capture
(e.g., photo, signature, GPS stamp) to further strengthen
trust. Digital rails as habit engines. Real -time payment
systems, mobile wallets, and QR -based payme nts reduce
checkout time, streamline refunds, and support
subscription models [14, 40]. CPS features —such as
automatic reconciliation of sensor -recorded delivery
events with payment status —reinforce perceived
usefulness and reduce friction in repeat purcha ses. As a
result, digital payment channels tend to enhance the
perceived usefulness –continuance path, particularly in
digitally mature cohorts [19, 21, 41]. Platforms therefore
face a “segmented equilibrium”: COD -heavy cohorts rely
on CPS -enabled risk buff ers, while digital -heavy cohorts
derive value from CPS -enabled speed and convenience.
Checkout choice thus becomes a moderator rather than a
simple demographic descriptor [5, 42].

Network effects, social commerce, and CPS
Social influence operates through  informational
and normative channels —family, peers, influencers, and
creators—signaling platform legitimacy and normalizing

CPS-enabled purchasing behavior [18]. In collectivist
cultures or tightly knit communities, the visibility of
others’ purchases and shared promotions can significantly
shape both initial adoption and continuance [16, 43]. CPS
enhances social commerce by enabling live tracking links,
delivery notifications, and user -generated content around
unboxing and delivery experiences, which in t urn
amplifies social influence on trust and usefulness
perceptions [10, 44].

Synthesis and hypotheses
Synthesizing these literatures, we derive six hypotheses:
H1–H3 posit that trust, logistics reliability, and
perceived usefulness positively influence pu rchase
intention. H4 states that trust, perceived usefulness,
logistics reliability, and social influence increase
continuance intention. H5 posits that COD strengthens the
trust–purchase intention path. H6 posits that digital
payment strengthens the usefu lness–continuance path.
These hypotheses are grounded in CPS -enabled
ecommerce, where logistics reliability and payment rails
are tightly intertwined with sensor -based tracking and
smart-device interfaces [14, 19, 45, 46].

METHODOLOGY
Design and setting
We conducted a multi -country, cross -sectional
survey with proportional quotas based on age, gender, and
urban city segments. Eight weeks of online data collection
were carried out in large metropolitan and secondary cities
in Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, and  Vietnam.
Respondents were recruited via CPS -enabled platforms
(e.g., app -based panels, mobile -optimized forms) to
mirror real online shopping environments [26].

Participants
Inclusion criteria were: adults (18+ years) who
had made at least one online purchase in the previous six
months. The final analytic sample comprised N = 2,412
respondents (Bangladesh 593; India 652; Indonesia 633;
Vietnam 534). Median age was 30 years; 50% were female;
62% were mobile -only shoppers; 48% had used COD at
least once in their last three purchases.

Measures
Digital Trust (TR):  Three dimensions —
competence, integrity, and security —measured with four
items each, adapted from IS trust scales.
Perceived Usefulness (PU) and Effort Expectancy (EE):
Four items each, adapted to CPS -enabled shopping tasks
(search, pay, track with sensors, and return).

Social Influence (SI):  Three items capturing family, peer,
and influencer/creator endorsements.
Logistics Reliability (LR):  Five items measuring CPS -
enabled on -time delivery, real -time tracking, accuracy of
delivery windows, pick -up and returns, and refund
timeliness.
Checkout Choice (CC): Behavioral indicator of payment in
the last three purchases (0 = COD, 1 = digital payment).
Purchase Intention (PI) and Continuance Intention (CI):
Three items each, capturing short -term intention and
long-term loyalty.
All items were measured on seven-point Likert scales.
Minor wording adjustments were piloted for linguistic and
cultural appropriateness in each market.

Procedures
Survey instruments were reviewed through expert
panels and cognitive interviews in all four markets. A two-
wave pilot (n ≈ 80 per country) was conducted to refine
wording and assess initial rel iability. The main survey
incorporated attention checks and randomization of item
blocks to reduce common method bias.

Ethics
The protocol received approval from an
institutional review board. Informed consent was obtained
electronically; participation wa s voluntary and
anonymous. Only non -sensitive demographic data were
collected, and no information that could uniquely identify
participants was stored.
Statistical analysis
We used Partial Least Squares Structural Equation
Modeling (PLS -SEM), appropriate f or predictive models
and for structures that combine formative and reflective
components. Bootstrapping with 10,000 resamples was
used for statistical inference. We assessed:
Reliability: Cronbach’s α and composite reliability (CR).
Convergent validity:  Average variance extracted (AVE ≥
0.50).
Discriminant validity: HTMT < 0.85.
Multicollinearity: VIF < 3.0.

The structural model estimated direct effects of
TR, PU, SI, and LR on PI and CI; CC was specified as a
moderator on TR→PI and PU→CI. Country dummies were
included as fixed effects. We also ran multi-group analyses
by country and checkout choice to test invariance.
Robustness checks included: (i) using effort expectancy
(EE) as an additional control; (ii) alternative coding of CC
(three-level ca tegorization); and (iii) excluding outliers
with Mahalanobis distance > 3 SD.

Power
With N = 2,412, α = 0.05, and small effect sizes (f²
= 0.02), simulation studies suggest that PLS -SEM has
sufficient power to detect moderated relationships and
cross-group differences in path coefficients.

RESULTS
Measurement model
All constructs exceeded recommended reliability
thresholds: Cronbach’s α ranged from .84 to .93; CR from
.88 to .95. AVE ranged from .56 (SI) to .73 (TR). HTMT
values were below 0.85 for all construct pairs, and three
items with problematic cross-loadings were removed.

Moderation by checkout choice
Checkout choice (CC) significantly moderated:

In CPS terms, COD leverages physical inspection, sensor -

perceived risk, whereas digital rails exploit CPS -enabled
instant refunds and seamless reordering to realize the full

DISCUSSION
Theoretical implications in a CPS context
First, the strong TR → PI path ( β = .41) supports
the view that initial purchases are risk-acceptance
decisions, where trust functions as a gateway for
usefulness beliefs to exert an effect [7, 22]. In CPS-enabled
platforms, this trust is reinforced by physical signals such
as real -time tracking, sensor -based delivery
confirmations, and perceptible refund speed [20, 28].
Second, logistics reliability (LR → PI = .27; LR → CI = .12)
demonstrates that operational, CPS -based performance
can rival or exceed abstract assurances in shaping both
adoption and continuance. Integrating operational
reliability into IS models narrows outcome uncertainty
and aligns with work on service qual ity and risk reduction
[20, 28, 30]. Third, PU → CI (.36) confirms that utilitarian
performance, once users are onboarded, drives routine use
and habit formation [4, 8, 24]. In CPS -enabled systems,
usefulness manifests via one -tap reordering, embedded
tracking, automated refund triggers, and intelligent
recommendations, translating CPS capabilities into
tangible customer value [10, 15, 21]. Fourth, checkout
choice effects (CC × TR → PI = .12; CC × PU → CI = .10)
formalize payment rails as moderators: COD enhances
trust-driven fulfillment , whereas digital payment
enhances performance-driven utility realization . These
dual tracks are tightly interwoven with CPS
infrastructures, which enable COD inspection  and digital
refund speed simultaneously [5, 11, 19]. Fifth, market-level
differences (e.g., LR salience in Indonesia/Bangladesh; PU
salience in India/Vietnam) highlight that CPS -enabled
architectures are cross-portable but not weight -invariant.
The same constructs operate, but their relative importance
depends on logistics infrastructure, payment systems, and
CPS deployment depth [1, 12, 25].

Managerial implications: CPS-based playbooks
For managers of CPS -enabled platforms, our findings
suggest several playbooks:
Design to meet trust/operations thresholds: Place
CPS-derived assurances —delivery-window accuracy,
verified courier identity, tracking milestones, refund -time
SLAs—close to the “Buy” button [14, 19]. Visualize reverse
logistics (pick-up options, refund routes) clearly in the app
[17, 20]. Engineer CPS-driven habit loops: Combine digital
rails with CPS features such as one -tap reordering, pre -
filled addresses validated via GPS, and instant refund
confirmations to highlight time savings and predictability
[19, 21, 24].
Segmented nudges based on check out choice: For
COD-heavy users, emphasize inspection -on-delivery,
tamper-evident packaging, and secure, sensor -logged
delivery, while offering gentle post -delivery incentives
(e.g., cashback for switching to digital next time) [5, 11, 19].
For digital-heavy users, strengthen subscription bundles,
real-time dispute resolution dashboards, and CPS-enabled
service recovery (e.g., proactive notifications when delays
exceed thresholds) [14, 16, 24]. Experimentation
blueprints can test combinations such as trust + logistics
badges (expected PI lift in COD cohorts), enhanced
tracking widgets (PI lift), and one-tap pay + instant refund
promises (CI lift in digital cohorts) [19, 26].

Policy implications: Building CPS -enhanced
markets
From a policy perspective, CPS -enabled ecommerce
requires:
Transparent refunds and disclosures:  Uniform
refund SLAs, standardized delivery -window disclosure
and enforceable pickup/return options can strengthen TR
→ PI and LR → PI [1, 20, 28]. Interoperable digital rails:

Real-time refund s, portable e -KYC, and chargeback -like
protections on instant payment schemes can lower
barriers to moving from COD to digital without excluding
vulnerable groups [3, 12, 21]. Address and courier
information infrastructures:  National address

Limitations and future research
The cross-sectional survey design restricts strong
causal inference; future w ork should employ staged field
experiments and longitudinal CPS telemetry to test
specific interventions (e.g., refund -time SLAs or tracking
enhancements). Self -reporting may introduce common
method bias, though we adopted procedural remedies such
as randomization and attention checks.

Future research should
Decompose trust into platform, courier, and
payment-provider components and examine how CPS
signals feed into each. Explicitly model live-commerce and
creator ecosystems as part of social influence. In corporate
financial inclusion metrics and digital literacy as
moderators, ensuring that CPS -enabled transitions to
digital payments do not marginalize unbanked or under -
banked populations. Track COD → mixed → digital
trajectories using multi-wave data and CPS telemetry (e.g.,
number of successful deliveries, refunds) to identify
tipping points that shift risk-averse consumers into digital-
heavy usage patterns.

Summary
Overall, our results support a “threshold-then-
utility” view in CPS -enabled ecommerce: trust and CPS -
riven logistics reliability jointly lower entry barriers to
purchase intention, while perceived usefulness, amplified
by digital payment rails and CPS functionalities, sustains
continuance. Checkout choic e moderates this dynamic by
positioning COD as a trust amplifier and digital rails as a
utility amplifier. Cross-market patterns indicate that while
core IS constructs remain applicable across Asia, their
relative weights depend on the maturity of CPS
infrastructures and payment ecosystems.

CONCLUSION
This study contributes to information systems and
management research on online consumer behavior in
Asia by embedding cyber -physical systems into the
analysis of digital trust, logistics reliability, and che ckout
choice. In CPS -enabled platforms, smart devices and
embedded sensors convert operational performance into
visible, trackable signals that reduce perceived risk and
enhance trust. Our findings indicate that trust and CPS -
enabled logistics reliability are central in lowering the
threshold for initial purchase intention, whereas perceived
usefulness—supported by CPS -based automation,
tracking, and seamless payment flows —dominates
continuance intention. Checkout choice moderates these
relationships in a w ay that resolves a regional paradox:
maintaining COD can increase conversion by
strengthening the trust –intention path for new or risk -
averse consumers, while digital payment rails, tightly
coupled with CPS features such as instant refunds and
automated reorders, enhance the usefulness–continuance
path for repeat purchasers. Market-level differences in the
salience of logistics versus usefulness suggest that while an
Asia-wide trust -first, rails -flexible CPS architecture is
feasible, its emphasis must be ca librated to infrastructure
maturity, payment penetration, and CPS deployment.
Strategically, platforms should stage investments across
the customer lifecycle: (i) pre -purchase (trust and CPS -
backed transparency), (ii) post-purchase (reliable, sensor-
based logistics and service recovery), (iii) cohort -specific
nudges (risk -buffered COD for beginners, convenience -
oriented digital rails for advanced users), and (iv)
ecosystem-level CPS standards for data, refunds, and
addresses. Future work combining surveys w ith CPS
telemetry and longitudinal or field -experimental designs
can further illuminate migration trajectories and ensure
that the shift to digital rails enhances inclusion rather than
creating new frictions.
