Abstract

Millet is one of the strong potential crops that could facilitate the transition to more plant -based
diets, enhance food security for the increasing global population, and address climate change
challenges. This research seeks to enhance the understanding of consumer behavior on millet
consumption in India and explore the factors that promote and hinder millet consumption by
applying the extended Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) model. The primary data was collected
by surveying Indian millet consumers to a ssess their consumption patterns. Data collection was
conducted online in the summer of 2024, reaching 500 respondents. For this investigation, we
employed partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) approach in conjunction
with the SmartP LS 4.0 software. According to the findings, consumer attitude, social influence
and perceived consumption feasibility are significant predictors of millet consumption intention,
while intention is positively associated with self-reported millet consumption behavior. Social and
functional risks operate as barriers within this pathway: functional risk is negatively associated
with attitudes and perceived behavioral control, while social risk is negatively associated with
subjective norms. Based on these findings, we suggest a marketing communication approach that
emphasizes the simplicity of millet preparation, affordability, availability and social familiarity.

Keywords
Millet consumption, consumer behavior, India, Theory of Planned Behavior

Introduction

Hunger, or nutrient shortage, is one of the key challenges confronting developing countries in
meeting the second Sustainable Development Goal (SDG2 - Zero Hunger) (United Nations 2025),
seeking to eradicate hunger and malnutrition in all aspects by 2030. It is a global challenge to feed
the growing population and tackle environmental problems, such as contamination of water
resources, soil degradation, and climate change. Therefore, it is essential to adopt a sustainable
approach to producing nutritious f ood that guarantees the security of food and nutrition
(Sukumaran Sreekala et al. 2023). Within this context, millet offers considerable potential.
Millet is unique in terms of nutrition, health benefits, and taste  (Chellappan and Peramaiyan
2024; Saleem e t al. 2023; Singh et al. 2025) . It is environmentally friendly due to less water
consumption during the plantation period, growth, and harvesting. Without the use of herbicides
or insecticides, millets may thrive in arid environments. Consumers' attitudes toward buying millet
are influenced by various factors, including environmental concerns, health consciousness, trust
in millet, millet quality, millet safety, and information about millet's advantages. Therefore,
producers and marketers are finding it increasingly important to underst and millet's purchasing
behavior. Millets are distinct and differ depending on the type, nutritional value, health advantages,
and cooking method (Kumar et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2025; Sagayanathan and Sudhagar 2025).
Recent efforts have been made by devel oping nations to promote millet. Millet, once a staple
in traditional Indian cooking, has diminished in popularity over the years (Sahoo and Mahapatra
2023). The Indian government implemented new policies to enhance production and consumption
(Sukumaran Sr eekala et al. 2023) . India is producing the most considerable portion of millet,
accounting for 41% of global production as of 2021, followed by Niger at approximately 12% and
China at around 8%. The estimated millet yield in India has more than doubled si nce 1966, even
as the area designated for cultivation has consistently decreased since 1971-72. Since 1966, there
has been an erratic trend in millet production, with fluctuating per capita availability over the years
(Harish et al. 2024).
Consumption level both in urban and rural households declined in India from 1977 -1978 to
2011-2012, according to the study by Sukumaran Sreekala et al. (2023) . Despite this, a distinct
situation still prevails in India with regard to millet consumption. As the world’s largest producer
of millet (FAOstat 2025) and the most populous country, India combines a long tradition of millet
consumption (Sukumaran Sreekala et al. 2023) with rapid urbanization (Gupta and Varghese
2021), changing dietary habits (Fardet et al. 2022)  and rising concern about non communicable
diseases (Murthy et al. 2009; Sharma et al. 2024) . This coexistence of high production volumes,
historically rooted consumption and recent declines in intake (FAOstat 2025) creates a paradoxical
situation: On the one hand, millet is advantageous in terms of availability, as it is drought-tolerant
(Kaur et al. 2024) , suitable for cultivation under diverse agroecological conditions (Kumar et al.
2024), and offers favorable nutritional value with respect to both macro - and micronutrients
(Dhiman et al. 2025; Issoufou et al. 2013), yet it does not occupy a central place in contemporary
urban diets. Understanding how Indian consumers perceive millet, and why they do or do not
choose to purchase consume it, is therefore cruci al for unlocking its potential contribution to
sustainable, healthy food systems.
This broader significance has recently been reflected in international efforts to reposition millet
as a key crop for sustainable food futures. Millet is one of the strong po tential crops that may
promote the transition to more plant-based diets and contribute to a solution for feeding the world's
increasing population, ensuring the world’s food security, and tackling climate change issues
(Sahoo and Mahapatra 2023). To keep the momentum going, the United Nations (UN) initiated a

because the primary outline of the country’s agricultural production system has been changed to
produce more food to fulfill their needs (Ghosh et al. 2020). Research by Meng et al. (2021) found
that, considering the nutritional and health benefits, consumers prefer millet products.
Unfortunately, only a few studies have comprehensively studied the current situation of the
different types of millets’ consumption (Bandyopadhyay et al. 2023; Chaturvedi et al. 2022; Kane-
Potaka et al. 2021), as well as the demand-supply gap in India (Sagayanathan and Sudhagar 2025;
Sukumaran Sreekala et al. 2023) . Research from Chande et al. (2021)  analyzed data from 128
randomly selected women of re productive age in Central -Tanzania. The findings indicated a
significant correlation between knowledge and behavior identity, as well as an effective correlation
among caregivers' intentions to consume pearl millet, suggesting that enhancing knowledge or
awareness of the nutritional benefits of pearl millet may serve as a key factor in increasing its
consumption among school -aged children (Chande et al. 2021) . However, Kane-Potaka et al.
(2021) found that millet’s taste is a significant factor deterring respondents from increasing millet
consumption, suggesting that health awareness alone is insufficient to enhance millet intake. These
contributions offer valuable descriptive insights; however, they are seldom grounded in samples
that accurately represent th e population across relevant characteristics of Indian consumers, and
they rarely employ a comprehensive behavioral framework such as the Theory of Planned
Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 2020) to model the latent psychosocial determinants of millet -related
decision-making.
Previous research has explored knowledge, health awareness, and attitudes related to millet and
traditional grains, but rarely examined underlying psychological constructs within a cohesive
framework. Limited understanding exists of how attitudin al factors, subjective norms, and
perceived behavioral control, as modeled by the TPB, influence millet consumption intentions and
behavior in India. Few studies consider perceived social risks (family and friends' opinions about
millet consumption) and fu nctional risks (perceived preparation difficulty, culinary uncertainty)
as barriers that weaken pro-millet attitudes. Addressing these gaps is crucial because understanding
behavioral drivers and barriers can inform targeted interventions beyond mere awareness-raising,
addressing consumers' actual concerns. Additionally, since India is a major millet producer,
changes in consumption could significantly impact food sustainability, farmer livelihoods, and
SDG2. This study aims to explain why millet consumptio n remains low despite policy efforts,
despite its potential.
To investigate millet consumption, we employed the  principles of the  TPB model as it has
demonstrated significant explanatory capacity in various domains, including the purchase of green

Conceptual model and hypotheses building

To justify the relevance of the proposed relationships in this study, a review of prior research
was necessary to determine whic h relationships had already been explored and the established
effects of those relationships. While developing the conceptual model, emphasis was placed on
studies of related topics, which informed the formulation of hypotheses and the construction of the
theoretical framework. The subsequent sections present the pertinent literature and outline the
hypotheses based on it.

Theory of Planned Behavior and associated hypotheses. The TPB is a prevalent framework for
analyzing and forecasting human behavior. It asserts that individuals' engagement in the intention
to consume is shaped primarily by three factors: attitudes, subjective norms (SN), and perceived
behavioral control (PBC), where attitudes denote consumer beliefs about the outcome of a
behavior and th eir evaluations of those outcomes. However, subjective norms denote social
pressure on consumers to engage in or refrain from a particular behavior, while perceived
behavioral control denotes conviction in their ability to execute a given behavior. A favor able
attitude, SN, and PBC collectively enhance consumers' intent to engage in a behavior. Stronger
intentions strengthen the probability of behavior execution (Ajzen 2020). Furthermore, the TPB
model is notable for its simplicity and predictive capability , leading to its extensive application
across diverse fields, particularly in consumer research. However, some researchers have criticized
the TPB for its focus on cognitive and rational factors, neglecting affective aspects of attitudes
(Bagozzi 1988; Spence and Townsend 2006).
Consumers' millet consumption behavior is positively affected by their attitude, feeling of taste,
health function, nutritional content, and quality of appearance; however, packaging has a negative
impact. It suggests that consumers focus more on millet products' inner attributes and functionality.
Consumer attitudes, however, differ from person to person because of both internal (e.g.,
psychological traits and emotional responses) and external (e.g., cultural status and social and
familial preferences) aspects (Lin et al. 2018) . Joshi et al. (2024)  stated that regiocentrism,
materialism SN, and PBC all significantly impact attitudes regarding regional food goods.
However, compared with low -intention consumers, high -intention consumer s had a more

H1: Consumers’ attitude toward millet a) positively influences millet consumption intention, and

According to Chande et al. (2021), SN play a key role in encouraging school -aged children to
consume more pearl millet. Additionally, they discovered that whereas health behavior identity
strongly predicted intention, knowledge considerably predicted health behavior identity. Proper
dietary composition is more likely to be ensured by i mproved knowledge and skills. In the
combined TPB and Health Belief Model paradigm, health behavior identity plays a mediating
function between intention and background and perception constructs. Meng et al. (2021)  noted
that pricing has a negative effect on customers' consumption behavior, whereas product advertising
has a favorable effect on SN and PBC. However, Sagayanathan and Sudhagar (2025) explored that
PBC, which measures the availability of time, money, energy, and opportunity to purchase millet,
is the top predictor, while consumer attitude, which has a favorable influence on the intention to
purchase millet, is the second predictor that influences the outcome. This suggests that consumers
have a favorable attitude about buying millet, and the SN is the last predictor that positively affects
the intention to buy millet. Consumers appreciate the views of friends, family, and doctors while
making decisions about buying millets. Chopra et al. (2025)  pointed out that social value had a
favorable impact on health benefits, showing that consumers originally attempted plant -based
foods to follow fitness and health influencers, fit in with the expanding trend, and get praise from
peers and celebrities. Joshi et al. (2024) found that PBC and SN had an indirect effect on attitude,
while regiocentrism and materialism had a direct effect. Materialistic consumers are more inclined
to identify with their location and its products, according to the association between materialism
and regiocentrism. The study demonstrated that PBC was positively correlated with regional food
product attitudes. Positive perceptions make consumers feel in control of their local food
consumption. Another perspective is that consumers who appreciate local food believe they have
the resources, skills, and opportunity to eat it. SN and PBC positively affect millet consumption
intention, demonstrating that social pressure to consume local food affects consumer behavior.
Therefore, the formulated hypotheses regarding the effects of SN and PBC are:
H2: SN a) positively influence millet consumption intention, and b) their association with self -
reported millet consumption behavior is mediated by intention.
H3: PBC a) positively influences millet consumption intention, and b) its association with s elf-
reported millet consumption behavior is mediated by intention.
In order to buy pearl millet goods, consumers need more knowledge about diverse uses, price
comparability, and health benefits. Marketers should concentrate on in-store sampling and provide
helpful advice through culinary demos on incorporating new food products into consumers'
lifestyles. Encouragement to buy things outside of their comfort zone and try new recipes, rather
than just avoiding a product because it is unfamiliar, can help achi eve this (Warin 2018). On the
other hand, people who know more about millet buy it. Therefore, the introduction of millet
products does not affect the consumption habits (Meng et al. 2021) . Concerns about food safety
significantly influence consumer purchasing decisions. Consequently, the main factors influencing
customer purchases of organic food items are the food's quality and the products' environmental
friendliness. Organic pulses and millets are also affecting consumer purchasing decisions due to
health maintenance, nutrition, and illness prevention aspects (Raju and Rukmani 2022). According

The impact of perceived social and functional risks. Few studies examine reasons for lower
millet consumption in India (Kane-Potaka et al. 2021; Miller-Tait et al. 2019; Sukumaran Sreekala
et al. 2023). Miller-Tait et al. (2019) found that public policies like minimum support prices and
subsidization promote rice and wheat over millet, known as “poor persons’ food”. Kane-Potaka et
al. (2021) identified taste as a key factor for reluctance to increase millet intake. Limited research
exists on millet consumption, especially regarding social and functional risks, which this paper
explores in the Indian context.

Social risks related hypothesis. Traditionally millet has been associated with the poor, “farmer’s
food” (Miller-Tait et al. 2019) , therefore limiting access to the general public, and in urban and
rural area, people in rural area have higher consumption than urban area (Sukumaran Sreekala et
al. 2023). However, social risks frequently serve to uphold established standards by discouraging
actions deemed socially undesirable. Customers may steer clear of particular brands or products
to avoid possible societal backlash, maintaining established conventions. This conduct fits the idea
of social risk, according to which people expect their social group will judge them negatively if
they don't behave in a way that is accepted (Hoffmann and Broekhuizen 2009) . Indirect and
detrimental SR can influence a consumer's decision to buy products like millet, particularly if those
products are not widely eaten in their social group. Those who are afraid of social rejection or of
being seen as out of the ordinary may refrain from buying things that do not fit in with the
consumption habits of their group. When the consumer is concerned about their social image and
their product decision is apparent to others, this effect is especially noticeable (Loebnitz et al.
2019). Given the (negative) perception of millet which may lead to lower consumption, we
hypothesize:
H5: SR a) directly and negatively influence SN b) indirectly and negatively influence the behavior
to consume millet.

Functional risks related hypothesis. As previously discussed, it is no coincidence that millet was
chosen as the grain of the year in 2023. In addition to its numerous beneficial properties, a study
involving over 15,000 participants across major cities in India Kane-Potaka et al. (2021) concluded
that better promotion of millet's health benefits and increased awareness of its diverse culinary
uses are crucial. This finding also highlights a significant issue from the perspective of functional
risk: many consumers lack sufficient knowledge abo ut the potential health benefits of millet and
are unfamiliar with how to incorporate it effectively into their daily diet (Das et al. 2024; Kumar
and Kotwal 2023) . Pearl millet's undervaluation and underutilization were caused in part by
consumers' ignorance and unfamiliarity with the grain. Their sentiments toward the grain's intake
were adversely affected by their concerns regarding its functionality (Cheng et al. 2024). Millets
are good for quick dinners because they are often simple to cook and quick to prepare. However,
FR exert a considerable influence on attitude and PBC, potentially impacting the decision to buy
millet indirectly. Consumers may adopt negative views and feel less equipped to include millet in
their diets if they believe it is difficult to prepare or if they are not familiar with its application.

Material and methods

Research design, data collection, sample and measurement. The study adopted a quantitative,
cross-sectional survey design grounded in the extended TPB framework. The target population
consisted of Indian consumers aged 18 -65 years who were aware of millet as a food product. A
stratified quota sampling procedure was used to obtain a sample that is approximately balanced
across key demographic and geographic strata of the Indian adult population with respect to gender
and age. Respondents were recruited nationwide through the C INT online access panel (CINT
2024) and invited to complete the survey. No specific city was purposively selected. Instead, the
fieldwork used preset quota controls to improve balance across key demographic and geographic
strata. Quotas were set for gender, age group, and broad region of residence. Data collection was
conducted online in India in the summer  of 2024 by a professional marketing research agency,
reaching 500 respondents. The study was approved by the University (protocol code 14/2023, 6
October 2023). The final sample matches the population proportions for gender (observed
proportion=0.51; test proportion=0.51; p=0.518) and closely approximates the age distribution of
the Indian population aged 18-65 (χ2=10.434, df=4, p=0.034) (United Nations 2020), as reported
in Table 1.
The sample can be further described based on respondents’ region, type of residence, perceived
income level, educational attainment, and their role in household food purchasing. Nearly half of
the respondents live in South India (25.4%) and West India (24.0%). A further 22.0% reside in
East India, while smaller proportions live in North India (14.2%), Central India (9.0%), and North-
East India (5.4%). In terms of settlement type, 73.6% of respondents live in urban areas, 16.6% in
semi-urban locations, and 9.8% in rural areas. Regarding education, the vast majority of
respondents have either graduated (53.0%) or hold a higher education (39.6%). A smaller share
has secondary education (5.6%), while very few reported another type of qualification (1.6%) or
no formal education at all (0.2%). Respondents were also asked how satisfied they felt with their
income situation. Overall, 26.2% reported being extremely satisfied, while 35.0% reported being
very satisfied. Others described themselves as moderately satisfied (21.4%) or slightly satisfied
(13.2%), whereas 4.2% said they were not satisfied at all.  With respect to food purchasing
responsibilities within the household, only a very small proportion of respondents reported having
no such role (1.8%). A larger share stated that they are partly responsible (15.4%), while the
majority (82.8%) is fully responsible for household food procurement.
The survey instrument was developed in English and refined through several rounds of
discussion within the research team to ensure clarity and contextual appropriateness for the Indian
setting. The questionnaire captured socio -demographic characteristics, millet consumption
frequency, and a set of latent psychosocial constructs derived from the extended TPB model. All

Data analysis. Data analysis was carried out using partial least squares structural equation
modelling (PLS -SEM) with SmartPLS  4.0 software (Ringle et al. 2024). This method is
appropriate given the study’s theory-driven but exploratory focus, the presence of multiple latent
constructs and the aim to estimate both direct and indirect effects within a relatively complex
model. Fol lowing current PLS -SEM guidelines, we first evaluated the measurement model to
establish the reliability and validity of the reflective constructs and then assessed the structural
model to test the hypothesized relationships between constructs. To create t he model, the
statements used in the material and method chapter were applied. In order to test the reliability of
the model, Cronbach's alpha (Cronbach 1951) was examined. The Cronbach's alpha value above
0.7 considered acceptable. Convergent validity was assessed using Average variance extracted
(AVE), and Composite reliability (CR) The AVE indicator calculates the construct's variance in
relation to the measurement error-induced variation (dos Santos and Cirillo 2023) its value above
0.5 is considered ac ceptable (Hair et al. 2022) . Indicator reliability was examined using outer
loading values, which at higher values indicate much commonality in the related indicators within
the constant. A value above 0.708 is considered acceptable (Hair et al. 2022) . We employed the
Fornell-Larcker test to check for discriminant validity (Hair et al. 2014) . Verifying whether a
structural model has collinearity issues is a crucial step in the process. We evaluate this by
examining the VIF (inner model) values (Hair et al. 2022). The Standardized Root Mean Square
Residual (SRMR) and the Normed Fit Index (NFI) are two metrics that measure model fit, which
is crucial to the study of the model. Hair et al. (2022) noted with regard to the SRMR value that
there is no specific val ue, to which the SRMR value should be adjusted, with lower values
indicating a better fit. However, as a rule of thumb, he mentioned a boundary value of 0.1. The
NFI would show perfect fit at a value of 1. A good match is indicated if the value is over 0.9 (Hair
et al. 2014).  To assess whether the relevant effects found significantly influence millet
consumption intention or behavior, bootstrapping analysis was conducted. This was done by

The primary objective of our research was to understand what the main factors are those
influence millet consumption intentions, and to unders tand factors that influence actual behavior
through intention. As shown in Table 6, millet consumption intention is most influenced by the
consumers' potential (PBC); if these conditions are met, intention is most likely to be formed. To
a lesser extent, w hat consumers' family, friends, and social environment think about the
respondent's consumption of millet (SN) and attitudes significantly shapes intention. The results
support H1/a, H2/a, H3/a, and H4, as attitude, subjective norms and perceived behaviora l control
are positively associated with intention, and intention is positively associated with self -reported
millet consumption behavior. The indirect effects reported in Table 6 further support H1 /b, H2/b
and H3/b, indicating that intention mediates the associations between the three TPB constructs and
behavior. The direct path from perceived behavio ral control to behavio r was not significant;
therefore, its role in behavior should be interpreted mainly through its indirect effect via intention.
H5/a,b and H6/a,b,c are also supported: social risk is negatively associated with subjective norms
and indirectly with behavio r, while functional risk is negatively associated with attitude and
perceived behavioral control and indirectly with behavior . Bootstrapped indirect effects indicate
that millet consumption intention mediates the associations of attitude, subjective norms, and
perceived behavioral control with self -reported millet consumption behavior. In addition, social
risk and functional risk show negativ e indirect effects on behavior through the TPB pathway,
indicating that these barriers weaken consumption behavior by constraining the antecedents of
intention. Based on the results, the model explained 77% (R2=0.77) of the intention’s variance and
29.1% ( R2=0.29) of the behavior’s variance, for social research, these values are considered
appropriate (Hair et al. 2022) . Effect size estimates  (f² values) refined the interpretation of the
structural model. PBC (f² = 0.284) and subjective norms (f²=0.222) showed moderate effects on
intention, whereas attitude had a smaller effect (f²=0.073). Intention exerted a small, near-moderate
effect on behavior (f²=0.146), while the direct effect of PBC on behavior was negligible (f²=0.000).
Functional risk had a small effect on attitude (f² = 0.026) and a negligible effect on PBC (f²=0.018),
while social risk had a negligible direct effect on subjective norms (f²=0.019). Predictive relevance
was further examined using PLSpredict. The Q²_predict values were positive for attitude (0.021),
perceived behavioral control (0.013), subjective norms (0.013), and intention (0.018), indicating
limited predictive relevance for these endogenous constructs. In contrast, the Q²_predict value for
behavior was slightly negative ( -0.013), suggesting that the model’s out -of-sample predictive
performance was not supported for the behavioral outcome.  These results suggest that the main
explanatory power of the model stems from the core TPB predictors, whereas the added risk
constructs operate as weaker direct barriers.

Discussion

Theoretical contribution. Millet is one of the crops that can facilitate the transition to a plant -
based diet and contribute to solving the food needs of a growing world population, which also has
implications for addressing climate change. Nevertheless, there is still little empirical research that
helps to understand the drivers and barriers to millet -related con sumer behavior. This study
addresses this research gap using evidence from a quota -based online panel survey conducted in

India. Consistent with the research gap identified in the Introduction, this study pursued three
interrelated research objectives. This study explores how the core constructs of the Theory of
Planned Behavior influence Indian consumers’ intentions  and behaviors  to consume millet. It
enhances the traditional TPB framework by adding perceived social and functional risks as barriers
affecting attitudes, norms, and control, while also impacting millet consu mption directly and
indirectly.
The TPB model was chosen as the framework for the study, which, despite numerous criticisms
and suggestions to avoid its use (Sniehotta et al. 2014; Sussman and Gifford 2019), has proven to
be a reliable framework in a wide variety of domains (Mucha 2024; Shin and Hancer 2016), thus
facilitating comparability of results. The results of the structural equation model provide clear
answers to the research objecti ves and empirically support the hypothesized relationships (H1 -
H6). In line with H1-H4 and the first research objective, attitudes, subjective norms and perceived
behavioral control all exert significant positive effects on consumption intention, and intention in
turn is a strong predictor of self-reported millet purchasing behavior. Perceived behavioral control
emerges as the strongest determinant of intention and an important indirect determinant of
behavior, underscoring the importance of perceived feasi bility. Addressing the second research
objective, H5 and H6 show that social and functional risks have significant negative effects on
subjective norms, attitudes and perceived behavioral control, and indirectly weaken millet
purchasing behavior. Finally, by jointly explaining 77% of the variance in intention and 29.1% of
the variance in behavior, the extended TPB model satisfies the third objective and confirms the
usefulness of integrating risk -related constructs into TPB -based analyses of sustainable foo d
consumption
The results of the present research confirm previous findings in several areas, while also
providing important additions to the international scientific literature, thus making a significant
scientific contribution. The results of hypothesis testing suggest that attitude is an important driver
for predicting intention and behavior to consume millet. This aligns with prior studies that initially
indicated the influence of individual beliefs on millet consumption among school -age children
(Chande et al. 2021). The present research confirmed this effect by using national panel data. The
use of the TPB has also been shown to be effective in examining additional drivers, as other
elements of the framework are also significant predictors of consumer intention and behavior
towards millet. Our results confirm the incentive effect of the consumer's social environment,
which supports the previous finding that the consumption of plant -based foods is positively
influenced by the trend effect from the consumer's social en vironment (Chopra et al. 2025) . Our
results show that of the three exogenous variables of the TPB, individual perceptions of feasibility
had the strongest effect on intention directly and behavior indirectly, supporting not only the
general literature on TPB but also research findings on plant-based foods and millet (Chande et al.
2021; Chopra et al. 2025; Sagayanathan and Sudhagar 2025 ). In addition to drivers, the present
study also took social and functional barriers into account that have the opposite e ffect on
purchasing behavior towards millet. However, SR frequently serve to uphold established standards
by discouraging actions deemed socially undesirable. Customers may steer clear of particular
brands or products to avoid possible societal backlash, m aintaining established conventions.
Indirect and detrimental SR can influence a consumer's decision to buy products like millet,
particularly if those products are not widely eaten in their social group. For millet, we have shown
that when the consumer is concerned about their social image and their product decision is apparent
to others, this effect is especially noticeable (Loebnitz et al. 2019). According to Kane-Potaka et
al. (2021) the creation of awareness of various millet cooking methods and further enhancing millet
products to gratify taste preferences will aid in altering the perception of millets; therefore,

consumption of millet will increase. Our study provided empirical evidence that FR directly
negatively affect attitudes and perceptions of fe asibility (PBC) towards millet consumption, thus
indirectly hindering millet consumption. This confirms that if consumers are not familiar with the
application of millet, it may result in a decreased desire to buy millet (Shah et al. 2024) . The
effectiveness of the TPB framework is supported by the fact that the model accounts for 77% of
the variance in the intent to consume millet, which is a very high proportion, compared, for
example, with the results on intention to purchase plant-based foods (53%) (Chopra et al. 2025).
The scientific contribution of this research is summarized in the following points. 1) We have
successfully applied the TPB framework to national panel data in the world's most populous
country. 2) We show that consumer attitude, the influence of the environment (SN) and individual
perception of consumption feasibility (PBC) are strong predictors of Indian consumers'
consumption intention and behavior towards millet. 3) We show that there are social and functional
barriers to Indian consum ers' consumption behavior towards millet. 4) This study shows that
functional risks have a negative impact on attitudes towards consumption millet. Taken together,
these findings indicate that the three research objectives formulated in the Introduction have been
met: the TPB core constructs are confirmed as key drivers of millet-related intentions and behavior,
perceived social and functional risks are validated as consequential barriers within the extended
TPB framework, and the joint consideration of dri vers and barriers substantially improves our
understanding of millet consumption behavior in India. This study advances the Theory of Planned
Behavior by showing that barriers like perceived social risk and functional risk influence millet
consumption decisions differently. Social risk weakens subjective norms, while functional risk
negatively associated with  attitudes and perceived  behavioral control, indicating diverse
mechanisms affect decision-making. The findings extend the TPB by demonstrating that intention
formation for traditional, socially repositioned foods like millet involves not only positive
motivations but also social disapproval and practical uncertainties.

Conclusion

Inadequate nutrition and hunger are significant issues in the developing world, presenting a
global challenge in sustaining the increasing population. Millet is a very promising crop that could
facilitate the transition to more plant -based diets, aid in addressing the challenges of feeding the
rising global population, enhance food security, and mitigate climate change concerns.
Nevertheless, little empirical research still helps to understand the drivers and barr iers to millet-
related consumer behavior. This study addresses this research gap with evidence from an
empirical, quota-based online panel survey from India, which is the world's most populous country
at present. TPB provides a theoretical framework for th e study.  According to the findings,
consumer attitude, the influence of the social environment (SN), and individual perception of
consumption feasibility (PBC) are strong predictors of Indian consumers' consumption intention
and behavior towards millet. However, there are social and functional barriers to Indian consumers'
consumption intention and behavior towards millet. This study shows that functional risks have a
negative impact on attitudes towards purchasing millet. According to our findings, we prop ose a
comprehensive marketing communication strategy that emphasizes the simplicity of millet
preparation, thus positively influencing attitudes; points out that millet is easy to obtain at
affordable prices, thus positively influencing PBC, and is sufficiently broad to reach a wider social
group, thus positively influencing SN and SR.

Practical implications. The findings of this research allow us to formulate important practical
recommendations for industry players, manufacturers, retailers, community marketing actors, and
even stakeholders. This study showed the importance of positive consumer attitudes toward millet
consumption. In light of this result, we would like to draw the attention of the sector to the
importance of appropriate education. Communic ating the benefits of millet consumption to
consumers positively influences the formation of compliance attitudes. According to our findings,
the consumer's immediate surroundings (SN) and subjective views of the feasibility of consuming
millet (PBC) signi ficantly influence consumer intention and behavior regarding millet.
Meanwhile, the findings of this research suggest that the barrier to positive attitudes is influenced
by functional risk (and thus indirectly by millet consumption), an effect that needs to be taken into
account when designing appropriate educational activities to shape attitudes.  Taking the negative
association of social risk into account, we suggest a marketing communication approach that
emphasizes the simplicity of millet preparation, highlights affordability and availability, and
presents millet consumption as socially familiar and acceptable. Such communication may support
more favorable attitudes, perceived behavioral control and subjective norms, although these
practical implications should be interpreted in light of the cross-sectional design.

Limitations and Further Research . This research acknowledges a number of limitations that
future research should take into account. The study relies on cross-sectional survey data collected
at a single point in time. As a result, the estimated relationships between the TPB constructs, risk
perceptions, intentions and behavior are associative rather than strictly causal, and temporal
dynamics in millet consumpti on cannot be captured. In addition, both the explanatory variables
and the behavioral outcome are based on self -reported survey responses, which may be affected
by recall bias, social desirability bias and common method variance. It was carried out on a sample
of 500 people, which, given the size of the total population, represents a significant limitation in
the generalizability of the results. A further limitation concerns the sampling frame. Although
quota controls were used for gender, age group, and broad region of residence, the study relied on
an online access panel and did not impose quota controls for all socioeconomic characteristics.
The findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence from a quota-based online sample rather
than from a fully nationally representative sample. The research ignored respondent demographics
and did not examine the moderating effects of these characteristics in the model. Furthermore, the
research did not include important constructs such as psychological or materia l barriers in the
model. We recommend that subsequent research be undertaken to address the current study's
limitations. Future research on a larger sample could take the demographic characteristics of
respondents into account and add additional constructs to the model. Longitudinal or experimental
designs would be needed to validate the causal ordering and to examine dynamic feedback effects.
Qualitative studies are proposed to identify these constructs.
