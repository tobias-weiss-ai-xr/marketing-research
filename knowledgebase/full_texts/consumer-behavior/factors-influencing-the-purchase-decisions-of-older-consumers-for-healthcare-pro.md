Abstract
Vietnam entered the ageing phase of its demographic
transition in 2011, and by 2025 people aged 60 and over
accounted for 14.5 percent of the national population.
Hanoi, with a resident population of more than 8.8 million,
hosts one of the largest urban groups of older people in the
country, and these consumers have become a visible and
fast-growing segment of the market for dietary supplements,
functional foods, home health -monitoring devices and other
healthcare products. Despite the commercial scale of t his
segment, the Vietnamese marketing literature still treats
older consumers either as an undifferentiated extension of
the adult market or as passive recipients of care. This
conceptual paper proposes a research model that explains
the purchase decisions of older consumers for healthcare
products in Hanoi. Drawing on the Theory of Planned
Behavior, the Health Belief Model and the consumer -
behavior literature on later -life decision making, the model
identifies four antecedents of attitude (health consciousness,
perceived product quality and benefits, perceived risk and
brand trust) and three direct determinants of the purchase
decision (attitude, reference -group influence and purchase
accessibility), with perceived price and value entering as a
further direct predictor. Attitude is positioned as a mediator,
and self -rated health status, living arrangement and income
are proposed as moderators  that capture the heterogeneity of
the older population. The paper formulates ten testable
hypotheses, specifies the measurement scales adapted for
older respondents, and outlines a survey design using
interviewer-assisted questionnaires and partial least squares
structural equation modeling. The proposed model offers a
basis for empirical work in Vietnam and for comparative
studies across ageing societies in Southeast Asia.

1. Introduction
Population ageing is no longer a distant projection for Vietnam. The share of people aged 60 and over passed the 10 percent
threshold in 2011, reached 11.9 percent in the 2019 census, and climbed to 14.5 percent in 2025, when the country’s
population stood at 102.3 million (Viet Nam Government Portal, 2026)  [33]. The speed of this transition matters as much as its
level: Vietnam is expected to move from an “ageing” to an “aged” society within roughly two decades, far faster than the
industrialised economies that went through the same shift in the twentieth century (Minagawa & Vu, 2021)  [19]. Hanoi
illustrates the trend at the urban scale. The capital had close to 960 thousand residents aged 60 and over as early as 2016, or
12.7 percent of its population at that time, and its total population had grown to more than 8.8 million by mid -2025 (Vu, 2025)
[34]. The municipal government has responded with a dedicated plan to implement the National Strategy on the Elderly to 2035
and with annual targets for periodic health examinations for older residents (Hanoi People’s Committee, 2025)  [12].
Alongside the demographic shift, a sizeable consumer market has formed around the health needs of this group. Industry
estimates value Vietnam’s functional food market at about USD 1.8 billion in 2025 and its dietary supplement market at
between USD 0.9 and 1.4 billion depending on the scope of the category, with double -digit annual growth forecast through the
early 2030s (IMARC, 2025; Ken Research, 2025)  [15, 17] . Sales channels have multiplied: pharmacy chains, direct -selling
networks, specialised health stores, television shopping and e -commerce platforms all compete for older buyers, and the
regulatory framework for health supplements has been tightened in response, including the first national contaminant limits
that took effect on 1 August 2025 (Ken Research, 2025)  [17]. Older consumers are therefore making purchase decisions in an
environment that is both richer in choice and harder to navigate than it was a decade ago.

Yet the academic literature on how older Vietnamese
consumers make these decisions is thin. Most published
Vietnamese studies on health -related purchasing focus on
functional foods or supplements among the general adult
population or among young consumers, and they rarely
sample people over 60 (Nguyen et al ., 2020)  [23]. Studies
that do address older people are typically descriptive
surveys from the public -health field, which document
prevalence of supplement use but do not model the decision
process. The marketing literature on older consumers from
other countries is more developed, but it cannot be
transplanted without adjustment. Older consumers in Hanoi
live in a distinctive context: a high rate of co -residence with
adult children, strong reliance on family and on physicians
for health information, limited pension inc ome for many,
and rapid but uneven exposure to digital retail channels. A
research model that reflects these conditions is needed
before meaningful empirical work can be done.
This paper responds to that need. Its purpose is to propose a
research model of the factors that influence the purchase
decisions of older consumers for healthcare products in
Hanoi, to derive testable hypotheses from that model, and to
outline a methodology for testing it. The paper is
conceptual: it does not report survey data. Its contribution
lies in combining three streams of theory (the Theory of
Planned Behavior, the Health Belief Model and the
consumer-behavior literature on ageing) into a single
integrated framework, in adapting that framework to the
Vietnamese urban context, and in specifying the
measurement and analytical approach in enough detail that
the model can be tested in subsequent work.
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section
2 defines the key concepts and reviews the theoretical
foundations. Section 3 reviews prior empirical studies and
identifies the research gap. Section 4 presents the proposed
model and develops the hypotheses. Section 5 outlines the
proposed research methodology. Section 6 discusses the
expected contributions and implications, and Section 7
concludes with limitations and directions for further
research.

2. Conceptual Background
2.1 Older consumers and healthcare products
Vietnamese law defines an older person (ngư ời cao tu ổi) as
a citizen aged 60 or above (National Assembly of Vietnam,
2009) [21]. This paper adopts the same threshold, which also
matches the cut -off used in national statistics and in most
Asian studies of later -life consumption. The group is far
from homogeneous. Gerontologists commonly distinguish
the “young-old” (60 to 69), the “old -old” (70 to 79) and the
“oldest-old” (80 and above), and these sub -groups differ
sharply in health, income, mobility and dependence on
others. Moschis (2012)  [20] argues that chronological age is
in fact a weak predictor of consumer behavior in later life;
what matters more are life events and circumstances such as
retirement, widowhood, the onset of chronic illness and
changes in living arrangement. This insight shapes the
moderating variables proposed later in this paper.
The term “healthcare products” (s ản ph ẩm chăm sóc s ức
khỏe) is used here to denote goods purchased by consumers
with the primary purpose of maintaining, protecting or
improving health, excluding prescription medicines and
medical services delivered by professionals. In the
Vietnamese market the category covers four main product
groups: (a) dietary supplements and health supplements in
the sense of Decree 15/2018/ND -CP, including vitamins,
minerals and herbal preparations; (b) functional foods and
beverages, such as fortified milk powders marketed to older
adults; (c) over -the-counter health products that do not
require a prescription; and (d) home health -monitoring and
support devices, such as blood -pressure monitors,
glucometers, massage equipment and mobility aids. These
product groups share several properties that make them an
analytically coherent category. They are credence goods
whose benefits are hard to verify even after use, they are
purchased repeatedly over long periods, they carry a
perceived risk to health if misused, and they are heavily
promoted through channels that older consumers find
persuasive, including television and personal selling.

2.2 Theory of Planned Behavior
The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) extends the earlier
Theory of Reasoned Action by adding perceived behavioral
control to attitude and subjective norm as predictors of
behavioral intention, which in turn predicts behavior
(Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975; Ajzen, 1991)  [8, 1]. Attitude reflects
the individual’s overall favorable or unfavorable evaluation
of performing the behavior. Subjective norm captures
perceived social pressure from important referents.
Perceived behavioral control refers to the perceived ease or
difficulty of performing the behavior, which depends on
resources, opportunities and skills. TPB has been applied
extensively to food choice and to the purchase of functional
foods and supplements, and meta -analytic evidence supports
its predictive validity in thes e domains (Urala &
Lähteenmäki, 2004; Verbeke, 2005) [31, 32].
The theory is especially useful for older consumers because
all three of its constructs map onto recognisable features of
later-life purchasing. Attitudes toward healthcare products
among older people are often strongly held and resistant to
change; subjective norms are powerful because of the
reliance on family members and health professionals; and
perceived behavioral control is salient because mobility,
income and digital literacy constrain what older consumers
can actually buy. Qualitative work with old er supplement
users in Bangkok found that their consumption patterns
aligned closely with these three constructs, with advice from
friends and television exerting a strong influence and high
price being read as a signal of quality (Ngamyingyod et al.,
2024) [22]. The present model retains the TPB structure but
unpacks each construct into more specific marketing
variables.

2.3 Health Belief Model
The Health Belief Model (HBM) was developed in public
health to explain why people take, or fail to take, preventive
health actions (Rosenstock, 1974)  [26]. Its core constructs are
perceived susceptibility to a health problem, perceived
severity of that problem, perceived benefits of the
preventive action, perceived barriers to taking it, cues to
action and, in later versions, self -efficacy. Marketing
scholars have borrowed the HBM to explain preventive
health consumption, and have shown that health
consciousness and perceived benefits of a product are strong
antecedents of attitudes toward health -related purchases
(Gould, 1988; Jayanti & Burns, 1998) [10, 16].
For the present model, the HBM contributes three ideas.
First, health consciousness, understood as a general

concern, is a stable trait -like variable that should influence
attitudes toward healthcare products regardless of the
specific product. Second, perceived benefits and perceived
barriers can be translated into the marketing constructs of
perceived product quality and benefits on the one hand and
perceived risk on the other. Third, cues to action, which in
the HBM include advice from physicians and messages in
the media, correspond to the reference -group influences
treated as a form of subjective norm in TPB. The two
theories are therefore complementary rather than competing,
and the model draws on both.

2.4 Consumer behavior in later life
A distinct body of research examines how ageing changes
the way consumers process information and make decisions.
Cognitive ageing reduces processing speed and working
memory, so older consumers rely more on heuristics, prior
experience and trusted brands and less on extensive
comparison of alternatives (Yoon et al., 2009) [36]. Lambert-
Pandraud and Laurent (2010)  [18] show that older consumers
prefer long -established brands, partly through attachment
and partly through declining innovativeness. Socioemotional
selectivity theory adds that as perceived time horizons
shrink, people give more weight to emotionally meaningful
goals and to relationships, which raises the influence of
close family members on decisions (Carstensen et al., 1999)
[4]. Perceived risk is also elevated in later life, especially for
products that affect health, and older consumers respond to
risk by seeking reassurance from brand reputation, from
professionals and from personal recommendation (Stone &
Grønhaug, 1993) [29].
Two further constructs from the broader consumer -behavior
literature complete the picture. Perceived value, defined as
the consumer’s overall assessment of the utility of a product
based on what is received and what is given (Zeithaml,
1988) [37], is central to a segment whose incomes are often
fixed and limited. Price is read not only as a sacrifice but
also as a quality cue (Dodds et al ., 1991)  [7], and older
consumers tend to rely on this cue more heavily than
younger ones. Reference -group influence, which Bearden
and Etzel (1982) [2] distinguish into informational, utilitarian
and value -expressive forms, operates for older consumers
mainly through family and health professionals rather than
through peers or media celebrities.

3. Prior Empirical Research and the Research Gap
Empirical studies on the purchase of healthcare products by
older consumers have accumulated mainly in East and
Southeast Asia over the past decade. In Thailand,
Wongwanit (2019)  [35] surveyed older consumers of
supplementary foods and found perceived benefit, word of
mouth and health consciousness to be the strongest
correlates of purchase intention. A later study in Bangkok
and its metropolitan area, based on 400 consumers aged 60
and above, confirmed that attitude toward supplement
consumption and ease of purchase were significantly related
to behavioral intention, and that family members were the
most influential referents (Boonyathawornchai & Thepanon,
2020) [3]. Ngamyingyod et al . (2024) [22], using in -depth
interviews with 21 older supplement users, identified several
problematic patterns, including prolonged use of the same
product without review, substitution of supplements for
prescribed medicines and reliance on friends and television
for product information. In Korea, a 2024 study of senior
consumers extended TPB with trust and found that trust in
the product and in the seller added explanatory power
beyond the three core TPB constructs (Oh, 2024) [24].
In Vietnam, Nguyen et al . (2020) [23] tested a TPB -based
model of functional yogurt purchase intention and found
attitude, subjective norm, perceived behavioral control and
health consciousness to be significant, but their sample was
drawn from the general adult population rather than from
older people. Other Vietnamese studies on functional foods
and supplements share this limitation, and the few that
include respondents over 60 do not analyse them separately.
Public-health surveys of older Vietnamese provide useful
descriptive context, such as the high proportion who
continue to work into their sixties and the limited access to
geriatric care, but they do not address purchasing behavior
(Minagawa & Vu, 2021) [19].

Three gaps follow from this review. The first is a population
gap: the determinants of healthcare -product purchasing have
not been modeled for older consumers in a Vietnamese city,
even though this group is the fastest -growing part of the
market. The second is a theoretical gap: existing studies
apply TPB in its generic form and do not integrate the
health-specific constructs of the HBM or the ageing -specific
insights of the later -life consumer literature, which leaves
the “why” behind attitudes and norms u nexplained. The
third is a methodological gap: most prior work measures
purchase intention rather than purchase decision, uses self -
administered questionnaires that are poorly suited to older
respondents, and does not examine the moderating role of
health status, living arrangement or income, which are
precisely the variables that make the older population
heterogeneous. The model proposed below is designed to
address all three gaps.

4. Proposed Research Model and Hypotheses
4.1 Overview of the model
The proposed model is shown in Fig 1. Its dependent
variable is the purchase decision for healthcare products,
defined as the older consumer’s actual choice to buy,
including the frequency and regularity of purchase over the
past twelve months. Attitude toward purchasing healthcare
products is the central mediating construct. Four antecedents
are hypothesised to shape attitude: health consciousness,
perceived product quality and benefits, perceived risk and
brand trust. Three further constructs are hypothes ised to
influence the purchase decision directly: reference -group
influence (the subjective -norm component of TPB),
purchase accessibility (the perceived -behavioral-control
component) and perceived price and value. Self -rated health
status, living arrangement and income are proposed as
moderators, and age sub -group, gender, education and prior
use are included as control variables.

The model deliberately separates the antecedents of attitude
from the direct determinants of the decision. This structure
follows TPB, in which attitude, subjective norm and
perceived behavioral control operate in parallel, but it
enriches the attitude construct with HBM -derived
antecedents so that the model can explain where favorable
or unfavorable attitudes come from. The structure also
allows the mediating role of attitude to be tested explicitly,
which most previous studies have not done.
4.2 Antecedents of attitude
Health Consciousness: Health consciousness refers to the
degree to which a person is attentive to their health,
integrates health concerns into daily life and is motivated to
take preventive action (Gould, 1988; Jayanti & Burns, 1998)
[10, 16] . Older people vary widely on this dimension: some
actively monitor their health and seek out products that may
protect it, while others attend to health only when illness
forces them to. Health -conscious consumers are more
receptive to the preventive promise of supplements,
functional foods and monitoring devices, and they evaluate
these products more favorably. Studies of functional food
acceptance confirm that health consciousness is one of the
most consistent predictors of a favorable attitude (Verbeke,
2005; Nguyen et al., 2020) [32, 23], and the same relationship
has been reported for older consumers in Thailand
(Wongwanit, 2019) [35]. Hence:
H1. Health consciousness has a positive effect on older
consumers’ attitude toward purchasing healthcare products.
Perceived product quality and benefits : Perceived quality
is the consumer’s judgment of a product’s overall excellence
or superiority (Zeithaml, 1988)  [37]. For healthcare products,
this judgment is bound up with the perceived health benefits
the product delivers: whether it is believed to strengthen
bones, improve sleep, control blood pressure or otherwise
address a felt need. Because healthcare products are
credence goods, perceived quality rests on cues such as
ingredients, country of origin, certification, packaging and
the claims made in promotion, rather than on direct
verification. The HBM treats perceived benefits as a core
driver of preventive health  action, and functional food
research shows that belief in a product’s efficacy is a
prerequisite for a favorable attitude (Urala & Lähteenmäki,
2004; Siró et al., 2008) [31, 28]. Hence:
H2. Perceived product quality and benefits have a positive
effect on older consumers’ attitude toward purchasing
healthcare products.
Perceived risk: Perceived risk is the consumer’s subjective
expectation of loss associated with a purchase, and for
healthcare products it has several dimensions: physical risk
(side effects or interactions with medicines), financial risk
(wasting money on an ineffective product), performance risk
(the product failing to deliver its promised benefit) and, in
the Vietnamese market, the risk of counterfeit or
unregistered products (Stone & Grønhaug, 1993)  [29]. Older
consumers are particularly sensitive to physical risk because
many take multiple prescription medicines, and they are
more exposed to financial risk because of fixed incomes.
Elevated perceived risk reduces the favorability of attitudes
toward the product category as a whole. Hence:
H3. Perceived risk has a negative effect on older consumers’
attitude toward purchasing healthcare products.
Brand trust : Brand trust is the consumer’s willingness to
rely on the brand’s ability to perform its stated function
(Chaudhuri & Holbrook, 2001)  [5]. It plays a compensating
role in markets where quality cannot be verified: a trusted
brand substitutes for the consumer’s own ability to evaluate
the product. The later -life consumer literature shows that
older consumers rely more on familiar and long -established
brands as a way of simplifying decisions and reducing risk
(Lambert-Pandraud & Laurent, 2010)  [18], and the Korean
study of senior supplement buyers found trust to be a
significant addition to the TPB framework (Oh, 2024)  [24]. In
Vietnam, brand trust for healthcare products is closely

H4. Brand trust has a positive effect on older consumers’

4.3 Direct determinants of the purchase decision
Attitude: Within TPB, attitude is the strongest and most
consistent predictor of intention and behavior (Ajzen, 1991)
[1], and empirical studies of supplement purchasing confirm
that attitude is the most salient factor and also mediates the
effects of other predictors (Zhu & Li, 2017; Nguyen et al.,
2020) [39, 23] . Older consumers with a favorable attitude
toward healthcare products will be more likely to buy them,
to buy them regularly and to allocate a larger share of their
spending to them. Hence:
H5. Attitude toward purchasing healthcare products has a
positive effect on older consumers’ purchase decision.
Reference-group influence : Reference-group influence
corresponds to the subjective -norm construct of TPB but is
specified here in terms of the referents that matter most for
older consumers in Hanoi: adult children and spouses,
physicians and pharmacists, and peers in the same age
group, including members of local associations of the
elderly. The influence of family is reinforced by the
Vietnamese pattern of co-residence and by the fact that adult
children often pay for, or physically purchase, products on
behalf of their parents. T he influence of health professionals
is reinforced by the trust older Vietnamese place in the
medical profession. Studies in Thailand and in Vietnam
consistently find subjective norm to be significant, with
family members the most influential referents (Nguyen et
al., 2020; Boonyathawornchai & Thepanon, 2020;
Ngamyingyod et al., 2024) [23, 3, 22]. Hence:
H6. Reference-group influence has a positive effect on older
consumers’ purchase decision for healthcare products.
Perceived price and value : Price enters the decision in two
ways. As a sacrifice, a higher price reduces the likelihood of
purchase, particularly for consumers on limited pensions. As
a quality signal, a higher price can raise perceived quality
and therefore purchase likelihood (Dodds et al ., 1991)  [7],
and qualitative evidence suggests older Asian consumers do
read high prices as a sign of superior quality (Ngamyingyod
et al ., 2024)  [22]. The construct used in this model is
therefore perceived price and value, following Zeithaml
(1988) [37] and Sweeney and Soutar (2001)  [30]: the
consumer’s judgment that the product is worth what it costs,
taking both the benefit received and the money given into
account. When older consumers judge a product to offer
good value, they are more likely to buy it. Hence:
H7. Perceived price and value have a positive effect on
older consumers’ purchase decision for healthcare products.
Purchase accessibility : Purchase accessibility corresponds
to perceived behavioral control in TPB and captures the
practical ease with which an older consumer can obtain the
product: proximity of pharmacies and stores, availability of
home delivery, the ability to order by telephone or through a
family member, clarity of product information and labeling,
and the availability of staff who can explain the product.
Mobility limitations, reduced vision and limited digital
literacy make these factors far more decisive for older
consumers than for younger ones. The Bangkok survey
found that ease of purchase and suitable delivery
arrangements were significantly related to supplement
consumption among people over 60 (Boonyathawornchai &
Thepanon, 2020) [3]. Hence:
H8. Purchase accessibility has a positive effect on older
consumers’ purchase decision for healthcare products.

4.4 Mediation
The model proposes that the four antecedents in Section 4.2
influence the purchase decision through attitude. This
structure is consistent with the TPB logic that beliefs shape
attitudes, which in turn shape behavior, and with evidence
that attitude partially mediates the effects of other predictors
on supplement purchase intention (Zhu & Li, 2017)  [39].
Testing mediation explicitly is important because it indicates
whether marketers and policy makers should aim at the
underlying beliefs (health consciousness, perceived quality,
perceived risk, brand trust) or at attitudes directly. Hence:
H9. Attitude mediates the effects of health consciousness
(H9a), perceived product quality and benefits (H9b),
perceived risk (H9c) and brand trust (H9d) on older
consumers’ purchase decision for healthcare products.

4.5 Moderators
Following Moschis (2012)  [20], the model treats the older
population as heterogeneous and proposes three moderators
that capture the main sources of that heterogeneity.
Self-rated health status : Older consumers who rate their
health as poor or who live with chronic conditions have a
more immediate and concrete motive to act on favorable
attitudes, so the link between attitude and purchase decision
should be stronger for them. Conversely, those in good
health may hold favorable attitudes without translating them
into regular purchases. Hence:
H10a. Self-rated health status moderates the relationship
between attitude and purchase decision, such that the
relationship is stronger for older consumers in poorer health.
Living arrangement : Older consumers who co -reside with
adult children are exposed to more frequent and more direct
family influence over purchases, and their children often act
as purchasing agents. Those who live alone or only with a
spouse rely more on their own judgment and on
professionals. Hence:
H10b. Living arrangement moderates the relationship
between reference -group influence and purchase decision,
such that the relationship is stronger for older consumers
who co-reside with adult children.
Income: For older consumers with low income, price acts
primarily as a constraint and value judgments weigh heavily
on the decision. For those with higher income, price is less
binding and the value construct loses some of its force.
Hence:
H10c. Income moderates the relationship between perceived
price and value and purchase decision, such that the
relationship is stronger for older consumers with lower
income.

4.6 Control variables
Age sub -group (60 to 69, 70 to 79, 80 and above), gender,
educational attainment and prior use of healthcare products
are included as controls. Prior use deserves particular
attention because habit is a strong predictor of repeated
purchase of supplements and because older consumers show
a marked tendency to continue with the same products over
long periods (Ngamyingyod et al., 2024) [22].

5. Proposed Research Methodology
5.1 Research design
The model is designed to be tested with a cross -sectional
survey of older consumers in Hanoi, preceded by a
qualitative phase to adapt the measurement scales. The
qualitative phase would consist of two focus groups with

5.3 Sampling and data collection
The target population is residents of Hanoi aged 60 and
above who have purchased at least one healthcare product
for their own use in the past twelve months. Because no
sampling frame exists for this population, a quota sampling
approach stratified by area type and age sub -group is
proposed. Following the reorganisation of local government
that took effect on 1 July 2025, Hanoi is administered

through ward - and commune -level units that span the
historical inner city, the urbanised districts and the peri -
urban and rural communes; the sample would include units
from each of these three area types to reflect differences in
income, retail access and living arrangement. Within each
area, respondents would be recruited through associations of
the elderly, ward health stations and pharmacies, with
quotas set for the three age sub-groups.
The minimum sample size for PLS -SEM with the proposed
number of predictors would be around 200, but a target of
350 to 400 valid responses is recommended to allow multi -

accommodate the higher rate of incomplete responses
typical of older samples (Hair et al., 2022) [11]. Data would
be collected through interviewer -assisted face -to-face
questionnaires rather than self-completion or online surveys,
with large -print instruments and trained interviewers,
because this method is more reliable for older respondents
and reduces the exclusion of those with limited literacy or
vision. Procedural remedies for common method bias,
including assurance of anonymity, psychological separation
of predictor and criterion items and varied response formats,
would be applied, and Harman’s sing le-factor test and a
marker-variable test would be used as statistical checks
(Podsakoff et al., 2003) [25].
5.4 Data Analysis
Partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS -
SEM) is proposed as the primary analytical technique
because the model is predictive in orientation, includes both
mediation and moderation, and may face non -normal data
from a quota sample (Hair et al ., 2022)  [11]. The
measurement model would be assessed for indicator
reliability, internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha and
composite reliability), convergent validity (average variance
extracted) and discriminant validity (Fornell –Larcker
criterion and the heterotrait –monotrait ratio). The structural
model would then be assessed through path coefficients and
their bootstrapped significance, coefficients of
determination, effect sizes and predictive relevance.
Mediation (H9) would be tested using the bootstrapped
indirect effects, and moderation (H10) would be tested
through interaction terms for the continuous moderator and
through multi -group analysis with permutation tests for the
categorical moderators. Where the sample permits, the
results would also be examined separately for the three age
sub-groups to check the stability of the model across the
older population.

6. Expected Contributions and Implications
6.1 Theoretical contributions
The model makes three theoretical contributions. It
integrates TPB with the HBM and the later -life consumer
literature into a single framework in which the antecedents
of attitude are specified rather than assumed, which moves
the explanation one step back from the “attitude predicts
behavior” result that most prior studies stop at. It introduces
moderators that reflect the heterogeneity of the older
population, following the argument that circumstances
rather than chronological age drive later -life consump tion.
And it specifies the dependent variable as purchase decision
with a behavioral component, rather than as intention, which
addresses a long -standing concern about the intention -
behavior gap in TPB research.

6.2 Managerial implications
If the hypotheses are supported, several implications for
companies marketing healthcare products to older
consumers in Hanoi would follow. Communication should
address the beliefs behind attitudes rather than attitudes
alone: it should supply credible evidence of product
benefits, reduce perceived physical and financial risk
through clear information about interactions and through
transparent pricing, and build brand trust through consistent
presence in pharmacies and endorsement by health
professionals. D istribution should be designed for
accessibility, with home delivery, telephone ordering, large -
print labeling and in-store staff trained to explain products to
older customers. Promotion should recognise that adult
children are often the real purchasing agents and should

1552
reach them as well as the older consumer. Pricing should be
set with attention to value perception among a segment with
limited income, and should avoid the practice of using high
prices purely as a quality signal.

6.3 Policy implications
The model also has implications for public policy. The
finding that older consumers rely heavily on family
members, friends and television for product information, if
confirmed in Hanoi, would support stronger regulation of
healthcare-product advertising directed at older audiences
and the development of accessible, trusted sources of
information through ward health stations and associations of
the elderly. Evidence on perceived risk would help the
authorities to target enforcement against counterfeit and
unregistered products, which are a particular hazard for
older consumers taking multiple medicines. More broadly,
understanding what drives healthcare -product purchasing
among older people would support the health -promotion
objectives of the National Strategy on the Elderly to 2035 by
distinguishing between purchases that genuinely improve
health and purchases driven by anxiety or persuasion.

7. Conclusion, Limitations and Future Research
This paper has proposed a research model of the factors
influencing the purchase decisions of older consumers for
healthcare products in Hanoi. The model combines the
Theory of Planned Behavior with the Health Belief Model
and with insights from research on consumer behavior in
later life. It identifies health consciousness, perceived
product quality and benefits, perceived risk and brand trust
as antecedents of attitude; attitude, reference -group
influence, perceived price and value, and purchase
accessibility as direct determinants of the purchase decision;
attitude as a mediator; and self -rated health status, living
arrangement and income as moderators. Ten hypotheses
have been developed, and a survey design using interviewer -
assisted questionnaires and PLS-SEM has been outlined.
As a conceptual paper, this study has obvious limitations.
The hypotheses remain to be tested, and the relative
importance of the constructs may prove different from what
the literature suggests. The proposed cross -sectional design
will identify relationships but cannot establish causality; a
longitudinal or experimental follow -up would be needed for
that purpose. The scope is limited to one city, and the
findings may not transfer to rural areas or to other
Vietnamese cities with different retail structures  and family
patterns. The healthcare -product category is broad, and
future work may need to distinguish between product
groups, since the determinants of buying a blood -pressure
monitor may differ from those of buying a herbal
supplement.
Several directions for future research follow. The model
could be tested comparatively in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh
City, or across Southeast Asian cities at similar stages of
population ageing, to examine the stability of the
relationships across contexts. The role of digital channels,
which this model treats only as one component of
accessibility, deserves separate study as older Vietnamese
consumers move online. And the role of adult children as
purchasing agents could be examined directly by surveying
parent-child dyads, which would clarify whose attitudes and
whose decisions are actually being measured when older
consumers buy healthcare products.
