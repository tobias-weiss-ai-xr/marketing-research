Within the theory of planned behavior, ambivalent attitudes (that are simultaneously positive and negative) can weaken the link between attitudes,
intentions and behavior. This study examined whether perceived healthiness of fast-food brands would weaken the relationship between positive
emotional attitudes toward fast-food brands and diet quality. In Study 1, university students in Australia completed the study in a lab; Study 2 (pre-
registered) was completed by US-based participants online. In both experiments, participants rated major fast food and beverage brand logos on
liking and perceived healthiness, completed dietary questionnaires (DFS, RED-13) and self-reported BMI and demographic data. Sensitivity
analyses excluded brands marketed as “healthy.” Consistently across both Australian and American samples, dietary fat and sugar intake was
predicted by both higher liking ratings of fast-food brand logos and reward-based eating styles. Contrary to expectations, these effects were not
significantly moderated by perceived healthiness of the brands. Fast-food brands that were more liked were also perceived to be healthier by those
consuming more dietary fat and sugar. These results point to a possible contribution of food brand marketing and brand loyalty in shaping
consumer behavior.
KEYWORDS
Fast-food logos, marketing, public health, diet quality, high-fat high-sugar diet
The modern food environment is replete with so-called “dis-
cretionary” food products that are often ultra-processed,
energy-dense, and high in free sugars, salt and saturated
fats. Discretionary foods and beverages are estimated to
comprise approximately a third of daily energy intake in
adults in Australia and the US (Mercado et al., 2017; Sui
et al., 2017). A key factor driving these population-wide
dietary-shifts is likely the aggressive marketing of discre-
tionary foods, with highly salient packaging featuring recog-
nizable logos that are designed to capture consumers’
attention and encourage purchase (Vermeir & Roose, 2020).
The rise of social media over the past decade has enabled
a new generation of digital food marketing strategies invol-
ving personalized and targeted advertising aimed at building
brand equity by increasing familiarity, liking, trust and
engagement (Aaker, 1992). Brand trust cultivated via social
media marketing has been shown to increase the likelihood
of purchase by consumers (Hanaysha, 2022). Ultra-processed
“junk” food brands are overrepresented in social media mar-
keting, with one study showing that two-thirds of foods
shown in social media advertisements were unhealthy and
over half were for sugar-sweetened beverages (Bragg et al.,
2020). Australian adolescents were exposed to 2.7 food
brands and one chain restaurant for every 10 minutes spent
online (Van Der Bend et al., 2022), while young adults aged

From a learning perspective, brand logos can be seen as
conditioned cues that become associated with the product’s
rewarding effects (palatable flavors and post-ingestive effects),
ultimately acquiring the ability to elicit craving and bias food
choice (Cohen & Babey, 2012). In animals, arbitrary stimuli
(e.g., tones) that previously signaled the delivery of food have
been seen to trigger eating in the absence of hunger (for review,
see Kendig & Corbit, 2024). Preschool children preferred foods
(e.g. French fries, hamburgers, carrots, apples) that were pre-
sented in McDonald’s® packaging compared to the same foods
presented in plain packaging (Robinson et al., 2007). However,
the impact of food brand logos on eating behavior (particularly
in adults) has not been extensively studied. While individual
differences in physiological reactivity to images of food has
long been associated with eating behavior and long-term weight
gain (Boswell & Kober, 2016), studies examining the effect of
brand logos on eating behavior are inconsistent (Boyland et al.,
2025). A recent systematic review reported that exposure to
brand-only marketing for food and sugar-sweetened beverages
consistently increases purchase intent but does not always
translate to consumption (Boyland et al., 2025).
The theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991) posits that atti-
tudes toward behaviors - in this case, unhealthy food consumption
- are key predictors of behavioral intention and action. Attitudes
are both cognitive (e.g., “eating McDonalds food is unhealthy”)
and emotional (e.g., “eating McDonalds is pleasurable”) and can
be complicated by the fact that people can often hold multiple,
conflicting attitudes toward a behavior (Conner et al., 2003).
Within the theory of planned behavior, ambivalent attitudes (that
are simultaneously positive and negative) can weaken the link
between attitudes, intentions and behavior. Conner et al. (2003)
reported that the link between attitudes and healthy eating beha-
vior was consistently impacted by ambivalent attitudes toward
eating healthily. Participants who endorsed both positive and
negative attitudes toward eating a healthier diet still had strong
intentions to eat a healthy diet, but this was not translated into
action. These findings may explain why the link between fast food
marketing and consumption appears to be particularly strong in
children and adolescents (Bagnato et al., 2023; Boyland et al.,
2016; Gascoyne et al., 2021; McCarthy et al., 2022) but less so in
adults (Boyland et al., 2025). Children are less likely to hold
conflicting ambivalent attitudes than adults, because their under-
standing of complex emotions and situations is still developing
(Donaldson & Westerman, 1986). Therefore, adults may endorse
positive attitudes toward fast food brands, but these might be
overall more ambivalent, making them less reliable predictors of
consumption.
Adults have been exposed to a lifetime of marketing and con-
ditioning of positive attitudes toward global food brands but may
also be more aware of the negative health consequences of eating
an unhealthy diet. In the current study, we operationalized these
health-related cognitions as perceived healthiness of food brands
(e.g., the extent to which participants believed products from
a brand were beneficial for their health). Lower perceived healthi-
ness of food brands could weaken the relationship between
positive attitudes and consumption. In line with this, a review of
Americans’ cognitive perceptions of fast food (Min et al., 2018)
found that negative attitudes, such as perceived health risks, were
associated with reduced consumption, whereas positive attitudes
such as convenience, taste and price were associated with
increased consumption (Min et al., 2018). While this study did
not assess emotional attitudes toward food brands (i.e., overall
liking of brands), it suggests a clear role for perceived health risks
of fast-food brands to reduce unhealthy food consumption. Lower
health literacy (understanding of the links between behavior and
health) and lower educational attainment each predict poorer diet
quality, including increased fast-food consumption, in American
adults (Kuczmarski, 2016; Namdar et al., 2021; Qi et al., 2023;
Shahavandi et al., 2021). People often overestimate the healthiness
of fast food (Stockton & Baker, 2013) or underestimate its caloric
content (Block et al., 2013), which may be related to increased
positive attitudes toward food brands and increased consumption.
Together this research suggests that poorer diet quality, and in
particular consumption of high-fat, high-sugar foods, should be
predicted by increased emotional liking of fast-food brands, but
that this attitude-behavior link may be moderated by the perceived
healthiness of food brands.
To explore this possibility, this study examined how positive
and negative attitudes toward fast food and sugar-sweetened
beverage brand logos were associated with general diet quality
in adults after controlling for demographic factors and general
propensity to endorse a reward-based eating style (characterized
by heightened cravings, reduced control over eating, and
a tendency to eat for pleasure rather than hunger). Attitudes
such as perceived healthiness, liking, familiarity and excitement
were selected due to their known influence on brand engage-
ment (Aaker, 1992; Hanaysha, 2022). Although fast-food
brands vary in their nutritional offerings, brand engagement is
expected to index broader discretionary food consumption pat-
terns, an assumption we test in the Results and Supplementary
Material, with analyses indicating good convergence with self-
reported dietary behavior. Our primary hypotheses were that:
(1) greater liking of food brands would be associated with
poorer dietary quality (i.e. more frequent intake of foods high
in fat and sugar); and (2) perceived healthiness of food brands
would weaken the relationship between positive attitudes and
consumption of food high in fat and sugar. These hypotheses
were pre-registered for Study 2 (https://osf.io/zpefd) in addition
to (3) the relationship between food brand attitudes and diet
quality would be moderated by education level, such that indi-
viduals with lower education levels may perceive fast food
brands as healthier, potentially due to lower health literacy
(Friis et al., 2016; Romero et al., 2023). Finally (4) the relation-
ship between general diet quality and brand logo attitudes
would be specific to food brands, and not to non-food brands.
Methods
Participants
In Study 1, we aimed to recruit at least 100 participants as
G*Power analysis indicated that this would give 85% power to

dictors (f2 = 0.15). In total, 114 students were recruited and
completed the study on a computer in a testing room with
a researcher present. In Study 2, we aimed to test 190 partici-
pants, assuming that approximately 10% of the sample would
need to be excluded due to the online nature of the testing. This
sample size (170 participants) would give 85% power to detect
a small-medium correlation of r = 0.22 (one tailed) and a small-
medium effect of f2 = 0.08 (one tailed), in a regression model
with five predictors. In total, 190 community participants were
recruited online via Prolific from the United States and com-
pleted the study on their own devices via Qualtrics. In both
studies participants were compensated financially for their time.
These studies were approved by the UTS Human Research
Ethics Committee (Study 1: ETH23-8040; Study 2: ETH24-
10282).
Questionnaires
Participants in both studies completed the following question-
naires (see Supplemental Materials for questionnaire items).
The Dietary Fat and Free-Sugar Questionnaire (DFS)
The DFS is a 26-item scale assessing self-reported frequency
of eating foods high in fat, high in sugar, and high in both fat
and sugar (Francis & Stevenson, 2013). Participants are
asked to indicate the frequency over the past year at which
they have consumed a variety of food and drinks. The DFS
has good test-retest reliability and correlates well with more
detailed methods of dietary assessment (e.g., quantitative
food frequency questionnaires; Francis & Stevenson, 2013).
The Reward-Based Eating Drive Scale-Revised (RED-13)
The RED is a 13-item scale that assesses preoccupation with
food, lack of control over eating, and lack of satiety, which are
features associated with eating for pleasure rather than for
homeostatic needs (Mason et al., 2017). Higher RED-13 scores
are correlated with higher BMI, greater likelihood of having
type 2 diabetes and greater cravings for savory and sweet foods
(Epel et al., 2014).
Demographics Questionnaire
Participants were asked to provide their age, gender, height and
weight. In Study 2, participants were also asked to also indicate
their highest level of education. For ethical reasons, providing
demographic information was optional.
Study 1 Procedure
Participants were first presented with a series of individual fast-
food and sugar-sweetened beverage brand logos on a computer
screen and rated their perceived liking on a scale from 0–9, with
the anchors “hate this brand” to “love this brand,” familiarity –
“extremely unfamiliar” to “extremely familiar” and perceived
health benefits – “no health benefits” to “very beneficial to
health.” The food brand logos were presented in random order
and included KFC, Subway, Domino’s, McDonald’s, Boost,
Starbucks, Hungry Jacks, Red Bull, Pepsi, Coke, Sprite,
Monster. Participants then completed the DFS, RED-13 and
demographic questionnaires.
Study 2 Procedure
The fast-food and sugar-sweetened beverage brand logos
used were Pepsi, Red Bull, Coke, Starbucks, Dunkin
Donuts, Dominos, KFC, McDonald’s, Subway, Wendy’s and
Burger King. Additionally, Study 2 also presented a set of
social media and technology brand logos: Apple, Facebook,
Google, Instagram, Netflix and TikTok. Brand order was
randomized. The same questions about liking, familiarity
and health benefits were asked as those in Study 1, but the
visual analogue scale was now presented with a slider and
ranged from 0–100, with only the anchors visible. An addi-
tional measure of perceived excitement with anchors “very
boring” to “very exciting” was collected in Study 2.
Participants also indicated the degree to which they inter-
acted with each brand (e.g. buying or using products): never,
less than once a month, once a month, couple of times
a month, once a week, couple of times a week or
every day. Participants then completed DFS, RED-13 and
demographic questionnaires. Two attention check questions
were embedded in the questionnaires.
Statistical Analyses
In both studies, an average rating of liking, familiarity and
health was calculated for each participant across all food
brands. Frequency of interaction and ratings across social
media brands were added in Study 2. Statistical analyses
for Study 2 were pre-registered (https://osf.io/zpefd ). For
ethical reasons participants could choose to skip demographic
and questionnaire items. Questionnaire scores were only cal-
culated for participants with complete data for the relevant
measure. To maximize the use of available data, participants
were included in analyses wherever a score had been
calculated.
For regression analyses, demographic covariates included
age and gender in both studies, and education level as an
indicator of socioeconomic status in Study 2 only, as Study 1
consisted of a university student sample with a restricted
range of educational attainment. Gender was entered as
a dummy coded binary predictor in regression analyses,
thus excluding individuals who identified otherwise or did
not report gender (7 participants in Study 1, 3 participants in
Study 2; see Results below). Interaction terms in regression
analyses were calculated after predictor variables were mean
centered to reduce multicollinearity. For all regression ana-
lyses, preliminary analyses were used to test for violations of
assumptions of normality, linearity, multicollinearity and
homoscedasticity. Normal probability plots of the standar-
dized residuals and scatterplot of residuals were also
inspected for major deviations from normality and violations
of assumptions. Outliers identified are reported below for
each regression analysis.

Results
Study 1
Participants
Participants were 114 adults (51 male, 56 female, 7 not
reported), of whom 107 completed logo ratings of familiarity,
liking and frequency. Demographic and questionnaire data for
the sample are reported in Table 1.
Higher Dietary Fat and Sugar Intake Was Associated With
Greater Liking and Perceived Healthiness of Fast-Food Brands
Brands received consistently high familiarity ratings (all brands
had mean familiarity > 7.7 out of a possible 9). Participants tended
to rate familiarity as high (ratings > 4), with 80% to 100% of
individual responses for each brand falling into this category.
Self-reported frequency of eating takeaway and fast food
(item 25 of the DFS) correlated positively with higher intake
of dietary fat and sugar (sum of all other items in the DFS), r
(103) = .337, p < .001. As shown in Figure 1A, liking of food
brands correlated positively with healthiness, r(105) = 0.40, p
< .001, and with familiarity ratings, r(105) = 0.23, p = .018.
Higher intake of dietary fat and sugar (DFS score) correlated
positively with liking, r(91) = 0.42, p < .001, and healthiness
ratings of food brands, r(91) = 0.24, p = .022 Figure 1B,C.
These remained statically significant after excluding brands
marketed as “healthy” (see Supplemental Materials).
Familiarity was not significantly correlated with total DFS
score, r(91) = .119, p = .256, nor healthiness, r(105) = −.01,
p = .941.
Higher Food Brand Liking and Reward-Based Eating Predict
Dietary Fat and Sugar Intake
We conducted hierarchical regression to identify whether the
interaction between brand liking and health predicted diet
quality after controlling for demographic and eating beha-
viors. Thus, we first entered total DFS score as the dependent
variable, with age and gender entered as predictors (level 1),
and then the liking and health ratings of food brands (cen-
tered), the interaction between them, and RED-13 scores
(level 2). Three participants were identified as outliers
(Mahalanobis distance p < .001) and were excluded.
Full model output can be seen in Table 2. The first model
with age and gender was not significant, but the full model
was. Liking ratings for fast-food logos were a significant
predictor but health ratings were not and nor was the inter-
action between these variables. RED-13 scores were also
significant in the model, suggesting that greater reward-
based eating also predicted more frequent consumption of
high-fat and high-sugar foods after controlling for brand
attitudes (see Figure 2).
BMI is Not Associated with Brand Attitudes or Reward-Based
Eating in Young Adults
The hierarchical regression analysis was repeated with BMI as
the dependent variable (see Table 2). BMI was not predicted by
age and gender, nor by the addition of liking, healthiness, their
interaction or RED-13 scores.

The Relationship Between Dietary Fat and Sugar Intake and

Moderated by Education Level
Years of education did not significantly correlate with liking, r

the interaction term between education level and perceived
healthiness were also entered as predictors (level 2).
As can be seen in Table 4, the first model was not signifi-
cant, suggesting that total DFS scores were not predicted by

perceived healthiness of food logos and education level was not
significant. This suggests that the relationship between per-

intake of high-fat and high-sugar foods was not moderated by
education levels.

Discussion
This research examined whether the perceived healthiness of
popular fast-food and sugar-sweetened beverage brands would
moderate the relationship between affective attitudes of the
brands and dietary fat and sugar intake in an adult population.
Importantly, perceived healthiness in the present study referred
specifically to participants’ beliefs about the extent to which
brands were beneficial for health, rather than broader constructs
such as nutritional health literacy or health consciousness (moti-
vation to maintain good health: Choi et al., 2021). We expected
that adults holding conflicting attitudes toward fast food brands,
defined as a positive emotional attitude alongside a lower per-
ceived healthiness of the brands, would report less dietary fat
and sugar intake compared to those with consistent positive

were not moderated by demographics factors (age, gender and
education level). Finally, BMI was not predicted by any of the
variables in the younger student sample (Study 1) and only
reward-based eating styles predicted BMI in the diverse sample
of US adults (Study 2).
The likelihood of engaging in behaviors like unhealthy food
consumption is predicted by the set of attitudes toward that
behavior, as outlined in the theory of planned behavior
(Ajzen, 1991). In turn, the strength of the link between atti-
tudes, intentions and behavior is thought to be weakened when
attitudes are ambivalent; that is, those with both positive and
negative aspects (Conner et al., 2003). This is likely to be the
case for adults’ attitudes toward fast food brands which, on the
one hand, are imbued with positive associations through global
marketing campaigns (Bragg et al., 2020; Nieto et al., 2022),
yet also have negative associations through awareness of the
adverse health consequences of eating an unhealthy diet (Dunn
et al., 2008; Min et al., 2018).
Both experiments in the present study show that individuals
who reported higher liking ratings for fast-food brands also
reported consuming diets higher in fat and sugar; critically,
perceived healthiness ratings did not seem to moderate this
relationship. While health ratings of food and beverage brands
were correlated with diet quality in simple bivariate analyses,
they were insufficient to predict eating behavior once other
factors were considered. Instead, positive emotional “liking”
about food brands predicted greater intake of fat and sugar.
Advertising aims to build brand equity by promoting
familiarity, liking, trust and engagement (Aaker, 1992) and
trust engendered by social media marketing increases purchase
intentions (Hanaysha, 2022). In line with this, the observed
associations between DFS scores, fast-food consumption fre-
quency, and brand interaction frequency provide evidence that
engagement with these brands tracks broader discretionary food
consumption patterns. Converging with this, supplemental ana-
lyses indicate that individuals with higher DFS scores more
rapidly categorized food-related logos, even at brief presenta-
tion durations, suggesting heightened familiarity with, or sal-
ience of, food-related cues. While within-brand nutritional
variability exists, these results point to an important role for
food brand marketing and brand loyalty in shaping dietary
behavior.
Higher reward-based eating and fast-food brand liking
were two distinct factors which independently predicted
more frequent consumption of foods high in fat and/or sugars
consistently across both Australian and American samples.
Reward-based eating (as measured with the RED-13) is char-
acterized by food cravings, hedonic eating and reduced beha-
vioral control over eating - the latter being another key
component of the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991).
These findings may have relevance for understanding the
multiple factors associated with unhealthy eating patterns.
Affective responses to fast-food brands and reward-based
eating tendencies may represent partially distinct correlates
of discretionary food consumption.
Previous research indicates that most individuals acknowl-
edge fast food is unhealthy (Dunn et al., 2008; Min et al.,
2018), and we hypothesized that perceived health ratings of
fast-food brands would weaken the relationship between lik-
ing and consumption. However, we observed a strong posi-
tive relationship between liking of fast-food brands, and
healthiness ratings. Specifically, we found that individuals
who more frequently interacted with fast-food brands also
rated their products as healthier and more liked. These results
are in line with a study reporting that brand commitment
influenced perceived healthiness ratings (Choi & Reid,

2018), but contrast with another which did not find an
association between frequency of fast-food intake and per-

Improving health literacy and health consciousness are
often argued to be useful targets for nutritional interventions
(Andrews et al., 2021; Choi et al., 2021; Qi et al., 2023;
Walters et al., 2020). Extending this logic, targeting per-
ceived healthiness of food brands may appear to be
a promising strategy to improve diet quality. However, our
data do not provide strong support for this. Although per-
ceived healthiness of food brands was associated with dietary
fat and sugar intake in bivariate correlational analyses, it did
not account for additional variance in dietary quality after
controlling for brand liking. Furthermore, perceived healthi-
ness did not moderate the relationship between brand liking
and dietary fat and sugar intake.
A possible explanation of our results is that those rating
fast-food brands as beneficial for their health have poorer
health literacy and lack understanding about the conse-
quences of diets high in fat and sugar. However, we found
limited support for this interpretation given that years of
education was not related to perceptions of fast-food brand
healthiness. While many studies have reported that education
level is a useful proxy for health literacy (e.g. Kuczmarski,
2016; Levin-Zamir et al., 2016), education did not predict
outcomes in either of our studies – the first sample being
university students and thus highly educated and the second
cohort being diverse in education level.
Given that education was not related to perceived healthi-
ness ratings of fast-food brands, an alternative explanation
for the current pattern of results is that positive emotional
appraisals of fast-food brands may shape perceived healthi-
ness. Although causal inferences cannot be drawn from these
cross-sectional data, individuals who habitually consume
higher levels of dietary fat and sugar could perceive fast-
food brands as healthier to downplay the personal health
risks associated with eating an unhealthy diet (Dunn et al.,
2008). Consistent with this interpretation of a “defensive
cognitive bias” (Croyle et al., 1997), research in tobacco
and other health-risk domains indicates that more favorable
affective evaluations are associated with lower perceived risk
and greater behavioral engagement (Popova et al., 2018). We
note however that we did not measure perceived health risk
directly in this study and perceived healthiness was assessed
using a single subjective measure, which may not reflect
knowledge-based or analytically-derived judgments. Prior
research suggests that such evaluations depend on both
objective knowledge and the ability to interpret nutritional
information (Andrews et al., 2021) as well as broader factors
such as health consciousness and literacy (Choi et al., 2021).
Future studies should more directly assess these constructs
and examine how they interact with affective brand responses
to influence dietary behavior.
There are further limitations to the current study. One
concerns the use of the DFS scale to assess diet quality.
The DFS scale measures the frequency of consumption,
rather than the actual amount consumed. While this scale
has been validated against a food diary and quantitative
food frequency questionnaires (Francis & Stevenson, 2013),
this may introduce measurement error in estimating total
dietary fat and sugar intake. This could also partially explain
the lack of association between BMI and DFS scores in both
studies. While we examined aspects of reward-based eating
which encompasses (lack of) eating control, participants were
not asked about other eating behaviors such as restricted
eating. Including more detailed assessment of dietary habits
and nutritional quality in future work will help elucidate the
relationship between liking and perceived healthiness of fast-
food brands, consumption and BMI. It will also be important
to assess additional socioeconomic indicators such as house-
hold income, which may influence dietary habits. In Study 2,
education level was included as a proxy indicator of socio-
economic status; however, this does not fully capture socio-
economic variation. In Study 1, the sample consisted of
university students, resulting in a relatively restricted range
of socioeconomic status. Lower socioeconomic status is asso-
ciated with greater exposure to fast-food environments and
higher fast-food consumption (Burgoine et al., 2016, 2018)
suggesting that socioeconomic factors may partially shape
the relationships observed here. Cost and convenience are
also important determinants of fast-food consumption
(Bryant & Dundes, 2008; Min et al., 2018) and should be
investigated in future studies. A further limitation is that fast-
food brands contain heterogeneous menu options, meaning
that brand-level attitudes do not directly map onto the nutri-
tional composition of all foods consumed within a given
brand. While we observed associations between frequency
of interaction with these brands and dietary fat and sugar
intake, we did not assess the specific items consumed within
each brand. Future research would benefit from more fine-
grained dietary assessment to clarify how brand-level evalua-
tions translate into specific food choices within and across
brands.
The overarching objective of this study was to examine
whether perceived healthiness of fast-food brands would
weaken the relationship between positive emotional attitudes
toward fast-food brands and diet quality. Contrary to our
hypotheses, neither perceived healthiness nor education mod-
erated this relationship. Instead, positive emotional attitudes
toward fast-food brands emerged as the primary correlate of
diet quality. These findings have implications that may prove
relevant in shaping public health policies around nutrition,
food choice and health. Specifically, they suggest that poli-
cies regulating the specific promotion of discretionary foods
(designed to enhance brand liking) may have greater impact
on consumer behavior than broader educational efforts
focused on the perceived healthiness of discretionary foods.
However, further experimental research is needed to establish
causality and determine the degree to which fast-food brand
logos influence eating behavior and food choice (Boyland
et al., 2025).
