# Bridging digital finance and environmental sustainability: the role of sustainable financial behavior in shaping green consumer choices in Sierra Leone

Abstract
This study investigates the interplay between digital financial literacy (DFL), environmental concern (EC), and green
consumer behavior (GCB) among urban consumers in Freetown, Sierra Leone, with particular attention to the mediat‑
ing role of sustainable financial behavior (SFB) and the moderating influence of perceived risk (PR). Adopting a quan‑
titative cross‑sectional design, data were collected from 500 usable responses and analyzed using partial least squares
structural equation modeling (PLS‑SEM) via SmartPLS. Results reveal that both DFL and EC significantly and positively
influence GCB, with DFL demonstrating a stronger direct effect (β = 0.517, p < 0.001) compared to EC (β = 0.124,
p = 0.012). SFB partially mediates both relationships, serving as a critical behavioral mechanism that translates finan‑
cial capability and environmental values into sustainable consumption practices. Perceived risk exhibited negative
but non‑significant moderation effects, suggesting that risk perceptions do not meaningfully attenuate the influ‑
ence of DFL or EC on green consumer behavior in this emerging digital economy context. The findings contribute
to sustainable finance literature by empirically validating SFB as a pivotal conduit linking digital literacy and environ‑
mental consciousness to actionable green behavior in a developing economy setting. Practically, the study advocates
for integrated policy interventions that combine digital financial education, environmental awareness campaigns,
and trust‑building mechanisms within digital and green marketplaces to advance Sustainable Development Goals
8, 12, and 13. By bridging behavioral finance, digital inclusion, and environmental sustainability, this research offers
a replicable framework for fostering responsible consumer lifestyles in resource‑constrained economies.

ing what people’s financial capabilities together with

Recent innovations in digital finance have changed the
way people access, control, and invest their money, ren -
dering DFL a critical skill for navigating today’s sustain -
ability terrain. DFL refers to the ability to actively use
digital devices to make reasoned financial decisions and
manage resources [1]. Research has provided evidence
that individuals who are computer literate in financial
terms tend to exhibit futuristic behavior regarding their
finances, including safety and savings [2, 3]. Further,
increased DFL directly lends to SDG 4 by supporting
knowledge and lifelong learning in SDG 8 through pro -
moting equal participation in the digital economy [4, 5].
Aside from financial literacy, EC (environmental
awareness and willingness to protect the natural environ-
ment) is an important factor in encouraging pro-environ-
mental consumption. Previous studies show that people
with a high level of environmental concern are more
likely to behave in a sustainable manner (such as buying
eco-friendly products and support for green innovation)
[6–8]. However, the conversion of EC into real green
behavior varies by socioeconomic context [9] especially
in developing countries, where affordability, digital acces-
sibility, and perceived risk affect consumer decision-mak-
ing [10].
Despite growing recognition of DFL and EC as inde -
pendent drivers of sustainable behavior, several critical
gaps remain. First, most studies examine DFL and EC
separately, without exploring their synergistic effects
on GCB [1, 6, 8]. Second, the mediating role of sustain -
able financial behavior (SFB)—citizens’ capacity to make
financial decisions aligned with sustainability principles
[11, 12]—remains empirically untested in this nexus, par-
ticularly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). Third, the bound -
ary conditions of these relationships remain unclear.
Perceived risk (PR), encompassing worries about finan -
cial loss, digital fraud, or product failure, can inhibit
GCB [13, 14], yet few studies have examined how PR
moderates the effects of DFL and EC on GCB in rapidly
digitalizing developing economies. Most critically, exist -
ing literature is concentrated in developed and emerg -
ing economies [1, 2, 6–8, 10, 11], with limited attention
to fragile states in SSA. Sierra Leone, despite rapid digi -
talization, remains largely unexplored in sustainabil -
ity research, with significant populations facing limited
access to financial education and sustainable products
[15].
This study addresses these gaps by examining how digi-
tal financial capabilities and environmental awareness
can be harnessed to promote sustainable consumption
in contexts marked by rapid digital transformation yet
persistent structural vulnerabilities. Urban Sierra Leone,
particularly Freetown, presents an ideal setting: residents
are increasingly connected to digital financial tools and
global environmental initiatives, yet operate in environ -
ments where perceived risks and resource constraints
remain salient. This research makes four distinct contri -
butions. Unlike prior studies examining DFL and EC sep-
arately [1, 6, 8], it develops an integrated model of their
simultaneous effects on GCB. It empirically tests SFB as a
mediating mechanism in this nexus for the first time [11,
12], identifies boundary conditions through PR modera -
tion [13, 14], and provides some of the earliest empirical
evidence in Sierra Leone, a post-conflict, rapidly digital -
izing economy, thereby extending predominantly Asia-
centric and OECD-focused literature [1, 2, 6–8, 10, 16] to
a critically under-researched context.
Literature review
Hypothesis development
Digital financial literacy and sustainable financial behavior
Digital financial literacy (DFL) has emerged as a critical
skill in the digital economy, enabling people to access,
develop, and apply their financial knowledge through
electronic media to make sound financial judgments [1].
Unlike conventional financial literacy, DFL combines the
knowledge of finance with digital aptitude, enabling peo -
ple to use online tools such as e-wallets, mobile banking,
and fintech apps responsibly [16, 17]. Expansion of digi -
tal finance in emerging economies has ushered in new
opportunities for promoting financial inclusion, but has
simultaneously brought to the fore the necessity of robust
DFL if inclusive, sustainable, and responsible financial
engagements are to be achieved [2]. Indeed, research has
increasingly stressed the fact that DFL provides people
with not only brighter financial decision-making but also
fosters SFB, i.e., expenses and savings that are consistent
with responsible, ethical conduct, respectful of sustain -
ability in the long run [3, 11]. DFL increases personal lev-
els of how financial behaviors, such as saving, investment,
and spending, can relate to environmental and social wel-
fare [15, 18]. Recent research has found that DFL is good
for the development of sustainable consumption and sav-
ings activities, because it may induce individuals to adopt
new technologies that can cut transaction costs and
environmental waste [19, 20]. In addition, improved digi-
tal literacy allows consumers to recognize trustworthy,
sustainability-driven financial products and consequently
raise the level of green financial belief [8]. This associa -
tion is consistent with SDG 4, as it enhances financial lit -
eracy, and with SDG 8, as it promotes greater inclusion.
Against the backdrop of urban Sierra Leone, where
digital financial systems are rapidly expanding while con -
sumer literacy remains low, understanding how DFL fos -
ters SFB is especially critical. As digital finance becomes

H1  Digital financial literacy positively impacts sustain -
able financial behavior.
Digital financial literacy and green consumer behavior
Green consumer behavior (GCB) refers to the extent to
which people consider environmental values and sus -
tainability in their buying and consumption behaviors
[6, 7]. In an age of sustainability marked by the con -
sumer demand for products and services being driven
by how they are brought to market, DFL has emerged
as a significant determinant. DFL empowers individu -
als with the ability to use digital tools to obtain sustain -
ability information, critique product claims, and make
informed decisions to reduce environmental impacts [8,
21]. Research findings also show that high-DLF consum -
ers are more likely to exhibit eco-friendly consumption
behavior (i.e., green buying, pro-environmental firm sup -
port, and unfriendly patronizing decisions) [3, 22]. And
there is more: digital platforms make it simple for such
consumers to research their ecological impact, follow
sustainable brands, and utilize digital payments that min-
imize paper waste and carbon emissions [23]. Besides,
DFL can promote consumer trust in green products by
enabling them to see trustworthy certificates and sustain-
ability signals online.
Additionally, financial literacy in combination with dig-
ital competence increases the perception of responsibility
toward the environment in consumer habits [11]. People
with higher levels of digital and financial literacy can bet -
ter manage their purchasing power in line with strate -
gic sustainability goals, thereby directly benefiting green
consumption. These actions drive progress toward SDG
12 and SDG 13, which focus on sustainable consumption
patterns and curbing environmental degradation.
This is particularly true in developing countries like
Sierra Leone. Mobile and online marketplaces are open -
ing up potential for sustainable consumer commerce.
But limited knowledge of digital and financial risks
keeps green purchasing uptake limited. Thus, building
DFL in cities could help to enable consumers to engage
proactively toward sustainable consumption, while also
directly contributing to national climate resilience and
responsible growth goals.
H2  Digital financial literacy positively impacts green
consumer behavior.
Environmental concern and sustainable financial behavior
Environmental concern (EC) represents individuals’
attitude toward environmental issues and the perceived
responsibility to protect the environment [6, 7]. EC has
been consistently found to be a significant psychologi -
cal antecedent of pro-environmental behaviors, attitudes
toward sustainability, ethical consumption, and responsi-
ble resource use [9]. In the age of sustainable economies,
EC not only influences peoples’ lifestyles and consumer
behavior but also permeates into the finance domain with
what has now become known as SFB [3, 11]. Evidence
shows that people with stronger environmental concern
are more likely to invest in green funds, support eco-
friendly businesses, and avoid any financial products that
damage the environment [12, 24, 25].
For example, [26] indicates that clients with enhanced
environmental consciousness are more inclined to
engage with green investment schemes, thereby estab -
lishing a link between environmental awareness and
healthy financial conduct. Similarly, [27] found that green
orientation affects people’s sustainable financial behavior
when ethical and environmental awareness is strong. The
relationship underscores the importance of EC in harmo-
nizing financial practices with the sustainability objec -
tives of SDGs 8 and 12. In urban Sierra Leone, financial
inclusion is on the rise, as is environmental awareness:
Concern for the environment could very well be the key
to helping people make good financial choices. Use or
support green banks, or invest in sustainable enterprises.
Such behavioral adjustments are crucial to comprehend
that financial inclusion is not only good for human well-
being but also for the sustainability of our planet.
H3  Environmental concern positively impacts sustain -
able financial behavior.
Environmental concern and green consumer behavior
Based on this discussion, green consumption obvi -
ously is a result of the environmental consciousness
that is reflected in people’s values and beliefs, thereby
translating such individual values and beliefs into envi -
ronmentally friendly buying behavior [7, 8]. Consum -
ers are developing a greater environmental awareness,
out of which grows their preference for products that
save on energy and can be reclaimed and recycled, as
well as environmentally sound manufactured products
[6, 28]. Environmental concern then serves as a motiva -
tion that connects environmental knowledge to actual
consumption behavior. There is empirical evidence of
a positive link between EC and GCB from several stud -
ies. For example, [9] reveals that consumers with high EC

are more inclined to purchase environmentally friendly
products and services. Similarly, [25] and [29] demon -
strate that the more EC encourages consumers to be
responsible from an environmental point of view (thereby
increasing their sense of perceived responsibility for the
environment), the greater their intention to buy eco-
friendly products. Besides, the study by [30] shows that
powerful EC can affect not just what individuals consume
but also action in concert to reduce carbon emissions and
support green industries. Although people are increas -
ingly aware of sustainability, the impact of EC on actual
green buying behavior differs across settings. In under -
developed nations like Sierra Leone, several constraints,
such as high prices, a shortage of green products, and low
digital awareness, diminish this link. However, as envi -
ronmental education improves and sustainable financial
systems develop, ECIs are expected to become increas -
ingly important for responsible consumer decisions [21,
31]. This link aligns with SDG 12 and SDG 13, placing
environmental knowledge at the heart of a sustainable
lifestyle. Furthermore, in Sierra Leone’s metropolitan
regions, where internet access and consumer activism
are gradually gaining momentum, promoting EC through
education, policy advocacy, and digital outreach would
go a long way toward increasing green consumer involve-
ment and environmental accountability.
H4  Environmental concern positively impacts green
consumer behavior.
The mediating role of sustainable financial behavior
The extant literature provides evidence that these rela -
tionships (i.e., DFL → GCB and EC → GCB) can be
effectively mediated by SFB. SFB is the group of socially
responsible, ethical, and environmentally attuned finan -
cial activities that capture an individual’s willingness to
sustain over time [11]. It embraces the idea of conscious
saving, responsible spending, ethical investment, and pri-
oritizing financial decisions that contribute to social and
environmental well-being [3, 12]. In a financial sense,
people with a higher level of DFL are more likely to
transfer financial knowledge into a pattern of sustainable
financial use. While actively using online resources and
tools, these individuals can rationalize resource use more
effectively, choose environmentally friendly products,
and conduct cashless or paperless transactions, thereby
reducing their individual footprints [16, 32]. This shift
in attitudes indicates that DFL not only enhances finan -
cial capabilities but also promotes sustainable practices,
thereby indirectly influencing GCB. In other words, SFB
serves as a broker, translating digital financial literacy
into tangible GCB, a critical step toward achieving SDG
4, SDG 8, and SDG 12. From an environmental stand -
point, EC also inspires people to adopt SFB by linking
ecological values to monetary choices. Environmentally
conscious consumers prefer ethical practices that do not
harm the planet, such as supporting sustainable busi -
nesses, reducing unnecessary consumption, and invest -
ing in renewable energy projects [7, 27].
This growing consciousness is guiding people to make
financial decisions as part of their broader environmen -
tal responsibility. For instance, [26] shows that EC is a
critical factor affecting the adoption of green invest -
ment and sustainable banking. EC prompts consumers
to adopt financially responsible decision-making and to
choose environmentally friendly goods and services that
support environmentally friendly behavior. Further, as
digital finance increasingly becomes an intrinsic part of
a consumer’s life, the confluence of EC and SFB is mov -
ing toward integrated sustainability. Consumers who
integrate environmental values into their monetary value
systems are more likely to translate green intentions
into action, thereby bridging the gap between attitude
and behavior [31, 33]. Therefore, our findings provide
evidence that SFB mediates their combined influence
in facilitating the behavioral mechanisms that enhance
GCB—translating awareness and values into uniform
pro-environmental behavior. Especially in African coun -
tries, where digital literacy and environmental aware -
ness are on the rise and sustainable consumption is an
untapped arena, the potential value of fostering SFB can -
not be underestimated when it comes to changing con -
sumer behavior. Digital financial education, combined
with sustainable feedback mechanisms, can enable con -
sumers to turn knowledge into measurable habits that
drive both personal financial stability and environmental
protection.

The moderating role of perceived risk
Although DFL and EC are hypothesized to improve GCB,
these relationships may weaken when moderated by PR,
given the significant role risk perceptions play in human
decision-making. PR denotes a consumer’s subjective
assessment of possible costs that he attributes to a prod -
uct, service, or monetary transaction. In the sustainability
and digital financial themes, perceived risk can manifest

as monetary loss, product ineffectiveness, data insecurity,
or uncertainty about whether a company’s claims about
its environmental product are true [14, 19]. Despite that,
DFL is expected to increase consumers knowledge to
make informed and sustainable decisions, but it may not
be conducive for GCB, especially when consumers per -
ceive financial or technological risk [20, 34]. Even though
informed customers have a preference for green technol -
ogy products or digital payments, they will not go for it
if the privacy, security, performance, and legitimacy of
such technologically savvy practices are doubtful [13].
There could be psychological barriers stemming from
perceived high risks associated with online transactions,
which lower customers’ propensity to engage in green
financial behaviors such as digital green investment and
purchasing sustainable products through e-commerce
[26, 32]. This moderating effect is empirically verified.
The literature suggests that the high levels of DFL but
perceived risk may lead to lower adoption of digital green
financial services [35, 36]. On the other hand, when per -
ceived risk is low, DFL can effectively turn into environ -
mentally friendly behavior by placing greater reliance on
and confidence in digital systems. This process indicates
that perceived risk does not cancel out or invalidate the
positive effects of DFL but rather governs the extent to
which digital knowledge translates into practical, sustain-
able conduct. In Africa, where digital financial infrastruc-
ture is nascent and consumer protection measures are
weak, perceived risk remains a critical influence on finan-
cial and consumption decisions. Policy measures and
consumer education aimed at addressing such risk per -
ceptions may increase the effectiveness of DFL in facili -
tating sustainable purchase behaviors. In the same vein,
perceived risk could also attenuate the impact of EC on
GCB. Consumers who care about the environment are
more likely to prefer green products; doubts about qual -
ity, veracity, and cost reduce their intention to use a green
alternative [28]. Consumers may want to know whether
an eco-friendly product actually offers environmental
benefits or whether sustainability claims are overstated, a
concern that has received popular attention due to some
greenwashing behaviors [14, 37]. Additional research
reveals that, in the face of a high perceived risk of finan -
cial or performance loss, consumers tend to revert to
conventional buying behavior regardless of their envi -
ronmental values [25]. For example, [29] also found
that young people who are committed to the environ -
ment sometimes avoid buying green products because
they fear the products may be less credible or unavail -
able. These types of risk perception constitute cogni -
tive obstacles that prevent environmental concern from
fully translating into sustainable consumer behavior [38].
Urban Sierra Leone is used as an example of such a con -
text, where environmental knowledge is increasing, yet
consumer confidence in environmental markets is weak,
and where risk perceptions could potentially impede the
transition from latent concern to behavioral outcomes.
Consumers might be less willing to invest in or purchase
green products if they are perceived as more financially
uncertain and less reliable. Mitigating these perceived
risks through established consumer assurance mecha -
nisms, certification standards, and transparency would
therefore serve as a boundary object that reinforces the
EC‐GCB nexus as discussed earlier.

Building on the preceding literature review, this
study develops an integrated conceptual framework to
explain the antecedents of GCB in urban Sierra Leone.
As depicted in Fig.  1, the framework positions DFL
and EC as key distal drivers of GCB, both directly and
indirectly through SFB. Specifically, the model tests
four direct paths (H1–H4), two mediation pathways
(H5a, H5b), and two moderation pathways (H6a, H6b)
involving perceived risk (PR). This framework extends
existing sustainability behavior models by incorporat -
ing digital literacy as a novel antecedent and by exam -
ining the mediating mechanism of sustainable financial
behavior—a critical yet underexplored construct in the
context of developing economies.
Methodology
Research design
This study adopts a quantitative research design using
a cross-sectional survey approach to examine the rela -
tionships between digital financial literacy, environ -
mental concern, sustainable financial behavior, green
consumer behavior, and the moderating role of per -
ceived risk in urban Sierra Leone. The cross-sectional
design is appropriate, as it allows for the collection
of data at a single point in time to test the hypoth -
esized relationships [39]. A positivist epistemological
approach underpins this research, assuming that social
phenomena can be measured objectively through struc -
tured instruments, enabling statistical generalization to
the broader population of consumers in Freetown.

Population and sampling
The target population comprises adult consumers (aged
18  years and above) residing in Freetown, Sierra Leone,
who have access to and actively use digital financial ser -
vices (e.g., mobile money, internet banking, fintech appli-
cations). Freetown was purposively selected as the study
setting due to its status as the economic and digital hub
of Sierra Leone, where financial inclusion initiatives and
digital infrastructure are most concentrated [40].
A multistage sampling technique was employed to
ensure representativeness and minimize selection bias.
First, Freetown was stratified into three main admin -
istrative zones—East End, Central, and West End—to
capture diverse socioeconomic characteristics. Second,
quota sampling was applied based on gender, age groups
(18–30, 31–45, 46–60, and 60 + years), and educational
attainment to align with Sierra Leone’s urban population
profile. Third, within each stratum, systematic random
sampling was used, in which every nth household or eli -
gible respondent encountered in high-footfall areas such
as markets, banking halls, mobile money agent points,
and community centers was approached. This multistage
approach ensured demographic representativeness while
reducing selection bias in the sampling process.
This study targeted 600 respondents and was deter -
mined based on three criteria. First, it exceeds the PLS-
SEM rule of thumb requiring a sample at least ten times
the maximum number of paths directed at any construct
[41], with four paths targeting GCB, the minimum is
40. Second, an a priori power analysis [42] for multi -
ple regression indicated a minimum of 138 respond -
ents. Third, the inclusion of mediation and moderation
effects warrants a larger sample to ensure stable esti -
mates and adequate power to detect smaller effects [43].
Data collection yielded 500 usable responses from 600
distributed questionnaires (83% response rate), satisfy -
ing all criteria. A structured questionnaire was devel -
oped comprising six sections corresponding to the study
constructs, along with demographic questions. The
questionnaire was administered in English, the official
language of Sierra Leone; however, for respondents with
limited English proficiency, trained enumerators pro -
vided clarifications in Krio (the lingua franca) to ensure
comprehension.
Data collection occurred over eight weeks (April–May
2025). The following steps were undertaken: First, pilot
testing with 30 respondents in Freetown (not included in
the main sample) to assess clarity, cultural appropriate -
ness, and reliability. Ambiguous or culturally incongru -
ent items were refined iteratively until comprehension
and face validity were confirmed. Second, respondents
were approached at strategic locations, including Mobile
money agent points (e.g., Orange Money, Afrimoney
kiosks); Banking halls (Rokel Commercial Bank, Union
Trust Bank, etc.); major markets (e.g., Big Market, Aber -
deen Market); University campuses (e.g., Fourah Bay Col-
lege, IPAM, Njala University urban campus, etc.); and
Community centers and business districts. Third, for eth-
ical considerations, informed consent was obtained from
all participants. Respondents were assured of anonym -
ity and confidentiality, and participation was voluntary;
respondents could withdraw at any time.

including purchasing eco-friendly products, support -
ing sustainable brands, reducing waste, and minimizing
ecological footprints. As sustainability becomes a global
priority, consumers are increasingly shifting toward envi-
ronmentally responsible products and services. Sample
item: “I prefer to buy environmentally friendly or eco-
labeled products. ” Perceived risk (PR) was measured
using a scale adapted from research on consumer risk
perception and financial decision-making [10, 13, 19].
Perceived risk is the subjective evaluation of potential
negative outcomes associated with financial or consump -

product failure, privacy breaches, fraud, or misleading
information, such as greenwashing. Sample item, ‘‘I am

cial services’’ .
Analytical approach
This study employed partial least squares structural equa-
tion modeling (PLS-SEM) using SmartPLS 4.0 to test the
hypothesized relationships. Prior to model estimation,
preliminary data screening assessed distributional prop -
erties through skewness and kurtosis statistics. Absolute
skewness values ranged from 0.18 to 0.87 and kurtosis
from − 0.42 to 1.35 across all items—well within accept -
able thresholds (skewness < 2; kurtosis < 7;) [41]. This
distributional profile further justified for the selection
of PLS-SEM over covariance-based SEM (CB-SEM) for
methodological reasons aligned with the study’s objec -
tives. First, PLS-SEM is suited for complex models incor -
porating mediation and moderation effects, as it provides
robust path coefficient estimates and facilitates boot -
strapping procedures for indirect and interaction effects.
Second, the research adopts a prediction-oriented rather
than purely confirmatory orientation; PLS-SEM prior -
itizes maximizing explained variance (R 2) and predic -
tive relevance (Q 2), which aligns with the study’s aim to
identify actionable drivers of GCB [44]. Finally, PLS-SEM
accommodates formative measurement models without
identification constraints, providing greater measure -
ment flexibility should constructs require formative spec-
ification in future iterations.
Data analysis followed a two-stage PLS-SEM pro -
tocol [41]. Stage 1 evaluated the measurement model:
internal consistency was confirmed via composite reli -
ability (> 0.70); convergent validity via average variance
extracted (> 0.50); and discriminant validity via the For -
nell–Larcker criterion and HTMT ratios (< 0.85). Stage
2 assessed the structural model using bootstrapping
(5,000 resamples) to estimate path coefficients, indirect
effects, and interaction terms with bias-corrected 95%

Common method bias (CMB)
Common method bias was rigorously assessed using
three complementary techniques to safeguard construct
validity in this single-source survey design. Procedur -
ally, anonymity assurances, temporal separation of item
blocks, and reverse-coded items minimized CMB dur -
ing data collection. Statistically, Harman’s single-factor
test revealed that a single unrotated factor accounted for
38.7% of total variance—well below the 50% threshold—
indicating no pervasive CMB [49]. Finally, Kock (2015)
[50] conducted a full collinearity VIF assessment—imple-
mented in SmartPLS by estimating a model with all
constructs specified as both reflective and formative—
yielding indicator-level VIFs ranging from 1.42 to 2.87,
all below the critical 3.3 threshold for CMB detection.

Hypothesis testing
Direct effects
The direct effect hypotheses were tested through boot -
strapping procedures with 5000 resamples, generating
path coefficients (β), t-statistics, p-values, and 95% con -
fidence intervals [52]. Table  7 presents the results of the
direct effects analysis. Hypothesis 1, which posited that
digital financial literacy positively impacts GCB, was
strongly supported. The path coefficient was positive and
significant (β = 0.517, t = 11.144, p < 0.001), indicating
that higher levels of digital financial literacy are associ -
ated with increased green consumer behavior among
urban consumers in Freetown. Hypothesis 2, proposing
that digital financial literacy positively impacts sustain -
able financial behavior, was also supported. The relation -
ship was positive and significant (β = 0.296, t = 8.397,
p < 0.001), confirming that digitally literate individuals
are more likely to engage in financially sustainable prac -
tices. Hypothesis 3, which predicted that environmental
concern positively impacts sustainable financial behav -
ior, received strong empirical support. The path coeffi -
cient was positive and substantial (β = 0.561, t = 15.714,
p < 0.001), representing the model’s strongest direct effect
and highlighting the pivotal role of environmental aware-
ness in shaping sustainable financial decisions. Hypoth -
esis 4, asserting that environmental concern positively
impacts GCB, was supported, albeit with a modest effect
size. The relationship was positive and statistically sig -
nificant (β = 0.124, t = 2.527, p = 0.012), confirming that
environmentally concerned consumers are more likely to
engage in green purchasing behaviors, though this effect
is considerably weaker than the influence of digital finan -
cial literacy. Figure  2 illustrates the results of the path
analysis.
Mediation effects
Mediation was examined using bootstrapped confidence
intervals for indirect effects, following contemporary
mediation analysis guidelines [46, 47]. Table  8 presents
the mediation analysis results. Hypothesis 5a, which
proposed that sustainable financial behavior mediates the
relationship between digital financial literacy and green
consumer behavior, was supported. The indirect effect
of DFL on GCB through SFB was positive and signifi -
cant (β = 0.050, t = 3.017, p = 0.003), with the confidence
interval excluding zero. Following Zhao et  al., (2010)
[47] typology, this represents complementary media -
tion, suggesting that while SFB serves as a mechanism
through which DFL influences GCB, a significant direct
effect remains. Hypothesis 5b, positing that sustain -
able financial behavior mediates the relationship between
environmental concern and green consumer behavior,
received strong support. The indirect effect of EC on
GCB through SFB was positive and significant (β = 0.094,
t = 3.172, p = 0.002). This also represents complemen -
tary mediation. Notably, the indirect effect accounts for
a substantial portion of the total effect, underscoring the
importance of SFB as a critical mechanism translating
environmental values into concrete green consumption
practices. Contemporary guidelines emphasize assess -
ing the significance of the indirect effect over traditional
approaches such as the Sobel test or variance accounted
for (VAF).

Discussions
The results of this study offer robust empirical support
for the direct-effect hypotheses. Hypothesis 1 was sup -
ported, indicating that digital financial literacy (DFL)
directly and positively affects green consumer behav -
ior (GCB) among urban consumers in Freetown. The
results show a strong positive relationship between DFL
and GCB, with β = 0.517, p < 0.001. As digital-era plat -
forms that provide access to financial and environmental
information become more prominent, digitally literate
consumers can more effectively compare prices, scruti -
nize eco-friendly product alternatives, and evaluate their
purchasing decisions from an environmental perspec -
tive. This aligns with the proposition that digital literacy
enables consumers to make informed and sustainable
choices, particularly in financial and consumption deci -

tal literacy facilitates green consumption via cognitive
and psychological pathways, including environmental
awareness and perceived control. These findings are also
consistent with previous empirical research that has iden-
tified financial literacy as a strong driver of SFB [11]. DFL
not only enhances financial decision-making but also
encourages responsible spending, long-term planning,
and investment in eco-friendly products and services.
DFL is a crucial aspect of bridging financial inclusion
gaps while advancing sustainability, especially in devel -
oping countries where digital financial instruments and
services such as mobile money are gaining momentum
[15]. The findings further confirm Hypothesis 2, which
posits that DFL has a positive effect on SFB. This positive
and significant relationship (β = 0.296, p < 0.001) suggests
that those who can use financial information technology
efficiently tend to engage in sustainable decision-making
processes, such as effective saving, budgeting, respon -
sible investing, and reducing excessive consumption,
through their use of banking tech platforms. This result
is consistent with the literature on the impact of finan -
cial literacy on long-term financially responsible behavior
[16]. Consumers with a high level of digital literacy tend

In developing economies, the interplay between DFL
and SFB is particularly critical, as financial instability and
low levels of financial literacy hinder sustainable develop-

literate consumers are more confident when navigating

concern (EC) positively affects SFB (β = 0.561, p < 0.001).
This result emphasizes the importance of environmen -
tal awareness in influencing financial choices. Given
the significant role of EC in this process, we posit that
those who are more cognizant of environmental issues
are predisposed to consider sustainability, as reflected in
their monetary choices. This may lead to cutting back on
wasteful purchases, buying eco-friendly products, and
patronizing companies in the green economy. This result
is in accordance with previous research indicating that
EC is a significant predictor of SFB [6, 7].
This finding also lends credence to the notion that envi-
ronmental values affect not only consumption but also
financial decisions. According to Rahman et  al. (2025)
[33], by increasing environmental literacy, individuals are
more inclined to adopt SFB. In the same way, [53] high -
light that green financial literacy enhances the overall
sustainable financial performance and behavior. Evidence
of increasing public awareness and SFB in Sierra Leone
may reflect growing environmental challenges, such as
climate change, deforestation, and urban waste manage -
ment. The study concluded that environmental educa -
tion and awareness campaigns can substantially influence
financial decision-making and promote sustainable
development. The findings also support Hypothesis 4,
which posits that EC positively impacts GCB (β = 0.124,
p = 0.012). Despite these statistically significant results,
the variables explain only a small share of the variation
in green consumption; these findings indicate that EC
alone is insufficient to stimulate greater green consump -
tion among urban consumers. This result aligns with
prior studies, which show that although EC affects green
buying behavior, it tends not to be significant on its own
because it is shaped by socioeconomic factors such as
income, affordability, and perceived risk [9]. Consumers
in developing economies may be environmentally con -
scious but unable to act on their concerns due to cost or
access constraints when it comes to green products. In
Freetown, higher costs and limited availability of green
products may moderate consumer behavior despite
concerns about environmental issues. The mediation
analysis shows how DFL and EC influence GCB through
SFB.
The findings support Hypothesis 5a and provide suf -
ficient evidence that SFB operates as a partial mediator
on the relationship between DFL and GCB (β = 0.050,
p = 0.003). This complementary mediation suggests that
individuals with digital literacy tend to adopt sustainable
financial habits such as budgeting, prudent spending, and
long-term planning more frequently than their counter -
parts and are therefore more likely to engage in green
consumption. This finding is consistent with previous
research that emphasizes that financial literacy promotes
sustainable behavior by strengthening financial disci -
pline, future orientation, and resource management [11].
In addition, mobile money and fintech platforms are digi-
tal financial tools that reduce transaction costs, increase
control over finances, and improve access to pertinent
information [1, 15], making it easier for responsible pur -
chases or long-term financial decisions. The small share
of variance explained (8.8%) indicates that this is not the
only pathway by which DFL drives GCB; other pathways,
such as awareness of local environmental conditions and
digital access to sustainability-related knowledge, also
play a role [8].
Hypothesis 5b is also strongly supported. SFB signifi -
cantly mediates the relationship between EC and GCB,
with a relatively large mediation effect (VAF = 43.1%).
This insight sheds light on the kind of sustainable con -
sumption facilitated by the translation of environmen -
tal values. Caring about the environment alone does
not necessarily lead to greener buying decisions. What
matters is whether that concern translates into tangi -
ble financial actions reducing unnecessary discretionary
spending, saving for sustainable purchases, or choosing
pro-environment products when budgets are limited.
An eco-conscious consumer may genuinely want to pur -
chase solar products or reduce their carbon footprint,
but unless they have the economic means to choose
greener options, that concern will never be acted on. This
is where SFB captures that practical, money-management
aspect of sustainability. It embodies the ability to convert
the will to go green into low-cost, similar choices. This
interpretation corroborates the value–action framework
in sustainability research, which suggests that attitudes
lead to behaviors through specific pathways of lifestyle
and financial decision-making [6, 33]. A strong mediation
effect yields an important practical message: Many efforts
to generate environmental awareness are ineffective if
someone lacks the financial means or the willingness
to modify spending habits. This is especially pertinent
in developing economies such as Sierra Leone, where
considerable cost-related and access barriers continue

acts as a mediator in the relationship between DFL and

blend digital literacy with green sensibility and respon -

These findings contribute to two separate fronts. First, we

Third, moderation analysis suggests that PR is not a
significant moderator of the relationships among DFL,
EC, and GCB. While the two interaction effects were
negative, suggesting that with higher PR, both the posi -
tive impacts of DFL and EC on GCB are marginal but
not significantly weakened. Theoretically, this negative
trajectory is consistent with well-established literature,
which asserts that perceived risk tends to hinder both
sustainable consumption by consumers and their use of
digital financial services [10, 13, 14]. Yet no such signifi -
cance is recorded, suggesting that risk perception plays a
very weak boundary-condition role in urban Freetown.
This attenuation can be accounted for by a few contex -
tual mediators. First, increasing experience with mobile
money and digital finance products—bolstered by exten -
sive financial inclusion programming—has thawed urban
customers’ risk aversion [15]. Second, we find that those
with higher DFL have the capability and the skill to rec -
ognize risks and to take steps to reduce their effects,
among others, which very much neutralizes the moder -
ating effect of PR. Third, consumers with environmental
concerns are so ecologically motivated that perceived
risks do not serve as a strong enough deterrent [6, 9]. The
implications are more meaningful for emerging markets.
In rapidly digitalizing, post-conflict economies like Sierra
Leone, continued exposure to systemic uncertainty may
increase baseline risk tolerance—a "risk normalization"
process that undermines traditional determinants of per -
ceived risk. Consumers who have navigated institutional
fragility and informal economic structures may develop
unique risk profiles that depart from those predicted
by theoretical models developed in stable, developed
economies. Furthermore, mobile-dominated finance in
Sierra Leone is organized into an ecosystem of network
operators and informal agent networks, which creates
qualitatively different risk experiences compared with the
traditional bank setting; this dimension of PR may at least
partly account for why aggregate measures do not con -
form to behaviorally relevant judgments. Although the
negative coefficients are theoretically appropriate, in this
context, perceived risk does not significantly moderate
DFL → GCB and EC → GCB associations. This indicates
that interventions designed to increase DFL and other
EC can be implemented with minimal risk, since their
drivers remain strong even under moderate consumer
perceptions of risk.
Conclusion
This study examined the relationships among digital
financial literacy (DFL), environmental concern (EC),
sustainable financial behavior (SFB), perceived risk (PR),
and green consumer behavior (GCB) among urban
consumers in Freetown, Sierra Leone. The researchers
adopted the PLS-SEM model to investigate the findings.
The findings confirm that both DFL and EC significantly
and positively influence GCB. Environmental concern
had the strongest effect on SFB, while DFL had a substan-
tial direct influence on green consumption. SFB partially
mediated the relationships between DFL, EC, and GCB,
underscoring its role as a critical mechanism for trans -
lating financial capability and environmental values into
sustainable consumption practices. Although perceived
risk showed a negative interaction effect, its moderat -
ing role was statistically insignificant, indicating that risk
perceptions do not meaningfully weaken the influence
of digital financial literacy or environmental concern on
green consumer behavior in this context.
Implications
The findings of this study enrich the literature on sus -
tainability and behavioral finance by providing a com -
prehensive framework that links digital financial literacy
and environmental concern within a single guiding theo -
retical model for green consumer behavior in a develop -
ing economy context. First, the results support previous
research that indicates financial literacy has an impact
beyond financial outcomes and plays a major role in sus -
tainable consumption. By showing that DFL both directly
and indirectly impacts GCB, this work contributes to
the emerging conversations surrounding digital sustain -
ability and approaches to expand financial capability as
an antecedent of responsible consumption [15]. Second,
the robustness of SFB as a mediator lends credence to
value–action mechanisms found in sample population
studies within environmental psychology that suggest
pro-environmental attitudes are actualized through life -
style and financial decision-making processes [6, 33].

Consequently, the results extend existing models by
empirically demonstrating SFB as an important behav -

In addition, the non-significant moderation effect of
perceived risk adds precision to theoretical propositions
that hold risk perceptions as a precondition for diminish-
ing pro-environmental behavior. The negative direction
is consistent with risk theory, but the lack of statistical
significance suggests that, in emerging digital contexts,
financial capability and environmental values may have
stable effects regardless of perceived uncertainty. This
adds layers to the current knowledge on boundary condi-
tions associated with sustainability behavior models, par -
ticularly in developing economies.
Practical Implications
The results emphasize the need to incorporate digital
financial literacy (DFL), financial inclusion, and envi -
ronmental education into national sustainability agen -
das. Policymakers in Sierra Leone must ensure that their
DFL initiatives explicitly incorporate sustainability com -
ponents, including responsible spending, awareness of
green investment opportunities, and long-term savings
plans. Financial literacy is known to help with sustain -
able financial and consumption behaviors [5, 16], so by
integrating it into any financial education, environmental
impacts could be enhanced. Financial institutions, fintech
providers, and development partners should leverage
digital platforms to promote accessible green financial
products. For example, mobile apps and USSD menus
can include behavioral nudges such as “Green Savings”
labels, carbon footprint trackers based on transaction
data, and prompts to round up purchases for reforesta -
tion investments. By embedding sustainability cues into
everyday financial interfaces, providers can lower the
cognitive effort required for users to act on their environ-
mental values. Enhancing consumer trust in digital trans-
actions and green products through transparency about
data security and product certification is equally critical.
Crucially, financial empowerment and environmental
awareness campaigns must operate in tandem. Consum -
ers need both the motivation to value sustainability and
the practical knowledge to translate those values into
action. Such integrated approaches directly contribute to
responsible consumption (SDG 12), financial resilience
(SDG 8), and climate action (SDG 13), offering a replica -
ble model for other developing economies.
Limitations and future research directions
The study has limitations despite its contributions. First,
because this study used a cross-sectional design, we can -
not make causal inferences about the links between DFL
and EC, SFB, and GCB. Future studies should employ a
longitudinal or experimental design to better capture
the dynamic causal processes throughout time. Second,
the self-report nature of the measurements can lead to
common-method and social-desirability bias, which is
particularly common in sustainability research. Third, the
study was limited to urban customers in Freetown, which
may limit the generalizability of the results to rural popu-
lations or to other developing economies with different
socioeconomic contexts. Furthermore, Sierra Leone’s
collectivist culture may influence the results, as con -
duct is determined by communal standards and shared
responsibility. Future research should consider additional
moderating variables, such as income level, governmen -
tal support, and the quality of digital infrastructure, to
deepen understanding of the conditions that can fos -
ter green behavior. Cross-country comparative research
could also increase our understanding of the cultural and
institutional implications of modeling sustainability on
financial literacy across countries. Furthermore, quali -
tative or mixed-methods approaches may be useful for
furthering our understanding of how consumers perceive
and integrate digital money with environmental ideals in
everyday decision-making.
