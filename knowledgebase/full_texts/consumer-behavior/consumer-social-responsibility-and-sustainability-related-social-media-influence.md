Introduction: Sustainable purchase intention among Generation Z is shaped by
consumers’ responsibility and exposure to sustainability-related information on
social media. However, the mechanisms linking Consumer Social Responsibility
(CSR) and Sustainability-Related Social Media Inﬂuence (SMIF) with Purchase
Intention (PI) require further examination. Drawing on the Stimulus–Organism–
Response(S–O–R)framework,thisstudyinvestigatesGreenTrust(GT)andGreen
Perceived Value (GPV) as mediating mechanisms.
Methods: A quantitative, cross-sectional design was employed. Data were
collected through an online questionnaire from 347 Generation Z consumers
aged 18–29 years in Chennai, Bengaluru, and Hyderabad using purposive
sampling. Exploratory Factor Analysis and Conﬁrmatory Factor Analysis assessed
the measurement structure, followed by Structural Equation Modeling to
examine hypothesized relationships. Bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000
resamples was used to evaluate indirect effects.
Results: CSR was signiﬁcantly and positively associated with GT and GPV,
whereas SMIF was signiﬁcantly and positively associated with GT but not GPV.
GT was signiﬁcantly and positively associated with PI, while GPV showed a
signiﬁcant negative association with PI. Mediation analysis indicated signiﬁcant
indirect associations between CSR and PI through GT and GPV, whereas indirect
associations between SMIF and PI through these mediators were not statistically
signiﬁcant.
Discussion: The ﬁndings indicate that CSR is associated with purchase intention
through consumers’ evaluations of trust and perceived value. The unexpected
negative association between GPV and PI highlights an empirical relationship
requiring investigation rather than a predetermined causal interpretation. The
studycontributestosustainableconsumerbehaviorresearchbyintegratingCSR,
sustainability-related social-media inﬂuence, GT, and GPV within an S–O–R
framework.

1 Introduction
Sustainable consumption has become an important concern
in consumer research as consumers increasingly encounter
products and marketing claims associated with environmental
responsibility. However, the availability of sustainable products
and environmental information does not necessarily correspond
with purchase intention, making it important to understand
the factors that shape consumers’ evaluations of such products.
Green purchase intention has been examined through several
consumer-level determinants, including environmental concern,
knowledge, perceived value, trust, and other attitudinal factors
(YadavandPathak, 2016 ; Zhuangetal., 2021 ). In this context,
Generation Z represents an important consumer group
because of its extensive engagement with digital and social-
media environments and its exposure to sustainability-related
information (DiPilloetal., 2025 ; Slepianetal., 2024 ). Recent
empirical research has speciﬁcally examined Generation Z’s
green purchasing behavior and identiﬁed Consumer Social
Responsibility and green consumer trust as relevant factors in
sustainable consumption (Borahetal., 2024 ). Similarly, research
among Generation Z has shown that social-media information
sharing is associated with green purchase intention, highlighting
the relevance of digitally mediated sustainability information
in contemporary consumption decisions (SunandXing, 2022 ).
These developments suggest that understanding sustainable
purchasing among Generation Z requires consideration of both
consumers’ own sense of environmental responsibility and
the sustainability-related information they encounter through
socialmedia.
Consumer Social Responsibility and Sustainability-Related
Social Media Inﬂuence may provide diﬀerent routes through
which sustainable-product evaluations are formed. Consumer
Social Responsibility concerns consumers’ recognition of their
responsibilityfortheenvironmentalconsequencesofconsumption,
whereas sustainability-related social-media inﬂuence reﬂects the
informational and social inﬂuence arising from sustainability-
related content encountered through digital platforms. Existing
evidence indicates that Consumer Social Responsibility is
relevant to Generation Z’s green purchasing behavior and is
associatedwithgreenconsumertrust( Borahetal.,2024 ;Ngoetal.,
2025), while research on social-media information sharing
demonstrates its relevance to Generation Z’s green purchase
intention (SunandXing, 2022 ). At the same time, the credibility
of environmental claims remains important because consumers
mayencounteruncertaintyregardingwhethersustainabilityclaims
and environmental performance are reliable. Green Trust has
therefore emerged as an important construct in green consumer
research (Chen, 2010), while Green Perceived Value captures
consumers’ evaluation of the environmental beneﬁts and value
associated with green products (ChenandChang, 2012 ). Recent
social-media research further demonstrates the importance of
Green Trust in explaining consumers’ responses to eco-friendly
products in digitally mediated settings ( WuandLong, 2024 ),
and research among Generation Z has similarly identiﬁed Green
Trust as relevant to green purchase intention (Rahmawatietal.,
2024). These ﬁndings indicate that responsibility and social-media
inﬂuence may not translate directly into purchase intention but
mayoperatethroughconsumers’evaluationsoftrustandperceived
environmentalvalue.
Despitethisgrowingbodyofresearch,therelationshipsamong
these constructs have not been suﬃciently integrated within a
singleframework.ExistingstudieshaveexaminedConsumerSocial
Responsibility, social-media information, Green Trust, Green
Perceived Value, and green purchase intention, but often focus
on particular antecedents or individual relationships rather than
examining the two sources of inﬂuence and the two evaluative
mechanisms simultaneously ( Borahetal., 2024 ; SunandXing,
2022; ChenandChang, 2012 ; WuandLong, 2024 ; Dangetal.,
2025).Accordingly,thepresentstudyexamineswhetherConsumer
Social Responsibility and Sustainability-Related Social Media
Inﬂuence are associated with Generation Z consumers’ Purchase
Intention toward sustainable products and whether Green Trust
and Green Perceived Value function as intervening mechanisms in
these relationships. The study therefore brings together consumer
responsibility and sustainability-related social-media inﬂuence
within an integrated framework and examines their relationships
with two distinct consumer evaluations—Green Trust and Green
Perceived Value—and subsequent Purchase Intention. By doing
so, the study seeks to provide a more focused understanding of
how responsibility and social-media inﬂuence are associated with
sustainablepurchasingamongGenerationZ.
2 Literature review and hypothesis
development
This study draws on the Stimulus–Organism–Response
(S–O–R) framework proposed by MehrabianandRussell (1974)
to explain how antecedent conditions are associated with
consumers’ internal evaluations and subsequent behavioral
responses. Although the original framework primarily emphasized
environmental stimuli and aﬀective states, subsequent consumer
research has extended the organismic stage to include cognitive
andevaluativeprocessessuchastrust,perceivedvalue,satisfaction,
and perceived usefulness ( Vieira, 2013; WuandLong, 2024 ;
HoangandTung,2024 ).
Importantly, the S–O–R framework contributes more than a
structural arrangement of predictors, mediators, and an outcome.
Its theoretical proposition is that antecedent conditions are
associated with a behavioral response through intervening internal
evaluations. Applied to the present study, this implies that
Consumer Social Responsibility and Sustainability-Related Social
Media Inﬂuence are not expected to be associated with Purchase
Intention only as direct antecedents; rather, their relationships
with Purchase Intention are expected to be explained, in
part, through consumers’ organismic evaluations of sustainable
products, represented by Green Trust and Green Perceived
Value, while the model also allows for direct relationships
with Purchase Intention. The framework therefore provides the
theoreticalbasisforexaminingwhetherthesecognitiveevaluations
constitute mechanisms linking the antecedent conditions to
PurchaseIntention.

Consumer Social Responsibility represents an individual-level
and relatively enduring orientation rather than an externally
imposed stimulus. Nevertheless, it is treated in this study as
an antecedent condition within the Stimulus component of an
extended S–O–R framework because the model conceptualizes
both the consumer’s pre-existing responsibility orientation and the
externally encountered social-media stimulus as conditions that
precede and are associated with the organismic evaluation stage.
CSR is not conceptualized as a moderator because the study does
notexaminewhetheritchangesthestrengthofrelationshipsamong
the other constructs; rather, CSR is examined as an antecedent
of the organismic evaluations. This treatment is consistent
with contemporary applications of S–O–R that accommodate
consumer characteristics and internal antecedent conditions
alongside externally generated stimuli ( Chopdaretal., 2022 ;
Maliketal.,2024 ).Forexample, Chopdaretal.(2022) incorporated
consumer characteristics and situational variables within the
stimulus component, while Maliketal. (2024) distinguished
internal stimuli from external stimuli such as online reviews
andeWOM.
In contrast, Sustainability-Related Social Media Inﬂuence
(SMIF) represents an externally encountered informational
and social stimulus arising from sustainability-related content,
recommendations, discussions, and interactions on social media.
Thus, CSR and SMIF diﬀer in their substantive nature, but both
function as antecedent conditions preceding the organismic
stage. Green Trust (GT) and Green Perceived Value (GPV)
constitute the organismic stage, representing consumers’ cognitive
evaluations of the credibility and value of sustainable products,
respectively. Purchase Intention (PI) represents the response
component. Accordingly, the S–O–R framework provides a
process-based explanation of how diﬀerent antecedent conditions
areassociatedwithconsumers’internalevaluationsandsubsequent
Purchase Intention, rather than merely arranging the constructs as
predictors,mediators,andanoutcome.

2.1 Consumer Social Responsibility, Green
Trust and Green Perceived Value
Consumer Social Responsibility reﬂects consumers’
recognition of the social and environmental consequences
of their consumption choices and their responsibility for
these consequences ( Schlaileetal., 2018 ; Woldeyohanis, 2025;
CaruanaandChatzidakis, 2014 ; Quazietal., 2016 ). In sustainable
consumption, consumers who recognize these responsibilities
may pay closer attention to whether sustainable products
and the organizations oﬀering them are consistent with their
environmental commitments. This attention may lead them to
evaluate the credibility and reliability of environmental claims
and product performance, which may contribute to conﬁdence
in sustainable products. Consumer Social Responsibility may
therefore be positively associated with Green Trust. Empirical
research among Generation Z also identiﬁes Consumer Social
Responsibility and Green Consumer Trust as relevant to green
consumption(Borahetal.,2024 ).Therefore:
H1: Consumer Social Responsibility is signiﬁcantly and
positivelyassociatedwithGreenTrust.

2.3 Green trust, green perceived value and
purchase intention
Green Trust represents consumers’ conﬁdence in the
environmental claims, performance, and commitments associated
with green products. When consumers regard these claims and
product performance as credible, uncertainty concerning the
reliability of sustainable products may be reduced, which may
increase their willingness to consider such products in purchase
decisions. Green Trust may therefore be positively associated with
Purchase Intention. This expectation is supported by research
identifying Green Trust as relevant to green purchase intention,
includingamongGenerationZconsumers( Rahmawatietal.,2024 ;
Halimetal.,2025 ;Filipetal.,2025 ).
Green Perceived Value reﬂects consumers’ evaluation of
the environmental beneﬁts and overall value of sustainable
products relative to the sacriﬁces involved in purchasing them.
When consumers perceive that the environmental beneﬁts of
a sustainable product justify the sacriﬁces required to obtain
it, these evaluations may make the product a more acceptable
option within the purchase decision. Green Perceived Value may
thereforebepositivelyassociatedwithPurchaseIntention.Previous
research has reported associations between Green Perceived Value
and green purchase-related outcomes ( JaiswalandKant, 2018 ;
Rivaetal., 2022 ; Cam, 2023; Karetal., 2024 ; Zhaoetal., 2024 ).
Recent evidence also identiﬁes perceived green value as relevant
toGenerationZ’sintentiontopurchaseeco-products( LiandShan,
2025;Filipetal.,2025 ).
Within the S–O–R framework, Green Trust and Green
Perceived Value represent distinct internal evaluations through
which consumers assess the credibility and value of sustainable
products before forming purchase-related responses. Examining
them simultaneously therefore allows their respective associations
withPurchaseIntentiontobeassessedwithinthesamemodel.

This study adopted a quantitative, cross-sectional research
design to examine the relationships among Consumer Social
Responsibility, Sustainability-Related Social Media Inﬂuence,
Green Trust, Green Perceived Value, and Purchase Intention
among Generation Z consumers. The explanatory design was
appropriate because the study sought to test the hypothesized
relationships and determine whether Green Trust and Green
Perceived Value mediate the relationships between the two

antecedent variables and Purchase Intention. The study was
theoretically grounded in the Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–

Sustainability-Related Social Media Inﬂuence representing the

3.2 Sample and data collection
The target population comprised Generation Z consumers
aged 18–29 residing in Chennai, Bengaluru, and Hyderabad
who had exposure to sustainability-related social-media content
and sustainable products. A non-probability purposive sampling
approach was employed to identify respondents meeting these
eligibility criteria. The questionnaire was administered online
and circulated through WhatsApp groups (17.87%), social-media
networks(40.35%),universitynetworks(30.26%),andprofessional
networks (11.53%). The questionnaire link was shared through
these networks to reach eligible Generation Z respondents,
and participation was voluntary. To reduce the possibility of
duplicatesubmissions,thesurveywasconﬁguredtoallowonlyone
response from each email address. No quotas were imposed for
occupation or primary social-media platform. The questionnaire
was distributed approximately equally across the three selected
cities to obtain broadly comparable representation from each
location. A screening process was conducted before the main
questionnairetoensurethatrespondentsmettheeligibilitycriteria.
Speciﬁcally, eligibility was restricted to respondents aged 18–29
yearstoensurethatallparticipantswereadultswithintheintended
Generation Z segment. The screening questions also ensured
that respondents had relevant exposure to sustainable products
and sustainability-related social-media content. Occupation and
primary social-media platform were collected as self-reported
characteristics of the respondents, and no quotas were imposed
for either characteristic. A total of 400 questionnaires were
distributed, and 347 complete and usable responses were retained
for the ﬁnal analysis. Of these, 114 respondents were from
Chennai, 117 from Bengaluru, and 116 from Hyderabad. Sample-
size adequacy was considered with reference to established
methodological guidance for covariance-based structural equation
modeling.BentlerandChou(1987) proposedconsideringtheratio
ofsamplesizetofreeparametersinstructuralmodels,while Jackson
(2003) showed that such ratios should be interpreted alongside
absolute sample size rather than treated as a universal criterion.
Moreover, simulation evidence indicates that SEM sample-size
requirements vary according to model characteristics, including
the number of factors and indicators, factor-loading magnitudes,
andstructuraleﬀects( Wolfetal.,2013 ).Thepresentstudyretained
347 complete and usable responses for a model comprising ﬁve
latent constructs and 16 observed indicators. Taken together, these
methodological considerations provided a basis for considering
the achieved sample appropriate for the planned EFA, CFA,
and covariance-based SEM, while recognizing that SEM sample-
size requirements are model-dependent rather than determined
by a single ﬁxed threshold. Prior to the main survey, a pilot
study involving 126 respondents was conducted to assess the
preliminary reliability, clarity, and contextual suitability of the
questionnaire. The internal consistency of the measurement scales
wasexaminedusingCronbach’salpha,withcoeﬃcientsof0.829for
Consumer Social Responsibility, 0.746 for Sustainability-Related
Social Media Inﬂuence, 0.779 for Green Perceived Value, 0.831
for Green Trust, and 0.914 for Purchase Intention. The results
indicated acceptable preliminary internal consistency across all
constructs. Based on pilot feedback and expert review, minor
wordingreﬁnementsweremadetoimproveclarity,readability,and
suitability for Generation Z respondents. For example, the Future
Purchase Intention item was revised from “I plan on buying eco-
friendlyoptionsgoingforward”to“Iintendtopurchasesustainable
products in the future.” Similarly, the Alternative Choice item
was reﬁned from “I prefer picking eco-friendly items instead of
standard, traditional goods” to “I am willing to choose sustainable
products over conventional alternatives.” These changes involved
only minor wording and presentation reﬁnements and did not
alter the underlying constructs or measurement structure. The
pilot study was used for preliminary instrument assessment and
reﬁnement rather than for establishing the ﬁnal factor structure.
The 126 pilot respondents were excluded from the ﬁnal sample
of 347 respondents used for the EFA, CFA, and structural
modelanalysis.

5 Discussion
The ﬁndings indicate that Consumer Social Responsibility
is positively associated with both Green Trust and Green
Perceived Value. The relationship between Consumer Social
Responsibility and Green Trust was positive and statistically
signiﬁcant (β = 0.264, p < 0.001), supporting H1. This suggests
that consumers who are more conscious of the social and
environmental consequences of their consumption decisions
may place greater conﬁdence in the environmental claims
and commitments associated with sustainable products. This
ﬁnding is consistent with Borahetal. (2024) , who highlighted
the relevance of Consumer Social Responsibility and Green
Consumer Trust in Generation Z’s green consumption. The
positive relationship between Consumer Social Responsibility
and Green Perceived Value ( β = 0.174, p = 0.008) further
suggests that consumers’ sense of responsibility is associated with
how they assess the value of sustainable products. When social
and environmental considerations are important in consumption
decisions, consumers may attach greater value to products that
reﬂecttheseconsiderations.

positively associated with Green Trust (β = 0.130, p = 0.045),
supporting H3. Sustainability-related information encountered
through social media may provide consumers with information
and social cues that help them assess the credibility of sustainable
products and environmental claims. Such sustainability-related
social-media content may increase familiarity with sustainability
issues and provide access to information from diﬀerent sources,
which may be associated with greater conﬁdence in sustainable
products. This ﬁnding is consistent with previous research linking
social-media-based sustainability communication with trust and

Overall, the ﬁndings highlight the distinct roles of Green
Trust and Green Perceived Value within the proposed model.
Green Trust signiﬁcantly mediated the relationship between
Consumer Social Responsibility and Purchase Intention,

relationship, although the indirect eﬀect was negative. In
contrast, neither Green Trust nor Green Perceived Value
signiﬁcantly mediated the relationship between Sustainability-

Taken together, these ﬁndings suggest that the proposed
mediating relationships receive selective empirical support,
with Consumer Social Responsibility showing signiﬁcant indirect
associations with Purchase Intention through both Green
Trust and Green Perceived Value, whereas the corresponding
relationships involving Sustainability-Related Social Media
Inﬂuence were not statistically signiﬁcant. Given the cross-
sectional nature of the study, these relationships should be
understood as associations consistent with the proposed S–

temporalprecedence.

7 Future research
Future studies could further investigate the negative
relationship between Green Perceived Value and Purchase
Intention identiﬁed in the present study to determine whether this
ﬁnding is replicated across diﬀerent samples and contexts. As the
present study focused on Generation Z consumers in Chennai,
Bengaluru, and Hyderabad, future research could examine other
geographical settings and consumer groups to assess the broader
applicability of the ﬁndings. The use of non-probability purposive
sampling and online recruitment may limit the generalisability
of the ﬁndings, particularly because the overrepresentation of
respondents from speciﬁc online communities or networks
