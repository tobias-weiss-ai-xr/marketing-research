ABSTRACT
Objective: To describe the social marketing strategies implemented by the phar-
maceutical industry and their potential impact on consumer behavior and access to
medicines, through a scoping review.
Methods: A scoping review was conducted following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines.
For data collection, scientific articles published between 2006 and 2025 in English and
Spanish were selected using the PCC criteria (Population, Concept, Context), which are
recommended for this type of research. The databases consulted were SciELO, Google
Scholar, and PubMed. Articles published in peer-reviewed academic journals were in-
cluded if their title, abstract, or keywords contained at least one of the descriptors used
in the search equation.
Results: Four categories of analysis emerged: 1) marketing and positioning, 2) cor-
porate social responsibility, communication, and ethics, 3) digital transformation and
innovation, and 4) pharmacy and access to medicines. A critical inflection point was
identified, highlighting the intersection of access to medicines with the role of com-
munity pharmacies, which are implementing strategies to balance social commitments
with commercial objectives.
Conclusions: The pharmaceutical industry’s social marketing strategies can be mul-
tidimensional. They have evolved from traditional corporate positioning to emerging
categories that integrate ethics, digital transformation, and public health innovation.
The current challenge lies in balancing business viability with the population’s health
and social needs.
INTRODUCTION
The pharmaceutical industry is considered a key player within a country’s productive
sector, as it contributes directly to economic development, scientific innovation, and
the preservation of public health [1]. In Colombia, for example, the COVID-19 pandemic
highlighted the major importance of this industry, not only because of its impact on
medicine production, but also because of its important role in logistics and supply
chains, research, and the strengthening of healthcare systems [2,3].

This industry has progressively incorporated social
marketing strategies. Unlike traditional commercial
marketing, which is primarily focused on economic efficiency,
social marketing applied to health can be defined as the use
of marketing techniques aimed at raising public awareness
and promoting changes in people’s (healthy) behaviours.
Social marketing has therefore emerged as a necessary
tool by influencing behaviours that benefit the community
[1]. Organisations within this industry align commercial
objectives with public health goals, moving from product-
centred strategies towards more comprehensive approaches
that include education, prevention, and social responsibility
[4,5,6,7].
Currently, digital misinformation, self-medication,
and the saturation of social media with advertising for
different products have created a gap between traditional
commercial strategies and the actual needs of the
public [5,8]. Companies in the sector have responded
by implementing social marketing and corporate social
responsibility (CSR) strategies in an inclusive, sustainable
manner that is consistent with corporate values, as a means
of rebuilding consumer trust and credibility and promoting
healthy behaviours over the long term [4]. At the community
level, pharmacies play an important role by integrating such
strategies into their business models through an educational
role [9,10].
Current evidence on social marketing within the
pharmaceutical industry is fragmented and varies in terms
of methodology, scope, and quality; few reviews integrate
the industry’s marketing strategies and their impact on
consumer behaviour [11,12]. Therefore, this study aims to
identify the current evidence on social marketing strategies
implemented by the pharmaceutical industry and their
potential impact on consumer behaviour and access to
medicines through a scoping review.
METHODS
This study was conducted as a scoping review, following
the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic
Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews)
guidelines as the main framework for the identification,
analysis, and selection of articles describing social
marketing strategies used by the pharmaceutical industry,
their characteristics, implementation approaches, and
impact on the community. The methodological design was
based on the framework developed in studies such as those
by Sharma et al. and Tricco et al. [13,14].
Scientific articles published between 2006 and 2025
were selected for data collection. The sources were searched
through the following databases: SciELO, Google Scholar,
and PubMed. The searches were conducted between
February and May 2025.
Search strategy
The selection process was conducted in three stages.
First, a search was performed using Boolean operators
and combinations of controlled terms (MeSH/DeCS) and
keywords in English and Spanish. The search equation was
adapted to the syntax of each database while maintaining
semantic equivalence. As an example, the basic search
structure is shown below:
“(Estrategias OR ‘planes estratégicos’ OR ‘strategic
plans’ OR ‘strategic approaches’) AND (‘Marketing social’ OR
‘mercadeo social’ OR ‘social marketing’ OR ‘social advertising’)
AND (‘industria farmacéutica’ OR ‘sector farmacéutico’ OR
‘pharmaceutical industry’ OR ‘pharmaceutical sector’) AND
(Impacto OR ‘efectos’ OR ‘resultados’ OR impact OR ‘effects’
OR ‘outcomes’)” .
Eligibility criteria
Given the scoping review design, the PCC (Population,
Concept, Context) criteria recommended for this type of
research were used. They were defined as follows:
 • Population: Patients, community pharmacy users, and
the general population exposed to marketing by phar-
maceutical companies.
 • Concept: Social marketing strategies, Corporate Social
Responsibility (CSR), impact, communication, ethics,
and associated stakeholders.
 • Context: Pharmaceutical sector, community pharma-
cies, and prevention and health promotion settings.
Research articles published in Spanish or English in
peer-reviewed academic journals were selected. Study
selection was performed by two independent reviewers.
Disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third
reviewer. The process was documented using a PRISMA-ScR
flow diagram (Figure 1). Finally, a descriptive synthesis and
thematic categorisation of the 27 articles were carried out.
Quality assessment: Following recommendations for
scoping reviews, studies were not excluded on the basis
of methodological quality. However, documents without
a verifiable methodology, with incomplete information or
without a clearly defined review process, those published in
languages other than those initially specified, and those that
were not directly relevant to the topic or research question
were excluded. Articles focused on commercial promotion,
clinical aspects, or purely pharmacological issues without a
social component were also excluded.

with an emphasis on the semantic dimension and the use of

Healthcare Marketing and Search Engine
Optimization
In recent years, social marketing has gained
considerable importance as an effective and essential
approach for promoting positive changes within the
population, for example by increasing public awareness and
encouraging changes in people’s behaviour (1). Currently,
the pharmaceutical sector and healthcare institutions
have incorporated this approach as a means of aligning
their commercial objectives with community well-being,
including marketing as part of both strategic and operational
public health strategies (1,16).
According to Siguenza et al. (16), notable strategies
include the adoption of regional mechanisms for joint
procurement of medicines and the establishment of
shared diagnostic laboratories, initiatives that have
improved operational efficiency and equitable access to
essential resources. Positioning should be understood as
being associated with attributes that secure a place in
the consumer’s mind (16,18,19). Digital marketing has
emerged as another key strategy in the pharmaceutical
industry, as it has become a tool that enables not only
greater communication reach but also stronger institutional
positioning (17,28). Nevertheless, these strategies must be
used ethically because, although effective for disseminating
information, they require strict regulation to prevent biased
or misleading messages.
In this regard, the analysis presented in an article
discussing the challenges and opportunities of Islamic
branding is illustrative (18). The concept of Halal, traditionally
understood as what is permitted under the Islamic faith, has
begun to evolve through its incorporation into marketing
strategies, creating opportunities in international markets.
The Halal logo goes beyond the scope of religious certification
and is becoming positioned as a new standard of quality,
safety, and ethical production demanded by consumers. This
may also extend to non-Muslim populations and is partly
attributable to the way brands construct narratives around
risk, suspicion, and spirituality that alter how consumers
interpret products (16,18,19).
Communication, Ethics and Corporate Social
Responsibility
Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR), in turn, plays
a strategic role. Faced with gaps in meeting the needs
of the population, companies can provide opportunities
(20,21), contributing to sustainable economic development,
improving quality of life, and obtaining reputational benefits
(22). When implemented appropriately, this practice can
make a substantial contribution to building trust and loyalty
among users towards the pharmaceutical industry and to
positioning the industry in collaboration with government
institutions (16,18,19).
Nevertheless, important challenges remain, including
practices that conflict with professional ethics, in which
patients’ health may be subordinated to commercial
interests through the promotion of medicines according
to purely commercial criteria and in the presence of clear
conflicts of interest (17,22). In the public perception, this
may contribute to the vague and harmful belief that only
a medicine from a particular pharmaceutical company or
in a specific presentation is effective. In reality, when the
active ingredient, strength, and pharmaceutical form are
equivalent, other brands may provide the same therapeutic
benefit without compromising quality, safety, or efficacy.
This is particularly relevant in resource-limited settings
(17). Transparency is therefore essential to ensure that
prescribing is guided by patients’ needs and that more
affordable alternatives are considered.
This shift not only supports the sustainability of treatment
for patients but also promotes a more transparent doctor–
patient relationship that is free from interests unrelated to
health (17), ensuring that patients receive clear, objective,
and sufficient information to make informed decisions and
thereby protecting their right to health and their autonomy
in the decision-making process (23). Some authors have
identified similarities between strategies used by industries
producing unhealthy products, such as tobacco, alcohol,
ultra-processed foods, and gambling products (25), and
those used by the pharmaceutical industry. Such practices
include manipulation of evidence, the use of narratives
and framing strategies, the creation of advocacy groups,
and policy substitution in collaboration with governmental
agencies (23,25), practices that clearly should not be
replicated.
For marketing, this implies an obligation to create
messages that are understandable to all audiences, based
on principles of distributive justice and accessibility, so
that individuals can make decisions based on accurate
information rather than fear, rumours, or lack of knowledge
(23,25). Beyond a single communication campaign, this
requires genuine advocacy and social support to ensure that
those most in need can access different types of products
required for disease prevention or healthcare without
becoming impoverished (23), while also engaging other
social, economic, and political stakeholders (20).
Intersectoral and interprofessional collaborations make
it possible to develop more comprehensive interventions,
increase public trust, and ensure that communication and
marketing activities are aligned with sustainable, long-term
public health objectives (20,24,26). This highlights that
trust in the healthcare system depends not only on industry
and government, but also on the active participation of
communities and other social stakeholders (27,28), beyond
isolated donations and projects.

Although these ideas and actions may genuinely seek to
improve health, they are also motivated by factors such as
strengthening corporate reputation, attracting and retaining
talent, opening new markets, and ensuring long-term
profitability. This situation illustrates how CSR can become
an opportunity to align commercial interests with genuine
social benefits, provided that transparency is ensured and
population well-being is prioritised (20,24,27), particularly
in vulnerable communities (21,22).
Traditional advertising and public relations are no longer
sufficient. Companies must also manage their relationships
with the political, social, and regulatory environment if they
wish to maintain credibility and a strong market position
(21). It is also important for companies to use public affairs
as a means of strengthening their relationships with external
stakeholders, including governments, non-governmental
organisations (NGOs), the media, customers, suppliers, and
other interested parties, thereby improving their corporate
image (21,24). These alliances help organisations gain
legitimacy, differentiate themselves from competitors, and
establish a coherent corporate narrative. Today, companies
that successfully align their communication messages with
social, regulatory, and environmental values do not merely
sell a product; they also convey trust and credibility, which
are fundamental assets in the current market.
One example is the response to the opioid crisis in the
United States. Researchers have proposed establishing
protocols based on strategies jointly defined with
communities, integrating their voices and needs into
the implementation of evidence-based practices (24,32).
Organisations should not merely design social responsibility
policies from their offices but should develop them
together with social stakeholders, thereby generating
greater legitimacy, credibility, and local engagement in the
communities where they operate (24,28,32).
Innovation and Digital Transformation in Health
Transformation within the sector increasingly depends
on new ways of adapting to change. For example, Industry
4.0 technologies used across different industries provide
new perspectives from a marketing standpoint. Sharma,
Malik, and Sharma (33) conducted a study using Latent
Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), a tool that makes it possible to
analyse how different sectors incorporate new technologies.
The study showed how marketing uses large volumes of
data together with specialised analytical tools to support
evidence-based decision-making. Other researchers (34)
have concluded that emerging technologies such as big
data, artificial intelligence, and the Internet of Things have
become key drivers in transforming organisational decision-
making, as well as the way organisations communicate with
consumers and establish sustainable market positioning
over time.
In addition, “green marketing” is no longer merely
a trend; it has become a strategic necessity. Companies
that successfully connect digital transformation with
sustainable practices are creating something highly
valuable: genuine trust among consumers and investors
(33). This requires reconsidering the marketing approach.
Developing impactful digital campaigns is not sufficient;
organisations must also establish strategic partnerships,
actively educate consumers, and ensure that practices are
aligned with current and future regulations. This represents
a particular challenge in developing countries, where there
remains a substantial gap in understanding how knowledge
is transferred across settings with different economic and
cultural realities (35).
Innovation and digital transformation encourage
a shift from traditional advertising towards integrated
communication strategies that build customer trust.
According to Zbuchea et al. (36), “the pandemic represented
an opportunity for organisational development and the
adoption of formal knowledge management practices” .
Companies that effectively communicated their ability to
adapt appeared more reliable and resilient during the most
critical periods. Furthermore, organisations with strong
foundations in knowledge management were able to respond
more rapidly and flexibly to changing market dynamics.
During periods of disruption, customers may value agility and
proximity as highly as the product itself, ultimately making
these attributes potential brand differentiators (34,36).
Digital tools not only help solve internal problems but can
also become part of the organisation’s value proposition.
This type of marketing demonstrates technological resilience
(33,35).
In this context, it is relevant to link these dynamics
with the search for sustainable and trustworthy alternatives
in sectors such as biotechnology. A specific example is
microalgae, which have demonstrated substantial potential
for producing high-value compounds such as antioxidants,
pigments, vitamins, and polyunsaturated fatty acids used
in the food, pharmaceutical, and environmental industries
(31). Their use not only responds to the demand for safer
and more natural products but also contributes to circular
economy and sustainability objectives because they can
grow using relatively few resources and have applications
in nutrition, bioenergy, and bioremediation.
Finally, the authors also note that “the appropriate use
of digital tools can positively influence the relationship
between laboratories and their patients, improving both
communication and service quality” (28). It is essential

Community Pharmacy and Access to Medicines
There is a social perception that community pharmacies
form part of the first level of healthcare. However, the
healthcare system does not formally include them within
its Primary Health Care policies or Integrated Health
Service Networks. This disconnect may represent a missed
opportunity to expand healthcare coverage and access to
health services, particularly in low-income communities
(12,37). Among the main barriers to their integration is
their economic model, characterised by profitability linked
to retail product sales, which in many cases compromises
their financial sustainability.
As a result of these pressures, many community
pharmacies have chosen to “diversify product sales rather
than offer professional services”, prioritising commercial
strategies driven by marketing or the need to ensure
financial sustainability over health policies or demand for
patient-centred care (9). From a healthcare perspective,
medicine dispensing remains the principal activity, followed
by the management of minor ailments and complementary
pharmaceutical recommendations. However, there are
no national standardised procedural guidelines for the
provision of these services, which hinders their integration
into the healthcare system (9,12) and, in turn, contributes
to a lack of assurance regarding access to non-counterfeit
medicines that meet high quality standards (12,37).
Research such as that conducted by Khalifa et al.
(38) shows that health-system preparedness and global
coordination are essential in exceptional circumstances,
such as the response to the COVID-19 pandemic. This
reinforces the importance of comprehensive and preventive
strategies during periods of crisis (38). Currently, one
of the most important concerns is the growing problem
of antibiotic resistance. Pathogens such as Escherichia
coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae,
Salmonella spp., and Staphylococcus aureus are becoming
increasingly difficult to treat because of the declining
effectiveness of traditional antibiotics (12). Therefore,
potential strategies for overcoming antibiotic resistance,
ranging from the development and innovation of new
medicines and combination therapies (29,30) to improved
surveillance, responsible antibiotic use, and stricter public
policies aimed at limiting indiscriminate use, are essential
as integrated components of the healthcare system.
Medicines such as hydroxychloroquine, ivermectin,
azithromycin, and doxycycline were frequently suggested
for the possible prophylaxis or treatment of COVID-19 in
both outpatients and hospitalised patients (12). Their rapid
establishment in the minds of physicians and patients was
driven more by early communication, traditional media,
and social networks than by conclusive scientific evidence.
Subsequent clinical trials demonstrated potential risks and
adverse effects. From a marketing perspective, this provides
a clear example of how a medicine can rapidly acquire
perceived value and lose it just as quickly, as well as the
fundamental role played by community settings and retail
sellers in generating and communicating information to the
public.
Godman et al. (37) emphasise that the excessive and
inappropriate use of antibiotics not only fuels antimicrobial
resistance but also places additional pressure on healthcare
systems, particularly in lower-resource countries (37,39).
Proposed measures to address this problem include
strengthening the education of healthcare professionals
and the community, implementing hospital antimicrobial
stewardship programmes, and developing an integrated
approach to regulation and incentives that support more
responsible consumption (12,37,39), beginning at these
initial levels of care, namely community pharmacies.
DISCUSSION
As a discussion, this study identified and analysed
marketing strategies used by the pharmaceutical industry
that, according to the studies reviewed, are oriented towards,
or reconsidered as, key strategies aimed at balancing
commercial or business interests with social needs. Although
challenges remain within this industry, substantial progress
has also been made through Corporate Social Responsibility
(CSR) frameworks (7,26,40), digital transformation, and
innovation (33,34,35). These developments help to define
more clearly a pathway towards social benefits, which
should be examined through the lenses of ethics, education,
and communication.
Misinformation, the inappropriate use of new media
and communication paradigms (16,25), particularly on
social networks (23), together with the specific contexts of
populations, their ideologies (18), and cultural diversity, are
factors that need to be addressed in order to prioritise the
most appropriate strategies for supporting end consumers
and, in doing so, ensure the social role played by this
industry. Nevertheless, it should be noted that this review
represents a rapid overview of social marketing strategies
and should be regarded as a starting point for considering
additional elements identified in the review, such as the
role of religion (18), politics (22), governments and other
organisations (1,24), as well as other sectors (20), including
education.

CONCLUSIONS
The study identified several emerging categories resulting
from the analysis. Positioning, for example, emerged as a key
element that organisations should consider in order to secure
a place in the consumer’s mind and achieve greater short-
term impact. Alongside this, Corporate Social Responsibility
(CSR) emerged as a prominent factor, supporting sustainable
development through a business approach that is closer to
the public and to social needs, while promoting stronger
and more synergistic relationships between industry and
consumers. In this particular context, interprofessional and
intersectoral support is required to ensure that business
objectives are aligned with social objectives.
Digital transformation, artificial intelligence and
innovation are also among the key contemporary factors
shaping management, ethics, and marketing strategies. These
developments place the sector in a context characterised
by the rapid growth of health-related discourse, media, and
technologies, which must be supported by ethical practices
and by consideration of the population’s needs, including
economic needs. For this reason, the study concludes by
highlighting the role of community pharmacies, the need
to include them in primary care policies, and, consequently,
the importance of ensuring access not only to medicines
but also to timely and ethical first-line care in order to
safeguard the right to health.
