The Transformation of the Sacred in Popular Culture: Religious Representations in the Context of
Simulation and Liquid Modernity

Abstract:In contemporary societies, popular culture has expanded beyond the spheres of entertainment
and leisure to become a central arena for meaning-making, identity construction, and the transformation
of social values. Within this context, religion and the sacred are no longer confined to institutional, ritual,
or doctrinal frameworks. Instead, they increasingly circulate th rough media, digital platforms, consumer
practices, and aesthetic forms. Popular culture therefore functions not only as a medium through which
religious meanings are communicated but also as a dynamic space in which the sacred is reproduced,
transformed, and recontextualized. This study aims to examine theoretically how religious
representations are transformed in popular culture and how the sacred is redefined under contemporary
cultural conditions. The analysis is guided by two main questions: how the sacred is represented in popular
culture and what kinds of semantic transformations these representations undergo, and to what extent
religious representations circulating through media, digital platforms, and consumer culture correspond
to traditional understandings of the sacred. Methodologically, the study is designed as a theoretical and
conceptual analysis rather than an empirical investigation. No original data collection or systematic field
research is conducted. Instead, examples from media, digital p latforms, and consumer culture are used
as illustrative references to clarify broader cultural tendencies. These examples are not treated as
empirical findings but as analytical tools that support the theoretical discussion. The theoretical
framework is based on Jean Baudrillard’s theory of simulation and Zygmunt Bauman’s concept of liquid
modernity. Baudrillard’s perspective explains how religious symbols and narratives become detached
from their transcendent meanings and circulate as simulacra within popu lar culture. In this process, the
sacred is transformed into a hyperreal form in which signs refer not to an original religious reality but to
other signs, images, and representations. Religion increasingly operates through media spectacle, visual
consumption, and symbolic exchange. As a result, sacred meanings are aestheticized, fragmented, and
rendered consumable. Bauman’s concept of liquid modernity offers a complementary perspective for
understanding how individuals relate to the sacred under conditions  of uncertainty, fluidity, and
individualization. In liquid modern societies, social structures, identities, and value systems become
increasingly unstable, while religious affiliation grows more selective, temporary, and personalized. The
sacred is no longer experienced primarily as a collective and binding reference but as a flexible resource
that individuals adopt, reinterpret, and sometimes abandon according to personal needs and lifestyle
preferences. Bauman describes this condition through metaphors s uch as the “spiritual supermarket,”
emphasizing the integration of religion and spirituality into consumer culture. Particular attention is given
to the rise of spiritual content production and spiritual influencers on digital platforms. The circulation of
Qur’anic verses, prayers, Sufi quotations, meditation practices, and energy -related rituals through
aesthetically curated and algorithmically promoted content illustrates how religious representation
increasingly becomes performance -oriented. Such example s demonstrate how the sacred is
reconstructed at the intersection of media visibility, consumer logic, and individualized spirituality. The
study argues that this transformation has two major consequences. First, the aestheticization and
commodification of  the sacred integrate it into consumer culture, blurring the boundaries between
religious meaning and market value. Second, the continuous redefinition of the sacred according to
individual preferences and cultural trends weakens its capacity to function a s a source of collective
cohesion and normative authority. By combining Baudrillard’s critique of simulation with Bauman’s
analysis of liquid modernity, the study offers a multidimensional framework for understanding the
contemporary transformation of the sacred. It demonstrates that popular culture not only secularizes the
sacred but also reproduces, reshapes, and commodifies it in new forms .
Keywords: Popular culture, religious representation, sacred, simulation, liquid modernity

Introduction
In traditional societies, the sacred was commonly defined through qualities such as transcendence,
immutability, and absoluteness, functioning as a stable and authoritative reference that structured both
collective life and individual meaning. Within religious worldviews, the sacred was embedded in ritual
practices, institutional frameworks, and shared symbolic systems that ensured its continuity and social
binding power. However, with the emergence of modernity  - and more decisively in postmodern social
formations - this conception has undergone a profound transformation. Processes such as secularization,
individualization, and the expansion of consumer culture have significantly altered the social conditions
under which the sacred is produced, experienced, and represented.
In contemporary societies, popular culture has emerged as one of the most influential arenas through
which the sacred is reproduced, circulat ed, and re -signified. Media industries, digital platforms, visual
culture, and consumer practices increasingly mediate religious symbols, narratives, and practices,
relocating them from institutional and ritual contexts into aesthetic, commercial, and ente rtainment-
oriented forms. Within this context, the central research questions guiding this study concern how the
sacred is represented in popular culture and what kinds of semantic shifts these representations entail,
as well as to what extent religious representations encountered in media, digital platforms, cinema, music,
and fashion correspond to traditional understandings of the sacred. Closely related to this inquiry is the
question of how the sacred is secularized, aestheticized, and transformed into a commodity within
contemporary processes of cultural production.
The primary aim of this research is to examine, on a theoretical level, the transformation of religious
symbols and sacred narratives in popular culture. Rather than focusing solely on the c ontent of religious
representations, the study emphasizes the contexts in which these representations are produced,
circulated, and consumed. Accordingly, it seeks to analyze how religion is integrated into popular cultural
forms through processes of aesth eticization, commodification, and individualization, and how these
processes give rise to new configurations of the sacred. This transformation of the sacred not only affects
religion itself but also reshapes social structures, individual identities, and s ystems of values. In this
respect, the study aims to offer a significant contribution to both cultural studies and the sociology of
religion.1
Despite the growing body of literature addressing the relationship between religion and popular
culture, much of the existing scholarship either remains descriptive or relies on a single theoretical
framework. Many studies document the increasing visibility of religious symbols in media and consumer
culture without sufficiently interrogating the broader theoretical i mplications of these developments.
Others privilege either media -centered explanations or individual -centered analyses, thereby offering
partial accounts of the transformation of the sacred. This study addresses this gap by integrating two
influential theo retical approaches  - Jean Baudrillard’s theory of simulation and Zygmunt Bauman’s
concept of liquid modernity  - within a single analytical framework, allowing for a more comprehensive
interpretation that captures both representational mechanisms and shifti ng modes of individual
engagement with the sacred.
Methodologically, the study is designed as a theoretical and conceptual analysis rather than an
empirical investigation. Religious representations in popular culture are not examined through systematic
case studies or data -driven methods; instead, selected examples from media, digital platforms, and

The theoretical framework of the study is grounded in the works of Jean Baudrillard and Zygmunt

once grounded in transcendent meanings and theological referents, become detached from their original

understanding the transformation of the sacred in popular culture. In doing so, it seeks not only to
demonstrate how popular culture reshapes religious meanings but also to evaluate the extent to which
these theoretical approaches illuminat e - or overlook  - key dimensions of contemporary religious
experience.
1. Conceptual Framework
1.1. Definition and Characteristics of Popular Culture
Popular culture refers to the totality of cultural practices, symbols, and products that are widely
produced, circulated, and consumed by large segments of society, particularly within modern and late -
modern social formations. It encompasses cultural forms disseminated through media, music, cinema,
fashion, television, and increasingly digital platforms, all o f which are characterized by their broad
accessibility and rapid circulation. Rather than being limited to a specific aesthetic domain, popular culture
functions as a dynamic field in which meanings, identities, and values are continuously negotiated and
reconfigured.
Raymond Williams emphasizes that the concept of the “popular” historically carries a dual meaning:
it refers both to what is favored by the people and to what is produced by the people themselves. 3 This
duality points to the ambivalent nature of popular culture, which is shaped simultaneously by bottom-up
cultural practices and top -down processes of industrial production. On the one hand, popular culture
emerges from everyday life, reflecting shared experiences, tastes, and symbolic repertoires. On the other
hand, it is systematically produced, packaged, and distributed through cultural industries that operate
according to economic and commercial imperatives. This tension between participation and production
is central to understanding popular culture as both a space of expression and a site of regulation.
Popular culture is often conceptualized in contrast to high culture. While high culture traditionally
includes elite and canonized forms such as classical literature, fine arts, and classical m usic - often
associated with education, refinement, and social privilege  - popular culture is characterized by its
orientation toward mass consumption and everyday accessibility. 4 This distinction does not merely

One of the defining features of popular culture is its close and constitutive relationship with the media.
Television, cinema, advertising, social media, and digi tal content platforms play a central role in the

Another crucial dimension of popular culture is its deep entanglement with consumer culture. Cultural
products circulating within popular culture do not function solely as carriers of meaning; they also acquire
value as commodities within capitalist markets. Jean Baudrillard argues that consumption in
contemporary societies is no longer driven primarily by material need but operates as a symbolic system
through which individuals construct, communicate, and differentiate their identities. 6 From this
perspective, popular cultural products - such as films, music, fashion items, and digital content - serve not
only as sources of entertainment but also as markers of lifesty le, belonging, and social positioning.
Consumption thus becomes a key mechanism through which cultural meanings are internalized and
displayed.
In this sense, popular culture constitutes a significant arena in which symbolic power is exercised and
contested. Cultural meanings are not passively absorbed by audiences but are continuously negotiated
within relations of power shaped by media institutions, market forces, and ideological frameworks.
Douglas Kellner emphasizes that popular culture should be unders tood as a site of ideological struggle,
where dominant meanings coexist with alternative interpretations and oppositional practices. 7 This
perspective underscores that popular culture is neither a neutral reflection of social reality nor merely a
domain of  leisure; rather, it is a contested space in which social norms, identities, and values are
produced, challenged, and transformed.
Taken together, these characteristics position popular culture as a central analytical category for
understanding contemporar y social life. Its close connections with media, consumer capitalism, and
everyday practices make it a crucial context in which the transformation of meaning  - including the
representation and reconfiguration of the sacred  - takes place. Within popular culture, religious symbols
and narratives are increasingly subjected to processes of aestheticization, commodification, and symbolic
rearticulation, rendering this domain particularly significant for analyzing the changing forms of religious
representation in modern societies.

within specific social and cultural contexts. From a sociological perspective, representation functions as a
meaning-making mechanism through which social realities are structured, communicated, and stabilized
by means of culturally shared codes. Stuart Hall emphasizes that representation is inseparable from power

Within cultural studies, representation is conceptualized as a multilayered process that operates
through language, images, symbols, and practices. Meaning is not produced solely through verbal

symbolic and ritual forms to render the sacred intelligible and accessible within the social world.
In this context, religious representatio n refers not only to references to God, the sacred, or the
metaphysical, but also to the cultural processes through which these references are shaped, circulated,
and reinterpreted. Religious representation encompasses the ways in which the sacred becomes visible,
meaningful, and communicable through symbols, rituals, narratives, and practices. Rather than being
fixed or immutable, these representations are historically and culturally situated, acquiring different
meanings as they move across social contexts and temporal frameworks.
For believers, religious representation constitutes the manifestation of the sacred within the worldly
realm. This manifestation is achieved through both material and immaterial forms, allowing the sacred to
be encountered, remembered, and experienced in everyday life. Religious symbols play a central role in
this process. In Islamic contexts, symbols such as the Kalima Tawḥīd, inscriptions of Allah and Muhammad,
mosque silhouettes, or the color green function not only as expressions of faith but also as visual markers
of collective identity and social belonging. 10 These symbols operate as condensed carriers of meaning,
linking individual belief to broader cultural and communal frameworks. However, their meanings are not
static; they are subject to reinterpretation as historical conditions and cultural environments change.
Rituals constitute the performative dimension of religious representation. Practices such as prayer,
fasting, pilgrimage, and religious ceremonies are not merely acts of devotion but structured forms through
which the sacred is enacted and sustained within collective life. Catherine Bell underscores that rituals
organize experience by establishing patterned actions that mediate the relationship between the sacred
and the social. 11 Through repetition and formalization, rituals anchor the sacred in time and space,
enabling its continuous reproduction in collective memory. The representational character of ritual lies in
the fact that the sacred is not encountered direct ly, but through symbolic actions that give it form and
meaning.
Sacred narratives represent another fundamental dimension of religious representation. Texts found
in holy scriptures such as the Qur’an, the Bible, and the Torah provide foundational narrativ es that
structure belief systems, moral norms, historical consciousness, and social order. Wilfred Cantwell Smith
notes that these narratives do not function merely as repositories of doctrinal content; rather, they shape
lived religious experience by offering interpretive frameworks through which individuals and communities
understand the world and their place within it. 12 Each act of reading, recitation, interpretation, or
performance reconstructs these narratives within the context of the present, allowin g them to remain
meaningful across time.

sacred is represented and made socially available. These forms of representation generate a shared

presence in the public sphere is no longer confined to traditional institutional settings but is rearticulated
within new social and cultural arenas. 13 This intersection renders religious representation a dynamic and
contested process, subject to transformation, recontextualization, and reinterpretation. Consequently,
religious representation should be understood not as a static reflection of sacred truth, but as a culturally
mediated and historically evolving practice.
1.3. The Transformation of the Sacred
In classical sociological and anthropological theories, the sacred is commonly defined in opposition to
the profane, that is, the realm of the ordinar y, the everyday, and the secular. One of the most influential
formulations of this distinction is provided by Emile Durkheim, who conceptualizes the sacred as that
which is set apart, forbidden, and surrounded by strong emotions such as reverence, awe, fea r, and
worship.14 For Durkheim, the sacred constitutes the foundational element of religious life, organizing
collective practices and moral boundaries while reinforcing social cohesion. Within this framework, the
sacred in traditional societies is closely associated with transcendence, ritual order, and stability,
functioning as an unquestioned source of authority that structures both cosmological understanding and
social life.
Mircea Eliade further elaborates this understanding by emphasizing the experiential dimension of the
sacred. According to Eliade, the sacred manifests itself through moments of hierophany, instances in
which the divine reveals itself within time and space, thereby breaking the homogeneity of the profane
world.15 These manifestations establish sacred centers, sacred times, and sacred narratives that provide
orientation and meaning for human existence. In traditional religious worldviews, the sacred is thus
inseparable from cosmic order, ritual repetition, and an absolute system of values  grounded in sacred
texts and traditions. It is not merely a belief but an all -encompassing structure that integrates the
individual, society, and the cosmos.
With the advent of modernity, this holistic structure began to erode under the influence of
secularization, rationalization, and differentiation. Scientific rationalism, bureaucratic organization, and
individual autonomy weakened the public authority of religious institutions, leading to the gradual
relocation of religion from the public sphere to the  private domain. During this process, the sacred lost
much of its capacity to function as a collective organi zing principle. However, as Jose  Casanova argues,
secularization does not necessarily entail the disappearance of religion or the sacred; rather, i t involves
their transformation and reconfiguration within new social conditions.16 The sacred, instead of vanishing,
re-emerges in altered forms that are no longer anchored exclusively in institutional religion.
Zygmunt Bauman’s conceptualization of modern ity provides an important perspective for
understanding this shift. In the context of liquid modernity, Bauman introduces the notion of “banal
sacredness” to describe the emergence of new, fragile, and everyday forms of sacralization. 17 As
individuals distance themselves from the binding authority of traditional religious structures, they begin
to generate alternative forms of sacred meaning within the sphere of ordinary life. These forms of
sacredness are no longer grounded in transcend ence or metaphysical depth but are instead shaped by
emotional attachment, nostalgia, identity, and social belonging. National symbols, charismatic public
figures, collective memories, and even consumer objects can become invested with a quasi-sacred status,
reflecting the displacement rather than the elimination of the sacred.

2. The Baudrillard Perspective: Simulation and Hyperreality
2.1. Stages of Simulation and the Representation of Religion
Jean Baudrillard’s theory of simulation was developed  to explain the transformation of reality and
meaning under conditions of late modernity, particularly in societies saturated by media, images, and
signs. According to Baudrillard, contemporary social life is no longer organized around the distinction
between reality and its representation; rather, representations increasingly precede, replace, and
ultimately dissolve the real. In this context, simulation refers to a system of models and signs that no
longer reflect reality but instead generate their own autonomous logic.20 What circulates in such a system
is not the real itself, but representations detached from any stable referent.
Baudrillard conceptualizes this process through four stages of simulation. In the first stage,
representation functions as a faithful reflection of reality. In the second stage, representation distorts or
alters reality while still maintaining a recognizable connection to it. In the third stage, representation
masks the absence of reality, creating the illusion that something re al exists behind the image. Finally, in
the fourth stage, the simulacrum bears no relation to any reality whatsoever and exists as a self-referential
system of signs -producing what Baudrillard calls hyperreality. 21 These stages do not simply describe a
linear historical progression but provide an analytical framework for understanding how meaning is
transformed under conditions of sign proliferation.
When applied to religion, these stages illuminate significant shifts in the representation of the sacred.
In traditional religious contexts, representations of the sacred - such as sacred texts, iconography, or ritual
symbols - largely correspond to the first and second stages of simulation. Although mediated and
symbolically constructed, these representations st ill refer to a transcendent reality and are embedded

operates as a reflection of the sacred but as a simulacrum that bears no necessary relation to transcendent
reality. On the plane of hyperreality, representations of the sacred offer emotionally satisfying but
ultimately superficial experiences that substitute for deeper forms of religious engagement. Simulation,
therefore, signifies not merely the disappearance of reality, but the collapse of stable meaning and the
reinvention of the sacred within a cultural environment saturated by signs, images, and consumption.

3. The Bauman Perspective: Liquid Modernity and the Ambiguity of the Sacred
3.1. Identity and the Crisis of Meaning in Liquid Modernity
Zygmunt Bauman conceptualizes postmodern society as an era of liquid modernity, a social condition
in which the solid structures, stable institutions, and endur ing value systems characteristic of classical
modernity gradually dissolve. 28 In this context, social forms no longer retain their shape long enough to
serve as reliable frameworks for action or identity. Flexibility, uncertainty, and transience become defining
features of social life, profoundly reshaping how individuals relate to institutions, traditions, and systems
of meaning. One of the most significant consequences of this transformation is the destabilization of
identity.
In liquid modernity, identity is no longer understood as a fixed, inherited, or coherent structure.
Instead, it becomes an ongoing project that must be continuously constructed, revised, and presented.
Bauman argues that individuals are compelled to assume responsibility for shaping their own identities in
a social environment that offers few stable reference  points.29 Identity thus turns into something to be
assembled, tested, and re -marketed in accordance with changing social expectations and personal
desires. This condition generates a persistent sense of insecurity, as individuals are deprived of long-term
guarantees of belonging and meaning.
This transformation has far-reaching implications for religious affiliation and the understanding of the
sacred. In contrast to traditional societies, where religious identity was largely inherited and socially
binding, religious belonging in liquid modernity becomes increasingly voluntary, selective, and
fragmented. Individuals no longer rely solely on collective religious frameworks but instead construct
personalized belief systems shaped by lifestyle choices and indiv idual preferences. Religion is detached
from its communal and institutional context and redefined as a resource for individual meaning -making.
Bauman notes that the modern individual simultaneously desires security and rootedness while fearing
long-term co mmitment; this tension results in an eclectic and often superficial engagement with
religion.30
Within this framework, religion functions not only as a source of spiritual orientation but also as an
object of consumption. Religious symbols, rituals, and nar ratives are approached through a “use -and-
dispose” logic, whereby individuals selectively appropriate certain elements while ignoring others.
Spiritual practices are valued insofar as they offer immediate emotional comfort, psychological balance,
or personal fulfillment. As Paul Heelas and Linda Woodhead argue, contemporary spirituality increasingly
prioritizes subjective experience and self -development over doctrinal coherence or communal
obligation.31 Concepts such as yoga, meditation, positive energy, or karma are widely consumed as
spiritual techniques, detached from their original theological and cultural contexts and reframed as tools
for personal well-being.
A similar dynamic can be observed in contemporary Islamic contexts, where symbolic elements of
religious practice are often emphasized over sustained ritual commitment. The circulation of prayer -
themed decorations, motivational religious quotes, or the frequent use of hashtags such as alhamdulillah
on social media exemplifies how religious expression is integrated into everyday self-presentation. In such
practices, the sacred functions less as a binding moral authority and more as a symbolic resource that
enhances personal identity and social visibility. These forms of engagement reflect Bauman’s bro ader
argument that religious meaning in liquid modernity is increasingly shaped by individual choice and
market logic.

Conclusion and Discussion
This study has offered a theoretical evaluation of how representations of the sacred are reconfigured
within popular culture through the conceptual frameworks of Jean Baudrillard’s theory of simulation and
Zygmunt Bauman’s notion of liquid modernity. Rather than presenting empirical findings, the discussion
has shown, at the level of theoretical analysis, that popular culture functions as a transformative arena in
which the sacred is continuously reproduced, recontextualized, and circulated through media,
consumption practices, and individualized forms of expression. In this sense, popular culture does not
simply eliminate or marginalize the sacred; instead, it reshapes it in ways that alter its social function,
symbolic depth, and normative authority. As also suggested by Miller’s analysis of religion in consumer
culture, the sacred becomes increasingly embe dded within patterns of consumption that privilege
accessibility, emotional resonance, and symbolic flexibility over institutional continuity or doctrinal
coherence.50
From Baudrillard’s perspective, the contemporary sacred increasingly operates at the level of
simulation. Religious symbols, narratives, and rituals no longer refer primarily to transcendent realities or
theological frameworks, but to other signs and representations circulating within media systems .51 As a
result, the sacred is transformed into a simulacrum  - an image of sacredness that offers emotional
resonance and aesthetic appeal without requiring engagement with its original religious referent. This
media - driven transformation produces what may be describe d as an “illusion of sacredne ss”, in which
signs of religion function as consumable spectacles rather than sources of existential or moral grounding.
Baudrillard’s approach is particularly effective in explaining how media logics  - visibility, repetition, and
spectacle - detach the sacred from lived religious experience and reinsert it into the economy of signs.
Bauman’s contribution, by contrast, shifts the analytical focus from representation itself to the ways
individuals relate to these representations under conditions of liquid mo dernity. In a social world
characterized by uncertainty, fragmentation, and the erosion of stable identities, the sacred emerges as
a flexible cultural resource that individuals selectively appropriate according to personal needs and
lifestyle choices .52 Religion, in this framework, no longer functions primarily as a binding collective
structure but as a customizable element of identity, shaped by the logic of choice, convenience, and
immediacy. Bauman’s critique of “easily consumable religion” provides a us eful lens for understanding
how spirituality is reframed as personal utility, emphasizing emotional comfort and accessibility rather
than long-term commitment or transcendental orientation.
Taken together, these two theoretical perspectives offer a complementary and multidimensional
interpretation of the contemporary transformation of the sacred. Baudrillard foregrounds the structural
and symbolic consequences of media saturation, while Bauman illuminates the subjective and consumer-
oriented dynamics of religious engagement. At the same time, the analysis points to certain limitations
within these approaches. Baudrillard’s theory, while highly effective in diagnosing the erosion of
referential meaning, tends to underplay the continuing significance of lived religious practices and the
agency of believers. Bauman’s framework, conversely, may overemphasize fluidity and individual choice,

Within this theoretical horizon, the sacred can no longer be understood as a fixed, singular, or
exclusively transcendent entity. Instead, it appears as a dynamic and contested representational domain,

practices. This transformation does not indicate the disappearance of the sacred, but rather its diffusion
into multiple forms - some secularized, some aestheticized, and others commodified. Popular culture thus

amplifying its visibility by embedding it within everyday cultural practices.
