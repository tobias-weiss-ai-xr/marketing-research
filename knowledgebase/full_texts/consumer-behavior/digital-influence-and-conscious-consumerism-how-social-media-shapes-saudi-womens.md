This study tests an integrated model of how social media dependence,
advertising content type, and influencer-related social proof shape conscious
consumer behavior among Saudi women during Ramadan, and examines
whether this relationship is homogeneous across age, education, and income.
A cross-sectional survey of 408 Saudi women was conducted during
Ramadan 1447 AH (2026 CE) using a 29-item validated questionnaire and
non-probability purposive sampling. Data was analyzed using simple linear
regression, PROCESS macro mediation (Model 4), and Kruskal–Wallis tests.
Social media platform reliance was significantly associated with conscious
consumer behavior (R2 = 0.436, β = 0.660, p < .001); authentic personal
content was a stronger predictor of engagement than promotional content
(R2 = 0.488, β = 0.699, p < .001); and social proof cues were more strongly
associated with purchase-relevant trust than direct influencer endorsement
(R2 = 0.417, β = 0.646, p < .001). Content-engagement interaction fully
mediated the path from content type to conscious consumer behavior
(β = 0.371, 95% CI [0.282, 0.463]). No significant differences by age, education,
or income were observed (Kruskal–Wallis, all p > .05, η2 < .01). Implications:
These findings suggest that, within this sample, engagement-driven and
social-proof-driven mechanisms are more strongly associated with conscious
purchase behavior than direct promotional or influencer-led messaging, and
that the Ramadan period may be associated with reduced demographic
differentiation in social media-driven consumer behavior. Contributions: This
study offers an empirically tested integrated framework linking media
dependence, content processing, and social validation mechanisms to
conscious consumer behavior in a Ramadan-specific, Saudi female sample,
and proposes two theoretical propositions for further testing—the “influencer
paradox” and the “Ramadan cultural universality hypothesis.”

1 Introduction
One of the most important topics in modern communication research is the
relationship between reliance on digital media and culturally embedded consumer
behavior. In this study, “digital media” refers broadly to internet-based communication
technologies, while “social media” refers specifically to interactive platforms—such as

Instagram, TikTok, and Snapchat—that allow users to create,
share, and respond to content; the present study focuses
specifically on social media. This intersection is especially
prominent in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) where there
are roughly 35 million social media users and universal internet
access (Saudi Communications and Space Commission, 2024).
Saudi women play a crucial role in this environment: they are
the main buyers in households, the fastest-growing group of
users of digital platforms and the main target market for a
rapidly growing influencer market that produces commercially
and culturally relevant content (Alsalloum and Gainous, 2025;
Berlin et al., 2025).
The temporal focus of the study is the month of Ramadan.
Although Ramadan is a single lunar month rather than an
extended season, it stands simultaneously as a period of
religious devotion, commercial expansion, and communication
event that annually restructures consumer behavior across
Muslim-majority societies worldwide, not the Arab world
alone. Increased spending on food, clothing, gifts, and
charitable donations; heightened advertising investment; and
social media usage reaching its annual peak are all empirically
documented (Al-Dosari and Al-Shuwaikhat, 2025). Influencer
credibility effects have been shown to be larger for Saudi
women than men (Mabkhot et al., 2022). Nevertheless, no
previous study has attempted to systematically analyze within a
single integrated framework the ways in which social media
influences Saudi women Ramadan consumer behavior. These
mechanisms include platform dependence, content type
differentiation, influencer and social proof effects, and
active engagement.
Despite this large and commercially important audience, no
previous study has systematically analyzed, within a single
integrated framework, how social media platform reliance,
advertising content type, and influencer-related social proof
jointly shape conscious consumer behavior among Saudi women
specifically during Ramadan. This represents a clear gap in the
literature on digital consumer behavior in Muslim-majority
markets: prior work has tended to examine these mechanisms in
isolation, and methodological gaps such as incomplete
discriminant-validity testing and limited assessment of common
method bias remain common in this body of research (see
Section 2 for a detailed review). The problem this study
addresses, therefore, is the absence of an empirically validated,
theory-integrated account of how Saudi women’s social media
use translates into conscious purchase behavior during a period
of heightened religious and commercial significance.
Accordingly, the objective of this study is to test an integrated
model of the mechanisms linking social media use to conscious
consumer behavior among Saudi women during Ramadan, and
to assess whether this relationship is homogeneous across age,
education, and income. This objective is addressed through the
following research question and sub-questions: RQ: In what
ways, and through what mechanisms, is social media
dependency associated with conscious consumer behavior
among Saudi women during Ramadan? RQ1: Is social media
platform reliance associated with conscious consumer behavior?
RQ2: Does authentic personal advertising content produce a
stronger association with purchase-related engagement than
direct promotional content? RQ3: Do social proof signals
function as a stronger trust mechanism than direct influencer
recommendation? RQ4: Does this pattern of associations differ
across age, educational level, or income? These questions are
translated into four formal hypotheses in Section 4, following
the literature review and theoretical framework presented in
Sections 2 and 3.
To address these questions, a cross-sectional survey of 408 Saudi
women was conducted during Ramadan 1447 AH (2026 CE) using
a validated 29-item questionnaire, and the data were analyzed using
simple linear regression, PROCESS macro mediation analysis, and
Kruskal–Wallis tests. The findings show that social media
platform reliance, authentic content engagement, and social-
proof-based trust are each significantly associated with conscious
consumer behavior, that content-engagement interaction mediates
the relationship between content type and purchase behavior, and
that these associations do not differ significantly by age,
education, or income. This study contributes an empirically
tested, integrated framework linking media dependence, content
processing, and social validation mechanisms to conscious
consumer behavior in a Ramadan-specific context and proposes
two theoretical propositions—the “influencer paradox” and the
“Ramadan cultural universality hypothesis”—for future testing.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2
reviews the relevant literature; Section 3 presents the theoretical
framework; Section 4 develops the study’s hypotheses and
conceptual model; Section 5 describes the materials and methods;
Section 6 reports the results; and Section 7 discusses the findings,
their theoretical and practical implications, and the
study’s limitations.
2 Literature review
This section reviews prior research relevant to social media
dependence, advertising content type, influencer-related social
proof, and Ramadan consumer behavior, and identifies at least
four gaps in the current literature that this study aims to
address. Firstly, social media constitutes the major means of
informational assistance and entertainment with findings based
on MSDT and dependency theory demonstrating that the
reliance on social media increases with uncertainty and
emotional salience (Jung and Moro, 2014; Loges, 1994). In their
study of 420 Saudi consumers, Al-Marwani (2025) using SEM-
AMOS model proved that social media network positively
impacts product purchase behavior due to informational and
normative reasons. According to Ba-Abdallah and Ansari
(2020), trust is the main factor affecting purchase intentions
among Arabic-speaking Saudi consumers, a finding that informs
Hypothesis 1 below (Section 4). Omori et al. (2026) further
developed the MSDT by proving that increased emotional
involvement leads to higher social media dependence for all
three goals, resulting in a self-reinforcing effect within the
context of emotionally and spiritually charged Ramadan period.
Secondly, related to digital advertisement content, Al-Dosari
and Al-Shuwaikhat (2025) in their survey of 600 Saudi social
media users concluded that interactive and visual advertisements
perform better than text-based ones. In addition, they found
that cultural alignment and influencer trust are the main factors
affecting purchase intentions. Similarly, Alharthi et al. (2026)

found that Ramadan-specific advertisements based on Islamic

Mabkhot et al. (2022) used PLS-SEM analysis for 312 Saudi

attractiveness positively influence purchase intentions with
credibility fully mediating the relationship between influencers
and purchase behavior. Interestingly, females turned out to be
more responsive than males. According to Berlin et al. (2025)
analyzing 512 Saudi women’s responses, the evaluation of
credibility goes in two steps: peripheral cues activation and
social validation. This phenomenon is referred to as “influencer
paradox” in this paper. This two-steps process is based on
theories developed by Fogg (2003) and Tseng and Fogg (1999).
Namely, they suggest that social proof signals serve as credibility
earned through social endorsement.
Thirdly, related to Ramadan consumer behavior, Wilson and
Liu (2011) proved that cultural-religious congruence is a
necessary condition for achieving brand trust in Muslim-
majority markets. Sandikci and Rice (2011) found that
practicing Islam and consumption of goods are mutually
dependent concepts explaining why Ramadan-themed
advertising affects purchase motivation better than non-specific
advertisements. Abosag and Farah (2014) found that brand
equity in Muslim-majority markets is affected by emotional
states formed during Ramadan. Finally, Hassan and Harun
(2016) found that Ramadan marketing in Islamic markets
provides different gratification structures compared to non-
Ramadan period justifying the contextual amplification
hypothesis formulated as H4. Thus, it seems that Ramadan
period acts as not only a time period but a structural factor
affecting all four theoretical frameworks applied in this study.

3 Theoretical framework
This study attempts to fill this knowledge gap by using a
mechanism-differentiated theoretical approach that includes
Media System Dependency Theory (MSDT; Ball-Rokeach and
DeFleur, 1976), the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM; Petty
and Cacioppo, 1986), Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT;
Katz et al., 1973), and the Digital Consumer Behavior
Framework (Ba-Abdallah and Ansari, 2020; Huang and
Benyoucef, 2013). The Digital Consumer Behavior Framework
provides insight into how platforms create an environment that
converts the traditionally individualistic process of purchasing
products to socially mediated behaviors. MSDT offers insight
into the development of platform dependency during the
Ramadan period. ELM considers the role of information type
and source credibility in influencing attitudes. UGT helps
understand consumer motivations underlying the choice of
platform and consumption of content. All four theories work in
tandem, not as separate constructs, to produce complementary
hypotheses, constructs, and predictions that are further
enhanced by Ramadan-specific context. Figure 1 below depicts
this relationship and how integration of the theories leads to
predictions about conscious consumer behavior.
The justification of this integration between four theories is on
three grounds. First, none of the four theories alone addresses the
full causal chain this study investigates: MSDT explains why
platform dependence intensifies during Ramadan but is silent on
how specific content is processed; ELM explains how content is
processed (central versus peripheral route) but is silent on why
platform reliance varies; UGT explains the motivational basis for
platform and content selection but does not specify behavioral
outcomes; and the Digital Consumer Behavior Framework
explains how socially mediated engagement translates into
purchase behavior but does not specify the upstream
psychological mechanisms that produce that engagement. Each
theory therefore supplies a structurally distinct link in the
hypothesized chain from platform dependence (H1) through
content processing (H2) and social validation (H3) to purchase
behavior, rather than four competing explanations of the same
link. Second, dropping any one theory would leave a
corresponding hypothesis theoretically ungrounded: removing
MSDT would leave H1 without a dependency mechanism,
removing ELM would leave H2 without a content-processing
mechanism, removing UGT would leave the motivational basis
for platform selection unstated, and removing the Digital
Consumer Behavior Framework would leave the engagement-to-
purchase link (the CEI mediation result) without a behavioral
framework. Third, this integration is consistent with recent calls
in the consumer behavior literature for theory triangulation
when a phenomenon (here, religiously and culturally
contextualized digital consumption) spans cognitive,
motivational, social, and behavioral levels of explanation that no
single theory was designed to jointly capture, and each theory is
mapped to a specific, non-overlapping hypothesis and
measurement axis (Table 2, Section 4) rather than applied
diffusely across all constructs, which constrains the framework’s
complexity to what the hypothesis structure requires.
There are four strands of prior literature that underlie the
study’s hypotheses. The first is a body of literature on MSDT
indicating that social media dependency increases in periods
characterized by heightened emotional sensitivity and
uncertainty, resulting in a heightened guidance effect on
behavior (Loges, 1994; Omori et al., 2026). The second involves
the use of the ELM to explore how in contexts associated with
influencer marketing, culturally relevant and authentic content
serves as a stimulus for a central route, while peripheral route
marketing produces lower purchase motivation (Jamil et al.,
2024; Park, 2024). The third strand concerns the literature on
social proofing, suggesting that collective social proofing
indicators such as likes, comments, and shares act as earned
credibility cues that have higher influence on purchases than
influencer endorsements (Berlin et al., 2025; Fogg, 2003). The
fourth involves the literature on Islamic consumer behavior that
indicates that the month of Ramadan creates structural
equivalence among media dependency and consumption
behaviors irrespective of individual socioeconomic statuses
(Hassan and Harun, 2016; Wilson and Liu, 2011). Beyond

5 Materials and methods

direction strength and significance of hypothesized relationships
between theoretically defined constructs can be measured
consistently across a sizable geographically dispersed sample thanks
to this design (Creswell and Creswell, 2018). It is consistent with
established practice in Saudi digital consumer research (Al-
Marwani, 2025; Alharthi et al., 2026; Mabkhot et al., 2022).

5.2 Participants and sampling
All Saudi women who are 18 years of age or older regularly use
social media and live in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia during
Ramadan make up the target demographic. Using closed
WhatsApp and Telegram community groups that were
maintained throughout all five administrative regions
participants self-selected for the study using non-probability
purposive sampling. This process is suitable when the goal is
not to generate population-level prevalence estimates but rather
to characterize mechanisms within a theoretically defined
behavioral population (Mabkhot et al., 2022; Saad et al., 2025).
During Ramadan 1447 AH (March–April 2026) the survey
was electronically disseminated throughout five regions using
Google Forms. Sampling characteristics were mirrored against
the General Authority for Statistics (2022) census of female
regional distribution in Saudi Arabia, used here as the reference
population benchmark. The distribution of women by
administrative region in the KSA census is roughly represented

five construct-axis means using independent-samples t-tests. No
significant differences were detected across any axis (all p > .10),

substantially threaten the validity of the findings. The single-

delimitation: findings are interpreted as specific to Saudi women
during Ramadan and are not generalized to Saudi men, other
Muslim-majority populations, or other times of year without
further empirical testing.
5.3 Measurement instrument
Data were collected via a structured Arabic-language
electronic questionnaire comprising six sections: (1) informed
consent and two eligibility screening items; (2) demographic
profile; (3) Social Media Platform Reliance (Axis 1: SMPR; 4
items); (4) Digital Advertising Content Type (Axis 2: DACT; 4
items); (5) Influencer and Digital Interaction (Axis 3: IDI; 4
items); (6) Content-Engagement Interaction (Axis 4: CEI; 4
items) and Conscious Consumer Behavior (Axis 5: CCB; 3
items). Items across all five axes were custom-developed for this
study based on the definitions and mechanisms specified by the
four guiding theories (Section 3), rather than adapted from a
single pre-existing scale; this approach was necessary because no
prior instrument jointly operationalizes platform reliance,

The five-point format adopted here did not appear to constrain
reliability in the present study, as all constructs exceeded the
conventional 0.70 threshold for Cronbach’s alpha and composite
reliability (Table 3). The rationale behind the use of a three-item
scale to measure the CCB is grounded both theoretically and
psychometrically. The underlying concept refers to a highly
specific set of behaviors that fall within its scope, including
informed, deliberate, and identity-congruent purchases;
therefore, there is no need to include any more measures to
capture its content. All three indicators showed standardized
factor loadings above 0.78 with an average variance extracted of
0.51, thus satisfying the criterion for convergent validity
suggested by Fornell and Larcker (1981).
A separate expert judgment was conducted to determine the
content validity of the questionnaire across three fields, namely
digital marketing, consumer behavior, and social media studies.
All 29 questions of the questionnaire were judged independently
by the three experts on a four-point scale. Inter-rater agreement
was measured using Krippendorff’s α coefficient, resulting in
α = 0.84, which reflects good inter-rater agreement (Krippendorff,
2004). Items with a mean score lower than 3.00 or where there

5.4 Common method bias assessment
Common method bias (CMB) was addressed through both
procedural controls and post-hoc statistical evaluation.
Procedurally, full anonymity was maintained throughout data
collection; Likert-scale items were randomized across construct
axes; and explicit informed consent was obtained, consistent
with Podsakoff et al.’s (2003) recommendations. Post-hoc
analysis involved two different tests. In the first test, the single-
factor exploratory factor analysis method introduced by Harman
was used on the complete set of 29 items. As per this test, the
one dominating factor emerging from this analysis explained
28.3% of the variance, a value that is lower than the minimum
level of 50% indicating the presence of significant CMB
(Podsakoff et al., 2003). The second post-hoc analysis involved
the use of the Common Latent Factor (CLF) test method, where
the use of AMOS software allowed for the introduction of a
latent variable to the 29 indicator items. The result revealed that
the fit of the CLF test (CFI = 0.961, RMSEA = 0.052) was not
significantly different from the baseline measurement
(ΔCFI = 0.002, ΔRMSEA = 0.002), with the highest change in any
one factor loading being less than 0.04.
5.5 Statistical analysis strategy
The study employed a four-layer analytical strategy. Layer 1
included descriptive statistics to characterize the sample and
response patterns across all construct axes. Layer 2 employed
simple linear regression to test H1 (SMPR → CCB), H2 (DACT
→ CEI), and H3 (IDI → CCB), reporting R, R2, Adjusted R2,
F-values with corrected degrees of freedom, standardized beta
coefficients (β), and 95% CIs for β, following Cohen’s (1988)
benchmarks (R2 ≥ 0.02 = small; ≥ 0.13 = medium; ≥ 0.26 = large).
Layer 3 applied Hayes’ (2018) PROCESS macro (Model 4, 10,000
bootstrap samples) to test content-engagement interaction (CEI) as
a formal mediator of the path from DACT to CCB, reporting the
indirect effect with 95% bootstrapped confidence intervals. Layer 4
applied the Kruskal–Wallis H test for H4, preceded by
Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk normality tests; non-
normality was confirmed (K-S D = 0.154, df = 408, p < .001; S-W
W = 0.943, df = 408, p = .004), justifying the non-parametric
approach (Field, 2018). Eta-squared (η2) quantified practical
significance. All analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics v. 26.

Each participant was supplied with an information sheet

identifiers were gathered at any point during data collection.
The research conformed to the guidelines of the Declaration of

7 Discussion
This study examined how social media platforms jointly shape
the purchase patterns and conscious consumer behavior of Saudi
women during Ramadan through an integrated four-framework
model. Three hypotheses were empirically supported with large
effect sizes, one theoretically significant null finding was
confirmed, and formal mediation analysis established CEI as the
operative mechanism linking advertising content to
purchase behavior.
7.1 Platform reliance and conscious
consumer behavior

consumer behavior (R2 = 0.436, F(1, 406) = 314.17, β = 0.660, 95%
CI [0.566, 0.754]), supporting H1 and corroborating MSDT’s
orientation goal prediction. Saudi women deliberately seek
behavioral guidance from social media platforms during
Ramadan, consistent with Al-Marwani’s (2025) SEM-AMOS
demonstration that social media compresses traditional purchase
decision stages through information provision and normative
signaling. From a UGT standpoint, information-seeking and
social interaction gratifications are the primary motivational
drivers of this dependency-to-behavior pathway, replicating
Alqutub’s (2023) Saudi market evidence in a Ramadan-specific
context. The measured response to items comparing social
media trust with traditional advertising indicates that Saudi
women exercise critical evaluation even within high-dependency
relationships—a pattern Omori et al. (2026) characterize as
informed platform dependency, consistent with ELM central-
route processing. The main distinction between the current
study and the previous studies on MSDT is the fact that both
structural dependency and critical evaluation take place
simultaneously among one group of people – namely Saudi

7.2 Authentic content as the dominant
persuasion format
Among the examined variables, content type stands out as the
most predictive factor, explaining 48.8% of the variance in
engagement-consumption behavior (R2 = 0.488, β = 0.699, 95%
CI [0.597, 0.801]). The large difference in means between
authentic personal content (M = 4.26) and paid advertisements
(M = 3.41) lends further support to the ELM central-route
prediction: authentic, culturally relevant content engages
consumers deeply in cognitive processes leading to greater
attitude formation and purchase intention than the prominence
cue linked to the ELM peripheral pathway alone. Our results
confirm those by Alharthi et al. (2026), Alsalloum and Gainous
(2025), and Jamil et al. (2024) who identify authenticity and
source credibility as the key determinants of purchase intention
following ELM in an influencer-based environment. More
importantly, unlike in previous studies that only tested the
relationship between content/authenticity/credibility and
purchase intention empirically, the current investigation
provides new evidence using PROCESS macro mediation
techniques. Specifically, our results reveal that there is no
independent link between content type and engagement-
controlled purchase intentions (β = 0.053, p = .705). In other
words, while content type predicts purchase intention, it is the
intermediary step of consumer engagement that is critical in
triggering the purchase behavior.
7.3 The influencer paradox: social proof
over direct recommendation
The findings of H3 (R2 = 0.417, β = 0.646, 95% CI [0.546,
0.746]) are consistent with a two-step process involved in
evaluating trust through exposure to an influencer: the first stage
involves product awareness from the peripheral credibility
provided by influencer exposure, while purchase intent is
established only after exposure to the social proof signals,
namely likes, shares, and number of comments, functioning as
peer-validated recommendations. These findings are compatible
with the two-stage credibility concept developed by Berlin et al.
(2025) and with the results of Abu-Alhaija and Elsawy (2026),
who have shown how the mediation effect of trust, and not
recommendation, plays the role in purchase intent in Saudi
influencer marketing. The influencer paradox proposed in this
study represents a more specific account of this association than
Fogg’s (2003) framework of earned credibility. While Fogg’s
framework considers social proof as just one type of credibility
among others, the mediation pattern observed in this research
suggests that social proof signals are more strongly associated
with purchase-relevant trust than are influencer
recommendation itself, within this cross-sectional sample. The
framework offered by Fogg is silent on the scenario where the
presence of a social proof signal is strongly associated with the
outcome on its own, and the influencer channel shows
comparatively little independent association. The results
obtained using the PROCESS macro represent, to our
knowledge, an early formal mediation analysis documenting this
pattern of social proof in a Muslim majority population during
Ramadan consumption. It is worth noting a conceptual nuance
here: social proof is typically theorized as a heuristic, relatively
low-effort cue, whereas the dependent variable in this study is
labeled conscious consumer behavior. To clarify, the CCB
measure does not assess the deliberateness of the trust-
formation process itself; it captures the delibera teness of the
final purchase decision—specifically, whether the purchase
was informed, intentional, and identity-congruent (Section
5.3)—regardless of whether the upstream trust signal that
informed it was processed heuristi cally or delibera tely. We
interpret this pattern as indicatin g that a heuristic trust signal
(social proof ) can still feed into a final purchase decision that
respondents experience and report as delibera te and identity-
congruent (the construct captured by the CCB items), rather
than as evidence that social proof itself is processed
consciously; this distinction is discussed further as a
limitatio n in Section 7.7.
7.4 The Ramadan cultural universality thesis
That there was no statistically significant demographic
differentiation by age (χ2(3) = 3.902, p = .272), education
(χ2(3) = 5.790, p = .122), and income (χ2(3) = 3.858, p = .277),
with all η2<.01, would be the study’s most important theoretical
implication. MSDT theorizes that structural media dependency
arises out of the relationship of people with the media system
and not their individual attributes (Ball-Rokeach and DeFleur,
1976). In contrast, the present results offer evidence that this is
indeed true on three demographic fronts, which cannot be said
of prior research within the context of digital consumers in
Saudi Arabia. Although prior scholarship in the domain of
Islamic marketing suggests that the month of Ramadan fosters
unique consumer psychologies (Abosag and Farah, 2014; Hassan
and Harun, 2016), none of these studies explain the underlying
mechanism that explains why there would be demographic
homogeneity in the first place. The cultural universality theory
of Ramadan does just that in suggesting that Ramadan raises the
levels of all four dependency goals of MSDT—orientation, social
understanding, play, and solitary play—and therefore induces
demographic homogeneity through convergent gratification-
seeking behavior that is structurally mediated. The mechanism,
generated from the interplay of MSDT structure and UGT
motivation, constitutes the theoretical contribution behind
this phenomenon.

The current study offers four contributions to theories of
communication and consumer behavior. First, it introduces an

empirically tested, integrated model of how social media-
associated Ramadan consumer behavior operates among Saudi
women by combining three models along with the Digital
Consumer Behavior Framework. Instead of combining
frameworks to serve only as a foundation for theory
development, each framework serves a unique purpose within
the theory development process by providing dependency
creation (MSDT), route choice of elaboration (ELM), motivation

associated with purchase-relevant trust than is influencer
recommendation in this sample; this association pattern, to our
knowledge, has not previously been documented in the earned-
credibility or source-credibility literature. Third, the CEI
mediation result repositions content authenticity from a
persuasion preference into a candidate precondition: culturally
resonant content shows little direct association with purchase
behavior on its own but is associated with the active
engagement that, in turn, is associated with it. Finally, the
current study proposes a candidate mechanism, termed
simultaneous multi-goal dependency elevation, consistent with
the Ramadan cultural universality hypothesis and offered here
for future testing rather than as confirmed proof.
7.6 Practical implications
This study proposes five recommendations based on the
findings for digital marketers, platform providers, and brand
managers trying to engage Saudi women in Ramadan activities.
First, authentic personal content – practical demonstrations of
the products being used during Ramadan – achieves the greatest
purchase-motivation payoff and should thus be emphasized
ahead of any form of paid media regardless of its wider reach.
Second, brands must design their campaigns in a manner which
facilitates the integration of engagement mechanics such as
community challenges, social review features, and share buttons.
This is necessary in order to transform passive platform usage
into elaborate activities which motivate purchases. Third,
leveraging social proof is the most effective way of generating
trust; content designed to encourage engagement through likes,
comments, and shares inspires purchase more strongly than
even credible influencer endorsements according to the
influencer paradox mentioned in Hypothesis 3. Fourth,
congruence with culture and religion becomes an imperative
condition instead of merely being a style choice; materials
lacking Ramadan values of family, hospitality, and spirituality
will not engage users on an identity basis for central route
processing and goal achievement according to MSDT
framework. Fifth, a pan-demographic approach is optimal for
Ramadan; the Ramadan universality thesis explained in
Hypothesis 4 provides the empirical evidence required for
this recommendation.
7.7 Limitations and future research
directions
A number of limitations affect the validity of these results.
First, the use of the cross-sectional study limits the ability to
draw causal inferences about the development of identified
patterns and makes time-related analysis impossible; future
researchers need to conduct longitudinal surveys tracking the
same group of participants throughout multiple Ramadan
periods. Second, while the purposive non-probability sample
ensures geographical representativeness across all five regions of
Saudi Arabia, statistical generalization from the current results
cannot be made due to its lack of probability; replication
requires stratified random sampling with purposeful
oversampling of certain demographic subpopulations. Third, an
estimated 34% response rate could lead to self-selection bias that
favors social media usage. This figure is calculated against a
denominator of summed group/channel membership counts
(Section 5.2), which is itself an upper-bound estimate of
exposure rather than a count of unique viewers, so the true
response rate may have been somewhat higher than 34% if not
all members were actually exposed to the invitation. Although
there were no statistically significant differences in responses to
the survey conducted early and later on, this limitation is noted.
Fourth, limiting the sample size to only women means that
future research will have to address the problem of cross-gender
comparison. While it is unknown whether the influencer
paradox and the Ramadan cultural universality hypothesis apply
to both genders equally, it is clear that additional research will
need to be performed on Saudi Arabian men. Fifth, while
common method bias was tested using two techniques—
Harman’s single-factor test and the Common Latent Factor
Approach—future studies require a more sophisticated analysis
that involves including theoretically irrelevant constructs into
the CFA model in the initial stage (Lindell and Whitney, 2001).
Sixth, H1–H3 were each tested using separate simple linear
regressions rather than a single structural model; because each
predictor was estimated without controlling for the others, the
observed associations could in principle be partly attributable to
variables not included in the model. Seventh, the item-level data
departed from multivariate normality (Section 5.3), which is a
limitation of the maximum-likelihood-based CFA fit statistics
reported in Section 5.3 and Table 3; robust or bootstrapped
estimation methods may be preferable in future replications.
Eighth, because social proof is generally theorized as a relatively
heuristic, low-effort cue, the association between social proof
and the conscious consumer behavior outcome reported under
H3 (Section 7.3) should be interpreted as evidence of a heuristic
input into a consciously experienced purchase decision, not as
evidence that social proof itself is processed deliberately; future
work could test this distinction directly using process-tracing or
response-time measures. As far as substance goes, future work
should focus on the effect of live streaming e-commerce,
augmented reality product visualization, the influence of virtual
influencers, and AI-based advertising. In order to evaluate cross-
national generalizability, the Ramadan cultural universality
hypothesis should be replicated in other Islamic societies such
as Indonesia, Malaysia, Turkey, and Egypt.
