ABSTRACT: Behavioral pricing research has long established that consumers rarely evaluate prices in a purely rational, utility -
maximizing manner, yet this insight remains underexplored in culturally embedded, artisanal market contexts where face-to-face
bargaining norms persi st. This study investigates how three well -documented behavioral biases -anchoring, loss aversion, and
mental accounting -shape consumers’ perceived price fairness and, in turn, their willingness to pay a premium for handicraft
products in Vietnam’s traditional craft villages. Grounded in prospect theory and mental accounting theory, the study develop s
and tests a structural model in which perceived price fairness mediates the relationships between the three biases and willingness
to pay, while the salience of local bargaining norms moderates the anchoring –fairness relationship. Data were collected from
consumers who had purch ased handicraft products at five well -known Red River Delta craft villages and analyzed using partial
least squares structural equation modeling (PLS -SEM) in SmartPLS4, complemented by fuzzy -set qualitative comparative analysis
(fsQCA) to uncover configura tional pathways to high willingness to pay. The findings extend behavioral pricing theory into an
emerging-market artisanal setting characterized by strong bargaining traditions and offer craft -village producers, cooperatives,
and tourism policymakers concrete, psychologically informed guidance for designing fairer and more sustainable pricing strategies
that protect both consumer trust and artisan livelihoods.
KEYWORDS: behavioral pricing biases; perceived price fairness; willingness to pay; PLS -SEM; fsQCA

1. INTRODUCTION
Pricing decisions sit at the intersection of economic rationality and psychological experience, and a substantial body of behavioral
economics research has demonstrated that consumers systematically depart from the expected -utility benchmark when judging
whether a price is acceptable (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Because reference points, framing, and the sequencing of information
shape perceived gains and losses rather than final wealth states, prices that are objectively identical can be judged as fair  or
exploitative depending on how they are anchored, framed, and mentally categorized by the buyer (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974;
Thaler, 1985). These insights have reshaped marketing practice in mature retail and digital -commerce settings, where discount
framing, reference-price displays, and partitioned pricing are now routinely engineered around known cognitive biases (Xia,
Monroe, & Cox, 2004). Yet the vast majority of this evidence has been generated in Western, standardized-price, low-negotiation
retail environments, leaving open the question of how behavioral pricing biases operate in markets where price is not a fixed,
unilaterally posted number but the outcome of a socially embedded bargaining encounter between buyer and seller.
Traditional craft villages occupy exactly this understudied terrain. Across Vietnam, several hundred long-established craft villages-
producing ceramics, silk, wood carving, and other handmade goods-supply both domestic consumers and international tourists in
transactions that blend artisanal production, cultural heritage, and residual bargaining custom (Hieu & Rasovska, 2017). In t hese
settings, the seller’s initial quoted price routinely functions a s a salient anchor (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974), the transac tion is
often mentally coded by the buyer as belonging to a discretionary “travel” or “souvenir” account rather than an ordinary
consumption account (Thaler, 1985), and the buyer’s ultimate willingness to pay is contingent on whether the negotiated outcome
is perceived as fair relative to a locally understood norm (Xia et al., 2004; Bolton, Warlop, & Alba, 2003). Because craft -village
livelihoods depend on households’ ability to capture a sustainable premium for authenticity and skill rather than compete pu rely
on cost, understanding how cognitive biases translate into perceived fairness and willingness to pay is not merely of academi c
interest; it bears directly on rural household income, the preservation of intangible cultural heritage, and the long -run viability of
an important segment of Vietnam’s embedded artisanal economy (Granovetter, 1985; North, 1990).

From a theoretical standpoint, three gaps motivate the present study. First, although anchoring, loss aversion, and mental
accounting have each been extensively documented in isolation, comparatively little empirical work has modeled them jointly a s
a structural system feeding into a single fairness-mediated pricing outcome, particularly outside experimental laboratory settings
(Kahneman, Knetsch, & Thaler, 1990). Second, existing behavioral pricing research has rarely engaged with contexts in which
bargaining is a culturally sanctioned, expected feature of the exchange rather than a deviation from a posted price, so the
boundary conditions under which anchoring biases translate into perceived unfairness remain poorly specified (Zeithaml, 1988) .
Third, the methodological toolkit conventionally applied to these questions-variable-based structural equation modeling-captures
net, average effects but is not designed to reveal the multiple, equally viable configurations of biases that can jointly produce high
willingness to pay, a limitation that configurational approaches such as fuzzy -set qualitative comparative analysis are explicitly
built to address (Ragin, 2008; Fiss, 2011).
This study addresses these gaps by developing and empirically testing a structural model linking anchoring bias, loss aversion, and
mental accounting to consumers’ willingness to pay a premium for craft-village handicrafts, through the mediating mechanism of
perceived price fairness and under the moderating influence of bargaining -norm salience. By combining partial least squares
structural equation modeling (PLS -SEM) with fsQCA, the study offers both a net -effects and a configurational account of how
behavioral biases shape pricing outcomes in a culturally embedded artisanal market. In doing so, it extends behavioral pricing
theory beyond its conventional Western retail context, provides Vietnamese craft -village producers, cooperatives, and cultural -
tourism policymakers with an evidence base for designing pricing practices that are simultaneously profitable and perceived as
fair, and offers a template for studying behavioral pricing in other emerging-market artisanal and heritage-tourism economies.
Beyond Vietnam, the theoretical and practical stakes of this question extend to the broader global handicraft and heritage-tourism
economy, in which small artisan enterprises across South and Southeast Asia, Latin America, and Sub -Saharan Africa similarly
depend on face-to-face price negotiation with a mixed base of domestic and international buyers (Hieu & Rasovska, 2017). Because
these enterprises typically operate outside formal price-regulation regimes and rely on embedded, trust-based transaction norms
rather than posted, unilaterally set prices (Granovetter, 1985; North, 1990), a rigorous behavioral account of how cognitive biases
and perceived fairness jointly determine willingness to pay carries direct relevance for artisan income sustainability and cultural-
heritage preservation well beyond the specific villages examined here. The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows: Sectio n 2
reviews the foundational theories and empirical literature underpinning the proposed model; Section 3 details the rese arch
methodology; Section 4 reports the measurement and structural model results; and Section 5 discusses the findings, theoretica l
and practical contributions, limitations, and directions for future research.

2. FOUNDATIONAL THEORIES AND LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1. Foundational theories
2.1.1. Prospect theory and loss aversion
Prospect theory, introduced by Kahneman and Tversky (1979), provides the foundational departure point for nearly all subsequent
behavioral pricing research. Contrary to expected -utility theory, which assumes that decision -makers evaluate outcomes against
absolute wealth states, prospect theory posits that individuals evaluate outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference p oint,
that the value function is concave for gains and convex for losses, and that losses are weighted more heavily than equivalent gains-
a property termed loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Applied to pricing, the reference point is typically the price a
consumer expects to pay, whether formed by a previously advertised price, a competitor’s price, or, critically for bargaining
contexts, the seller’s opening quotation (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Because the initial quotation anchors subsequent judgment
through an insufficient -adjustment process, buyers who receive a high opening price often settle at a final price that remains
anchored well above what an unbiased assessment of value would justify, even after extensive negotiation (Tversky & Kahneman,
1974). Loss aversion compounds this dynamic: once a buyer has invested time, effort, and emotional energy in a bargaining
episode, walking away without a purchase is coded as a loss relative to the anticipated outcome, which increases the buyer’s
willingness to accept an unfavorable but still positive settlement rather than forfeit the transaction entirely (Kahneman, Kn etsch,
& Thaler, 1990). The endowment-effect literature further demonstrates that the psychological ownership generated by handling
and provisionally “possessing” a hand -crafted object during a bargaining encounter inflates the buyer’s subsequent valuation of
that object, reinforcing the anchor set by the seller (Kahneman et al., 1990). These mechanisms jointly imply that in craft -village
transactions, where face -to-face bargaining is normatively expected, both anchoring and loss aversion are unusually salient
relative to fixed-price retail contexts, and their operation is plausibly conditioned by how strongly the buyer perceives bargaining
itself to be a legitimate, expected part of the exchange.

2.1.2. Mental accounting theory
A second foundational pillar is Thaler’s (1985) theory of mental accounting, which models  consumer choice as governed not by a
single, fungible budget constraint but by a set of psychologically segregated accounts, each associated with its own reference price
and its own rules of evaluation. Central to this framework is the concept of “transac tion utility,” the perceived pleasure or
displeasure derived from the terms of the deal itself, independent of the acquisition utility derived from the good (Thaler, 1985).
When consumers classify a purchase as belonging to a discretionary or hedonic accou nt-such as a “vacation” or “souvenir”
account-they apply more lenient reference prices and are willing to tolerate larger transaction -utility losses than they would for
an equivalent purchase coded within an ordinary household account (Thaler, 1985). Tourists purchasing handicrafts at a craft
village are a paradigmatic instance of this phenomenon: because the purchase is mentally bracketed within a bounded, non -
recurring “trip” account rather than a recurring consumption category, evaluations of price fairn ess are plausibly loosened, but
only up to a point, because perceived unfairness -rather than the absolute price level -remains the proximate trigger of
dissatisfaction and reduced willingness to pay (Xia et al., 2004; Bolton et al., 2003). Mental accounting theory thus supplie s the
second cognitive mechanism through which the present study expects craft -village consumers’ pricing judgments to diverge
systematically from a purely rational assessment of intrinsic product value.
Taken together, prospect theory and mental accounting theory suggest that a buyer’s ultimate judgment of whether a craft-village
price is fair, and hence how much premium the buyer is willing to pay, is not a direct function of the good’s objective attri butes
but is jointly constructed through anchoring, loss aversion, and the mental account within which the purchase is classified. This
composite, multi-bias view motivates the present study’s decision to model anchoring, loss aversion, and mental accounting  as
parallel exogenous constructs converging on a single fairness-mediated outcome, rather than examining any one bias in isolation,
as much of the existing experimental literature has tended to do (Kahneman et al., 1990; Thaler, 1985).
2.2. Review of empirical and relevant studies
2.2.1. Anchoring bias in pricing and negotiation contexts
Anchoring effects were first documented by Tversky and Kahneman (1974) as a general judgmental heuristic whereby numeric
estimates are insufficiently adjusted away from an initially presented value. Applied specifically to negotiation and pricing
contexts, this heuristic implies that the first price quotation offered in a bargaining episode functions as a powerful anchor on the
eventual settlement price, an effect that persists even when the anchor is transparently arbitrary or strategically inflated (Tversky
& Kahneman, 1974). In market contexts that retain face-to-face bargaining-street markets, bazaars, and craft villages among them-
the seller’s opening price is rarely a neutral piece of information; it is a strategic anchor deliberately set above the sell er’s true
reservation price in anticipation of downward negotiation. Because buyers typically underestimate how far the anchor shou ld be
discounted, final transaction prices in such settings tend to remain systematically elevated relative to a hypothetica l unbiased
valuation (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Kahneman & Tversky, 1979).
2.2.2. Loss aversion and bargaining persistence
Loss aversion, the differential weighting of losses relative to equivalent gains, has been shown experimentally to generate
reluctance to abandon a negotiation once a buyer has become psychologically invested in it, and to inflate a buyer’s valuatio n of
a good once it is provisionally “possessed,” even temporarily, during a bargaining encounter (Kahneman et al., 1990). This
endowment-linked mechanism is especially relevant to craft -village transactions, in which sellers often encourage buyers to
physically handle, wear, or otherwise interact with handicraft items prior to price negotiation, a practice that, consistent with the
endowment-effect literature, plausibly strengthens the buyer’s subsequent willingness to accept a seller -favorable settlement
rather than relinquish a good already mentally “owned” (Kahneman et al., 1990).
2.2.3. Mental accounting in tourism and souvenir consumption
Thaler’s (1985) mental accounting framework has been widely applied to explain why consumers exhibit systematically different
price sensitivity across nominally similar purchase categories, depending on which mental account the expenditure is coded
within. Tourism and souvenir spending is a canonical example of a “vacation account,” within which consumers apply looser
reference prices and derive positive transaction utility from purchases that would be judged as poor value within an everyday
household budge t (Thaler, 1985). Because this looser accounting is bounded rather than unlimited, mental accounting theory
implies that consumers remain sensitive to violations of a locally understood fairness norm even within a discretionary account, a
boundary condition that the present study operationalizes through the mediating construct of perceived price fairness (Thaler,
1985; Xia et al., 2004).

2.2.4. Perceived price fairness as a mediating mechanism
Perceived price fairness has been consistently identified in the pricing literature as the proximate cognitive judgment throu gh
which upstream biases and contextual cues are translated into downstream purchase and payment behavior (Xia et al., 2004;
Bolton et al., 2003; Zeithaml, 1988). Rather than evaluating a price in isolation, consumers compare the offered price against a
reference transaction-a competitor’s price, a prior price, or a socially expected outcome-and judge fairness according to whether
the seller appears to have exploited a position of relative power (Xia et al., 2004). Zeithaml’s (1988) means -end model further
establishes that perceived price is only one input into an overarching value judgment that also incorporates perceived qualit y,
implying that fairness perceptions, rather than raw price levels, are the more proximate determinant of downstream willingness
to pay. This body of work motivates the present study’s specification of perceived price fairness as the mediating mechanism
linking anchoring, loss aversion, and mental accounting to willingness to pay.
2.2.5. Bargaining-norm salience as a boundary condition
Because the meaning and legitimacy of a price anchor depends on whether bargaining itself is understood as a normatively
sanctioned feature of the exchange, the strength of social and cultural bargaining norms is expected to moderate how anchorin g
translates into fairness judgments (Granovetter, 1985). Economic -sociological accounts of embedded exchange argue that
transactions in markets with strong bargaining traditions are governed by locally shared expectations about acceptable
negotiation conduct, such that a high opening anchor is discounted as an expected opening move rather than judged as an unfair
imposition, whereas the same anchor presented in a market without such a tradition would be judged more harshly (Granovetter,
1985; North, 1990). This theo retical logic underpins the present study’s inclusion of bargaining -norm salience as a moderator of
the anchoring–fairness relationship.
2.2.6. Willingness to pay a premium for authenticity and craftsmanship
Finally, research on craft and heritage tourism situates craft -village handicrafts within a broader category of goods whose value
derives substantially from perceived authenticity, cultural heritage, and embodied artisanal skill rather than from functional utility
alone (Hieu & Rasovska, 2017). This literature suggests that willingness to pay a premium for such goods is jointly determine d by
the buyer’s appraisal of intrinsic craftsmanship and by the fairness of the price -setting process itself, reinforcing  the theoretical
centrality of perceived price fairness as the mediating construct linking cognitive biases to the study’s ultimate dependent variable.
2.2.7. Behavioral pricing within embedded, informal artisanal economies
A final strand of relevant literature situates craft -village pricing within the broader study of embedded and informal economic
exchange. Granovetter (1985) argues that economic transactions are never purely atomistic market exchanges but are embedded
within ongoing social relations and locally shared norms that shape what counts as acceptable conduct, including acceptable
pricing and negotiation behavior. North (1990) extends this argument by showing that informal institutions -customary rules,
conventions, and shared expectations that are never formally codified -often exert as much influence over transaction outcomes
as formal price regulation, particularly in settings such as craft villages where formal price -setting institutions are largely absent.
Read together with the craft-village tourism literature (Hieu & Rasovska, 2017), this body of work suggests that behavioral pricing
biases documented in formal, regulated retail markets cannot be assumed to operate identically in embedded, informal artisana l
exchange, reinforcing the theoretical rationale for treating bargaining-norm salience as a distinct, culturally grounded moderating
construct rather than a simple control variable.
2.3. Proposed research model
Building on the theoretical and empirical foundations reviewed above, this study proposes a structural model comprising three
exogenous behavioral -bias constructs, one mediating construct, one moderating construct, and one endogenous outcome
construct, estimated using PLS-SEM and complemented with fsQCA.
Anchoring bias (ANC) is defined as the extent to which a consumer’s price judgment remains tied to the initial price quoted by the
seller. Consistent with anchoring-and-adjustment theory, this study hypothesizes that stronger anchoring reduces perceived price
fairness, because consumers who remain more tightly anchored to an inflated opening quotation are, by construction, less able
to adjust toward an independently formed fair-value estimate (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974).
H1: Anchoring bias is negatively associated with perceived price fairness.
Loss aversion (LOA) is defined as the consumer’s differential sensitivity to the prospective loss of forfeiting a bargaining episode
relative to the equivalent gain of a favorable settlement. Because loss -averse consumers are theorized to accept less favor able
outcomes to avoid the psychological cost of walking away empty -handed, this study hypothesizes a negative direct relationship
with perceived price fairness (Kahneman et al., 1990).
H2: Loss aversion is negatively associated with perceived price fairness.

Mental accounting (MEA) is defined as the extent to which a consumer classifies the handicraft purchase within a discretionar y,
hedonic “travel” or “souvenir” account rather than an ordinary consumption account. Because discretionary -account framing is
theorized to loosen the reference price against which fairness is judged and to generate positive transaction utility independen t
of acquisition utility, this study hypothesizes a positive relationship with perceived price fairness (Thaler, 1985).
H3: Mental accounting is positively associated with perceived price fairness.
Perceived price fairness (PPF) is modeled as the mediating construct through which the three upstream biases exert their influence
on willingness to pay. Consistent with the pricing literature’s consensus that fairness judgments are the proximate driver of
downstream payment behavior, this study hypothesizes a positive direct relationship between perceived price fairness and
willingness to pay a premium, together with significant indirect effects of ANC, LOA, and MEA transmitted through PPF (Xia et  al.,
2004; Zeithaml, 1988).
H4: Perceived price fairness is positively associated with willingness to pay a premium.
H5: Perceived price fairness mediates the relationships between anchoring bias, loss aversion, mental accounting, and willingness
to pay a premium.
Bargaining-norm salience (BNS) captures the extent to which the consumer perceives face -to-face price negotiation as a locally
legitimate, expected feature of the craft-village exchange. Drawing on the economic-sociological argument that the interpretation
of a price anchor is embedded within locally shared negotiation norms, this study hypothesizes that bargaining -norm salience
moderates the anchoring –fairness relationship, such that the negative effect of anchoring on perceived fairness is attenuated
among consumers for whom bargaining is highly salient as an expected norm (Granovetter, 1985).
H6: Bargaining-norm salience moderates the relationship between anchoring bias and perceived price fairness, such that the
negative effect is weaker when bargaining-norm salience is high.
Willingness to pay a premium (WTP) is the study’s ultimate endogenous construct, defined as the consumer’s stated willingness
to pay above a baseline reference price for a handicraft product perceived as authentic and skillfully crafted. This construc t
operationalizes the practical, managerially relevant outcome that links consumer psychology to craft -village producers’ capacity
to set and sustain profitable, fair pricing strategies.
Collectively, hypotheses H1 –H6 specify a moderated -mediation structural model in which anchoring bias, loss aversion, and
mental accounting exert both direct and fairness-mediated effects on willingness to pay a premium, with bargaining-norm salience
conditioning the strength of the anchoring pathway. This specification allows the study to test not only whether behavioral biases
matter for pricing outcomes in a craft -village setting, but the specific cognitive and cultural mechanisms through which they
operate, and, through the complementary fsQCA analysis reported in Section 4.3, to identify the configurations of biases that are
jointly sufficient for high willingness to pay.

3. RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
3.1. Research Design
This study adopted a quantitative, cross -sectional survey design grounded in a positivist research paradigm, consistent with the
study’s objective of testing a pre -specified structural model of hypothesized causal relationships among latent behavioral
constructs (Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003). A structured, self -administered questionnaire was used to
operationalize each construct through multi -item reflective indicators adapted from previously validated scales in the behavioral
pricing and consumer psychology literature. The study combined two complementary analytical techniques: partial least squares
structural equation modeling (PLS -SEM), implemented in SmartPLS4, to estimate the hypothesized net effects, mediation, and
moderation specified in Section 2.3; and fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA), to identify configurations of the three
behavioral biases and bargaining-norm salience that are jointly sufficient for high willingness to pay, thereby complementing the
symmetric, correlational logic of PLS-SEM with an asymmetric, configurational perspective (Ragin, 2008; Fiss, 2011). Because the
constructs at hand are individually well established in prior behavioral-pricing scholarship but had not previously been assembled
into a si ngle, culturally situated structural model, PLS -SEM was judged appropriate given its suitability for theory development
and prediction-oriented research with moderately complex path models (Chin, 1998).
3.2. Data Collection
Data were collected between March and July 2025 from consumers who had completed a purchase of at least one handicraft
product at one of five well -established craft villages in Vietnam’s Red River Delta region: Bat Trang (ceramics), Van Phuc (silk),
Dong Ky (wood carving and furniture), Chuon Ngo (mother -of-pearl inlay), and Non Chuong (conical hats). These villages were
purposively selected to represent a cross-section of craft categories and price points while retaining a shared institutional feature:
an active, though declining, tradition of face-to-face price negotiation between artisan-vendors and buyers.

A structured questionnaire was administered on -site to both domestic Vietnam ese consumers and international tourists
immediately following the completion of a purchase, using intercept sampling at designated village entry and exit points,

tourism-operator networks to reach respondents who had purchased within the preceding two weeks. A total of 480

check item, exhibiting straight -lining response patterns, or containing more than 10 percent missing data, 412 valid responses
were retained for analysis, corresponding to an effective usable response rate of 85.8 percent. The final sample compri sed 54.1
percent female and 45.9 percent male respondents, with 61.4 percent domestic Vietnamese consumers and 38.6 percent
international tourists; respondent age ranged from 19 to 68 years (M = 34.7, SD = 11.2). Common method bias was addressed

the use of varied response -scale anchors, and statistically, through Harman’s single -factor test and a full -collinearity variance

The study protocol received prior approval from the relevant institutional research ethics committee, and all respondents provided
informed consent before participating; participation was voluntary, anonymous, and uncompensated beyond a small handicraft -
themed token of appreciation. Sampling combined purposive selection of craft villages with systematic intercept sampling of
consumers within each village, a procedure commonly used in tourism and consumer -behavior fieldwork where a complete
sampling frame of transient visitors is unavailable. Sample size was determined ex ante using the minimum -R² heuristic for PLS -
SEM at a significance level of .05, statistical power of .80, and an anticipated minimum R² of .10 for the model’s most compl ex
construct, which indicated a minimum required sample of 146 respondents for the largest number of predictors (three) directed
at a single endogenous construct; the achieved sample of 412 therefore substantially exceeded the minimum threshold and
supported adequately powered bootstrapped significance testing.
3.3. Measurement & Validation
All constructs were operationalized using multi-item, seven-point Likert-type scales (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree), with
items adapted and contextually reworded for the craft-village bargaining setting from previously validated instruments. Anchoring
bias (four items) was adapted from established anchoring-and-adjustment measures grounded in Tversky and Kahneman’s (1974)
heuristics-and-biases framework. Loss aversion (four items) was adapted from experimental loss-aversion and endowment-effect
scales derived from Kahneman et al. (1990). Mental accounting (four items) was adapted from Thaler’s (1985) mental accounting
and transaction-utility framework as operationalized in subsequent tourism -spending research. Bargaining-norm salience (three
items), the study’s moderating construct, was newly developed for this study to capture the perceived legitimacy and social
expectedness of price negotiation, informed by Granovetter’s (1985) concept of embedded exchange. Perceived price fairness
(four items) was adapted from Xia et al.’s (2004) and Bolton et al.’s (2003) price -fairness perception scales. Willingness to pay a
premium (four items) was adapted from Zeithaml’s (1988) perceived-value framework, operationalized as the respondent’s stated
willingness to pay above a stated baseline reference price. All items were translated into Vietnamese and back -translated into
English by independent bilingual researchers to ensure semantic equivalence, and the instrument was piloted with 30 respondents
prior to fu ll deployment, resulting in minor wording refinements to two items. As reported in Section 4.1, the resulting 23 -item
measurement model was assessed for internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability), indicator
reliability (standardized factor loadings), convergent validity (average variance extracted), and discriminant validity, using both
the Fornell -Larcker criterion (Fornell & Larcker, 1981) and the heterotrait -monotrait ratio of correlations (Henseler, Ringle, &
Sarstedt, 2015).
3.4. Analytical Procedure
Consistent with the two -step approach recommended for PLS -SEM (Chin, 1998), the measurement model was assessed prior to
the structural model. Exploratory factor analysis using principal component extraction with varimax rotation was first conduc ted
to confirm the expected six-factor item structure, followed by confirmatory assessment of loadings, reliability, and validity within
SmartPLS4. The structural model was then estimated using the standard PLS algorithm, and the significance of path coefficient s
was assessed using a nonparametric bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 resamples and a bias -corrected and accelerated (BCa)
confidence-interval approach. Model predictive power was assessed via the coefficient of determination (R²) for endogenous
constructs, effect sizes (f²) for individual predictors, and predictive relevance (Q²) obtained through the blindfolding procedure.
The moderating effect of bargaining -norm salience on the anchoring –fairness relationship was estimated using the two -stage
product-indicator approach in SmartPLS4. Mediation was assessed following the bootstrapped indirect -effect approach
appropriate to variance-based SEM. Multigroup analysis (PLS-MGA) was conducted to compare path coefficients across domestic

5. DISCUSSION OF RESEARCH RESULTS AND CONCLUSIONS
The results reported in Section 4 are consistent with, and extend, the theoretical logic developed in Sections 2 and 3. Both
anchoring bias and loss aversion exerted significant negative effects on perceived price fairness, and mental accounting exerted a
significant positive effect, replicating, in a craft -village bargaining context, the mechanisms long documented in prospect theory
and mental accounting research (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Thaler, 1985). The full mediation of all three effects through
perceived price fairness, evidenced by the non-significant direct paths reported in Table 5 alongside the significant indirect effects
in Table 7, supports Zeithaml’s (1988) contention that fairness, rather than the raw magnitude of a bias, is the proximate cognitive
gateway through which pricing psychology is translated into willingness to pay. This pattern refines the loss -aversion and
endowment-effect literature (Kahneman et al., 1990) by specifying fairness perception, rather than a direct valuation shift, as the
operative channel in a socially embedded bargaining context.

The significant, fairness -attenuating interaction between bargaining -norm salience and anchoring bias (H6) provides direct
empirical support for the embeddedness argument that the interpretation of a price anchor is conditioned by locally shared
negotiation norms (Granovetter, 1985). Where bargaining is understood by the consumer as an expected, legitimate feature of
the exchange, an inflated opening quotation is discounted as a conventional opening move rather than judged as exploitative,
consistent with e conomic-sociological accounts of how institutional context shapes the interpretation of ostensibly identical
market behavior (Granovetter, 1985; North, 1990). This result extends behavioral pricing theory, whose canonical evidence bas e
has been drawn overw helmingly from fixed -price retail and laboratory settings, into a market form -face-to-face artisanal
bargaining-in which anchoring’s effect on fairness is not fixed but culturally conditioned.
The multigroup comparison indicated that the anchoring –fairness path was significantly stronger among international tourists
than among domestic Vietnamese consumers, plausibly reflecting the international segment’s comparative unfamiliarity with local
bargaining conventions and correspondingly weaker calibration of what constitutes a legitimate opening anchor. This pattern
echoes long -standing findings from behavioral economics that unfamiliarity with the reference distribution of an anchoring
stimulus amplifies its distorting effect on judgment (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974), and it carries a direct practical implication: craft-
village vendors and destination -management organizations may need to calibrate opening -price transparency differently for
domestic and international buyers to avoid perceptions of exploitation that could damage repeat visitation and word -of-mouth
reputation.
The complementary fsQCA analysis revealed that no single behavioral bias was individually necessary for high willingness to p ay;
rather, several distinct configurations-each combining mental accounting with either the absence of loss aversion or the presence
of bargaining-norm salience-were independently sufficient to produce a high -willingness-to-pay outcome. This configurational,
equifinal pattern is consistent with Fiss’s (2011) argument that causally complex organizational and market phenomena are
frequently better represented by multiple sufficient recipes than by a single dominant causal pathway, and it demonstrates the
complementary analytical value of pairing a symmetric, net-effects method such as PLS-SEM with an asymmetric, configurational
method such as fsQCA (Ragin, 2008).
Theoretically, this study makes three contributions. First, it integrates anchoring, loss aversion, and mental accounting wit hin a
single fairness -mediated structural model, moving beyond the largely isolated, single -bias experimental designs that have
characterized much of the behavioral pricing literature. Second, it introduces bargaining -norm salience as a culturally grounded
boundary condition on anchoring effects, extending behavioral pricing theory into artisanal, negotiation-based market forms that
remain common across much of the emerging -market and heritage-tourism economy. Third, by combining PLS -SEM with fsQCA,
it demonstrates how variance-based and configurational methods can be used jointly to triangulate both the average causal effect
and the plurality of sufficient behavioral configurations underlying a single pricing outcome.
From a practical standpoint, the findings suggest that craft -village cooperatives, individual artisan -vendors, and cultural-tourism
authorities should treat perceived fairness, rather than price level per se, as the primary lever for sustaining both consum er
goodwill and artisan income. Because mental accounting and bargaining-norm salience both operate to loosen or preserve fairness
judgments, interventions such as transparent, story -based pricing narratives that frame the transaction within a heritage or
experiential account, alongside modest vendor training in calibrating opening -price anchors differently for international versus
domestic buyers, could allow craft villages to sustain premium pricing without triggering perceptions of exploitation.
At a broader policy level, national and provincial tourism authorities responsible for promoting Vietnam’s craft -village network
could incorporate the present findings into destination-management guidelines, for example by encouraging standardized, though
not necessarily fixed, price -range disclosures at village entry points that recalibrate visitor expectations without eliminating the
cultural practice of bargaining altogether. Because the fsQCA results indicate that bargaining-norm salience combined with mental
accounting represents one of the most consistent configurational routes to high willingness to pay, policy interventions that
preserve and even actively communicate the bargaining tradition as an authentic cultural experience, rather than treating i t as a
market imperfection to be eliminated, may be more effective than interventions that impose uniform fixed pricing. Such an
approach would align commercial sustainability for artisan households with the preservation of an intangible cultural practice that
is itself part of the craft village’s tourism appeal (Hieu & Rasovska, 2017).
Several limitations qualify these conclusions. The cross-sectional design precludes strong causal inference despite the theoretically
grounded directional hypotheses; the sample was drawn from five Red River Delta villages and may not generalize to craft
communities in other regions of Vietnam or to other emerging -market artisanal-tourism contexts; and self -reported willingness-
to-pay measures, while standard in the pricing literature, remain an imperfect proxy for incentive-compatible purchase behavior.
Future research could usefully extend this model longitudinally, incorporate revealed rather than stated willingness -to-pay
measures, and examine additional boundary conditions such as vendor -buyer familiarity or repeated-interaction history.
