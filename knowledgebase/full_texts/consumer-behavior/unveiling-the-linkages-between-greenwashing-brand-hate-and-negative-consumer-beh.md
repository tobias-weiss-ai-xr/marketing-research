Abstract
Numerous companies are striving to improve their environmental reputation by highlighting their sustainability initiatives
to the public. To do this, they are adopting green marketing strategies to gain a competitive advantage and appeal to
environmentally aware consumers. However, not all claims made through green marketing truly reflect the companies’
actual environmental practices, leading to perceptions of greenwashing (GW). While previous research has focused on the
financial and ethical consequences of GW, this study fills a key gap by exploring the emotional and behavioral reactions
it triggers. In particular, this study, guided by expectancy violations theory, explores how GW contributes to brand hate
(BH) and leads to negative consumer actions, such as consumer-brand boycott (CBB) and consumer-brand sabotage (CBS),
while also investigating whether environmental concern (EC) influences the GW-BH relationship. To investigate these
dynamics, data was gathered from 391 participants in Yemen using a convenience sampling technique and analyzed
through structural equation modeling. The findings indicate that GW positively contributes to BH (b = 0.577, p < 0.01)
at confidence intervals (0.445, 0.660), which in turn significantly influences both CBB (b = 0.499, p < 0.01) at confidence
intervals (0.338, 0.644), and CBS (b = 0.600, p < 0.01) at confidence intervals (0.459,0.728). However, the moderating effect
of EC on the GW-BH relationship was not supported (b = 0.053, p > 0.05) at confidence intervals (− 0.011, 0.186). This study
extends EVT by demonstrating how unmet ethical and environmental expectations trigger intense consumer backlash,
particularly within a collectivist and religiously influenced cultural context like Yemen. The findings also underscore the
importance of cultural and ethical considerations in consumer responses to corporate sustainability claims. Practically,
businesses should prioritize transparency and authenticity in their environmental initiatives to prevent reputational
damage and consumer distrust. Moreover, companies operating in culturally sensitive markets must align sustainability
claims with genuine environmental efforts to maintain credibility and consumer loyalty.

1 Introduction
In recent years, the growing emphasis on environmental consciousness has driven consumers to favor companies per -
ceived as socially and environmentally responsible [3 , 11, 13]. This shift has encouraged corporations to adopt sustain-
ability initiatives that not only address ecological challenges but also provide competitive advantages. Social and envi-
ronmental initiatives have been shown to enhance brand loyalty and influence purchasing decisions [61], underscoring
the importance of authenticity in corporate sustainability efforts [59, 65]. At the same time, stakeholders increasingly
demand genuine ecological efforts from businesses, compelling firms to align their operations with ethical and sustain-
able standards [15]. However, some companies exploit these expectations through greenwashing, misleading consumers
about their environmental practices or product benefits [65, 69].
Greenwashing (GW), defined as "strategies used to mislead consumers regarding the ecological practices of a com-
pany, or the ecological benefits of a product/service" [65], poses significant risks to both companies and consumers.
Such deceptive practices undermine trust, damage reputations, and provoke strong emotional and behavioral reactions
[57]. When consumers perceive CSR efforts as insincere, this deception can backfire, eroding trust and resulting in severe
reputational harm [68]. In extreme cases, the negative repercussions of GW can outweigh any potential benefits of social
and environmental initiatives [47, 60].
While previous research has explored the reputational and legal consequences of GW [47, 60], the emotional and
behavioral impacts—particularly the role of GW in triggering brand hate (BH)—are still unclear [56, 65]. Indeed, BH, an
emotional response that arises when consumers feel betrayed by a brand [48] as a potential result of deceptive marketing
practices, such as GW [45, 65], is becoming an increasingly important area of research. This emotion often manifests in
negative consumer behaviors, such as consumer-brand boycott (CBB), and hostile behaviors, such as consumer-brand
sabotage (CBS) [63], which pose significant risks to businesses by undermining trust and diminishing brand equity [1 ,
65]. Existing studies have primarily examined BH in terms of brand avoidance and negative word-of-mouth (see Table 1),
leaving gaps that require further research to understand how GW incites destructive behaviors such as boycotts and
vandalism in diverse cultural, social, and economic contexts.
In this regard, Yemen presents a unique and underexplored context for studying consumer reactions to corporate mis-
conduct, including GW. As an Islamic society, Yemen is deeply influenced by ethical and religious values that emphasize
honesty, social responsibility, and environmental stewardship [14, 17]. In Islamic teachings, honesty, transparency, and
environmental stewardship are considered moral obligations, which means that misleading environmental claims can
be perceived not only as corporate dishonesty but also as violations of religious principles [52]. As a result, GW in Yemen
may provoke stronger negative reactions compared to societies where ethical and religious considerations are less pro-
nounced in consumer decision-making. Moreover, Yemen’s collectivist culture fosters a strong sense of community and
social responsibility, which influences consumer behaviors. Research suggests that consumers in collectivist cultures are
more likely to engage in punitive actions, such as brand boycotts when they perceive a company’s actions as unethical
[55]. In the context of GW, this collectivist tendency may intensify the effects of BH, leading to widespread CBB and CBS
as collective forms of resistance against deceptive practices.
Related to this, the ASDA’A BCW Arab Youth Survey (2023) revealed that 58% of Arab youth are ready to boycott brands
that damage the environment, rising to 65% in Gulf Cooperation Council states, which share cultural and economic ties
with Yemen (https:// araby ouths urvey. com/ en/). This highlights a growing consumer awareness and intolerance toward
deceptive sustainability claims. Moreover, due to ongoing socio-economic challenges in Yemen, including resource
scarcity and environmental degradation, consumers are particularly sensitive to corporate environmental misconduct
[4]. Misleading sustainability claims may therefore be seen as exploitative, deepening consumer distrust and reinforcing
negative behaviors like BH, CBB, and CBS. These factors make Yemen, with cultural and religious values, an important
setting for understanding how consumers react to GW.
Furthermore, previous research suggests that consumers with high levels of environmental concern (EC) may be
more sensitive to GW and, therefore, more likely to develop negative emotions such as BH when they perceive a brand’s
ecological claims to be deceptive [35]. For example, De Jong et al. [27] suggest that consumers with high EC are more
likely to identify GW and react negatively, as they have a deeper personal investment in protecting the environment.
Furthermore, Singh and Wagner [64] indicated that BH is fueled by environmental and societal well-being concerns,
expected social gains, and uniqueness. However, there remains a need for empirical research to validate these claims
and examine whether EC moderates the GW-BH relationship.

Based on the above, this study aims to address these gaps by investigating the linkages between GW, BH, and nega-
tive consumer behaviors, focusing on the CBB and CBS, using the expectancy violations theory (EVT) as a theoretical
lens. Additionally, it explores whether EC moderates the GW-BH relationship. To sum up, this study aims to answer the
following questions:
RQ1. How does GW affect BH?
RQ2. What is the effect of BH on CBB and CBS?
RQ3. Does EC moderate the relationship between GW and BH?
By addressing these questions, this study contributes to the literature by situating GW within the EVT framework to
offer a deeper understanding of how unmet ethical and environmental expectations influence consumer psychology and
behavior. In addition, it examined whether EC moderates the GW-BH relationship, offering insights into how consumer
attributes shape emotional and behavioral responses to corporate deception. Furthermore, it extends the literature
by contextualizing these dynamics within a society where ecological issues are intertwined with cultural and religious
values, providing novel insights for businesses operating in diverse markets.
The remainder of this study is structured as follows: Sects. 2 and 3 present the theoretical background and hypothesis
development, respectively. Section 4 details the research methodology, including data collection procedures, measure-
ment instruments, and analytical techniques. Section 5 reports the empirical findings, followed by Sect. 6, which discusses
the results in relation to existing literature, offering theoretical and practical implications, and acknowledging limitations
and avenues for future research.
2  Theoretical background
2.1  Expectancy violations theory
EVT posits that individuals develop expectations about behaviors in specific contexts, and when these expectations are
violated, the outcomes can trigger emotional and behavioral responses [7 , 10]. Applied to the context of GW, EVT pro -
vides a theoretical lens to understand how deceptive environmental claims violate consumers’ ethical and trust-based
expectations. When companies exaggerate or fabricate their environmental practices, they breach the high expectations
consumers hold for ethical behavior, particularly in the realm of CSR and sustainability [65]. This breach of trust creates
a significant expectancy violation, eliciting emotional responses such as disappointment, frustration, and anger [58].
These emotions are further intensified when consumers feel personally invested in environmental issues, as they may
perceive GW as an affront to their values [73].
In this study, we argue that GW constitutes a direct expectancy violation because consumers anticipate brands to
be transparent and genuine in their sustainability claims [9]. When these claims are revealed as deceptive, the violation
triggers emotional responses, including BH. This intense negative emotion often manifests in retaliatory behaviors, such
as CBB and CBS, as consumers seek to penalize the brand for betraying their trust [48, 63]. In addition, EC amplifies the
salience of ethical expectations, making violations more severe for environmentally conscious consumers. Individuals
with high EC hold stricter expectations regarding a brand’s sustainability practices and are thus more likely to perceive
GW as a significant ethical breach [27]. EVT thus provides a compelling framework to predict how unmet ethical and
environmental expectations escalate into strong emotional and behavioral reactions. For instance, Santos et al. [65] illus-
trated how expectancy violations in green marketing led to heightened consumer skepticism and negative emotions,
particularly among environmentally conscious consumers. Similarly, Sajid et al. [53] explored the link between GW and
brand avoidance, emphasizing the role of unmet expectations in driving negative consumer behaviors. These studies
underscore the relevance of EVT in understanding consumer reactions to GW and provide a foundation for extending
the theory to explore emotional and behavioral outcomes such as BH, CBB, and CBS.
2.2  Greenwashing
GW refers to “a deliberate communicative behavior aimed at misleading or deceiving stakeholders” [27], p. 77), and it has
become a significant area of focus in both academic research and business practice, particularly in marketing. Scholars
argue that GW practices primarily exploit stakeholders’ EC, ultimately obstructing consumers’ ability to make informed,
conscious, and authentic decisions [23]. These deceptive practices have a broad negative impact on brand perception, as
consumer skepticism about a product or brand increases [56, 65]. Such perceptions undermine consumer trust, leading

2.3  Brand hate and negative consumer behaviors
BH is described as a powerful negative emotion characterized by aversion and detachment from a brand and its associ-
ated values [48, 65]. This emotion can be understood through its dual nature: it has both active and passive dimensions.
The active aspect includes feelings of anger, disgust, or contempt, while the passive side involves emotions such as fear,
disappointment, dehumanization, and shame [74]. BH is often triggered by a variety of factors, such as brand injustices,
unsatisfactory past experiences, symbolic incongruity, ideological conflicts, rumors, and poor relationships with the
brand [40, 48]. Research has shown that BH influences a range of behavioral responses, which are shaped by factors like
the intensity of the hate and specific individual or social circumstances [48]. These responses can be broadly categorized
into avoidance, approach, and attack behaviors [49].
Avoidance behaviors, often referred to as "flight" or "passive reactions" [34], involve actions such as avoiding purchases,
switching brands, or reducing patronage [26, 40]. These responses reflect consumer disengagement and signal their
desire to distance themselves from the brand [63]. Such behaviors are viewed as instrumental in consumer boycotts, as
they provide a means for individuals to express their discontent [48]. In the aftermath of a brand transgression, com-
munication between the brand and consumer is typically minimal, which hinders the company’s ability to address the
issue and rebuild trust. At times, avoidance behaviors coexist with or precede attack-type responses, which are more
direct and confrontational.
In contrast, approach-type behaviors involve customers taking active steps to address the wrong, such as protesting
or filing complaints with the company or third-party entities [63, 72, 74]. These behaviors are less passive than avoidance
responses and signify a desire to restore the relationship with the brand. While approach-type reactions offer businesses
an opportunity to correct the situation and repair the consumer relationship, failure to address the issue adequately may
escalate the situation, leading to more aggressive attack-type responses. Thus, BH can manifest as a dynamic sequence
of behaviors, with varying intensity and forms depending on the consumer’s emotional investment and the brand’s
response.
3  Hypotheses development
3.1  Greenwashing and brand hate
BH often arises when businesses fail to align with consumers’ moral or social expectations, a phenomenon termed
ideological incompatibility [39]. When brands uphold coherent values and ethical practices, they tend to elicit positive
consumer responses. However, inconsistencies between corporate actions and consumer expectations can be deeply
damaging, often provoking negative reactions [65]. GW exemplifies such a breach of trust, as brands falsely portray
environmental commitments while neglecting meaningful ecological action [66]. This perceived deception not only
undermines brand credibility but also evokes strong emotional responses such as anger and betrayal, key elements of
BH [48, 75].
EVT offers a useful lens to understand this dynamic, as it suggests that violations of ethical and environmental expec-
tations trigger intense emotional reactions [65]. In Islamic countries such as Yemen, where Islamic teachings strongly
emphasize integrity, deception in sustainability claims is not only perceived as unethical but may also be viewed as a
violation of religious principles [52]. Moreover, a collectivist culture fosters strong community-oriented decision-making,
meaning that negative sentiments toward a brand can quickly spread through social networks, increasing the likelihood
of BH as a collective emotional response [55]. Indeed, when consumers realize they have been misled, their disappoint-
ment can escalate into more extreme negative emotions, such as BH [49]. This emotional trajectory highlights how GW
practices, seen as morally irresponsible, exacerbate consumer resentment and dissatisfaction [1 ]. The literature further

H1 GW is positively related to BH.
3.1.1  Brand hate and consumer‑brand boycott
Customers often boycott a brand when they perceive its actions as harmful or ethically unacceptable [54]. This behav -
ior extends beyond simple brand avoidance, which involves passive distancing from a brand to a more active strategy
aimed at achieving social or personal goals [50, 63]. CBB is frequently driven by consumers’ desire to influence corpo -
rate practices, express self-identity, or uphold moral standards [30, 63]. EVT suggests that when brands breach ethical
expectations, consumers may retaliate through behaviors aimed at penalizing the brand [59]. This is particularly relevant
in Yemen, where collectivist and religious values heighten the moral obligation to reject deceptive companies. In this
context, boycotts may not be purely individual actions, but collective movements driven by societal norms and shared
ethical standards [55].
These strong emotional reactions often motivate consumers to retaliate against brands through boycotts, not just as a
form of self-expression but also as a moral obligation to challenge unethical practices [48, 63]. Scholars have highlighted
the connection between BH and boycotting behavior,for instance, Atwal et al. [19] examined how BH unfolds in stages,
influencing collective action, while Muhamad et al. [55] found that BH often stems from a need for self-improvement and
social justice. Collectively, these findings emphasize that consumers’ desire to restore their self-worth or seek retribution
against deceptive brands can trigger organized or individual boycotts [63]. Therefore, we argue that customers who
develop intense negative feelings toward a brand are more likely to boycott it as a means of preserving their self-worth
and moral integrity. Consequently, we assume that:
H2 BH is positively related to CBB.
3.1.2  Brand hate and consumer‑brand sabotage
Furthermore, research in psychology highlights that hateful feelings often drive individuals to seek retribution through
harmful actions when their self-worth or group values are threatened. In consumer behavior, such hostility manifests as
acts aimed at degrading or damaging a brand, often fueled by perceived brand misconduct or ideological incompatibility
[63]. This negative emotion, referred to as BH, has been shown to prompt consumers to engage in aggressive behaviors,
including CBS, where consumers deliberately harm a brand’s reputation, frequently foregoing personal gain to achieve
this [29, 74]. Digital platforms have further empowered consumers to publicly expose and discredit brands they perceive
as deceitful, amplifying the impact of CBS [63].
The enduring nature of BH often results in sustained rumination, wherein consumers continuously dwell on negative
experiences, fostering persistent hostility toward the brand [2 , 48]. From the perspective of EVT, this hostile escalation
occurs when brands breach ethical or environmental expectations, leaving consumers feeling betrayed and driven to
seek vengeance [6]. In a cultural context such as Yemen, where collective justice and social responsibility are emphasized,
consumers may feel an ethical duty to warn others about deceptive brands. Religious and moral imperatives play a key
role in encouraging consumers to actively discourage engagement with companies that engage in unethical practices
[55]. Empirical evidence supports this relationship, showing that BH significantly increases the likelihood of CBS as con-
sumers retaliate against brands for unmet ethical expectations [63]. Thus, we suggest that:
H3 BH is positively related to CBS.
3.1.3  Moderating role of environmental concern
Addressing consumers’ EC is increasingly recognized as a strategic approach to enhancing brand preference and loyalty
[46]. Research demonstrates that environmentally conscious consumers are more likely to engage in pro-environmental
behaviors, such as recycling and purchasing eco-friendly products, and tend to reward companies that align with their

4  Methods
4.1  Sample
To achieve the study’s objectives, a quantitative approach was adopted, where data was gathered using a questionnaire
tool from a sample of consumers in Yemen over 2 months (March–April, 2024). A convenience sampling method was
adopted to collect the required data to analyze the research model due to the lack of an available sampling frame. Con-
venience sampling is “a non-probability sampling method where participants from the target population are selected
based on their accessibility, ease of contact, and willingness to participate” [67]. This approach is commonly utilized in
business research to address issues like non-response bias and low participation rates [70]. Its practicality lies in being
cost-effective and straightforward, enabling researchers to easily access respondents with similar demographic profiles
[38]. This method is commonly used in similar research studies, such as those by Al Koliby et al. [4] and Al-Swidi et al. [14].
The survey was shared via email and WhatsApp groups to reach a diverse and extensive range of respondents.
Prior to distributing the questionnaire, we conducted a two-stage pretest to minimize potential bias in data collection
and guarantee the scale’s validity and reliability. In the first stage, several academics and professionals specializing in
marketing reviewed the wording and purpose of the questions. In the second stage, following the guidance of Perneger
et al. [8], we solicited feedback from 10 participants regarding the question format, wording, length, and sequence. Based
on their feedback, we made only minor adjustments, primarily rearranging the order of some questions, before finalizing
the questionnaire for data collection.
Initially, 500 questionnaires were distributed, clearly explaining the topic and purpose of the survey. Out of these, 398
were received, while 391 were deemed complete and suitable for analysis, resulting in a response rate of 78%. This rate
is relatively high compared to those reported in previous studies [ 12, 18]. The final sample comprised 391 responses,
meeting the requirements for conducting “partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM)” as outlined by

4.2  Measures
In this study, we employed a questionnaire to gather the data necessary for evaluating the relationships within the
proposed model. Regarding the measures of the study variables, the variable measurement items were adapted from
previous studies. Responses were evaluated using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 5 (strongly agree) to 1 (strongly
disagree).
Overall, five items were used to measure GW adapted from Santos et al. [65]. BH was measured with five items adapted
from Sameeni et al. [63], while negative consumer behaviors were measured with nine items adopted form Sameeni et al.
[63], which evaluated CBB (four items) and CBS (five items). Finally, the moderator variable was measured with four items
adopted from Ha [ 35]. Indeed, the rationale for selecting EC, as a moderator, stems from previous research indicating
that consumers with higher levels of environmental concern tend to be more sensitive to corporate deception regarding
ecological claims [73]. By including EC as a moderator, we aim to test whether consumers with higher environmental
concern are more likely to respond negatively to GW, thereby offering insights into how personal values related to
environmental sustainability shape consumer emotions and behaviors. Appendix 1 shows the items of the variables.
4.3  Common method bias
To evaluate the potential for common method bias (CMB), we conducted Harman’s single-factor test in accordance with
the methodology established by Podsakoff and Organ [5]. This test is a well-established method for determining whether
a single factor accounts for a large portion of the variance in the data, which could suggest that CMB is affecting the
results. Our analysis indicated that the first factor explained 29.33% of the total variance within the dataset. This figure
is significantly below the 50.0% threshold typically used as a standard in CMB evaluations. Consequently, these results
imply that CMB is not a major concern in our study, thereby enhancing the validity of our findings and reinforcing the
reliability of the conclusions drawn from the data.
5  Data analysis and results
To analyze the proposed model in this study, the PLS-SEM approach was utilized through SmartPLS 4, following the
guidelines established by Ringle et al. [62]. The widespread use of PLS-SEM in management research is attributed to
its numerous advantages [32, 33]. Interestingly, PLS-SEM offers higher statistical power compared to covariance-based
SEM (CB-SEM), especially when dealing with complex models and smaller sample sizes, making it particularly suitable
for prediction-focused research [16, 32, 41]. In summary, the PLS model consists of two interconnected components: the
“measurement model” and the “structural model. ”

should be greater than 0.60 [21], Cronbach’s alpha, and composite reliability (CR), which should be above 0.70, following
the guidelines provided by Hair et al. [37]. As shown in Table 2, the values of all parameters exceeded the cutoff, indicat-
ing the reliability and validity of the model.

more likely they are to develop an intense hate or resentment towards that brand. The result emphasizes that brands
should ensure their ecological claims are genuine and not merely marketing tactics, as failing to do so can severely dam-
age their reputation and consumer loyalty. Indeed, consumers in Yemen may view misleading environmental claims not

As expected, the significant paths from BH to CBB and CBS further underscore the detrimental impacts of BH on con-
sumer actions, leading to substantial negative outcomes for brands. This in turn significantly increases both CBB and
CBS. These results are consistent with the previous studies the revealed that BH has an effect on CBB and CBS (e.g., [63]).
They also support the notion that the decision to boycott is motivated by internal factors, such as BH [55]. In this sense,
deceptive ecological claims by brands (i.e., GW) lead to strong negative emotions, such as BH among consumers, which
then drive negative behaviors, such as CBB and CBS. In this context, consumer-brand boycott—the refusal to purchase or
support a brand—can be seen as a collective action rooted in social and moral responsibility. Consumers in Yemen may
boycott brands not only as an expression of personal dissatisfaction but also to protect their community from deception
and to uphold shared values of honesty and transparency. Similarly, consumer-brand sabotage—more active forms of
harming the brand, such as spreading negative word-of-mouth or actively dissuading others from engaging with the
brand—can arise from a deep sense of injustice. In a community-oriented culture like Yemen’s, consumers may feel a
duty to warn others about deceptive practices, especially when it involves claims related to the environment or social
responsibility, which are becoming increasingly important globally.
Interestingly, the results also demonstrate that EC does not moderate the relationship between GW and BH. While
prior studies (e.g., [27, 35]) suggest that consumers with high EC tend to react more negatively to GW, our results did not
support the moderating role of EC in the GW-BH relationship. A potential explanation for this finding is that in Yemen,
ethical and religious norms play a stronger role in shaping consumer responses to deceptive environmental claims than
individual variations in EC. In other words, the moral and collective condemnation of GW may be so deeply ingrained
that even consumers with low EC exhibit strong negative reactions to corporate deception. This highlights an important
cultural distinction: while EC may be a key determinant of consumer reactions to GW in more individualistic societies,
in collectivist cultures, societal norms and ethical values appear to exert a stronger influence. This aligns with previous
findings in collectivist cultures, where shared ethical values often drive consumer behaviors more than personal atti-
tudes [55]. In contrast, our results differ from those in Western and individualistic societies, which found that EC strongly
moderates the GW-BH relationship (e.g., [73]).
Another plausible explanation is that EC, as measured in our study, may not fully capture the complexity of environ-
mental awareness in Yemen. Given the country’s socio-economic challenges, consumers may prioritize immediate eco-
nomic concerns over environmental sustainability, leading to a more uniform response to GW across different levels of EC.
These findings indicate that further investigation is needed to fully understand the dynamics between GW, BH, and EC.
6.1  Implications for theory
This study offers significant theoretical contributions by extending EVT in the context of GW. Previous research on EVT
primarily explores expectancy violations in interpersonal and organizational settings. Our findings demonstrate that
GW constitutes a salient violation of ethical and environmental expectations, especially in socio-cultural contexts where
honesty and collective welfare are highly valued. Specifically, we show that unmet expectations regarding corporate
environmental claims evoke intense negative emotions, such as BH, and drive retaliatory consumer behaviors, includ-
ing boycotts and sabotage. Moreover, while prior studies (e.g., [65]) primarily focus on the reputational and financial
consequences of GW, our research uniquely highlights the emotional and behavioral impacts. By situating GW within
the EVT framework, we contribute to a deeper understanding of how ethical violations influence consumer psychology
and actions. Notably, our study addresses an underexplored cultural dimension by examining these dynamics in Yemen,

Additionally, our findings challenge existing assumptions about the moderating role of EC. Contrary to expectations,
EC did not significantly influence the GW–BH relationship. This suggests that the emotional responses to GW may be
universal, overriding individual differences in environmental awareness. Future research could explore alternative mod-
erators, such as trust in corporate claims or general skepticism toward marketing practices, to refine theoretical models
further.
Moreover, Yemen’s cultural and religious context plays a crucial role in shaping consumer reactions to GW, where
Yemen’s socio-cultural fabric is deeply rooted in values of integrity, communal welfare, and ethical behavior. These values
heighten sensitivity to deceptive practices, particularly those perceived as exploiting environmental or social concerns
for commercial gain. For instance, misleading ecological claims are viewed not only as corporate dishonesty but also as
violations of ethical and religious standards, intensifying consumer backlash. Comparing our findings with studies con-
ducted in other regions reveals both similarities and differences. For example, research from China [73] and Portugal [65]
also identifies brand hate and boycotts as responses to GW. However, the intensity of these reactions in Yemen appears
amplified by the cultural emphasis on honesty and community welfare. This underscores the need for global brands to
consider cultural nuances when designing and implementing sustainability claims.
Finally, our findings have implications for the generalizability of GW research. While the emotional and behavioral
impacts identified here may resonate across cultures, the specific triggers and intensities are likely shaped by local socio-
economic and cultural conditions. Researchers should exercise caution when applying these results to more industrialized
or culturally distinct contexts.
6.2  Implications for practice
The study also offers several practical implications for companies and marketers. Essentially, companies should be aware
of the potential negative consequences of GW. The findings underscore the importance of honest and transparent mar-
keting practices, as deceptive claims about ecological benefits can lead to significant consumer backlash in the form
of BH, CBB, and CBS. In the context of Yemen, where ethical and religious values are significant, consumers may react
particularly strongly to perceived deception, making brands vulnerable to collective consumer actions. Therefore, brands
operating in Yemen and similar contexts should be acutely aware of the cultural and ethical sensitivities surrounding
ecological claims. To avoid greenwashing, businesses should adopt transparent and verifiable sustainability practices.
This includes adhering to recognized environmental standards, such as ISO 14001, and providing third-party certifica-
tions to substantiate their environmental claims. Clear and honest communication about sustainability efforts is crucial,
avoiding vague or exaggerated claims that may mislead consumers. Instead, companies should focus on providing
tangible evidence of their environmental initiatives, such as measurable reductions in carbon emissions, water conserva-
tion efforts, or sustainable sourcing practices. Moreover, businesses should engage in proactive consumer education by
openly sharing progress reports, sustainability impact assessments, and corporate responsibility initiatives. Establishing
open channels for consumer feedback and responding to concerns transparently can help maintain trust and credibility.
Companies should also integrate sustainability into their core business strategy rather than treating it as a marketing
tool, ensuring long-term commitment to environmental responsibility rather than short-term public relations gains.
Additionally, considering the current study’s findings, companies should develop effective crisis management and
communication strategies to mitigate the effects of BH. Given that BH can lead to severe consumer reactions, businesses
should have crisis management plans in place that emphasize prompt and genuine responses to allegations of GW.
If a company is accused of greenwashing, it should acknowledge concerns, take corrective action, and communicate
improvements transparently to rebuild consumer trust. Additionally, firms can enhance their reputation by engaging with
environmental organizations and collaborating with stakeholders to develop credible, science-based sustainability goals.
Finally, marketers should consider the varying impacts of GW on different consumer segments. While EC was not
found to moderate the GW-BH relationship, it remains a significant consumer attribute that can influence perceptions
and behaviors in other contexts. Therefore, brands should not assume that environmentally conscious consumers are
the only ones affected by GW; all consumers in Yemen are likely to respond similarly.

6.3  Limitations and future research
The limitations of this study create opportunities for future research. First, this study employed convenience sampling,
which may affect the generalizability of the results. Although it is practical and efficient for data collection, does not guar-
antee that the sample is representative of the broader population, requiring caution should be exercised when applying
these findings to other contexts. Therefore, future research could employ probability sampling techniques to enhance the
external validity of the results. Second, while our focus on Yemen provides valuable cultural insights, expanding the study
to include multiple countries would offer a broader comparative perspective. Third, the unexpected non-significance of
EC as a moderator warrants further investigation. Future research should explore alternative models by integrating trust
or skepticism toward corporate sustainability claims as potential moderators instead of EC and examine how these factors
interact with cultural and contextual variables, which might have a stronger influence on the relationship between GW
and BH. Further qualitative research could also provide deeper insights into the underlying psychological mechanisms
that drive consumer responses to GW. Fourth, the study focused exclusively on direct relationships within the proposed
model, and neither indirect effect nor mediation analysis of BH was explored. Therefore, future research could extend this
framework by testing the mediating role of BH, and utilizing bootstrapping techniques to estimate indirect effects. Such
analyses would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying the relationships and their
theoretical implications. Lastly, qualitative or mixed-method approaches could deepen our understanding of the psy -
chological mechanisms underlying consumer responses to GW, offering richer insights into this complex phenomenon.
