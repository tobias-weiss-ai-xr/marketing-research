Abstract. This article offers a game-theoretic survey of retailer discount policies, discussing
both the rationale for retailer discounting and the structure and persistence of the discounts
from the viewpoint of strategic reasoning. The review builds on three analytical levels—
supply chain interactions, market competition and consumer behavior—to bring together
theoretical and empirical works to show that discounts are strategic tools, not just reductions
in prices. Discount contract is a coordination mechanism at the supply chain level that can
help to reduce double marginalization, but its effectiveness will be determined by the cost
structure, the contract structure and the bargaining power distribution. At the market
competition level, discounting is a strategy choice, as is reflected by the choice of everyday
low prices (ELP) versus high-low pricing (HLP), with different equilibrium strategies, and
promotional pricing may be a means to maintain implicit collusion. Standard demand
assumption – discount effectiveness depends on interaction with reference prices, as well as
loss aversion, at the consumer behavior level. The article suggests that a full understanding
of retail discounting can only be achieved when all three levels are considered together, and
that game theory will continue to be an essential component for the understanding of the
strategic complexity of discount policies and will have implications for retail strategy,
supply chain coordination, and antitrust evaluation.
Keywords: Retail discounting, Game theory, Supply chain coordination, Price competition
1. Introduction
One of the most noticeable and significant decisions that enterprises make with regard to their prices
is retail discounting. Enter a supermarket, any e-commerce app or just take a glance at the weekly
promotion poster, you will see some kind of price cut, either percentage based or buy one get one
free or exclusive to loyalty members. On the surface, it's nothing but a simple marketing strategy;
but in reality, it's a product of suppliers, rival retailers and consumers pursuing their own self-
interest. These actors' actions create a complex strategic calculation as a result [1]. A natural
language for analyzing these interactions is provided by game theory, which enables the to model
each party as a rational decision-maker whose payoff is dependent on the decisions made by the
other parties.
So, the question of this article is: Why do retailers discount? How can game-theoretic reasoning
help one understand discount policies and their structure and continuity? These questions are
important because discounting is not the transfer of the seller's surplus to the buyer. Its impacts on

supply chain participants and can lead to price wars. But what is actually required is not only
descriptive marketing research, it's also formal strategic analysis, to understand these dynamics.
There are a number of game-theoretic models that are relevant. Stackelberg game is a model that
describes that one player in the supply chain, typically the manufacturer or the powerful retailer,
takes action first and the other player acts optimally [2]. Discount Nash equilibrium framework can
be used to analyze simultaneous price competition among retailers [2]. Repeated games can account
for the fact that although the use of promotional pricing may not be collectively optimal, it can still
become an equilibrium, as Lal [3] illustrates in the case of competitive encroachment. Recent
behavioral game theory incorporates ideas from prospect theory – namely reference dependent
preferences and loss aversion – to account for the fact that consumers may be scrutinising discounts
against internal reference prices instead of just the absolute prices [4].
Independent businesses have been known to fail to achieve maximum total channel profits
because of double marginalization [5]. This inefficiency can be partly remedied by incentives
through the use of price discount contracts, but the effectiveness of these contracts will depend on
where the bargaining power lies and who bears the costs [6]. The everyday low pricing vs. high-low
pricing decision is a reflection of a fundamental difference in strategy, rather than a marketing
preference in competitive markets [7]. Consumer perception of the discount may differ from the
actual discount, depending on different reference prices [8]. These observations indicate that retail
discounting ought to be analyzed on three fronts: the supply chain, competition in the market and
consumer behavior.
This article comprises four sections. The literature regarding retailer discounting, including game
theory (GT) research, is surveyed at three levels of analysis: the supply chain relationship, the
competition among retailers, and the point of view of consumers. The implications of these findings
are discussed in Section 3 and limitations and future directions for research are identified. The
objective of the complete text is to demonstrate that game theories are not merely an abstract
modeling game, but a useful tool to help understand the form of discount policies.
2. Literature review
2.1. Supply chain level
At the supply chain level, discount policies are best understood as coordination mechanisms. When
manufacturers and retailers operate as independent enterprises, they maximize their own profits, and
the resulting equilibrium often fails to maximize total channel profit. This is known as "double
marginalization" [5]. This inefficiency can be addressed by pricing discount contracts (which can
align the incentives). Cai et al. [6] considered simple price discount contracts in the 2-channel
supply chain with a supplier selling both directly via the internet and indirectly through an
independent retail channel. They discovered that price discount contracts are found in the supplier
Stackelberg, the retailer Stackelberg and the Nash game. You get a better result than you would if
you didn't have a contract. This finding is important because, despite the simplicity of the discount
scheme, it demonstrates that a discount scheme can decrease channel conflict when consideration is
given to the bargaining power of the contracting parties when designing the contract.
It is crucial who the Stackelberg leader is. A supplier-led chain is one in which the manufacturer
determines the wholesale price, whilst the retailer determines the retail price and makes promotional
decisions. As large retail chains with large market power becomes more and more prevalent, the

De Giovanni et al. [10] carry out a dynamic analysis, in which a consignment contract is used
together with a cooperative advertising or price discount program. They do especially well and their
results are inspiring. The cooperative advertising program is a profit-Pareto improving program
when the manufacturer decides to choose it; that is, the retailer is always better off. If the
manufacturer decides to use price discount, the retailer can always be worse off because the only
discount is on the retailer's margin. But, if production costs are sufficiently high, the price discount
mechanism will turn out to be Pareto-improving, as well: the decrease in the retail price will lead to
more than enough additional demand to compensate for the margin compression. Low production
costs gives retailers an incentive not to discount and they would like to discount at zero. This
discovery indicates that retailing discount policy is dependent on cost conditions and may or may
not be desirable from the retailer's point of view. This is the end result of the retailers' reluctance to
discount.
Cachon [11] takes a further step and looks at contracts with advance purchase discount. Retailers
can give an advance discount to persuade consumers to buy early, relieving retailers from inventory
risks and enhancing the supply chain efficiency under certain demand distributions, as shown in his
model. Its strategic rationale is akin to quantity discounts: discounts are not only price reductions
but also are a way of allocating risk and information. Vertical restraints, also in the form of discount
schemes, can also be interpreted as double marginalization and opportunistic behavior, noted Tirole
[12]. Overall, it can be concluded that supply chain discount policies is more of a contract design
problem than a simple pricing decision.
2.2. Market competition
Discount policies can be strategic weapons when multiple retailers are competing for the same set of
consumers. There is a classic tension between everyday low pricing (EDLP) and high-low (Hi-Lo) –
which offers more or less regular high prices with deep discounts at certain moments. Park et al. [7]
set up a game-theoretic model of this choice and conducted econometric analysis based on store-
level data. Their results reveal that the equilibrium price of an EDLP retailer primarily depends on
his own cost level and not on the price level of the competitor, while the equilibrium price of a Hi-
Lo retailer primarily depends on the product collection (promotional basket) he chooses to discount
and the level of his cost. This imbalance is more than just that: EDLP retailers try to compete on cost
efficiency; Hi-Lo retailers try to compete on promotional intensity, and they're not the same retailer.
Lal gave an early and seminal game-theoretic account of the use of promotion and why it
continues in the oligopolistic case. Under the assumption that the number of switchers is sufficiently
large and the enterprises' discount rate is sufficiently low, he argued that an alternating promotion
can be a subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium of an infinite-horizon repeated game with three
enterprises and a market consisting of switchers and loyal consumers. This is the same as an implicit
collusion as an ongoing strategy, leading firms provide promotion to ward off the potential third
entrants and share the defense costs. A non-obvious takeaway is that apparently "counterproductive"
discounting may contribute to the stabilization of the market structure.

Behavioural and informational complexity was added to the scenario in later research. Park et al.
[7] demonstrate that for retailers, when consumers are waiting in anticipation of discount offers, they
have to decide between building demand and managing expectations. In a repeated oligopoly
setting, Tirole [12] showed that with a high discount factor and observed actions, price wars and
promotion cycles can be equilibria. Gibbons [2] put these feelings into form, with the folk theorem:
a variety of outcomes can be sustained as long as enterprises interact frequently enough and take
sufficient care to consider future profits, such as continuous discounting. The literature illustrates
that, while both the standard Bertrand and Stackelberg settings are easy to analyze, they might
underestimate the strategic complexity of the actual retail contest: it is a contest of responses from
competitors and expectations from consumers.
2.3. Consumer behavior
A typical assumption of game-theoretic discounting models is that consumers are reacting to levels
of prices. But an increasing number of studies documented how consumers respond to price
changes, based on internal reference points, and how this reference dependence has a systematic
effect of retailer discounting strategies. The theoretical foundation is based on prospect theory of
Kahneman and Tversky [4]: People perceive outcome as a gain or loss rather than a quantity. Losses
are coded as larger than equivalent gains and a price below the reference price is coded as a gain,
whereas a price above the reference price is coded as a loss.
Dynamic game models of the supply chain implications of reference effects are formalized. Jindal
et al. [8] investigated a vertical chain with some consumers having reference-dependent demand
with respect to the manufacturer's suggested retail price. They discovered that when the suggested
price is considered in equilibrium, the price will not be raised by retailers but will be lowered or the
same. Discounts will be offered when competition is intense, the percentage of consumers
susceptible to reference cues is high, and the high price recommendations are credible. This is a
discovery that sheds light on why MSRP (manufacturer suggested retail price) often serve as a
starting point for retailers to discount, and why discount isn't just a matter of price reduction, it's a
matter of change.
This behavioral mechanism has been confirmed by experimental evidence. As demonstrated by
Ailawadi et al. [13], promotion induced-stockpiling interacts with reference prices, and alters
consumers' future purchase expectations. Neslin [14] examined the literature on sales promotion and
summarized the findings regarding the strong influence that the presentation of a discount (as a
percentage reduction, as a dollar amount, or by comparing with the advertised reference price) has
on the perception of monetary gain and may create preferences between otherwise identical offers.
For retailers, this means that the discount form is not a subordinate of the economic reality, but the
presentation of the discount is part of the decision. Blattberg and Neslin [1] also pointed out that
promotion management must be familiar with the economic and psychological aspects of price cuts.
2.4. Summary
From the literature above, three insights are revealed: First, at the supply chain level, discount
policies can be seen as coordination devices and their effectiveness is related to cost conditions, the
structure of the contract and the distribution of bargaining power between the manufacturer and the
retailer. While simple price discount contracts may be superior to no-contract situations, the gains
from such a contract may not be equally divided, and when cost conditions are favorable, retailers
may not want to discount at all [10].

Second, discounting is a market competition level strategic choice in repeated games. In addition,
the EDLP–Hi-Lo binary is descriptive as well as indicative of different equilibrium strategies,
namely EDLP firms compete on cost and Hi-Lo firms compete on promotional intensity [7]. That
the intuition behind the notion of discount is competitive is challenged by Lal's [3] analysis, which
demonstrates that such discounts can sustain implicit collusion.
Third, for the level of consumer's behaviour, the effectiveness of a discount depends on the
interaction with consumers' reference price and loss aversion. When the price drops, consumers buy
less; only when the price is lower than their expected price (or below their reference price) will they
buy more [4]. This insight into consumer behavior introduces a nuance into the typical demand
curve and suggests that retailers can proactively control the reference price through suggested retail
prices, ads or timing the promotion—a choice that can be as intentional as setting a price.
The three levels are not mutually exclusive. The cost structure in competitive pricing decisions is
determined by the supply chain contracts, while competitive dynamics affect consumers' reference
prices. Thus, the complete game-theoretic analysis of retail discounting needs to take all three levels
into account at the same time, even though the tractability requirements of the analysis may involve
examining one level at a time.
3. Discussion
The theory and practice of retailer discount policies has several implications from the game-
theoretic literature. The key takeaway is that discounts are not only price reductions – they are
strategic tools. When a retailer discounts he/she may be taken aback by the outcome if the retailer
doesn't think about the action suppliers will take, the reaction of competitors, and the reference price
updates by consumers. As detailed in the models in this article, a 20% price cut to the same model
can result in coordination or conflict, in gains or losses in market share, in an increase or a loss in
consumer surplus, depending on the strategic context in which it resides.
The second implication is on the role of information and timing. Stackelberg models indicate that
it could be more beneficial to play first, but not always. Cai et al. [6] found that in the dual-channel
supply chain they studied, it is possible that the leader can gain or lose benefits depending on what
kind of contractual terms it offers and conflicts of channel. Likewise, under repeated game models,
the viability of the promotional equilibria is affected by the firms' discount rates and the share of
switching consumers [3]. These results warn not to draw any definite conclusions: game theory is
conditional, it is not universal.
The take-home message for retail managers is that there are three points to consider in designing
discount policy. Firstly, what is the context within the supply chain? A successful price reduction for
a manufacturer-driven chain may not be successful for a retailer-driven chain and vice versa [9].
Secondly, what is the competition configuration? The best practice for the depth and frequency of
discounts depends upon the type of environment: EDLP or Hi-Lo, and the market type: oligopolistic
or monopolistically competitive [7]. Thirdly, how do consumers develop their reference prices? A
large absolute discount can be considered small if the price to be discounted is high, but a small
absolute discount can be considered generous if the price to be discounted is very low [8].
The current literature has limitations, indicating further research directions. Most of the game-
theoretic retail discounting models are based on complete rationality and common knowledge of the
game structure. Behavioral game theory has started to lift up these assumptions; however, the
incorporation of reference-dependent preferences into dynamic models of the supply chain is still
not fully complete [4, 10]. Moreover, the empirical testing of game-theoretic predictions has been
behind in comparison with the development of the theory. Work such as that by Park et al. [7] in

The last observation relates to the normative aspect. Discount policies may therefore have
antitrust implications that go beyond a simple consumer-welfare. A discount, which may seem
favourable to the consumers for the short period, may ultimately, in the equilibrium position,
decrease the competition, which, in turn, may cause the prices to rise in the long run. This potential
does not imply that cuts can be bad, but it does indicate that the welfare impacts of cuts are not as
straightforward as they might seem. It is this analytical machinery that is provided by game theory,
making it an indispensable tool for understanding retailer discount policies, and for evaluating the
above effects rigorously.
4. Conclusion
This review has studied the retailer discount policies in the light of game theory, which is a synthesis
of literature in three analytical levels: interactions among the supply chain, competition among
markets and consumer behavior. The main point is that discounts are strategic tools that can have
various effects depending on the strategic context in which they are used. In terms of the supply
chain, the idea of discount policies is a coordination mechanism which can help to align
manufacturers and retailers incentives, and reduce double marginalization. But they depend on cost
conditions, the structure of the contract, and bargaining power distribution. The impact of discount
contracts is highly dependent on who the Stackelberg leader is, and, even if feasible, it may be
advantageous for the retailers to avoid discounting under certain cost conditions. Discounting is not
just a competitive reaction in the market competition level, it is a strategy in repeated games. The
EDLP–Hi-Lo dichotomy is evidence of different equilibrium strategies and the same applies to the
promotion price where the intuition would otherwise be that the provision of a discount is inherently
competitive. Discount effectiveness at consumer behaviour level is determined by the reference
prices and loss aversion which means that retailers can manipulate reference prices through
suggested retail prices, advertising and timing discounts in a strategic way. These three levels are
interdependent with each other: supply chain contracts drive cost structures, competitive dynamics
drive reference prices, and consumers' reactions drive strategy sustainability. For Retail Managers,
designing discount policy is a question of context of the supply chain, competitive structures, and
how the consumer's reference price is formed. Future research should give effect to the influence of
reference-dependent preferences in dynamic supply chain models, and test the game-theoretic
predictions using data from actual retail promotions. Lastly, discount policies may have antitrust
implications other than those implied by a simple consumer-welfare calculation if they can be used
to maintain implicit collusion. This is the analytical machinery provided by game theory and it
remains as a powerful tool to analyze the complex welfare effects rigorously and remains
indispensable in understanding retailer discount policies.
