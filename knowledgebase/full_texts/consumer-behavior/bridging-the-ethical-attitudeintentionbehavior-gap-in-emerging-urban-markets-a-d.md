Abstract
Although interest in ethical consumption is rising both globally and in Vietnam, actual purchasing
behavior continues to lag substantially behind stated attitudes and intentions - in Hanoi, while 72%
of consumers express willingness to pay a premium for green products, only 12 –18% maintain this
habit daily, reflecting a classic attitude–intention–behavior gap that remains insufficiently theorized
in the context of emerging urban markets. Existing literature applies the Theory of Planned Behavior,
Value–Belief–Norm theory, and institutional theory in a fragmented manner, while studies conducted
in Vietnam rely almost exclusively on primary-survey testing of isolated factors, lacking a theoretical
synthesis grounded in a specific institutional context. Using a desk-research method - systematically
synthesizing, analyzing, evaluating, and comparing academic literature and published secondary
data on Hanoi - this paper integrates the three theoretical frameworks into a unified conceptual
model in which institutional trust, greenwashing skepticism, and price–value trade-offs serve as three
central moderating mechanisms, illustrated by the empirical finding that all “Vietnam Green Label”
certifications have been inactive since 2020. Four theoretical propositions (P1–P4) are advanced as
a foundation for future empirical testing. The paper contributes theoretically by extending
institutional theory to the level of individual consumer behavior, while offering practical implications
for businesses, certification bodies, and policymakers in transitional urban economies.

Keywords: ethical consumption; attitude–intention–behavior gap; institutional trust; greenwashing
skepticism; emerging market; Hanoi, Vietnam

1. Introduction
Over the past two decades, ethical consumption h as shifted from a peripheral concern to a central
research domain in consumer behavior and business ethics (Arman & Mark-Herbert, 2024). Yet this
rise in awareness has not been matched by a proportional increase in actual purchasing behavior: even
in the U nited States - one of the most mature markets for sustainable consumption - Consumer
Packaged Goods (CPG) products marketed with sustainability attributes reached only an 18.5%
market share in 2023, despite this representing the largest single -year increase in five years (NYU
Stern Center for Sustainable Business & Circana, 2024). In Vietnam, this gap is even more
pronounced: according to a survey by NielsenIQ in partnership with the Vietnam Business Council
for Sustainable Development (VBCSD), 72% of consu mers report a willingness to pay a premium
for green products, yet only 12–18% of residents in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City maintain this habit
daily (as cited via the Ministry of Industry and Trade portal, 2025). This near -60-percentage-point
gap is a quant itative illustration of the phenomenon international scholarship terms the attitude –
intention–behavior gap, first systematically theorized by Carrington et al. (2010), who showed that
this gap depends on consumers’ degree of prior planning, their actual be havioral control, and the
situational context of purchasing.  Explanations of how intention forms typically draw on Ajzen’s
(1991) Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and Stern’s (2000) Value –Belief–Norm theory (VBN).

More recently, two complementary theoretical streams have proven closely relevant: the concept of
institutional voids - describing conditions in which market-supporting institutions are absent, weak,
or fail to fulfill their expected role (Mair & Marti, 2009) - and greenwashing skepticism, which erodes
green trust and thereby impedes the translation of intention into behavior (de Sio et al., 2022).
The problem is that these three theoretical streams are currently applied in a fragmented manner,
lacking a systematic synthesis that situates them within the context of emerging urban markets. Even
within Vietnam, existing studies - such as Le et al.’s (2020) work on generational and gender
differences in ethical consumption - remain confined to empirical testing via primary surveys, while
the most recent global systematic review (Arman & Mark -Herbert, 2024) is universal in scope and
not anchored to any specific institutional context.
Motivated by this gap, the present study asks: what mechanisms help bridge the attitude –behavior
gap in ethical consumption in emerging urban markets, and how do they operate in Hanoi? Using a
desk-research method, the paper pursues three contributions: (i) integrating the three theoretical
frameworks into a unified conceptual model; (ii) on the practical/managerial front, providing a basis
for retailers, manufacturers, and certification bodies in Hanoi to redesign communication,
certification, and pricing strategies aimed at genuinely narrowing - rather than merely describing -
the 60-percentage-point gap noted above; and (ii i) offering policy implications for improving eco -
label transparency, a barrier repeatedly identified as a root cause in Hanoi -based surveys. The
remainder of the paper proceeds as follows: Section 2 presents the methodology; Section 3 reviews
the theoretical foundations; Section 4 analyzes the Hanoi context; Section 5 proposes the integrated
conceptual framework; Sections 6–7 offer discussion and conclusion.

2. Research Design and Methodology
This study adopts a desk -research method - systematically synthesizing, analyzing, evaluating, and
comparing published literature - rather than collecting primary data, because the paper’s core
objective is theory -building through the integration of existing conceptual frameworks into a new
institutional context, rather than testing a specific hypothesis. According to Snyder (2019), when the
research purpose is to synthesize dispersed knowledge in order to generate a new conceptual
framework or research agenda, a structured literature review is a more appropriate choice  than
empirical research, which can only test relationships that have already been theorized.

Literature Search Strategy. The literature search and selection process followed the spirit of the
Scientific Procedures and Rationales for Systematic Literature Reviews (SPAR -4-SLR) protocol of
Paul et al. (2021), comprising three stages: (i) Assembling - searching the Scopus, Web of Science,
and Google Scholar databases using the keyword clusters “ethical consumption,” “attitude–behavior
gap,” “green purchase intention,” “institutional voids,” and “greenwashing skepticism,” combined
with “Vietnam”/“Hanoi”; (ii) Arranging - classifying the literature into three theoretical thematic
groups (Theory of Planned Behavior, Value–Belief–Norm theory, institutional theory) and removing
duplicate or non -peer-reviewed sources; and (iii) Assessing - prioritizing peer -reviewed works
published between 2015 and 2026 to ensure currency, while retaining certain earlier foundational
works (e.g., Ajzen, 1991; Stern, 2000) whose core theoretical value remains unsuperseded.

Secondary Data Sources. Alongside academic literature, the study draws on two supplementary
categories of secondary data to anchor the theoretical argument in practice: (i) published market
survey reports, such as the NielsenIQ survey conducted with the Vietnam Business Council f or
Sustainable Development and the Sustainable Market Share Index of the NYU Stern Center for
Sustainable Business, both cited in Section 1; and (ii) publicly available institutional data, specifically
the World Bank’s Worldwide Governance Indicators (World Bank, 2025), used in Section 4 to
position Vietnam’s institutional quality within a regional comparison.

Analytical Method. The literature was processed using thematic synthesis following the three steps

3. Theoretical Foundations
3.1. Conceptualizing the Attitude–Intention–Behavior Gap: Global Evidence
As noted in Section 1, the attitude–intention–behavior gap was theorized by Carrington et al. (2010)
as a function of three factors: consumers’ degree of prior planning, actual behavioral control, and the
situational context of purchasing. Quantitative evi dence suggests this is not a phenomenon specific
to ethical consumption but a general psychological regularity: synthesizing ten prior meta -analyses,
Sheeran (2002) estimated a sample-weighted average correlation between intention and behavior of
only r ≈ 0.53 - meaning intention explains roughly 28% of the variance in actual behavior, with the
remainder governed by factors beyond an individual’s awareness or control.
More notably, specific to ethical consumption, Auger and Devinney (2007) identified a serious
methodological limitation: most empirical research on ethical consumption intention relies on
unconstrained self-report surveys, which tend to substantially inflate consumers’ true willingness due
to social desirability bias. This finding suggests an important critique applicable to the very statistics
cited in Section 1: the figure of 72% of Vietnamese consumers “willing to pay more for green
products” - collected via a similar survey method - likely reflects stated willingness that exceeds true
willingness, meaning the 60 -percentage-point gap with actual behavior (12 –18%) may partly stem
from this measurement limitation itself, rather than reflecting a purely behavioral gap.

3.2. Theory of Planned Behavior
Ajzen’s (1991) Theory of Planned Behavior attributes behavioral intention to three antecedents:
attitude toward the behavior, subjective norm - perceived social pressure to perform or not perform
the behavior - and perceived behavioral control (PBC) - the degree to which an individual feels
capable of performing the behavior. This is the most widely applied theoretical framework in
Vietnam’s green and ethical consumption research.
However, this theory reveals two core limitations when applied to the present study. First, as in the
critique by Auger and Devinney (2007) noted above, both the “attitude” and “subjective norm”
components are measured through self -report, making them susc eptible to social desirability bias -
particularly acute for morally loaded questions. Second, and more important for this paper’s
argument, “perceived behavioral control” in the Theory of Planned Behavior remains a subjective
psychological construct of the individual, rather than being equivalent to structural barriers - such as
the distribution system, regulatory enforcement, or certification infrastructure - that lie beyond the
awareness and control of any single consumer. This is precisely where institutional theory, presented
in Section 3.4, can address the explanatory gap left by the Theory of Planned Behavior.

3.3. Value–Belief–Norm Theory
To partly address the limitations above, Stern (2000) proposed the Value –Belief–Norm theory,
describing a four-link causal chain: personal values (particularly altruistic and biospheric values) →
beliefs about the environmental consequences of consumption → ascription of personal responsibility
for those consequences → personal norm - a felt moral obligation driving pro -environmental
behavior. Compared with the Theory of Planned Behavior, which leans toward rational calculation
(attitude–benefit), Value –Belief–Norm theory has an advantage in explaining the intrinsic moral
motivation behind responsible consumption behavior.

Nevertheless, this theory’s limitation for the present research objective lies in the same blind spot as
the Theory of Planned Behavior: the entire values–beliefs–norm causal chain remains at the level of
individual psychology, while the final transition - from personal norm to actual purchasing behavior
- depends on market conditions beyond individual control, such as price, product availability, and the
reliability of information/labeling. Stern (2000) himself acknowledged that “contextual forces” may
facilitate or hinder the translation of personal norms  into behavior, but did not develop these factors
into a full theoretical framework - a gap that institutional theory, presented next in Section 3.4, has
the potential to fill.

3.4. Institutional Theory and Institutional Voids
If the Theory of Planned Behavior and Value –Belief–Norm theory adequately explain intention
formation at the individual psychological level, both leave open the question: what determines
whether a personal norm or intention is actually translated into purchasing behavior? Institutional
theory offers an answer at the level of market structure. Following North’s (1990) classic definition,
institutions are the “rules of the game” of a society - the formal constraints (laws, regulations,
certification systems) and informal constraints (norms, commercial customs) that shape economic
exchange behavior. Extending this concept to developing-economy contexts, Mair and Marti (2009)
introduced the concept of institutional voids, describing conditions in which market -supporting
institutions - such as certification systems, contract -enforcement mechanisms, or transparent
information channels - are absent, weak, or fail to fulfill their expected role.
Applied to the context of ethical consumption, institutional voids manifest in three specific respects:
(i) lax enforcement of “green”/“clean” labeling standards, crea ting room for greenwashing; (ii) an
informal distribution system (traditional markets, small -scale retail) operating largely outside the
oversight of formal certification mechanisms; and (iii) governance/regulatory quality overall -
measurable through indices such as the World Bank’s Worldwide Governance Indicators introduced
in Section 2 - remaining at a moderate level relative to developed economies. This institutional void
does not affect consumption behavior directly, but indirectly, by eroding institut ional trust and
fostering greenwashing skepticism - a mechanism that de Sio et al. (2022) demonstrated plays a
mediating role, weakening green trust and thereby impeding the translation of intention into
purchasing behavior. In other words, institutional theory does not replace but complements the two
individual-psychology frameworks above, by explaining why even a strong personal norm can still
fail to translate into behavior when the surrounding institutional environment is not trustworthy
enough for the individual to “dare” act on that norm.
A limitation must nonetheless be acknowledged: institutional theory in its original form was
developed chiefly to explain the behavior of firms and organizations (entrepreneurship, market-entry
strategy), not individual consumer behavior. Extending the con cept of institutional voids to the
consumer level - as this paper does - is therefore itself a theoretical extension that should be presented
explicitly as a deliberate contribution rather than a mechanical application.

3.5. Critical Synthesis: Three Theories, One Causal Chain
Placed side by side, the three theoretical frameworks do not compete but complement one another
along a single causal chain: Value–Belief–Norm theory explains the moral origin of intention (from
personal values to norms); the Theory of Planned Behavior exp lains the cognitive mechanism
translating norms/attitudes into behavioral intention; and institutional theory - through the mediating
mechanisms of institutional trust and greenwashing skepticism - explains why that intention succeeds
or fails to translate into actual purchasing behavior within a specific market. Table 1 summarizes a
comparison of the three theoretical frameworks according to criteria directly relevant to the research

4. Contextualizing the Gap in Hanoi’s Urban Market
4.1. Informal Distribution Structure
Hanoi’s distribution landscape is the first factor explaining why certification mechanisms and
transparent information - a prerequisite for personal ethical norms to translate into purchasing
behavior - struggle to function effectively. According to the Chairman of the Vietnam Association of
Retailers, before the COVID -19 pandemic, modern retail channels (supermarkets, convenience
stores, shopping malls) a ccounted for only about 24% of the market, while traditional channels
(neighborhood markets, small grocery stores) accounted for as much as 76%; after the pandemic, the
share of traditional channels rose even further, to about 82%, for three consecutive ye ars, and only
by 2024 did modern retail recover to a 25% share - crossing the one -quarter threshold for the first
time, yet still considerably lower than regional peers such as Singapore or Thailand. This means that
most everyday consumption transactions in major cities such as Hanoi - where consumers select food,
fast-moving consumer goods, and household items - still occur at points of sale outside the direct
oversight of the formal eco-label certification system, which is designed primarily to operate through
modern distribution channels with controlled inputs.

4.2. Prevalence of Greenwashing and Consumer Skepticism
The most concrete evidence of an institutional void in this domain comes from the national
environmental certification system itself. According to an investigation by Báo Thanh tra - the press
organ of the Government Inspectorate - based on confirmation from the Environment Department

formal institutions fail to fulfill their expected role, that space does not disappear but is instead
occupied by unreliable substitute mechanisms, cr eating fertile ground for greenwashing to spread.
Theoretically, this is also precisely the mechanism demonstrated by de Sio et al. (2022): when “green”
claims lack credible verification, greenwashing skepticism rises, green trust declines, and the
intention to purchase green products - even when already formed at the level of individual attitude -
struggles to translate into actual purchasing behavior.

4.3. Institutional Trust: Enforcement versus Written Strategy
The institutional void described above is not an isolated phenomenon but reflects a more systemic
feature of governance quality. According to the World Bank’s Worldwide Governance Indicators,
Vietnam’s percentile rank on Regulatory Quality - the government’s capacity to formulate and
implement policies and regulations that support private-sector development - reached only 38.21% in
2023, meaning most countries surveyed worldwide are rated as having better regulatory quality than
Vietnam. A notable contrast emerges between this enforcement indicator and the ambitious macro -
level strategic documents - such as the National Green Growth Strategy or the Ministry of Industry
and Trade reports promoting green consumption cited in Section 1 - revealing a gap between written
policy commitments and on -the-ground enforcement capacity, exactly as predicted by institutional -
void theory. For Hanoi consumers, the direct consequence is that even when an intention to purchase
ethical products exists, they lack a trustworthy institutional mechanism to verify whether a product’s
“green”/“clean” claims genuinely reflect its actual attributes - a structural barrier that no individual-
psychology theory alone (Sections 3.2, 3.3) can resolve.

4.4. Price–Value Trade-off in an Emerging Middle-Class Context
The final dimension concerns income constraints. According to the General Statistics Office’s 2024
Household Living Standards Survey, Hanoi’s average per capita income reached VND 6.86
million/month - about 30% higher than the national average (VND 5.4 million/month) - yet in
absolute terms remains modest relative to the cost of living in a city with one of the country’s highest
per capita Gross Regional Domestic Products (GRDP) (approximately VND 163. 5 million/year).
Meanwhile, a study on willingness to pay for green products in Vietnam by Ho, Le, and Vu (2024) -
conducted in the southern region, so extrapolation to the Hanoi context warrants caution - found that
67.04% of respondents were willing to pay more than a 10% premium for green products. Placing
these two figures side by side, and recalling the methodological critique of Auger and Devinney
(2007) noted in Section 3.1, one can see that self -reported willingness to pay - prone to inflation -
collides with a concrete household budget reality: at an average income of VND 6.86 million/month,
a 10–25% price premium on frequently purchased items (food, personal care products) represents a
non-trivial share of spending, particularly for emerging middle -class households without a stable
financial cushion. This constitutes the price –value trade-off mechanism, which - together with the
institutional trust and greenwashing skepticism mechanisms presented above - forms the three central
moderating mechanisms to be integrated into the conceptual framework in Section 5.

5. Toward an Integrated Conceptual Framework
5.1. Integrative Logic
The conceptual framework proposed in Figure 1 operationalizes precisely the causal chain
synthesized critically in Section 3.5. The left half of the model - comprising Value–Belief–Norm
theory and the Theory of Planned Behavior - operates at the level of individual psychology, jointly
converging to form Ethical Consumption Intention (the focal construct, represented as a diamond to
emphasize its pivotal role in the model). The core point of departure from most existing Vietnam -
based research - which typically stops at measuring the Intention → Behavior relationship as a direct
straight line - lies in the “institutional gate” inserted between these two variables. This gate comprises
three mechanisms (P1 –P3), each anchored directly to a specific empirical finding from Hanoi
presented in Section 4, rather than being assumed in the abstract. Enclosing the entire gate is a
boundary cond ition (P4), reflecting the finding in Section 4.1 that the strength of these three
mechanisms is not uniform across distribution channels.

5.3. Theoretical Propositions
P1 - Institutional trust positively moderates the Intention → Behavior relationship. When
consumers trust the government’s capacity to enforce regulations and its environmental certification
system, a given level of intention has a higher probability of translating into actual purchasing
behavior; conversely, under conditions of weak regul atory enforcement quality - as reflected in
Vietnam’s Regulatory Quality percentile rank of only 38.21% (Section 4.3) - the same level of
intention will have a lower conversion probability.

P2 - Greenwashing skepticism acts as a negative mediator, weakening the Intention → Behavior
relationship by eroding green trust. The degree of this skepticism is proportional to the scale of the
institutional void in the eco -label certification system - concretely evidenced by the fact that all
“Vietnam Green Label” certifications have been inactive since 2020 (Section 4.2) - such that, in
markets with a large certification void, even a strong ethical intention may still fail to translate into
behavior.

P3 - The price–value trade-off moderates the Intention → Behavior relationship according to a
nonlinear threshold tied to household income constraints. When the price premium of an ethical
product falls below the acceptable threshold of the household budget (benchmarked against Hanoi’s
average per capita income, Section 4.4), the intention -to-behavior conversion process is reinforced;
when the premium exceeds this threshold, the process weakens regardless of how strong the initial
intention was.

P4 - The moderating strength of P1 –P3 is not uniform across distribution channels: it is
stronger in traditional/informal retail channels than in modern retail channels. Because most
everyday consumption transactions in Hanoi still occur through traditional channels - where oversight
and certification mechanisms are considerably weaker than in modern channels (Section 4.1) - a given
level of ethical consumption intention will have a lower probability of converting into actual behavior
when the transaction occurs through a traditional channel compared with a modern channel, even
when the three moderating mechanisms P1–P3 are held constant.

6. Discussion
The conceptual framework proposed in Section 5 adds a perspective still missing from the Vietnam-
based literature on the attitude –intention–behavior gap. The most recent and directly relevant
empirical study - Duong (2024), conducted on a representative sample of 583 consumers across five
major Vietnamese cities using a mall -intercept survey method - likewise applies the Theory of
Planned Behavior combined with a moderated -mediation model, but identifies the moderating
mechanism at the firm level: individua l companies’ environmental corporate social responsibility
(CSR) initiatives. This paper’s framework differs from, and complements, that finding by identifying
moderating mechanisms at the market/institutional level - institutional trust, greenwashing
skepticism, and price–value trade-offs - which lie beyond the control of any single firm. These two
approaches are not contradictory but complementary across two different levels of analysis: an
effective firm-level CSR initiative (per Duong, 2024) is unlikely  to be effective if the surrounding
institutional environment (the market level, per this paper’s framework) is not trustworthy enough for
consumers to recognize that initiative as genuine. At the same time, compared with the global
systematic review by Ar man and Mark -Herbert (2024) - which synthesizes a universal analytical
framework - this paper contributes by anchoring the analytical framework in a specific, empirically
verifiable institutional context rather than remaining at a purely theoretical level of generality.
This paper’s core theoretical contribution lies in extending the scope of institutional theory -
originally developed to explain organizational and firm behavior (Mair & Marti, 2009; North, 1990)
- to the level of individual consumption behavior, an extension that has been identified as necessary

For retailers and consumer -goods manufacturers in Hanoi, the finding in Section 4.2 implies that
investing in “green” claims lacking independent verification (as in the “Global Green Label Index”

certification bodies and regulators, the fact that the official “Vietnam Green Label” system has been
inactive since 2020 without a credible replacement mechanism (Section 4.2) constitutes a specific,
remediable policy gap - unlike more structural, harder-to-address barriers such as household income
(P3). This is a notable point for policymakers: among the three moderating mechanisms P1 –P3,
mechanism P2 (greenwashing skepticism, stemming direc tly from the institutional void in
certification) is the mechanism with the lowest policy -intervention cost but the highest potential
impact, and should therefore be prioritized in the short term over pursuing longer-term goals such as
raising household income or comprehensively reforming regulatory quality.
The generalizability of this analytical framework extends beyond Hanoi. A recent empirical study in
the Philippines by Ilagan et al. (2024), using an extended version of the Theory of Planned Behavior
with 500 consumers, found that an affordability gap weakens the attitude –behavior link, while also
identifying “perceived authority support” - conceptually close to the institutional trust construct (P1)
in this paper’s framework - as a significant predictor. This convergence, emerging independently in
another emerging urban market in the region, reinforces the argument that the three moderating
mechanisms P1–P3 are not unique to Hanoi but reflect a common pattern among transitional  urban
markets sharing sim ilar characteristics of institutional voids and income constraints - opening an
avenue for cross-city comparative testing in future research.

7. Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Research
Conclusion. This paper has constructed an integrated conceptual framework combining three
theoretical foundations - Value–Belief–Norm theory, the Theory of Planned Behavior, and
institutional theory - to explain the mechanisms that narrow the attitude –intention–behavior gap in
ethical consumption within a specific emerging urban market, Hanoi. Through a desk -research
method, the paper has: (i) shown that the three theoretical frameworks, when placed si de by side,
complement rather than compete with one another, each explaining a distinct segment of the Values
→ Intention → Behavior causal chain (Section 3.5); (ii) anchored three central moderating
mechanisms - institutional trust, greenwashing skepticism, and price –value trade-offs - to concrete,
verifiable empirical evidence from Hanoi, including the notable finding that the entire “Vietnam
Green Label” certification system has been inactive since 2020 (Section 4.2); and (iii) formalized
four theoretical propositions (P1 –P4) together  with a visual conceptual model (Figure 1) as a
foundation for future empirical research.

Limitations. The paper carries the inherent limitations of the desk-research method noted in Section
2: propositions P1–P4 have not been statistically tested and therefore remain hypothetical rather than
causal conclusions. The reliability of certain arguments depends on the quality of the secondary data
sources used - the willingness-to-pay survey figures cited (e.g., in Section 4.4) are themselves subject
to the same social -desirability-bias limitation flagged by Auger and Devinney (2007). In addition,
some illustrative data (such as the study by Ho et al., 2024) were collected in the southern region, so
extrapolation to the Hanoi context should be undertaken with caution, as noted in Section 4.4.
