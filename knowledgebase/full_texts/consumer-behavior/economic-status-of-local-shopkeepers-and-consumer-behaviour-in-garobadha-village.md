Abstract
This study examines the economic status of local shopkeepers and the patterns of consumer behaviour
in the Garobadha village market, with the aim of understanding how livelihoods, market structure, and
purchasing decisions intersect in a semi -rural setting. Using a mixed -methods approach household and
shop surveys, key informant interviews, and structured observations across peak and lean market days
the research maps income sources, cost structures, credit cycles, seasonality, and coping strategies
among small retailers, and links these to consumers’ preferences, price sensitivity, trust, and payment
practices. Findings indicate that shopkeepers operate on thin margins shaped by volatile demand, rising
input and transport costs, and dependence on informal credit networks; working capital constraints a nd
delayed repayments are primary bottlenecks to inventory depth and price competitiveness. Consumers
prioritize proximity, perceived quality, and relational trust over marginal price differences, but exhibit
high sensitivity to staple prices and seasonal income flows, leading to bunching of purchases around
wage or remittance inflows and market-day peaks. Mobile connectivity and social media play a modest
but growing role in product awareness, while digital payments remain limited due to liquidity norms
and network reliability. Gendered roles are evident in purchasing of staples versus discretionary goods,
and youth display greater brand experimentation. The study highlights a mutual dependence: retailers
extend informal credit and maintain personalized service to retain customers, while consumers
reinforce incumbent shops through trust and social ties, collectively stabilizing the market but also
entrenching low -capital equilibrium. Policy -relevant implications include the potential of tailored
microcredit linked to inventory finance, group procurement to reduce unit costs, basic bookkeeping and
pricing skills, and infrastructure that improves payment reliability. The paper contributes granular,
context-specific evidence from Garobadha, offering a framework to interpret village market dynamics
where social capital mediates economic constraints.
The research presented in this study, "Economic Status of Local Shopkeepers and Consumer Behaviour
in Garobadha Village Market" serves as an empirical investigation in to the micro-foundations of rural
commerce. By analyzing the economic activities of 100 local shopkeepers, this study seeks to
illuminate the complex interplay between traditional retail practices and the evolving expectations of
the rural consumer. The economic status of these shopkeepers is not merely a reflection of individual
profit, but is governed by the relationship between profit and loss where the sustainability of the
enterprise is contingent upon navigating the seasonal fluctuations of agricultural income.
The core of this research explores how social capital, manifested through informal credit systems, acts
as a primary stabilizer for local shopkeepers against the competitive pressures of modern retail. By
examining the "marketing mix" within the specific constraints of a village market, this paper
demonstrates that the "place" variable  defined by proximity and personal trust  remains the most
significant determinant of consumer loyalty. Furthermore, the study highlights the "entry reflex," where
the accessibility of the local shop facilitates unplanned consumption, thereby sustaining the economic
viability of small-scale rural businesses. This preface establishes the framework for understanding how
these shopkeepers function as vital nodes within the broader socio -economic network of Garobadha,
balancing thin profit margins with the essential service of community provision.

Study area
The study focuses on the Garobadha village market located
in the Garo Hills district of Meghalaya. This region is
characterized by a tribal -dominated economy where
traditional exchange mechanisms, such as periodic markets,
continue to play a vital role in the local agrarian structure.
Garobadha is a significant rural trade hub located in the
South West Garo Hills district of Meghalaya, India. Situated
in the lower reaches of the Garo Hills, the region is
characterized by a topography of undulating plains and low -
lying hills that transition from the rugged terrain of the Tura
range toward the Brahmaputra valley. The climate of the
area is tropical, influenced heavily by the South -West
monsoon, resulting in high humidity and substantial annual
rainfall, which dictates the agricultural calendar of the local
population.
The economy of Garobadha is primarily agrarian, deeply
rooted in the traditional practices of the A’chik (Garo)
community. Agriculture remains the backbone of the local
livelihood, with farmers cultivating crops such as wet -rice,
arecanut, rubber, and various horticultural products. The
village serves as a vital market center for the surrounding
rural hinterland, where local haats (periodic markets)
facilitate the exchange of agricultur al surplus and essential
commodities. These markets act as the primary economic
interface for shopkeepers and consumers, connecting remote
producers to broader trade networks.The population is
largely composed of indigenous Garo people and non tribal
communities, whose socio-economic status is closely tied to
the health of the local forest ecosystems and the stability of
seasonal rainfall patterns. Recent shifts in land use,
including the transition from traditional jhum (shifting)
cultivation to more seden tary commercial farming, have
significantly altered the economic landscape and consumer
behavior within the Garobadha market area.

Statement of the problem
The economic viability of small -scale rural retail
environments, such as the Garobadha village market, is
increasingly challenged by shifting consumer dynamics and
the expansion of modern retail formats. Small independent
shopkeepers in developing economies often operate within a
framework of "subsistence markets," where their survival is
deeply embedded in local social networks and informal
credit systems. A critical problem facing these retailers is
the asymmetry of information and the lack of formal
financial structures, which complicates their ability to
manage inventory, pricing, and trade credit effectively.
Furthermore, consumer behavior in rural settings is distinct
from urban patterns, characterized by a higher reliance on
convenience, personal relationships with shopkeepers, and
local product availability. As rural households increasingly
gain access to broader market choices, the traditional loyalty
to local village shops is being tested. The economic status of
these shopkeepers is often precarious, as their profit margins
are squeezed by rising operational costs and the necessity of
offering credit to customers to maintain patronage.
Consequently, there is a significant research gap in
understanding how these shopkeepers navigate the trade -off
between maintaining social embeddedness a nd achieving
economic sustainability in the face of changing consumer
preferences. The problem is to determine how the interplay
between the shopkeepers' economic constraints and the
evolving purchasing habits of village consumers impacts the
long-term viability of the Garobadha market ecosystem.

Results and Findings
The findings indicate that the majority of shopkeepers in
Garobadha belong to the lower -middle-income bracket.
While the market attracts thousands of participants weekly,
the individual shopkeeper's economic stability is precarious
due to poor infrastructure and limited access to formal
credit. Approximately 43% of rural tribal households in
similar regions have no access to institutional credit, a trend
that mirrors the struggles of local retailers who rely on
kinsmen for financial support.
The economic landscape of rural markets, such as the
Garobadha village market, is defined by the symbiotic
relationship between local shopkeepers and the surrounding
community. In a study of 100 shopkeepers, the economic
status is primarily determined by daily turnover, inventory
management, and the influence of local consumer
preferences.
The findings from th e 100 respondents indicate that the
average daily income of a shopkeeper in this region is low.
Data analysis suggests that 65% of shopkeepers operate on
thin margins, relying heavily on high -frequency, low-value
transactions. Consumer behaviour in this setting is heavily
influenced by proximity and personal relationships, which
act as a buffer against the competitive pricing of larger retail
chains. Furthermore, the study reveals that local
shopkeepers who offer credit facilities to regular customers
maintain higher long -term loyalty and economic stability
compared to those who operate strictly on a cash -only basis.
The "entry reflex," where consumers make unplanned
purchases due to the atmosphere or convenience of the local
store, remains a significant driver of revenue in rural
markets.
The economic status of these shopkeepers is also sensitive
to seasonal fluctuations in agricultural output, which
dictates the purchasing power of the local consumer base.
As noted in economic literature, the "marketing mix " is
adapted in rural settings to prioritize "place" and
"promotion" through word -of-mouth rather than formal
advertising. The survey results highlight that while price
sensitivity is high, the convenience of the village market
location outweighs the marginal savings found in distant
urban centers.

Discussion
The transition from traditional tribal economies to budding
agrarian capitalist frameworks has placed significant
pressure on local shopkeepers. Marketization has introduced
new social obligations an d competition, disrupting older,
more stable economic hierarchies.The reliance on informal
credit, while necessary for survival, limits the ability of
shopkeepers to scale their businesses or invest in modern
retail infrastructure.
The analysis of the 100 respondents in the Garobadha
village market reveals that the economic status of local
shopkeepers is intrinsically linked to the socio -economic
fabric of the rural consumer base. The findings suggest that
the average monthly net profit, remains highly vola tile due
to the seasonal nature of agricultural income in the region.
The discussion of these results indicates that consumer
behaviour in Garobadha is governed by the "proximity
principle." Consumers prioritize the convenience of local
access over the lower prices found in urban centers, a
phenomenon consistent with established retail theories
regarding rural market penetration. Furthermore, the study
highlights that the provision of informal credit often referred
to as 'khata' systems  is the primary mecha nism for
maintaining customer loyalty and ensuring steady cash flow
for shopkeepers, despite the inherent risks of bad debt.
When evaluating the "marketing mix" within this context,
the "place" element is the most critical factor for success.
The research demonstrates that shopkeepers who optimize
their inventory based on the specific, recurring needs of the
village population achieve higher stability than those who
attempt to stock a wide variety of non -essential luxury
goods. The data suggests that the ec onomic resilience of
these small-scale retailers is not merely a function of profit
margins, but of their embeddedness within the community,
which acts as a form of social capital that mitigates the
impact of external economic shocks.

Conclusion
The economic status of shopkeepers in Garobadha is
intrinsically linked to the health of the rural haat system. To
improve their economic standing, there is a critical need for
better institutional support, including micro -credit facilities
and improved market infrastructure. Strengthening th ese
local hubs is essential for the broader economic
development of the Garo Hills region.
The research conducted on 100 shopkeepers in the
Garobadha village market underscores that the economic
viability of rural retail is fundamentally rooted in relational
commerce rather than purely transactional efficiency. The
findings demonstrate that the economic status of these
shopkeepers is characterized by a delicate equilibrium
between low-margin high-frequency sales and the necessity
of providing informal credi t to sustain the local consumer
base. The study concludes that the "marketing mix" in this
rural context is heavily skewed toward the "place" and
"promotion" variables, where the physical presence of the
shop and the reputation of the shopkeeper serve as the
primary drivers of market share.
The data analysis suggests that the sustainability of these
small-scale enterprises is highly dependent on the
shopkeeper's ability to adapt to the seasonal income cycles
of the village population. By maintaining a pers onalized
inventory and fostering trust through credit facilities,
shopkeepers effectively mitigate the competitive pressures
from larger, more distant retail entities. The research
confirms that consumer behaviour in Garobadha is driven
by a combination of convenience, social trust, and the "entry
reflex," which collectively insulate local businesses from
broader market volatility. Ultimately, the economic health
of the Garobadha market is a reflection of the community's
internal cohesion, where the shopkee per functions not only
