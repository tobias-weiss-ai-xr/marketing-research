Abstract
This study investigates the relationship between digital nance and consumption penetration, aiming to
explore whether digital nance exacerbates consumption penetration and its mechanisms and heterogeneous
impacts. Based on the 2013–2019 China Household Finance Survey (CHFS) data from Southwestern
University of Finance and Economics and the Peking University Digital Financial Inclusion Index (BDUI)
matching sample, we conduct empirical research through constructing a panel xed effects model, combined
with robustness tests, instrumental variable methods, heterogeneity analysis, and mediation models.
Consumption penetration refers to the phenomenon where low-income groups borrow to imitate high-income
consumption patterns. We construct a Consumption Penetration Index (TDCI) using three dimensions: ritual
consumption structure upgrading, debt-driven consumption intensity, and consumption pattern convergence,
with the Digital Finance Development Index (DFI) as the core explanatory variable while controlling for multiple
household and regional variables. Empirical results show that digital nance development signicantly
intensies the consumption penetration effect, which remains robust after modifying the sample scope,
variable measurement methods, and endogeneity control. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that this effect is
more pronounced in regions with lower economic development levels, middle-aged groups (36–59 years old),
and low-education populations (middle school or below). Mechanism verication conrms that digital nance
enhances credit accessibility and relaxes credit constraints, enabling non-auent households to emulate high-
income consumption patternsFunding support is the key path to inuence consumption inltration.The
research conclusions provide a new perspective for understanding consumer behavior in the digital economy
era. At the policy level, while promoting the universality of digital nance, we should take targeted measures to
prevent excessive lending risks, strengthen nancial literacy education for specic groups, and guide healthy
consumption.
1. Introduction
This study is based on three backgrounds. First, the importance of consumption in the macro economy;
second, the increasing income inequality that constrains consumption; and third, the development of digital
nance.
In the macroeconomic landscape, consumption has become increasingly vital [1]. As noted by an associate
researcher at the Institute of Economics, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, "Chinas deepening new
industrialization, informatization, urbanization, and agricultural modernization have made consumption a
fundamental driver of economic growth." Amid shifting global economic dynamics—particularly the declining
share of investment and exports—consumptions role as one of the "three engines" of economic growth has
grown more critical. The Central Economic Work Conference emphasized this point, proposing to "stimulate
potential consumption and expand productive investments to create a virtuous cycle where consumption and
investment reinforce each other" [2]. Consumption not only serves as a direct engine for economic growth but
also plays a pivotal role in balancing domestic and international economic development. In an era where
economic globalization faces headwinds, achieving qualitative improvements through exports has become
challenging, necessitating greater reliance on domestic demand—particularly consumption—to drive growth.
Expanding consumption stimulates investment growth, while increased investment unlocks consumption
potential, forming a mutually reinforcing virtuous cycle that facilitates smooth domestic circulation, resolves

In China, income inequality has long been a prominent issue in socio-economic development. Signicant
disparities persist between urban and rural areas, as well as across different regions, with substantial gaps in

According to 2019 data from the Peoples Bank of China, the wealthiest 10% of the population held 49% of total
wealth, while the poorest 40% owned merely 8%. Such wealth disparity has led to consumption penetration [5],
a phenomenon where lower-income groups resort to debt-nanced consumption to match high-income
counterparts spending levels as wealth gaps widen. While this pattern is common in developed countries,
Chinas rising housing prices and increasing living costs have intensied debt burdens for low-income
households, making consumption penetration increasingly evident. Specically, these individuals may borrow
to maintain basic living standards or pursue higher quality of life, which not only strains their nances but also
profoundly impacts consumption patterns and economic growth models. The development of digital nance
has further accelerated this trend through more accessible consumer credit. Therefore, understanding
consumption penetrations denition and manifestations is crucial for policy-makingIt is of great signicance

consumption [6].
In traditional nancial systems, low-income groups often struggle to obtain loans due to insucient credit
histories and unstable incomes, which limits their ability to emulate high-income consumption patterns.
However, with the widespread adoption of digital nancial products like Ant Huabei and JD Baitiao, these low-
threshold digital solutions have made it easier for low-income individuals to adopt high-income consumption
habits. These products essentially provide users with virtual credit cards that allow "spend now, pay later"
transactions, signicantly reducing credit constraints and stimulating latent consumer demand [7]. Digital
inclusive nance inuences household consumption levels through convenient digital payment methods and
multi-channel nancing options, greatly enhancing consumption convenience while igniting consumer desires

payment accessibility and shortens shopping time, thereby increasing consumption. Additionally, the

consumption inltration, which has a profound impact on the economic situation of low-income groups. From
a macro perspective, it may also lead to the aggravation of nancial risks and the formation of bad social
customs.

2.1 Research on consumption inltration
Marianne Bertrand and Adair Morse (2013) investigated whether rising incomes and consumption at the top of

consumption" effect [11]. The study revealed that if top-income growth had matched median income growth

Moreover, they found middle-income families showed heightened sensitivity to consumption at the top income
level—particularly for goods and services with higher income elasticity and greater visibility—a nding

auent households exposed to higher top income levels reported greater nancial stress, with elevated top

3.2 Variable description
(1) The dependent variable: consumption inltration index (TDCI)
To comprehensively assess the extent of consumption penetration, this study develops a three-dimensional
composite index based on non-auent households spending patterns. The rst dimension, Cultural
Consumption Structure Upgrade (CS1), measures the proportion of non-essential expenditures in total
household spending. Non-essential consumption is dened by aggregating expenditures categorized as
"entertainment and leisure,"   "cultural education,"   "premium durable goods," and "tourism services" from the
China Household Survey (CHFS) data, with total consumption calculated as annual household expenditure.
Higher CS1 values indicate greater alignment between non-auent households consumption behaviors and
those of high-income groups. The second dimension, Debt Consumption Intensity (CS2), quanties household
debt-to-consumption ratio by excluding business-related liabilities and focusing on consumer debt such as
credit card overdrafts and consumer loans. This metric reects how non-auent households borrow to
emulate high-income consumption patterns. The third dimension, Consumption Pattern Convergence (CS3),
evaluates behavioral similarity between non-auent and auent households. Following the relative income

consumption penetration calculation formula is: TDCI= α ·CS1 + β ·CS2 + γ ·CS3

4. Research hypotheses and model setting
4.1 Theoretical analysis
The consumption penetration phenomenon fundamentally represents a dynamic process where high-income
consumption patterns gradually extend to middle-and lower-income groups, driven by the cross-class
transmission of consumption demonstration effects. In traditional nancial ecosystems, this diffusion was
constrained by limited credit accessibility and high information acquisition costs, resulting in relatively
restricted speed and coverage. The emergence of digital nance, however, has reshaped the consumption
ecosystem through technological empowerment, providing new momentum for consumption penetration. 
From an institutional perspective, digital nances impact on consumption penetration manifests through three
dimensions. First, the comprehensive integration of payment scenarios eliminates spatial-temporal barriers in
consumer behavior. High-income groups premium consumption patterns become more observable through
digital channels like social platforms and payment statements, reinforcing the psychological chain where low-
income groups emulate high-income models. Second, innovations in credit assessment technologies have
broken free from traditional reliance on collateral, enabling non-auent households to access consumer credit
products like Weidai and Huabei more conveniently. This provides nancial support for imitating high-income
consumption patterns. Third, the widespread adoption of low-threshold wealth management tools such as
funds and digital currency investments has transformed household savings behaviors. Some families may
reduce precautionary savings to boost immediate consumption, further amplifying the consumption
penetration effect.
Building on Dusenberrys relative income hypothesis, individual consumption is inuenced not only by personal
income but also by the combined effects of the "ratchet effect" and "demonstration effect". Digital nance
reduces information asymmetry and credit constraints through consumer assessments, inuencer
recommendations, and dynamic credit limits. This mechanism amplies the demonstration effect of high-
income groups consumption while enabling low-income groups to emulate such behaviors, ultimately
deepening the penetration of consumption into lower-income demographics.

5.4 Mechanism analysis
Table 1-9 examines the mediating role of credit availability (CA) in digital nance (DFI) inuencing the
transmission of consumption (TDCI) through a three-step regression method. As a core indicator measuring
regional credit environment accessibility, CAs calculation process comprehensively considers data availability,
regional disparities, and economic aggregate impacts. The specic construction methodology is detailed
below.
Firstly, using the "Regional Financial Operation Report" released by the Peoples Bank of China as the core data
source, we extracted the annual loan balance growth rate for each province. For provinces with missing direct
data in certain years, we employed an interpolation method using adjacent years. If the loan balance of a
province in 2016 is known, the 2017 balance = 2016 balance × (1 + 2017 growth rate), and so on, to ll in the
time series data. Secondly, we calculated preliminary per capita loan balances by dividing provincial loan
balances by their resident populations (data from the China Statistical Yearbook), eliminating the impact of
population size differences on total credit volume and more accurately reecting individual access to credit
resources. Thirdly, to address the uneven distribution of urban-rural credit resources, we introduced urban-rural
loan structure weights. Referring to rural loan proportions in local statistical yearbooks and combining them
with urban-rural population numbers, we used a weighted average method to calculate adjusted per capita
loan balances. The formula is: Adjusted per capita loan balance = (Urban population × Urban per capita loan +
Rural population × Rural per capita loan) / Total resident population). Here, urban per capita loan = (Total
loans × Urban loan ratio) / Urban population, and rural per capita loan follows the same calculation method to

Page 15/27
correct underestimation of credit accessibility caused by urban-rural dual structures. Finally, to eliminate
interference from regional economic scale differences, we referenced the Theil index scoreThe analytical
approach incorporates the ratio of per capita loan balance to regional GDP into the adjustment model,
ultimately deriving the Credit Availability (CA) indicator through natural logarithmic transformation. The
formula is: CA = ln(adjusted per capita loan balance / regional per capita GDP). This methodology preserves
the absolute level information of credit resources while eliminating the impact of economic size differences,
ensuring comparability in credit availability across regions with varying development levels. Through these
four processing steps, the Credit Availability (CA) is obtained.
In the mediation effect analysis results presented in Table 1-9, Model m32 uses credit availability (CA) as the
dependent variable to examine whether digital nance signicantly affects the relaxation of credit constraints.
The results show that DFI has a coecient of 0.003*** (p<0.01), indicating a signicant positive correlation.
This demonstrates that the development of digital nance signicantly enhances regional credit availability.
This satises the rst condition for mediation effects: the independent variable (DFI) exerts a signicant
positive inuence on the mediating variable (CA), conrming that digital nance can indeed relax credit
constraints through technological empowerment.
The model m33 incorporates both DFI and CA as explanatory variables to examine whether the mediating
variable mediates the relationship between digital nance and consumption leakage. The results show that the
coecient of credit availability (CA) is 0.029** (p<0.05), indicating a signicant positive effect. This suggests
that improved credit availability itself signicantly exacerbates consumption leakage, meaning that after credit
constraints are relaxed, less auent households can more easily imitate high-income groups consumption
through borrowing, satisfying the second condition of the mediating effect. The coecient of digital nance
(DFI) is 0.05*** (p<0.01), remaining signicantly positive and slightly higher than the total effect (0.044). This
indicates that beyond credit availability, digital nance may inuence consumption leakage through other
pathways, though the mediating role of credit availability has been identied.
To rigorously test the signicance of the mediating effect, the table supplements the Sobel test results with a
Z-value of 0.53124 and a P-value of 0.002 (p<0.01), which passes the signicance test. This indicates that the
mediating effect of credit availability (CA) is statistically signicant, conrming that digital nance enhances
the mechanism of consumption penetration through improved credit accessibility.
Through three-step regression and Sobel tests, Hypothesis 3 is fully validated. The development of digital
nance indeed provides nancial support for non-auent households to emulate high-income consumption
patterns by easing credit constraints (improving credit accessibility), thereby intensifying the consumption
penetration effect. The core logic of this mechanism lies in: Digital nance breaks through traditional credit
limitations on collateral and geographical restrictions, enabling low-credit households to more easily obtain
consumer credit, thereby narrowing the consumption gap with high-income groups.
Furthermore, the results show that the DFI coecient slightly increased rather than decreased after
introducing the mediating variable. This may be because credit availability serves as one of the core
mechanisms through which digital nance inuences consumption penetration, though not the only one.
Digital nance may also enhance demonstration effects by reducing information asymmetry or improve
consumption immediacy through payment convenience—these pathways create a synergistic effect with

credit availability. The complementary relationship between credit availability and other functions of digital
nance collectively drives consumption penetration. This nding deepens our understanding of digital
nances operational mechanisms: its impact on consumption penetration isnt merely about providing funds,
but rather about lowering nancing barriers and amplifying demonstration effects through coordinated
synergies, with credit availability being the quantiable key mediating factor.

This chapter reveals the inherent logic of how digital nance inuences consumption penetration through

borrowing risks; For middle-aged and less-educated groups, nancial literacy education should be intensied
