Introduction: The visual phenomenon of cosmetic culture on digital platforms
has intensified and expanded significantly over the past decade, driven by a shift
in consumer behavior toward the digital ecosystem and the rise of social media
as the primary venue for advertising, brand communication, and the
construction of consumer identity. This study examines the representation of
ideology through visual signs in cosmetic advertisements for Wardah (local)
and Pond’s (global) on social media platforms such as Instagram.
Methods: This research employs a qualitative case study methodology, utilizing
visual semiotic analysis of advertising materials collected from Instagram.
Results: The findings indicate that Wardah constructs an image of reliable and
comfortable protection for an active lifestyle, aligning with the values of
modesty and naturalness. In contrast, Pond’s positions itself as a scientific
solution that offers superior results and global aspirations. These ideological
representations differ significantly because of the use of specific visual signs:
Wardah emphasizes symbols of naturalness and modesty, whereas Pond’s
highlights symbols of science, luxury, and global standards.
Discussion: The diversity of ideologies presented by brands such as Wardah and
Pond’s reflects the varied cultures of consumption and the potential of social
media to either support or challenge prevailing beauty norms.

1 Introduction
The new media have profoundly altered the advertising landscape. Traditionally,
advertisements primarily aimed to inform consumers about products, their benefits,
and their availability to consumers. In contrast, contemporary advertising has become
increasingly complex, encompassing not only the promotion of products but also the
communication of the values and aspirations of the target audience (Jhally, 2016).
Marketers now have the capability to tailor their strategies to specific demographics,
interests, online behavior, and even emotional states. Digital platforms enable the
creation of advertisements that align with individual preferences. A comprehensive
understanding of the visual components of advertisements is essential for analyzing
how messages are conveyed in the current media environment (Darmawan, 2006).
Digital advertising, which now dominates marketing communications, no longer
functions merely as a tool for promoting products or services. In contrast, digital
advertising has evolved into a far more powerful and complex medium, capable of
disseminating ideologies, shaping audience perceptions, manipulating aspirations and

Jhally, 2016). Additionally, research on platform regulation and
digital brand culture elucidates how data-driven advertising
models engender ethical, privacy, and transparency challenges,
which, particularly in the cosmetics sector, can shape consumer
perceptions of global and local brands (Strycharz et al., 2019).
Global market data underscores the importance of digital
advertising in the cosmetics category: the cosmetics e-commerce
ecosystem shows a significant contribution from products such
as serums, lipsticks, and lip gloss as engines of sales growth
(Letsoin and Riorini, 2023). In Indonesia, this trend is
reinforced by the dominance of local brands accredited as halal
by LPPOM MUI and their ability to aggressively leverage digital
platforms to build their brand image, especially Wardah and its
partners (LPPOM MUI, 2021).
Amelia et al. showed that digital communication strategies via
Instagram play a crucial role in shaping Wardah’s brand image as
a halal product in Indonesia, given the intense competition in the
beauty industry (Amelia et al., 2025). Rohman and Hidayat (2024)
state that marketing content on TikTok can enhance brand image
and awareness, which, in turn, leads to purchase decisions through
intermediate pathways such as brand perception and awareness.
Ekowati et al. ’s findings emphasized that visual content and
storytelling are the main mechanisms for instilling positive
emotions and trust in halal brands such as Wardah (Ekowati
et al., 2023).
The ideological significance of visual signs in digital
cosmetic advertisements is of both theoretical and practical
importance to advertisers. Theoretically, semiotics offers a
framework for analyzing the construction of meaning in visual
signs through three levels of analysis: denotation (objective
visibility), connotation (culturally associated meaning), and
deeper ideological codes (ideals promoted by the brand). In
the context of Wardah advertisements—as a halal brand
positioning itself within narratives of religious observance,
product authenticity, and Islamic family values—the visual
signs tend to affirm an identity that is closely aligned with
Indonesia’s Muslim consumer base. Conversely, as a global
brand, Ponds tends to convey a universal beauty ideology, the
harmony of a modern lifestyle, and cross-cultural aspirations.
Comparing the visual representations of both brands enables
an analysis of how local and global brands shape ideological
meanings through visual signs at the same level but with
different cultural affinities (Piatti-Farnell, 2024; Yalçinkaya and
Yusufoğlu, 2025).
Furthermore, research on “visual storytelling marketing”
indicates that narrative visuals in cosmetic advertisements can
foster strong emotional connections with consumers and
influence halal-local perceptions of the Wardah brand (Ekowati
et al., 2023). These findings corroborate the notion that digital
advertising serves not only as a promotional tool but also as a
medium for shaping consumer identity and ideology through
structured, systematic visual cues.
The phenomenon under consideration has several dimensions.
Primarily, a distinction exists between the ideological
representations embodied by local brands, such as Wardah, and
global brands, such as Pond’s. This distinction may create a
contrast between cultural identities and religious values in
digital advertising. Previous research has demonstrated that halal
brands such as Wardah utilize halal narratives and religious
values to cultivate trust among Indonesian Muslim consumers
(Amelia et al., 2025; Rohman and Hidayat, 2024). Conversely,
global brands such as Pond’s may emphasize universal beauty
aesthetics that are widely accepted across cultures, potentially
fostering dualism between local identities and global aspirations.
Understanding these two frameworks of representation is
crucial, as they can influence consumer preferences, brand
loyalty, and purchasing decisions in various ways, contingent
upon cultural and religious contexts (Piatti-Farnell, 2024;
Yalçinkaya and Yusufoğlu, 2025).
Second, the semiotic dimension inherent in visual signs is
often undervalued when assessing the direct communicative
impact of advertising, such as likes, shares, and sales
conversions. Semiotic research recognizes that visual signs in
digital advertising encapsulate deeper ideological codes that can
construct gender norms, cultural identities, and power dynamics
between brands and consumers. For example, a semiotic analysis
of cultural visuals within cosmetic advertisements reveals how
images of gender, class, and race can be subtly portrayed,
thereby influencing consumers’ self-perception and consumption
behaviors (Yılmaz, A., and Kır, B. 2025).
Third, there is a significant gap in research that
comprehensively examines how brand institutions, whether local
or global, disseminate ideologies through visual symbols in
digital advertising, particularly within the Indonesian context.
While numerous studies have concentrated on the effects of
marketing content on brand image and brand awareness in
general (Arinata et al., 2022; Fitryarini, 2013) or have
investigated the visual narrative aspect of Wardah as a halal
cosmetic brand through analyses of visual language and
storytelling, integrative studies remain scarce. Specifically, there
is a lack of research connecting semiotic analysis with a
comparative examination of ideological representation between
local and global brands, especially in the highly dynamic
domain of digital platforms.
In light of the preceding discussion, it is evident that the
digital landscape elevates visual content as a pivotal element in
shaping brand image and influencing purchasing decisions, with
ideological ramifications that warrant a systematic investigation.
Within the Indonesian context, Wardah, a halal brand that
capitalizes on religious narratives and the trust of Muslim
consumers, serves as a significant case study for examining how
visual symbols can consistently and persuasively convey
ideologies. Conversely, as a global brand, presents an intriguing
opportunity for a comparative study to assess the extent to
which visual ideology can be effectively communicated across
cultures while retaining its appeal in the local market.
Consequently, this study integrates semiotic analysis with a
comparative examination of local and global brands within the
digital platform environment, underscoring how visual symbols
can function as ideological instruments in contemporary
cosmetic advertising.

2.1 Sampling rationale
The two advertisements were selected using purposive
criterion-based sampling. First, Wardah and Pond’s were
selected because they represent two of the most established and
competing personal care brands in the Indonesian market.
According to the 2023 Top Brand Index survey conducted by
Frontier Research, both brands hold “Top Brand” status in
directly competing skincare subcategories, such as facial
moisturizers (Pond’s 16.4% vs. Wardah 15.3%) and facial
serums (Wardah Lightening Serum 18.7% vs. Pond’s Ultra
Luminous Serum 15.3%), indicating sustained, closely matched
consumer recognition between a locally positioned halal brand
and a globally positioned brand within the same product
ecosystem (Top Brand Award, 2023a), as documented in the
official award gallery (Top Brand Award, 2023b). Second, within
this established rivalry, the sunscreen category was selected as
the specific point of comparison because both brands launched
new flagship sunscreen products within a closely overlapping
period in October 2025 (Wardah Ultra Proof Protection,
October 14, 2025; Pond’s UV Miracle Matte, October 24, 2025),
representing each brand’s most current and strategically
emphasized visual campaigns at the time of data collection.
Given that this near-simultaneous launch made these two
advertisements the most directly comparable and strategically
relevant sunscreen campaigns available from both brands at the
time of data collection, no alternative candidate advertisements
were formally compared prior to their selection. The two
advertisements were identified and selected directly based on the
above criteria. While this study analyzes a single advertisement
per brand, it is treated as an illustrative case—a detailed
instance of each brand’s broader visual strategy at a specific
market moment—rather than a claim of full representativeness
across all campaigns. This limitation is further addressed in the
limitations section.
2.2 Data collection and analytical validation
Primary data were collected through purposive sampling from
Instagram advertisements featuring sunscreen products from both
brands. The sample for Wardah’s sunscreen product was derived
from an advertisement published on October 14, 2025, and the
sample for Pond’s was derived from an advertisement published
on October 24, 2025. To strengthen the trustworthiness of the
interpretive findings, this study employed investigator
triangulation: the two authors independently coded each
advertisement using the icon-index-symbol framework and
identified denotative, indexical, and symbolic meanings without
prior discussion. The independent codings were then compared,
and discrepancies in interpretation were resolved through
discussion and re-examination of the visual material until a
consensus was reached. This process helped reduce the risk of
single-researcher interpretive bias and ensured that the
ideological readings presented in Tables 1, 2 reflect an
interpretation validated by two analysts rather than a single
subjective reading.
2.2.1 Analytical framework: from sign to ideology
The semiotic analysis is grounded in the triadic framework of
icon, index, and symbol, supplemented by discourse analysis of
the function of visual signs in contemporary marketing. The
central premise is based on Peirce’s semiotic theory, which
facilitates the analysis of signs and meanings in advertisements.
This study operationalizes the movement from Peirce’s triadic
sign categories to ideological interpretation in three analytical
steps. First, each visual element in the advertisement was
decomposed into its icon (the perceptual resemblance, e.g., a
model in a hijab), index (the causal/associative link, e.g., a hijab
indexing religious observance), and symbol (the conventional,
culturally agreed meaning, e.g., modesty as a value). Second,
symbols recurring across multiple images within the same
advertisement were grouped into thematic clusters (e.g.,
“modesty,” “naturalness,” “reliability” for Wardah; “science,”
“luxury,” “global standards” for Pond’s), following the logic that
ideology operates not through isolated signs but through the
repetition and reinforcement of symbolic clusters across a sign
system. Third, these thematic clusters were interpreted against the
socio-cultural and commercial context of each brand (see Data
collection and analytical validation above) to identify the
underlying ideological position—that is, the value system or
worldview that the advertisement naturalizes as desirable or
“common sense” for the viewer. It is acknowledged that Peirce’s
triad, unlike Barthes’ denotation-connotation-myth model, was
not originally designed as a tool for ideology critique; this study
therefore extends the symbol category by treating clusters of
symbols as functionally equivalent to Barthes’ notion of “myth”—
culturally naturalized meaning—while retaining Peirce’s
terminology for consistency with the sign-level analysis presented
in Tables 1, 2.

3.1 Visual analysis of Wardah advertisement
as a local product
Wardah is one of the largest domestic cosmetic brands in
Indonesia, established by Nurhayati Subakat in 1995, with a
brand concept centered on halal cosmetics. Nurhayati, the
founder and current Chief Executive Officer of PT Paragon
Technology and Innovation, has adeptly developed Wardah into
a leading halal cosmetic brand in Indonesia, characterized by
product innovations that integrate pharmaceutical quality with
Islamic principles. Similar to other cosmetic companies, Wardah
produces a diverse range of products. Wardah’s latest flagship
offering is its skincare line, which provides a range of products
tailored to the specific needs and skin types of users. These
typically include a comprehensive set of products such as facial
cleansers, moisturizing creams, and sunscreens. Wardah markets
its skincare products individually.

Upon analyzing the five descriptions of the Wardah Ultra
Proof Protection advertisements, it is evident that Wardah
constructs a robust ideology that emphasizes reliable,
comfortable, and natural protection. This ideology enables the
audience to lead an active and cheerful lifestyle without concern,
aligning with the brand’s values of halal principles, modesty,

ideology embedded in the Wardah Ultra Proof Protection

3.1.1 A strong and reliable protection ideology
The product embodies the ideology of robust and dependable
protection against external threats, particularly exposure to
sunlight, which is regarded as a potentially hazardous natural
element. This ideology is constructed through a system of signs
that consistently cultivates images of strength, reliability and
security (Table 1). Denotatively, the packaging, featuring the
words “SPF 50” and the use of orange or turquoise green colors,
serves as an icon indicating intensive protection and freshness.
At the indexical level, the SPF 50 designation, terms such as
“Waterproof” and “Sweatproof,” and visual representations of
clear skies and outdoor activities such as padel sports suggest a
direct association with the product’s effectiveness and durability
in extreme conditions. Meanwhile, on the symbolic level,
slogans such as “Strong,” “Strong under the sun,” and “Stay
protected” construct an ideological narrative about the safety,
resilience, and peace of mind that the brand promises to
consumers (Figure 1, picture 2).
From an ideological perspective, the construction of these
signs reinforces Wardah’s position as a trustworthy and
responsible skin protection brand. This product not only fulfils
the physical function of sunscreen but also cultivates a cultural
discourse surrounding a protected, modern, and active body.
Consequently, the ideology of “strong and reliable protection”
functions as a representational strategy that integrates elements
of science, beauty, and trust, while also emphasizing that
activities under the sun are no longer perceived as a threat but
rather symbolize freedom and self-control within the context of
a modern lifestyle.

3.1.3 The ideology of natural beauty, halal, and
personal values
The product embodies an ideology of beauty that is natural,
halal, and deeply rooted in personal values, consistent with
Wardah’s brand identity. The foundational concept is predicated
on the principles of halal integrity, modesty, and beauty derived
from nature, alongside gentle skin care. The use of visual
elements, such as a turquoise-green hijab and a light blue shirt
on the model, complemented by natural colors like orange and
turquoise green, exemplifies the harmony between elegance
and simplicity. Natural settings, including blue skies, beaches,
and wooden elements, further enhance the sense of nature and
tranquility. The primary symbol, the Wardah logo, explicitly
represents the brand’s spiritual and ethical values, while the
depiction of a model wearing a hijab and modest attire affirms
adherence to Islamic norms and a graceful aesthetic (Figure 1,
image 3). Ideologically, this representation positions Wardah as
a brand offering skin protection and ethical, authentic, and
responsible beauty solutions. Here, beauty is conceptualized not
merely as physical appearance but as an expression of
wholeness, integrating spirituality, ethics, and aesthetics.
Consequently, Wardah presents the image of a modern woman:
graceful, confident, and true to herself–all within a framework
of goodness and alignment with nature.
In summary, the Wardah Ultra Proof Protection advertisement
positions its product not only as a sunscreen but also as an essential
partner that enables the audience to lead an active, healthy, and
joyful lifestyle with full confidence and without concern while
remaining true to their personal values and the brand identity of
Wardah (Table 1). These advertisements emphasize both the
functional (strong protection, comfort, natural) and emotional
(freedom, happiness, confidence) aspects of the product, all
framed by Wardah’s robust brand identity.

3.2.1 A functional and solution-oriented beauty
ideology
Pond’s advertisements strategically employed various indices
to establish scientific credibility and emphasize the product’s
functional benefits. The designation “SPF 50 PA++++” serves
not merely as a label but as a quantitative index that effectively
communicates a high level of protection against UVA and UVB
radiation. This index represents a measurable assurance of
protection, instilling consumer confidence in a product’s ability
to withstand intense sunlight. Descriptors such as “breathable,”
“non-sticky,” and “fast absorbed” function as indexical
indicators of the product’s physical attributes and user
experience, suggesting a lightweight, comfortable, and
unobtrusive application. In the realm of skincare, a pleasant
texture on the skin serves as an index of the product quality and
sophistication (Figure 2, picture 3).
While the index provides functional evidence, the symbolism
inherent in the term “Miracle Matte” is instrumental in
constructing a narrative of exceptional efficacy. The term
“Miracle” itself is a potent symbol, connoting something
extraordinary and delivering results that surpasses expectations.
However, within the context of skincare products, this
symbolism is integrated with rationality through functional
claims. “Miracle Matte” is presented not as a magical
phenomenon but as a rational solution to the issues of excessive
shine and large pores, which frequently concern consumers,
particularly those with oily skin (Figure 2, picture 1).
Visual representations, such as “before-after” comparisons—
exemplified in advertisements featuring images of a paint roller
or demonstrations of effects on the cheek—or the depiction of
impeccably smooth and matte skin serve as iconic illustrations
of the promised transformation (Figure 2, pictures 3 and 4). The
“soft focus” effect, as symbolized in the on-screen text, further
reinforces the notion of a guaranteed and demonstrable
transformation. This is not merely a promise; it is a visual
demonstration that persuasively indicates that the product can
indeed and visibly achieve the desired outcomes. Ideologically,
the Pond’s UV Miracle Matte advertisement embodies the
perspective that beauty can be attained through a scientific,

3.2.2 The ideology of comfort, convenience, and
a pleasant user experience
The advertisement’s emphasis on comfort, convenience, and
pleasant user experience underscores that contemporary skincare
products prioritize not only the final outcomes but also the
provision of a practical and efficient user experience. These
products are engineered to be user-friendly and offer maximum
comfort without compromising the user’s appearance or
lifestyle. Descriptors such as “lightweight,” “non-sticky,” and
“fast absorbed,” along with visuals illustrating the ease of
application, such as the product’s rapidly absorbing texture,
enhance the perception of a product that is both light and easy
to use. The slogan “It’s lightweight, Non-Sticky, Fast Absorbed”
and the reported percentage of user satisfaction highlight the
product’s reliability in delivering a positive consumer
experience. Furthermore, visual elements featuring models who
appear relaxed, either leaning on the product or interacting with
a version of themselves holding the product, symbolize a natural
and harmonious relationship between the user and the product
(Table 2). Ideologically, this message asserts that beauty need
not be burdensome or a complex ritual but rather an integral
part of a modern lifestyle that values efficiency, comfort, and
confidence in daily life.
3.2.3 Global, modern, and aspirational beauty
ideology
The advertisement promotes a global, modern, and
aspirational beauty ideology, suggesting that ideal beauty aligns
with international standards characterized by youthfulness,
success, and a fashionable lifestyle. The product is strategically
positioned within a global visual culture that prioritizes clean,
modern, and sophisticated aesthetics. The depiction of models
with fair skin, long hair, and universally accepted facial features
embodies the aspiration towards a globally acknowledged image
of beauty. The Pond’s brand reinforces this image as a symbol
of trust and premium quality, while visual elements such as gold
packaging, a contemporary urban backdrop, and trendy casual
fashion accentuate the impression of exclusivity and modernity.
The slogan “Don’t Miss Out!” instills a sense of urgency and
relevance, encouraging the audience to engage with the
prevailing beauty trend. Ideologically, this advertisement posits
that beauty transcends mere physical appearance, representing a
symbol of social achievement and an aspirational lifestyle that
adheres to global standards of youthfulness, success, and luxury
—standards purportedly attainable through the consumption of
this product (Table 2).
3.2.4 The ideology of brand reliability and proven
trust
The advertisement underscores the ideology of brand
reliability and established trust, emphasizing Pond’s reputation
as a globally recognized brand known for delivering high-quality
skincare products. This message is fundamentally supported by
scientific claims and consumer approval data that substantiate
the product’s efficacy, such as the SPF 50 PA++++ rating and
affirmations such as “99% agree” or “98% agree,” which bolster
the credibility of tangible results. The Pond’s logo functions as a
symbol of trust, already embedded in consumers’ perceptions,
while the gold packaging signifies premium quality, luxury, and
consistent excellence. Ideologically, this advertisement posits that
beauty can be attained through products that are proven to be
safe, effective, and reliable. Consequently, Pond’s is not merely
marketing a product but also offering a sense of security and
confidence supported by extensive experience, research, and
sustained public trust.
The advertisement for Pond’s UV Miracle Matte sunscreen
constructs the ideology that contemporary beauty is
characterized by intelligent, functional, comfortable, and
scientifically validated solutions, accessible through reliable
products from leading international brands. This product not
only provides skin protection but also enhances appearance,
boosts confidence, and enables users to engage in their modern
lifestyles without concerns. This ideology contrasts with that of
brands that emphasize local authenticity and modesty, such as
Wardah. Pond places greater emphasis on effectiveness,
convenience, visually satisfying results, and alignment with
global beauty standards associated with youthfulness and
success. Through the application of Peirce’s semiotics, it is
evident how each element—from packaging color to word
choice—synergistically operates to instill these values and
aspirations in the audience in the following ways.
3.3 Comparative synthesis of ideological
strategies
While Sections 3.1 and 3.2 examined Wardah and Pond’s
separately, this section synthesizes the two analyses to clarify
how each brand’s visual strategy constructs a distinct—and in
several respects, oppositional—ideological position within the
same product category and cultural market.
3.3.1 Naturalness and modesty versus scientific
efficacy
Wardah’s advertisement consistently privileges signs of
naturalness (beach, wood, muted earth tones) and modesty
(hijab, covered clothing, soft color palette) as the primary
carriers of ideological meaning, with functional claims (SPF,
waterproof, sweatproof) serving a supporting role. Pond’s
advertisement inverts this emphasis: functional and quantified
claims (SPF 50 PA++++, “99% agree,” “minimize pores by
23%”) occupy the foreground, while symbolic elements (gold
packaging, the term “Miracle”) serve to dramatize an underlying
rhetoric of scientific proof. Where Wardah asks the viewer to
trust the brand because it embodies the right values, Pond’s asks
the viewer to trust the brand because it demonstrates
measurable results. This distinction echoes a broader tension
identified in advertising theory between value-based and
evidence-based persuasion (Berger, 2013; Frank and Wernick,

1993), and situates the two brands at opposite poles of that
spectrum within a single product category.
3.3.2 Halal identity versus global beauty standards
Wardah’s repeated use of hijab-wearing models and halal-
coded language positions the product within a specifically
Indonesian Muslim consumer identity, consistent with prior
findings that halal branding functions as a trust-building
mechanism among Indonesian consumers (Amelia et al., 2025;
Ekowati et al., 2023). Pond’s, by contrast, constructs a
deliberately deculturalized ideal - fair skin, long hair, urban
cosmopolitan styling - that is legible as “global” precisely
because it is not marked as locally specific. This asymmetry
illustrates a recurring pattern in local-versus-global brand
positioning: the local brand marks cultural identity explicitly as
a value proposition, while the global brand naturalizes its own
cultural specificity (a Western-influenced beauty ideal) as a
universal, unmarked standard (Piatti-Farnell, 2024). In the
Indonesian context, this dynamic is significant because it
positions two visibly different - yet almost equally desirable -
beauty ideals side by side in the same digital feed, requiring
consumers to continuously negotiate between culturally-
affirming and globally-aspirational identities.
3.3.3 Reliable protection versus aspirational
transformation
Wardah frames its product as reliable infrastructure for an
already-active life - protection that enables the consumer to
continue an existing lifestyle without disruption (“Worry Free,”
“Stay Active”). Pond’s frames its product as a transformative
agent that changes the consumer’s condition or appearance
(“before-after” visuals, the large-model/mini-model composition
in Picture 4). This distinction maps onto what might be
described as a maintenance ideology (Wardah) versus a
transformation ideology (Pond’s): the former promises stability
and continuity, the latter promises visible, measurable change.
Both are persuasive strategies, but they appeal to different
underlying consumer anxieties—Wardah to the fear of
disruption during activity, Pond’s to the fear of inadequacy in
appearance.
3.3.4 Synthesis
Taken together, these three axes of comparison suggest that
Wardah and Pond’s do not simply offer different content of
beauty ideology, but occupy structurally distinct positions on
several related continua: value-based vs. evidence-based
persuasion, culturally-marked vs. culturally-unmarked identity,
and maintenance vs. transformation as the promised outcome.
This patterned divergence, rather than isolated thematic
differences, constitutes the clearest evidence that Instagram
advertising in the Indonesian cosmetics market functions as a
site where competing ideologies of beauty, identity, and
legitimacy are simultaneously and visibly negotiated (see Section
3.5 for further theoretical elaboration of this structural
divergence).
3.4 Visual ideology representation in
Wardah and Ponds advertisements in
society
According to the visual analysis conducted by Wardah and
Pond, it can be inferred that the ideological representation
within the advertisements of both brands has the potential to
influence societal behavior as consumers.
The model traces five sequential stages—visual signs, semiotic
meaning, ideological representation, consumer identity, and social
implications—showing how Wardah’s symbol-led register and
Pond’s index-led register diverge through the first four stages before
converging on a shared outcome: the negotiation of competing
beauty norms within Indonesian digital consumer culture.
The ideology conveyed through cosmetic advertisements,
whether by Ponds or Wardah, has considerable potential to alter
societal perceptions of female beauty. This transformation may
extend beyond a mere focus on physical appearance to
encompass a more comprehensive understanding that includes
genuine self-expression and the significance of skin health itself.
When the communicated ideology is imbued with positive
values, advertisements can evolve into a potent mechanism for
cultivating a more responsible consumer culture (Figure 3).
This notion closely aligns with Gramsci’s theory of cultural
hegemony. Gramsci (1971) posited that the dominant group
sustains its power not solely through coercion but also through
the consent of society, whereby the values and worldview of the
dominant group are widely accepted as “common sense.” In
critical discourse, advertisements are frequently perceived as
powerful instruments of cultural domination and control.
Through repetitive visual and narrative representations,
advertisements can propagate narrow, unrealistic, and often
detrimental beauty standards (e.g., an obsession with perfection,
skin whitening, or unattainable body images) that reflect the
economic and ideological interests of dominant groups. If these
standards are accepted uncritically, they can generate social
pressure and incite a consumer culture focused on fulfilling
desires that are often unhealthy and unsustainable.
Gramsci’s theory encompasses the notion of counter-
hegemony. Advertisements that embody positive values and
challenge detrimental dominant norms can function as
instruments of counter-hegemony. In this regard, advertising
can act as a catalyst for social change, fostering new awareness
in the following areas:
1. A Broader Definition of Beauty: Advertisements that
promote authentic self-expression, such as those by
Wardah, which emphasize natural beauty and modesty,
contest uniform and often exclusionary global beauty
standards. This approach encourages self-acceptance and
diversity in the beauty industry.
2. Importance of Skin Health: Advertisements that
underscore protection, long-term skin health, and the use
of safe ingredients, as exemplified by the functional
claims of POND’S or the natural values of Wardah, can
redirect the focus from immediate beauty to responsible
skincare practices.

3. Conscious and Responsible Consumption: Advertisements

presented interact with broader cultural power structures,
potentially challenging harmful norms and promoting a

consumption. Digital technology, particularly social media,
plays a pivotal role in representing and consuming these
ideologies. Platforms such as Instagram have become the
primary venues for brands to convey the ideological messages
of their products.
3.5 Structural divergence and theoritical
implications
Beyond thematic differences, the two brands diverge
structurally in the sign system in which they concentrate their
ideological work. Wardah’s advertisement invests most heavily at
the level of the symbol—the hijab, the color palette associated with
modesty, and Islamic-inflected language (“halal,” “natural”)—
while its indexical evidence (SPF numbers, “waterproof,”
“sweatproof”) largely functions to support rather than carry the
ideological message. Pond’s advertisement inverts this structure:
its ideological weight rests primarily on the index—quantified
claims (“SPF 50 PA++++,” “99% agree,” “minimize pores by
23%”)—with the symbolic layer (gold packaging, “Miracle”)
serving to dramatize what is fundamentally a rhetoric of
measurable proof. This structural asymmetry suggests that local/
halal and global/scientific branding do not merely differ in
content (modesty vs. luxury) but in the semiotic register
through which they seek legitimacy: one through communal-
cultural consensus (symbol), the other through empirical
demonstration (index).
This distinction has implications for the existing debates on
visual communication. It extends Gramsci’s (1971) concept of
hegemony by suggesting that consent can be manufactured
through two distinct epistemic routes: the affective-cultural
route (Wardah, appealing to shared religious identity as self-
evidently true) and the rational-scientific route (Pond’s,
appealing to data as self-evidently true), both of which

4 Limitations
This study had several limitations. First, the analysis is based
on a single advertisement (comprising a multi-image carousel)
from each brand, selected as an illustrative case of each brand’s
visual strategy at a specific market moment (October 2025)
rather than as a fully representative sample of each brand’s
overall advertising output. Wardah and Pond each maintain a
continuously evolving portfolio of campaigns across multiple
product lines, and their ideological emphasis may vary across
campaigns, seasons, or platforms. Future research would benefit
from analyzing a larger corpus of advertisements over time to
assess the consistency and evolution of the ideological patterns
identified here. Second, the market-positioning data used to
justify the brand pairing (Top Brand Index, Frontier Research)
reflect 2023 survey figures, the most recent publicly available
comparative data at the time of writing; more recent shifts in
market share, particularly following both brands’ 2025 sunscreen
launches, could not be incorporated into the analysis. Third, this
study’s interpretation of ideological meaning is based on
researcher-conducted semiotic analysis rather than audience
reception data; as such, the ideological readings proposed here
represent plausible, theoretically grounded interpretations rather
than empirically confirmed audience responses. Future studies
could complement this textual analysis with audience reception
research (e.g., interviews or focus groups) to examine how
Indonesian consumers negotiate the meanings identified in
this study.
5 Conclusion
This study contributes to visual communication theory
by demonstrating that local and global cosmetic brands differ not
only in the content of their ideological appeals but also in the
semiotic mechanism through which legitimacy is constructed—
symbol-driven cultural consensus vs. index-driven empirical proof
—offering a structural, rather than purely thematic, framework
for comparing local and global advertising ideologies.
Pond’s and Wardah advertisements strategically employ
visual semiotics to convey distinct beauty ideologies that
align with their brand identities and target demographics.
Wardah utilizes visuals that emphasize elements of nature,
modesty, and authenticity to construct an ideology of genuine
and respectable beauty that resonates with personal and
cultural values. These visual signs symbolize the freedom to
maintain an active lifestyle without concern, supported by
comfortable and natural skin protection. This approach aligns
with the concept of counter-hegemony , promoting self-
acceptance and diversity in beauty standards for women.
Conversely, Pond’s employs visuals that highlight luxury,
scientific imagery, and global beauty standards to construct
an ideology of superior beauty that is effective and focused
on achieving perfection. Pond’s products serve as symbols
of tools to attain global aspirations and a successful, modern
lifestyle.
The divergence in ideological representation illustrates how
both brands leverage visual semiotics to differentiate their
market position. Wardah crafts a narrative of meaningful and
responsible beauty, while Pond’s offers advanced and
aspirational beauty solutions that are more effective. Both
brands effectively utilized digital platforms to communicate their
ideological messages, albeit with differing visual strategies that
reflect their core values.
This analysis underscores the significance of digital
cosmetic advertising as a domain in which beauty ideologies
are constructed and negotiated. By comprehending how
visual signs function to construct meaning, consumers can
critically engage with these messages and recognize the
potential of advertising as a tool of cultural domination and
as a means to promote a more positive and responsible
perspective on beauty and consumption. Social media has
emerged as the primary arena for cosmetic brands to
showcase their products and actively shape and disseminate
beauty ideologies. Wardah and Pond exemplify how diverse
visual strategies on these platforms can attract varied
audience segments, reflecting a broad spectrum of beauty
aspirations, from those rooted in personal values to those
aligned with global beauty trends.
