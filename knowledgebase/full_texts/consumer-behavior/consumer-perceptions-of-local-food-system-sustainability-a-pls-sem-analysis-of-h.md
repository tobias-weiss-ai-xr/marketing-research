Abstract
In recent years, growing attention has been devoted to the development of sus -
tainable food systems, with particular emphasis on short food supply chains. The
research aimed to identify the factors and their interrelationships that influence
Hungarian consumers’ behavior when purchasing local food, within the framework
of environmental and economic sustainability. The empirical investigation was con -
ducted using data obtained from a nationwide questionnaire survey, comprising 635
valid responses. The dataset was analyzed using multivariate statistical techniques,
with a primary focus on Structural Equation Modeling. Specifically, Partial Least
Squares Structural Equation Modeling was employed due to its suitability for model
construction and theory development in sustainability research. A reflective mea -
surement model was developed, yielding five latent variables that jointly account
for consumer decision-making in relation to farmers’ markets and local food. The
results demonstrate that consumer attitudes toward farmers’ markets are shaped by
a combination of altruistic (environmental and social) motives and self-oriented
benefits (such as quality and health). The model explains approximately 24% of the
variance in consumers’ perceived sustainability of local food systems. Among the
statistically supported explanatory variables, health emerged as the most influential
factor, followed by market offer experience and access convenience. The identified
factor structure has several practical implications, including improving producer
markets, formulating targeted marketing strategies, and developing policy measures
that may support sustainable consumption patterns. These findings provide a mul -
tidisciplinary foundation for future research in agri-food policy, contribute to the
theoretical understanding of sustainable food consumption, and offer insights into
the efficacy of local food system initiatives.
Keywords Sustainability · Local food · Short food supply chains · Consumer
behavior · Farmers’ market

Introduction
The industrialization of agricultural production and the dominance of conventional
technologies have, particularly since the post-war modernization wave from the
1960s onward, increasingly marginalized small-scale farms from mainstream com -
mercial channels in many European and North American contexts. In East-Central
Europe, including Hungary, this transformation followed a distinct trajectory shaped
by collectivization, post-1989 restructuring, and a renewed visibility of small-scale
and direct-marketing producers. This trend has widened the physical and social dis -
tance between producers and final consumers, while an increasing number of inter -
mediaries have become involved in the distribution chain. However, the magnitude
and timing of this process vary considerably across countries, and several European
regions have retained direct sales models that align closely with contemporary local
food systems (Marsden et al. 2000; Renting et al. 2003). Short food supply chains
(SFSCs) and local food systems offer alternative solutions to bridge these gaps by
fostering direct links between producers and consumers. Local food systems estab -
lish interconnected networks designed to enhance food security and promote sustain-
ability at the community level (Dunne et al. 2011). In particular, local food systems
that incorporate SFSCs have also emerged as promising approaches to sustainable
development, emphasizing proximity between producers and consumers, shortening
supply chains, and fostering more direct relationships (Jia et al. 2024; Hyland et al.
2024; Menéndez et al. 2025; Ritter and Orbán 2025).
Nevertheless, the sustainability of local food systems should not be regarded as
an inherent or self-evident characteristic. Although local food is frequently associ -
ated with positive environmental, social and economic outcomes, consumers often
evaluate sustainability through subjective interpretations rather than objective assess-
ments alone (Hutchings et al. 2024). These evaluations are influenced by perceived
benefits, trust in producers, transparency of information, personal experiences, and
beliefs associated with local food consumption (Corry et al. 2023). Consequently,
perceived sustainability has emerged as an important construct for understanding
consumer behaviour in local food systems, reflecting how consumers interpret and
assess sustainability-related attributes rather than the objectively measurable sustain-

ability performance of the system itself (Aksakalli Bayraktar et al. 2023). Despite the
growing body of research on local food consumption and short food supply chains,
relatively limited attention has been paid to how consumers construct perceptions of
the sustainability of local food systems and which factors jointly shape these percep-
tions. Existing studies have primarily focused on purchasing intentions, trust, will -
ingness to pay, or individual sustainability-related attributes in isolation. Therefore,
there remains a need for integrative models that explain perceived sustainability as a
multidimensional consumer evaluation.
However, despite the increasing use of perception-based sustainability constructs
in academic literature, there is often a lack of alignment between their conceptual
definition and empirical operationalization. It is evident that the concept of perceived
sustainability in local food systems is characterized by significant heterogeneity. This
is due to the fact that disparate studies employ a range of indicators that reflect eco -
nomic, environmental and social evaluations to varying extents. This can create a dis-
crepancy between the construct’s theoretical scope and its measurement. Accordingly,
the present study places explicit emphasis on improving the conceptual-operational
alignment of perceived sustainability and on positioning constructs that integrate key
attitudinal and experimental dimensions within local food systems research.
The aim of the research was to develop and empirically test a theoretical model
identifying the perception-based factors and their interrelationships shaping Hungar-
ian farmers’ market (FM) visitors’ assessments of the environmental and economic
sustainability of local food systems. By examining perceived sustainability as a mul-
tidimensional consumer evaluation, the study contributes to the literature on sus -
tainable food consumption and short food supply chains and provides insights into
the mechanisms through which consumers interpret the sustainability of local food
systems.
Literature review
Different definitions of alternative food systems often converge around core charac -
teristics such as alterity, proximity, relationality, and a commitment to sustainability.
Alternative food networks (AFNs) and SFSCs are frequently used to describe similar
socio-economic phenomena. Within this context, civic food networks are typically
considered a specific subset of AFNs, distinguished by their emphasis on community
engagement and active citizen participation (Michel-Villarreal et al. 2021). A SFSC
refers to a system involving only a limited number of economic participants who
collaborate to strengthen local economies and maintain close geographic and social
ties among producers, processors, and consumers (Mancini et al. 2019). It also con-
tributes to sustainability by providing high-quality food and minimizing agriculture’s
environmental impact (Lamine and Vagneron 2020).
The typology of SFSCs includes both traditional (e.g., farmers’ markets, roadside
stands) and more innovative models (e.g., box schemes, community supported agri -
culture), all of which aim to promote proximity and sustainability in food provisioning
(Marsden et al. 2000; Renting et al. 2003; Santini and y Paloma 2013). Environmen-
tal sustainability is a key dimension, as consumers often associate short supply chains

with lower food miles, seasonality, biodiversity support, resource circulation, and
reduced use of chemicals (Galli et al. 2015; Lami et al. 2023; Ušča and Aļeksējeva
2023; Jia et al. 2024; Menéndez et al. 2025). It should be noted, however, that the
cited literature predominantly reports consumer perceptions of these environmental
attributes rather than objective measurements of outcomes, such as life-cycle green -
house gas emissions, biodiversity indicators, or resource-use intensity. SFSCs also
contribute to economic sustainability by supporting local economies and empower -
ing small-scale producers (Brown and Miller 2008; Filippini et al. 2023; Helgheim et
al. 2024). Thus, the social function of short supply chains, especially FMs, represents
one of the major strengths, as they help strengthen community ties, increase trust, and
improve producers’ livelihoods. (Futamura 2007; Brown and Miller 2008; Vittersø et
al. 2019; Filippini et al. 2023; Helgheim et al. 2024). However, Azima and Mundler
(2022) add that the social, environmental, and economic benefits associated with
SFSCs, while real, should be interpreted with caution.
Numerous studies indicate that consumers perceive local foods as fresher, health-
ier, and of higher quality, often associating them with environmental benefits (Lami
et al. 2023; Acella et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2024; Sánchez Brenes et al. 2024; Menén-
dez et al. 2025). Trust plays a crucial role in perceptions of local products: consum -
ers who trust producers and the transparency of SFSCs are more likely to purchase
local products (László et al. 2024). In addition, consumers see buying local food as a
means of supporting farmers, strengthening the rural economy, and increasing com -
munity well-being (Enthoven and Van den Broek 2021; Ferreiro et al. 2021; Filippini
et al. 2023; Zhong 2023; Artha et al. 2024). However, the concept of “local prod -
uct” remains inconsistent in the scientific literature, as no uniform definition exists
at either the European or national level (Carter 1997; Dunne et al. 2011). The geo-
graphical distance interpreted as “local” varies across countries: in the United States,
it is often defined as within 400 miles (approx. 650 km) (Martinez et al. 2010), while
in Europe it typically ranges from 20 to 100 km (Augère-Granier 2016).
Numerous studies have defined and analyzed SFSCs, highlighting their adapt -
ability to evolving consumer demands (Marsden et al. 2000; Renting et al. 2003).
At the same time, many producers utilize multiple marketing channels concurrently
to enhance profitability (Brown and Miller 2008; Martinez et al. 2010). Consumer
attitudes play a crucial role in the success and sustainability of local food systems.
Research shows that consumers are increasingly attracted to SFSCs due to their per-
ceived benefits, including environmental sustainability, support for local economies,
and access to fresher, higher-quality products. However, consumer engagement with
SFSCs varies widely, and research has identified distinct market segments based on
attitudes and behaviors. European and Hungarian studies have identified distinct
market segments using cluster and factor analysis, while in Japan, the ‘sustainable
food seekers’ segment has emerged (Kovács et al. 2022; Hyland et al. 2024; Takagi
et al. 2025).
Across the reviewed studies, several findings recur consistently. Health consider -
ations, perceived freshness and product quality, and trust in producers emerge as the
most frequently reported motivators of local food purchasing, while convenience,
price sensitivity, and limited product range represent persistent constraints. These
determinants can be grouped along the conventional distinction between intrinsic

motivators (personal values, beliefs, and health orientation, which shape consum -
ers’ propensity to choose local food irrespective of market conditions) and extrinsic
motivators that depend on the structural characteristics of the supply chain itself.
Among the intrinsic dimensions, quality and taste are repeatedly identified as primary
drivers of repeat purchase (Acella et al. 2024; Sánchez Brenes et al. 2024), whereas
the extrinsic dimension is dominated by price–value trade-offs, with some consumers
willing to pay a premium for local products and others remaining sensitive to price
differentials and substituting cheaper alternatives (Mustapa and Kallas 2025), and by
convenience and accessibility, with the availability of local products in supermarkets
or on online platforms substantially shaping purchasing decisions (Chang et al. 2022;
Hyland et al. 2024). What remains less clearly established across this body of work
is how these motivators jointly shape consumers’ broader perceptions of the sustain-
ability of the local food system, beyond the act of purchase itself.
In addition, several factors hinder the spread of SFSCs, including limited seasonal
availability, a lack of consumer knowledge about the provenance, certifications, and
seasonality of local products, high prices, and the greater convenience offered by
supermarkets (Aouinait et al. 2022; Chang et al. 2022; Gorton et al. 2023; Hyland
et al. 2024). For instance, consumers in Germany, Spain, Greece, and Hungary often
find it difficult to access a wide range of local products in one place, which dis -
courages regular purchases from SFSCs (Chang et al. 2022). These barriers operate
at distinct levels, ranging from product-level constraints (price, perceived range) to
structural and informational asymmetries that disproportionately affect occasional
buyers and rural-area consumers with limited physical access to FMs. In response to
these barriers, the literature has proposed several mitigation strategies. These include
improving accessibility, enhancing marketing efforts, and providing education about
the benefits of SFSCs. For instance, promoting SFSCs through online platforms and
aggregating local products in a single location can increase convenience for consum-
ers (Chang et al. 2022). Furthermore, educating consumers about the environmental
and social benefits of SFSCs, including labelling schemes that signal provenance
and production practices, can encourage greater participation (Aouinait et al. 2022).
Moreover, several studies highlight that online platforms and low-cost digital tech -
nologies enhance the resilience, visibility, and competitiveness of SFSCs (González-
Azcárate et al. 2021, 2022, 2023; Ritter and Orbán 2025).
The consumer-level dynamics outlined above unfold within an institutional envi -
ronment shaped by policy. Although purchasing decisions are taken at the household
scale, the broader sustainability outcomes of SFSCs depend on supportive regula -
tory, rural development, and financing frameworks. In Europe, initiatives such as the
“Strength2Food” project have underscored the importance of policy interventions in
promoting SFSCs (Vittersø et al. 2019). Similarly, innovative financing tools, such
as civic crowdfunding, have been successfully used to support local food projects in
Madrid, Spain (González-Azcárate et al. 2023). Finland’s long-standing commitment
to providing free school meals offers a unique institutional context for examining
the challenges of public sector food procurement. This setting enables researchers
to explore how national policies intersect with local food systems and sustainability
goals (Lehtinen 2012).

Taken together, the consumer-level, structural, and policy-level perspectives
reviewed above leave three interrelated research gaps unresolved. First, while indi -
vidual motivational factors influencing local food purchasing behavior have been
extensively documented, their simultaneous structural interrelationships have rarely
been examined within a unified modeling framework. Existing Hungarian and
broader European studies have predominantly employed cluster and factor analysis
to identify consumer segments (Kovács et al. 2022; Hyland et al. 2024), yet the path
relationships among attitudinal constructs, and their joint explanatory power with
respect to sustainability outcomes, remain underexplored. Second, the empirical lit -
erature on SFSC consumer behavior is heavily concentrated in Western European and
North American contexts (Vittersø et al. 2019; González-Azcárate et al. 2021, 2022,
2023; Lehtinen 2012; Futamura 2007), while Central and Eastern European markets,
including Hungary, present distinct institutional, agricultural, and consumer-cultural
conditions that cannot be straightforwardly generalized from these settings. Third,
the concept of sustainable local food systems has largely been treated as an objective
economic outcome, with insufficient attention paid to how consumers construct and
perceive it through their purchasing behavior and value orientations.
The present study addresses all three gaps by developing and empirically testing
an integrated reflective Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-
SEM) model based on a nationwide purposive sample, in which sustainable local
food systems are operationalized as a perception-based latent construct shaped by
quality, health, consumer experience, availability, and family experience.
Theoretical model and hypothesis development
The present study is theoretically grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)
(Ajzen 1991), a well-established psychological framework used to explain the deter-
minants of human behavior. According to TPB, the performance of a behavior is
determined by an individual’s behavioral intention, which is shaped by three inde -
pendent precursors: attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control.
However, the present model does not aim to directly measure behavioral intention.
Rather, it follows the underlying logic of TPB by examining how attitudinal, social,
experiential, and accessibility-related factors shape consumers’ perceptions of the
sustainability of local food systems. Therefore, the framework is considered TPB-
inspired and extends the traditional model by incorporating experiential dimensions
relevant to sustainable food consumption.
The model operationalizes TPB-related dimensions through specific latent con -
structs associated with sustainable consumption. Quality and health are conceptu -
alized as cognitive components of attitude, representing consumer’s instrumental
evaluations of local product attributes. Family experience reflects social influences
and collective habits within the primary social unit and is conceptually related to
subjective norms. Access convenience is associated with perceived behavioral con -
trol, reflecting the structural facilitators or barriers that influence access to local food
products. Additionally, market offer experience is included as an effective facilitator
that bridges the gap between social context and the formation of intention. Draw -
ing on TPB-related concepts and broader attitudinal frameworks, these factors are

The conceptualization of a sustainable local food system is inherently multidimen-
sional. In this research, the construct is defined by three pillars: supporting the local
economy, promoting locally grown and produced goods, and mitigating environmen-
tal contamination. Regarding the first factor, Wardle et al. (2023) highlight that FMs
foster social interactions that influence attendance patterns and generate local eco -
nomic multipliers. The ‘field-to-fork’ approach (Edwards-Jones 2010) aligns with
environmental sustainability by minimizing carbon footprints, although its effective-
ness depends on specific food chain characteristics. Finally, as emphasized by Jung
et al. ( 2024), reducing ‘food miles’ represents a key strategy for lowering regional
transport-related emissions.
In terms of quality, it is essential to highlight the position of Bocken et al. (2016),
who argue that durable and high-quality products necessitate fewer resources in the
long term, thereby aligning with consumers’ expectations regarding the circular
economy and sustainability. Moreover, the emergence of superior products in the
economy fosters sustained consumer confidence (Zeithaml et al. 1996). Such con -
sumer loyalty can generate stable demand, which, when combined with brand loy -
alty, is often perceived to support the development of a more sustainable economic
model. A systematic literature review by Bayram et al. (2024) also found that quality
is a critical element of the circular economy and, consequently, of perceived sustain-

H1  Quality has a significant positive impact on consumers’ perceived sustainability 
of local food systems.
The relationship between health perceptions and consumers’ assessment of the
sustainability of local food systems is well-supported in the context of local food
purchasing behavior. Consumers who prioritize health tend to associate FM prod -
ucts with fresher, more natural, and minimally processed food, thereby perceiving
local food systems as potentially contributing to both personal and community well-
being (Conner et al. 2010; Hu et al. 2021). This association is further reinforced by
the growing body of evidence suggesting that health-conscious consumers are more
likely to engage with sustainable consumption practices and to attribute positive sus-
tainability perceptions to locally sourced products (Magnusson et al. 2001; Hyland
et al. 2024). In this study, health is therefore defined not as an objective indicator of
physical well-being, but as a consumer perception reflecting the degree to which FM
products are associated with healthier lifestyles and sustainable food choices.
H2  Health  has a significant positive impact on consumers’ perceived sustainability 
of local food systems.
The market offer experience plays a significant role in shaping consumer percep -
tions and purchasing behavior within local food systems. Consumers evaluate not
only the products themselves but also attributes such as freshness, quality, authentic-
ity, traceability, product variety, fair pricing, and supply reliability (Adams and Salois
2010; Feldmann and Hamm 2015; Ingrassia et al. 2017). Additionally, local food
markets strengthen consumers’ connections to food origins and local communities,
increasing perceived value and satisfaction (McDaniel et al. 2021; László and Wahlen
2024). Consequently, positive market offer experiences foster consumer engagement,
trust, and repeat purchasing behavior (Maró et al. 2023).
H3  Market  offer  experience  has  a  significant  positive  impact  on  consumers’  per-
ceived sustainability of local food systems.
Availability has been demonstrated to influence the consumers’ views on the
viability of a sustainable local food system. However, according to the findings of
Genova and Tonet (2025), this influence extends beyond physical aspects to include
subjective dimensions. The subjective aspect primarily relates to product accessibil -
ity, as limited visibility hinders consumer decision-making. This issue is particularly
relevant for consumers who rarely visit FMs, where not only the product quality but
also the knowledge of salespeople play an important role in product selection. Con -
sistent with the findings of Weissmann and Hock ( 2021), spatial distance has been
found to exert a direct influence on purchase intention. Furthermore, locally available

H4  Access  convenience  has  a  significant  positive  impact  on  consumers’  perceived

In accordance with the conclusions of Essiz and Mandrik ( 2022) on the correla -

communication within the family and the subsequent exchange of experiences can
influence environmentally conscious consumer attitudes through parental role mod -

members can transfer and adapt their values and knowledge regarding sustainability
to their parents. Consequently, family decision-making units can be transferred and
consolidated within the household, depending on specific behaviors associated with
perceived sustainable economic thinking. The findings of Wang and Zhang ( 2025)
complement this by showing that parents who observe unsustainable behavior in
their children are willing to invest significantly more energy in demonstrating more

The theoretical model is designed to examine a complex system of interrelation -
ships in order to measure the components of consumer attitudes toward sustainability.
These hypotheses may help address certain research gaps, including the gap identi -
fied by Vidal-Ayuso et al. ( 2023) regarding the analysis of the post-purchase phase
of sustainable consumption. The first, third, and fourth hypotheses of this study are
particularly relevant to this perspective, as factors such as quality, accessibility, and
customer experience can all play a crucial role in fostering sustainable consump -
tion patterns and their ongoing maintenance. Furthermore, Sivapalan et al. ( 2021)
revealed that models examining green consumption behavior do not adequately inte-
grate personal and consumption-centric values. The present paper, therefore, pos -
its hypotheses concerning quality ( H1), health ( H2), and family ( H5) as potential
contributors to bridging this gap in green consumption research. Consequently, the
theoretical model is well-suited to addressing these research gaps and generating
novel insights.
Methodology
The methodology employed in this analysis is structural modeling, which is suit -
able for facilitating government and corporate decision-making through behavioral
econometric representation. The analyses were conducted using the SPSS Statistics
25 and Smart PLS 3 software.

Measurement model
The present research did not aim to develop an indicator based on manifest vari -
ables; therefore, the reflective model was employed. Furthermore, in sustainability
research, the use of both formative and reflective measurement models is strongly rec-
ommended. However, for economics-oriented studies, the reflective model appears
particularly suitable (Khan et al. 2016).
Partial least squares-based structural equation modeling
When PLS-SEM is applied in an exploratory manner, especially in cases where mani-
fest variables have not previously been combined in a similar way, it is essential to
evaluate the reliability, validity, and adequacy of the proposed model (Zhang et al.
2015). PLS-SEM is particularly advantageous in scenarios where the sample size
is limited, yet the model complexity is sufficient, as evidenced by numerous stud -
ies (Sarstedt et al. 2016). Nevertheless, it also performs effectively with large sam -
ples, offering a flexible and robust methodological framework (Sarstedt et al. 2021).
The non-parametric nature of the PLS-SEM methodology (Hair and Alamer 2022)
enables the construction of complex models (e.g., involving 8–10 latent variables).
The applicability of PLS-SEM should be verified through a series of preliminary
tests. First, the loadings of individual items (manifest variables) must be examined,
these should ideally exceed 0.7 to ensure indicator reliability (Hair et al. 2021). Next,
a correlation test must be performed, which can be achieved by evaluating the het -
erotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT) and the Fornell-Larcker criterion. For the HTMT,
values below 0.9 should be excluded, as they may cause problems in the construction.
Although the reliability of PLS-SEM is well-established, its model fit indicators
are still evolving, and universally accepted thresholds have not yet been defined
(Dash and Paul 2021). However, the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR)
and the normalized fit index (NFI) are commonly reported as fit measures for PLS-
SEM models (Krebsbach 2013). According to prior research, NFI values above 0.5
and approaching 1.0 are considered acceptable (Lohmöller 1989), while SRMR val-
ues below 0.08 indicate a good model fit (Rozman et al. 2020). All model fit indices
in the present study were computed using SmartPLS 3.2.9, which provides appropri-
ate functionality for these analyses.
Data collection
Given the social science focus of the research, the data were collected through a
questionnaire survey of consumers in Hungary (Table 1). The analysis of the survey
questionnaire employed a combination of PLS-SEM and closed-ended questions,
with Likert items adapted from a previously validated survey instrument in Hungary
(Kacz et al. 2022) to enhance the model’s reliability and validity. Likert scale ques -
tions of the questionnaire employed a 5-point scale ranging from “strongly disagree”
to “strongly agree”, consistent with a validated questionnaire. The use of five-point
Likert scales was advantageous due to their intuitive nature and ease of comprehen -

sion, which facilitate more accurate responses (Chen et al. 2015). The survey was
conducted in the second half of 2024, reaching a total of 710 respondents.
Theoretically, the sample size would have permitted the implementation of a
covariance-based structural equation model (CB-SEM). However, the study’s objec-
tives were deemed more appropriately addressed by PLS-SEM. The present study
was primarily prediction-oriented and exploratory, with the objective of elucidat -
ing the relationships among latent constructs that determine market offer experience
and access convenience, and to maximize the explanation of variance in the target
variable. As the research did not aim to rigorously test an existing theory but rather
to identify the mechanisms of action between variables and to develop a novel theo-
retical framework, the PLS-SEM methodology enabled more robust estimates. This
procedure handles complex model structures and potential non-normal distributions
among measurement items with greater efficiency, thereby ensuring greater stability
in structural relationships. Notably, this approach liberates the analysis from the strict
fit constraints characteristic of CB-SEM.
During data clearing, incomplete or identical responses were excluded, resulting
in a validity rate of approximately 90%. Data collection was carried out using purpo-
sive sampling, as the objective was to reach consumers using FMs. To achieve this,
Hungarian institutions associated with the short supply chain – including the Hungar-
ian National Rural Network, the Ministry of Agriculture, the Hungarian Chamber of
Agriculture, the Association of Small-Scale Producers, and the Association of Con -
scious Consumers – participated in the online dissemination of the questionnaire.
These organizations provide extensive consumer coverage across Hungary, enabling
a nationwide targeted analysis. Their collaboration offered an effective means of
reaching FM consumers through established networks, thereby providing a critical
input for achieving the research objectives.
During the survey, it was imperative to engage consumers who regularly use FMs.
To this end, a criterion was devised requiring the inclusion of respondents who visit
an FM at least a few times a year in the subsequent analysis. To maintain the integrity
of the study, respondents who did not use FMs were excluded from the analysis. Con-
sequently, non-users were excluded from the analysis, and this group was not treated
as a moderator variable.

structed model. First, common method bias was examined using Harman’s single
factor test. In this procedure, all items included in the model are loaded onto a single
factor and analyzed for their explanatory power. If the explained variance attributable
to a single factor exceeds 50%, it may indicate the presence of bias within the model
(Harman 1960). In the constructed model, the variance in the explanation by the
manifest variables in a single factor was 25.96%, suggesting that no common method
bias was detected. A subsequent bias assessment was conducted via a bias-corrected
bootstrap test for the path coefficients, with the results presented in Table 7.
In the context of the bias-corrected bootstrap test, as outlined in the recom -

a particularly salient factor among consumers who deliberately opt for farm-fresh
products, making them more willing to pay a premium. The particular salience of
health in the Hungarian sample may reflect the broader post-socialist food culture, in
which direct producer contact and unprocessed, locally grown products are perceived
as a reliable alternative to the industrialized food supply – a cultural predisposition
that amplifies the health–sustainability association beyond what is typically observed
in Western European consumer studies. Comparable studies from Mediterranean
Europe (Filippini et al. 2023) more frequently emphasize community embeddedness,
while Northern European studies (Hyland et al. 2024) more often highlight envi -
ronmental certification schemes. The Hungarian pattern, in which individual health
benefits dominate, may therefore indicate that consumer engagement with sustain -
able local food systems in Central and Eastern Europe currently operates through
individual rather than collective rationales. The predominance of individual benefits
(e.g., health) over community factors indicates a certain degree of self-interest among
consumers. This phenomenon can be explained by the framework of egoistic altru -
ism, as consumers support sustainable local systems primarily because they offer
direct, tangible rewards.
In terms of economic status, these consumers tend to belong to relatively higher-
income segments of the sample, which, according to the developed model, may rep-
resent a limitation of a sustainable local food system. This interpretation is supported
by Allen’s (2010) research, which demonstrates that products available at FMs are
not necessarily indicative of fairness. The existence of price disparities and restricted
market access can foster social inequalities, excluding economically disadvantaged
consumers from the FM customer base. Consequently, FM producers whose products
do not align with consumers’ expectations should consider adjusting pricing strate -
gies to reduce negative experiences and improve customer loyalty.
The model also indicates that availability (β = 0.168) and family experience
(β = 0.164) exert the least influence on the consumers’ perception of a sustainable
local food system. In the existing literature, both availability (Guillen-Royo 2010)
and family experience (La Rosa and Johnson 2021) have been regarded as prerequi-
sites for sustainability. In accordance with these findings, the present study suggests
that personal and direct consumer benefits (health) outweigh social or community-
level factors (e.g., family experience, accessibility) as motivating influences. There -
fore, it can be concluded that even among consumers who frequent FMs, self-interest
remains a stronger motivator than collective or public interest.

spatial proximity serves as a catalyst for developing consumer expertise and appre -

could further enhance transparency and information sharing. Related initiatives, such
as composting programs, may also serves as a valuable demonstration of sustain -

transport challenges, as it impacts the frequency of visits to FMs (Horvath et al.

Addressing infrastructure deficiencies could facilitate enhanced accessibility

a salient consideration in the context of FMs (Benassai-Dalmau et al. 2025).
Hence, the establishment of temporary or mobile FMs in specific settlements
is recommended, provided that the transportation process does not compromise
product freshness. Alternatively, adjusting the operational hours of existing FMs
to better align with community schedules could also enhance accessibility and
consumer participation.

Limitations and future research directions
The study provides valuable insights; however, certain limitations require caution in
interpreting the results. Despite the relatively large sample size ( n = 635), the ques -
tionnaire analysis is cross-sectional, which constrains the temporal relevance of the
findings. Despite the time constraints imposed on the survey, the procedural mea -
sures implemented during data collection ensured the maintenance of anonymity.
Consequently, respondents were made aware in advance that there were no right or
wrong answers. Furthermore, the questions in the questionnaire were presented in a
randomized order, thereby reducing response bias.
Although institutions involved in the distribution of the questionnaire were fully
aligned with the research objectives, the sample does not reflect the national popula-
tion. The purposive sampling approach has important consequences for the inter -
pretation of the findings. As the sample is not randomly selected and specifically
focused on FM consumers, the results cannot be considered representative of the
general population. Additionally, it is important to acknowledge the potential influ -
ence of geographical bias, as the study focuses on Hungary, and may not accurately
reflect the perspectives of consumers in other countries. This limitation complicates
the generalization of the findings to broader populations, although the study’s con -
struct demonstrates adequate reliability, and validity.
Another limitation of the study is selection bias arising from targeted sampling.
Given that data collection was conducted through institutions linked to FMs, it is
probable that the sample included respondents who were already committed to the
issue. The applied sampling strategy was imperative because several core latent
constructs in the theoretical framework, such as market offer experience and access
convenience, are explicitly predicated on active customer transactions and on-site
experiences, which non-buyers would not be able to evaluate. Consequently, includ-
ing non-buyers would have compromised the validity of the reflective measurement
model.
However, the approach carries significant implications and limits the scope
of interpretation. The findings reflect the attitudes of an already engaged, health-
conscious, higher-income consumer segment rather than the general public, as the
sample consists solely of active market visitors. Furthermore, the analysis does not
account for structural barriers related to the geographic context. Depending on the
urban–rural divide, consumers in remote or rural areas of Hungary often face sig -
nificant constraints, such as a total absence of regular farmers’ markets or poor rural
road infrastructure. The exclusion of a broader demographic from short food sup -
ply chains stems from a lack of physical access, creating a structural mechanism of
exclusion. Future research should therefore use a randomized national sampling that
includes both buyers and non-buyers. It should explicitly include geographic location
and regional infrastructure as moderating variables to capture the barriers faced by
those currently excluded from local food systems.

The bias tests also indicate that the developed model is appropriate, although some
distortion was observed in the quality dimension with respect to its impact on the
perceived sustainability of local food systems. The findings of this study, therefore,
contradict those of other studies (Juran and Godfrey 1999; Chen 2010), calling into
question the assumption that quality influences perceptions of sustainability.
Beyond the methodological limitations, several constraints related to the interpre-
tation and discussion of the results should also be acknowledged. Firstly, the interpre-
tation of statistically significant relationships may be overemphasized, particularly
given the model’s relatively low explanatory power and small effect sizes. Secondly,
it is imperative to acknowledge that the discourse inherently entails a certain degree
of theoretical inference, in which explanations are predicated on plausible, albeit
not empirically validated, mechanisms. Therefore, it is imperative to acknowledge
that these interpretations should be regarded as indicative rather than confirmatory.
Thirdly, the comparative evaluation of the relative importance of constructs is based
on model-specific coefficients. These coefficients may vary depending on measure -
ment choices, sample composition, or model specification. Consequently, the identi-
fied ranking of effects should not be interpreted as universally stable across contexts.
In conclusion, the implications of the findings may indicate contextual characteristics
of the studied consumer group, as well as their market structure, consumer prefer -
ences, or cultural attitudes towards sustainability.
For future research, it is recommended that the analysis be conducted on sam -
ples representative of the national population to improve the generalizability of the
results. Furthermore, given the validity of the model demonstrated in this study, it
would be worthwhile extending the analysis to other countries to explore consumer
perceptions of sustainable local food systems. In addition, conducting more in-depth
analyses focusing on the relationship between quality and sustainability is advised to
ensure the generalizability of the results.
Conclusion
The objective of this study was to develop a theoretical model that analyses the
role and significance of factors determining the perceived sustainability of local
food systems. The PLS-SEM method was employed, and the model was based on
a previously validated questionnaire and items. The following ranking of factors
influencing the perception of a sustainable food system can be posited: health
(1), market offer experience (2), access convenience (3), and family experience
(4). The ranking should be read in light of the small effect sizes documented in
the structural model: although health and market offer experience emerged as the
most consistently supported correlates of perceived sustainability, the magnitude
of their associations remains modest. This suggests that fostering perceived sus -
tainability of local food systems is unlikely to depend on a single dominant fac -
tor, but rather on the coordinated, incremental contribution of several attitudinal
and experiential dimensions, in which FM organizers and producers occupy a
particularly influential position.
