# The Influence of Green Consumer Behavior Factors on Consumers' Repurchase Intention for Eco-Friendly Household Products

This article examines how cognitive and personal factors of green consumer behavior influence consumers'
repurchase intention for eco-friendly household products. The study responds to the growing relevance of
sustainable consumption and to the need for more detailed evidence on the household goods sector, which
has received less attention than food and cosmetics in previous research. A quantitative online survey was
conducted in Lithuania, and 399 valid responses were analyzed using reliability analysis, descriptive statistics,
Spearman correlation and linear regression. The results show that both cognitive and personal factors are
statistically significant predictors of repurchase intention. Personal factors have a slightly stronger explana -
tory effect than cognitive factors, although the difference is marginal. Attitudes toward eco-friendly products
and perceived behavioral control demonstrate the strongest effects, while perceived green value also has a
positive and significant influence. Brand trust is positively related to repurchase intention, but its explanatory
power is lower than that of the other constructs. The findings suggest that transparent information about
health and environmental benefits, strengthened product credibility and trust-building communication are
important for encouraging repeated purchases of eco-friendly household products. The article contributes to
green consumer behavior research by focusing on repurchase intention in the household products sector and
by comparing the relative influence of cognitive and personal determinants.

2. Literature Review2. Literature Review
Consumer behavior is a complex process that
includes many stages and determines the marketing
strategies of organizations and the intention of
individuals to purchase a product or service. The
factors that determine the intention to purchase
can be different and, accordingly, depend on the
situation, the worldview or personality traits of
individuals. The prices of goods and services,
consumer income, needs and available information
about the consumer properties of goods and
services are also of great importance for consumer
behavior. Organizations are increasingly faced
with the challenges of the growing green market,
therefore, it is appropriate to start the analysis of
the concept of green consumer behavior with the
examination of the classical term, theories and
factors of consumer behavior and the concepts of
the green market.
According to Manuere et al. (2022), consumer
behavior is an important field of study that examines
the consumption choices of individuals, groups or
organizations, the purchasing and use habits of
goods or services and the ideas, experiences and
needs associated with these processes, as well as the
ways in which products and services are disposed
of. According to Peneda de Oliveira and Sousa,
(2020), consumer behavior is characterized by
mental and emotional activity in order to satisfy
emerging needs and desires. Therefore, consumer
behavior research allows us to understand the daily
routine of consumers and their daily lives, as well as
their relationship with products and services.
Chopra et al. (2020) distinguish five stages
of consumer behavior: problem recognition,
information search, evaluation of alternatives, final
decision, post-purchase decisions. In each stage,
consumer behavior manifests itself differently: in
the first, the consumer may recognize or discover
a problem after seeing an advertising message or
hearing information from the environment. In this
case, a significant difference between the desired
and the actual (unsatisfactory) situation is finally
perceived, therefore the desire and decision process
are activated. In the second stage, the consumer
collects information to fulfill the emerging desire
- searches for information on the Internet, in the
environment, shows interest and involvement.
The third stage is characterized by the fact that the
collected information is evaluated and compared,

Many theories are used to understand and analyze
consumer behavior. The most commonly cited
in scientific literature are: The Theory of Planned
Behavior is used to analyze consumer intentions
regarding a specific behavior through attitudes,
subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control
(Sheoran and Kumar, 2022). In addition, this
theory emphasizes that the consumer's desire to
purchase a product or service helps to best predict
future consumer behavior, since behavior arises
from individual intentions. In this case, all three
aspects - attitude, subjective norms, and perceived
behavioral control - are understood as indicators
that determine the desire to strive for a certain
behavior. Attitude reflects a positive or negative
assessment, subjective norms - perceived social
pressure and the opinion of others, and perceived
behavioral control - the individual's own assessment
of the ability to perform any specific behavior in a
given situation. In addition, the Theory of Planned
Behavior model is one of the most commonly used
models to understand and explain human behavior.
Another approach, the theory of consumption
values, explains why consumers choose a product,
product category or brand by focusing on perceived
consumption values (Tanrikulu, 2021). These
values are divided into five subgroups: functional,
conditional, emotional, social and epistemic value.
Functional is defined as the perceived benefit or
advantage, conditional - as the perceived benefit
due to the result, when faced with a specific
situation or circumstances, emotional - as the
perceived benefit due to the arousal of feelings or
emotional state, social - as the perceived benefit
due to interaction with the social environment,
and epistemic - as the perceived benefit due to the
aroused curiosity or desire to satisfy the desire for
knowledge. According to the goal framing theory
described by Farhat et al. (2022), goals are also a
very important factor governing the behavior of
individuals. In addition, human behavior is the
result of many incentives. According to one branch
of the theory, goal-directed framing theory, the
media focuses attention on specific events and then
frames and gives them meaning. “Framing, ” or the
way in which an object is presented to individuals,
also determines how information is processed and
used or interpreted in the future. Green marketing
is a prime example of this, as it emphasizes green
consumer behavior and often plays a crucial role
in communicating a key message to consumers
through social media that influences consumers’
overall environmental concerns. Another theory
often used to study consumer behavior is the self-
determination theory. According to Gilal et al.
(2020), the motivation for individual behavior is
divided into two types: intrinsic and extrinsic. This
includes the pleasure of engaging in a certain activity
(intrinsic motivation) or the pursuit of rewards,
praise, and avoidance of punishment (extrinsic
motivation). In a broader context, two additional
types of motivation are distinguished between
extrinsic and intrinsic motivation: introjected and
identified motivation. In the case of introjected
motivation, the individual's behavior is manifested
in order to avoid shame or disapproval from
others. Identified motivation is observed when the
individual recognizes the importance and benefits
of an action or behavior and is therefore motivated
to behave in this way. In addition, this approach
is complemented by social cognitive theory,
which, according to Khan et al. (2020), explains
consumer behavior not only through personal,
but also through environmental factors. Personal

However, despite all the insights, it is important
to remember that consumer behavior is changing.
Over time and with the rapid growth of technology
and human needs, consumer behavior has become
even more complicated. The need for sustainable
development increased at the global level in the
1980s, when in 1983 the World Commission
on Sustainable Development established the
Brundtland Commission to unite countries
in addressing the problem of unsustainable
development. Since then, with increasing awareness
of climate change issues, continuous environmental
degradation and growing concern for protecting
the planet, many countries have developed and
continue to improve strategies to combat climate
change, and from a consumer perspective, they are
trying to understand the factors that influence a
more sustainable lifestyle and consumer behavior
that contributes to a sustainable future. Thus,
growing concern for the environment has also
led to the formation of a purchasing model that
prioritizes environmentally friendly products.
The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development
recognizes the environmental impact of society’s
consumption patterns and lifestyles and establishes
goals for promoting sustainable and healthy
lifestyles and green consumption (Aljanabi and AL-
Hadban, 2023). In particular, Goal 12 emphasizes
the need to integrate environmental, social and
economic elements in order to ensure sustainable
consumption and production patterns.
As environmental awareness grew, the concept of
green consumer behavior emerged, encompassing
green consumers who prefer products that do not
pose a risk to health or the environment. An even
broader concept, green consumerism, has also
emerged, encompassing social awareness of green
consumer behavior. In addition, the latter behavior
is also defined as a shift to recycled products and
an ethical decision-making process, during which
consumers are not only users of the purchased
product, but also carry out socially responsible
activities with their actions. Therefore, in this
case, additional stimuli appear for consumers.

The aim of the study is to determine which green

Most studies mention the importance of con -
sumers' personal characteristics in their eco-
friendly product choices (Piligrimienė et al., 2020;
Wijekoon, and Sabri, 2021; Ahmed et al., 2023).
In addition, personal factors such as environmen -

a direct and significant influence on eco-friendly
purchase intentions, and the cognitive factor - the
perceived green value of the product's values and
direct benefits - is one of the main drivers of pur -
chase behavior. Based on these insights, hypoth -
eses H1 and H2 are formulated:
H1 – Cognitive factors have a stronger effect on
consumers' repurchase intention for eco-friendly
products than personal factors.
H2 – The cognitive factor "perceived green val -
ue" has a positive effect on consumers' repurchase
intention for eco-friendly products.
In the theory of planned behavior, perceived
behavioral control, along with two other variables
(attitude and subjective norms), affects consumer
intentions, and according to Zhuang et al. (2021), it
was also found to have a positive effect on consum-
er purchase intentions for eco-friendly products.
In addition, attitude influences consumer behavior
in adopting eco-friendly product purchase behav -
ior. Based on these insights, it is assumed that the
influence of the latter factors on repurchase inten -
tion is also significant. Hypotheses H3 and H4
were formulated to assess this:
H3 – The cognitive factor "perceived behavioral
control" has a positive effect on consumers' repur -
chase intention for eco-friendly products.
H4 – The personal factor "attitude toward eco-
friendly products" has a positive effect on consum -
ers' repurchase intention for eco-friendly products.
According to Asan et al. (2024), the experience
of an eco-friendly brand has the strongest influ -
ence on eco-friendly brand loyalty, and one of the
main factors that make up the experience is brand
trust. Therefore, it is assumed that brand trust is
significant not only in the growth of loyalty to
eco-friendly products, but also in the intention to
repurchase. Based on this, the H5 hypothesis was
formulated:
H5 – The personal factor "brand trust" has a pos-
itive influence on consumers' repurchase intention
for eco-friendly products.

4. Discussion of Results4. Discussion of Results
A total of 498 respondents participated in the
survey. However, based on responses to the first
control question concerning the purchase behav -
ior of eco-friendly household products, 91 respon -
dents were removed from further analysis because
they did not purchase such products. Further data
screening showed that some responses distorted the
research results because they represented outliers;
therefore, those responses were excluded. As a re -
sult, 399 questionnaires were analyzed in the study.
The distribution of respondents by gender was
uneven. Statistical analysis showed that the survey
included 130 men (32.6%), 243 women (60.9%) and
26 respondents who selected another gender cate -
gory (6.5%). Considering these data, gender groups
were not compared further because the distribution
was not sufficiently balanced. Another important
aspect is that the distribution by education was also
uneven: more than half of the respondents had a
university degree (210), while substantially fewer
had non-university higher education (61), and even
fewer respondents had lower levels of education.
The distribution of respondents by age and in -
come was relatively similar across the groups. The
average age of the respondents was 34 years, and
average monthly income was EUR 1,557.44. The
number of respondents was relatively balanced
across the three age groups (142, 139 and 118, re -
spectively) and across the five monthly income
groups (87, 92, 82, 82 and 56, respectively). This
distribution is important for the study because it
makes it possible to assess the behavior of respon -
dents of different ages and income levels in the con-
text of purchasing eco-friendly products.
The analysis then turns to respondents' repur -
chase behavior. The first questionnaire items were
general questions intended to identify how often
respondents purchase such products and how they
understand the concepts of green consumer behav-
ior and eco-friendly products.
The diagram (Figure 1) shows that the most fre -
quent responses concerning purchase frequency
were "once or several times per month" (215) and
"once every six months" (125). This distribution ap-
pears logical, considering that the question focused
on eco-friendly household products, which are not
usually first-necessity goods.
Based on the theoretical material, respondents
were then presented with four definitions explain -
ing green consumer behavior and eco-friendly
products. The purpose was to identify respondents'
opinions and level of understanding (Table 4).
The table shows that nearly half of the respon -
dents understand green consumer behavior and
eco-friendly products as a complex phenomenon
composed of several dimensions (197 responses),
ranging from purchasing to investments and other
practices. This indicates that many respondents
understand that green consumer behavior is not
limited to the purchase of eco-friendly products,
which was the view selected by the smallest group
of respondents (55).
Respondents' purchasing behavior was then
compared across age groups. The patterns were
found to be relatively similar. The charts below il -
lustrate these results (Figure 2).
The charts show that respondents in all age
groups most frequently agreed that they regularly
purchase eco-friendly products, had purchased
several eco-friendly products during the previous
month and had chosen eco-friendly products in -
stead of conventional ones during their most recent
shopping trip. A considerable number of respon -
dents also remained neutral and selected the option
"neither disagree nor agree".
To assess what most strongly determines the de-
cision to repurchase eco-friendly household prod -
ucts, respondents were asked to choose one of six
factors: price, quality, product availability, health
concern, environmental concern or label informa -
tion. Statistical analysis showed that the three most
influential aspects were health concern (114), envi-
ronmental concern (107) and product quality (94).

5. Methodological Limitations5. Methodological Limitations
The methodological limitations of this study
should also be acknowledged. First, the study was
based on a quantitative online survey and used
convenience sampling. Although the final sample
size was sufficient for statistical analysis and cor -
responded to the calculated sample requirement,
the use of non-probability sampling limits the gen-
eralizability of the results to the entire population
of Lithuanian consumers. The sample reflects only
those respondents who were reachable through on-
line channels and who agreed to participate in the
survey.
Second, the study focused only on consum -
ers who had experience purchasing eco-friendly
household products. Respondents who did not pur-
chase such products were excluded from further
analysis. This decision was methodologically justi -
fied because the study examined repurchase inten -
tion; however, it also means that the findings do not
explain the barriers or motives of consumers who
do not buy eco-friendly household products at all.
Third, the research relied on self-reported data.
Respondents evaluated their own attitudes, per -
ceived behavioral control, perceived green value,
brand trust and repurchase intention. Therefore,
the results may be affected by social desirability
bias, especially because environmentally responsi -
ble behavior is often perceived as socially desirable.
In addition, stated intentions do not always corre -
spond to actual future purchasing behavior.
Fourth, the study examined selected cogni -
tive and personal factors only. Other potentially
important determinants, such as price sensitivity,
product availability, trust in eco-labels, household
composition, income constraints, previous product
experience, greenwashing perceptions and social
influence, were not included as independent con -
structs in the tested model. Their inclusion in fu -
ture research could provide a more comprehensive
explanation of eco-friendly household product re -
purchase behavior.
Finally, the empirical analysis was based on
cross-sectional data, meaning that all variables
were measured at one point in time. Therefore, the
results reveal statistically significant associations
and predictive relationships between the analyzed
constructs, but they do not allow strong causal con-
clusions. Future studies could apply longitudinal
or experimental research designs to examine how
green consumer behavior and repurchase intention
change over time and under different market or
communication conditions.
6. Conclusions and Policy Implications6. Conclusions and Policy Implications
The literature review summarized the concept
of green consumer behavior and distinguished
the categories of factors that determine green con -
sumer behavior in the market: internal and external
factors. Internal factors include cognitive, personal
and sociodemographic categories, whereas exter -
nal factors include cultural and contextual factors,
product-related factors, consumption-related fac -
tors, behavioral factors, green marketing and social
media factors. A broader definition of these catego-
ries makes it possible to identify and analyze deeper
motives behind consumers' decisions to purchase
eco-friendly products.
The research results show that internal factors
have the greatest influence on consumers' repur -
chase intention for eco-friendly products. Among
these, the personal factor group has the strongest
effect, explaining 35% of the variance in the deci -
sion, although the empirical testing of the proposed
model also revealed that the difference between the
impact of personal and cognitive factors is only
one percentage point. The quantitative results show
that the cognitive factors - perceived green value
and perceived behavioral control - positively affect
consumers' repurchase intention for eco-friendly
household products, each explaining approximately
31% of the variance. The personal factors of brand
