Background: Pre-prepared foods are increasingly popular because of their con-
venience but have also raised concerns about food safety and nutritional risks.
Drawing on the evolutionary needs perspective and protection motivation theory,
this study examines the association between parental protection motivation and
consumers’ purchase intention toward pre-prepared foods, as well as the medi-
ating role of perceived risk and the moderating roles of health consciousness and
information sensitivity in the mediation process.
Methods: A cross-sectional online survey was conducted with 985 Chinese
consumers. Confirmatory factor analysis in AMOS 28.0 assessed the measure -
ment model, while PROCESS Model 4 and two Model 7 analyses in SPSS 30.0,
using 5,000 bootstrap resamples, tested mediation and moderated mediation,
respectively.
Results: Parental protection motivation was negatively associated with purchase
intention toward pre-prepared foods (β = −0.173, p < 0.001). Perceived risk sig-
nificantly mediated this relationship [indirect effect = −0.280, 95% CI (−0.372,
−0.196)]. The indices of moderated mediation were significant for both health
consciousness [index = −0.185, 95% CI (−0.282, −0.112)] and information sensi-
tivity [index = 0.066, 95% CI (0.016, 0.123)]. The mediating effect was significant
at high but not low levels of health consciousness, whereas it was significant at
low but not high levels of information sensitivity.
Conclusion: Parental protection motivation was associated with lower pur -
chase intention through perceived risk, and this mediating effect was stronger
at higher levels of health consciousness but weaker at higher levels of informa -
tion sensitivity. These findings deepen the protection-oriented explanation of
parenting-related motives in consumer behavior and complement research on
pre-prepared food consumption by directing attention beyond proximate expla-
nations toward an ultimate-level motivational antecedent.

1 Introduction
The widespread adoption of industrial food production has accel-
erated the integration of pre-prepared foods into online delivery plat-
forms, chain restaurants, and households. Their standardization,
cost-effectiveness, and convenience are transforming food consump-
tion (Wong et al., 2025). Pre-prepared food refers to products that
undergo preliminary processing and packaging and require only heat-
ing or no further preparation before consumption (He et al., 2026).
According to Fortune Business Insights (2026), the global pre-pre-
pared food market was valued at $190.71 billion in 2025 and is pro-
jected to reach $326.50 billion by 2034, indicating significant
commercial potential. However, rapid market growth has raised public
concerns about food safety, nutritional quality, and additive use, which
may threaten the industry’s sustainable development. Recent debates
between Chinese influencer Luo Y onghao and the restaurant chain
Xibei have sparked widespread discussion about the safety, quality,
and reliability of these products (China Daily, 2025).
The tension between convenience and health protection is par-
ticularly salient in children’s diets, where parental protective instincts
often lead to resistance against pre-prepared foods. Public criticism
grew after Luo Y onghao revealed that Xibei’s children’s meals con-
tained frozen broccoli with a shelf life of up to 24 months. Similarly,
in September 2023, the introduction of pre-packaged meals in some
primary and secondary schools in Jiangxi Province faced strong oppo-
sition, with over 80% of parents disapproving in a survey of 1.09 mil-
lion participants (Sina Weibo 1). As the primary custodians of
children’s nutritional well-being, parents assess pre-prepared foods
through the lens of their potential effects on their children’s health.
Such assessments embody the concept of parental protection motiva-
tion, which is characterized as the impetus to alter behavior in order
to safeguard one’s offspring (Buckels et al., 2015). Consequently, when
pre-prepared foods are appraised with regard to children’s health and
safety, consumer evaluations may transition from a focus on conve-
nience to one centered on protection-driven risk assessment. While
these cases illustrate the practical relevance of parental protection in
household food decisions, current literature has yet to examine
whether this protection-oriented motivation is associated with con-
sumers’ risk perceptions and their purchase intentions toward pre-
prepared foods.
Prior research has provided valuable insights into the factors
influencing consumers’ acceptance and consumption of pre-prepared
foods. Existing studies have examined consumers’ purchase intention
toward pre-prepared food, highlighting the importance of intrinsic
product characteristics such as price, taste, and packaging (Liu et al.,
2025; Xiong et al., 2023; Xu and Sun, 2026), as well as psychological
factors including perceived value, trust, and attitude (Liu et al., 2025;
Wong et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ). Additionally, the negative
impact of perceived risk on purchase intention has been well estab-
lished in the literature (He et al., 2026; Xiong et al., 2023). The evolu-
tionary needs perspective distinguishes between proximate
explanations, which focus on the immediate psychological processes
underlying behavior, and ultimate explanations, which address the
recurrent adaptive problems that these processes have evolved to

al., 2010; Griskevicius and Kenrick, 2013). Within this perspective, the

Meanwhile, consumer research on broad parenting motives has
increasingly demonstrated that the fundamental motivation to care

2019; Liang et al., 2023; Roy et al., 2026). These broad motives encom-
pass a wide range of child-centered concerns and goals, such as invest-
ing time and resources in children’s education and long-term
development. Within the framework of the parental care motivational
system, Hofer et al. (2018) identify two conceptually distinct dimen-
sions: nurturance and protection. Nurturance pertains to the inclina-
tion to perceive infants as emotionally rewarding and to respond with
tenderness and supportive caregiving, whereas protection involves the
drive to safeguard children from harm, closely associated with threat
detection, harm prevention, and heightened risk vigilance. Given that
pre-prepared foods frequently elicit concerns regarding food safety,
nutritional quality, and transparency of information (He et al., 2026;
Wong et al., 2025), the protective component of parental caregiving is
especially pertinent to consumers’ assessments of these products.
Taken together, these two research streams reveal three related
gaps. First, research on pre-prepared food consumption has focused
primarily on proximate product attributes and cognitive evaluations,
while the potential relevance of evolutionarily based motives has been
inadequately explored. Second, consumer research on parenting-
related motives has yet to clarify the relationship between the protec-
tive component of parental caregiving and consumers’ evaluations of
pre-prepared foods. Third, there has been insufficient investigation
into the cognitive mechanisms and boundary conditions that govern
this relationship. These gaps are particularly pertinent in a consump-
tion context where the convenience offered by pre-prepared foods
intersects with concerns about children’s health and safety.
An evolutionary needs perspective provides a useful basis for
addressing these gaps. The evolutionary needs perspective posits that
human decision-making is influenced not only by immediate assess-
ments but also by fundamental motives that have evolved to address
recurrent adaptive challenges, including self-protection, disease avoid-
ance, social affiliation, and kin care (Confer et al., 2010; Griskevicius
and Kenrick, 2013). The parental protection (kin care) system evolved
to ensure offspring survival and well-being, guiding parents to priori-
tize safety, health, and nutrition. Applying this framework clarifies
why parents may resist or selectively choose certain convenience or
cost-effective pre-prepared foods (Liang et al., 2023) thereby provid-
ing a more nuanced understanding of contemporary consumer deci-
sion-making processes. Additionally, protection motivation theory
posits that protective behaviors are influenced by threat appraisal,
offering a psychological mechanism through which perceived risk
mediates the relationship between parental protection motives and
purchase intentions (Floyd et al., 2000; Rogers, 1975). Thus, the evo-
lutionary needs perspective explains the motivational origin of paren-
tal protection concerns, whereas protection motivation theory
explains how such concerns may be translated into risk-based con-
sumer responses.
Accordingly, the objective of this study is to examine the relation-
ship between parental protection motivation and consumers’ purchase
intention toward pre-prepared foods, the mediating role of perceived

2 Theoretical background
To situate the current study within the extant literature, previous
research is organized into two streams: (1) parenting-related motiva-
tion and consumer behavior and (2) antecedents of pre-prepared food
consumption. Within the first stream, broad parenting-related moti-
vation refers to studies that examine parenting or caregiving goals
without isolating their protective component, whereas parental pro-
tection motivation refers specifically to the motivation to shield chil-
dren from potential harm or risk. Within the second stream,
proximate factors refer to product characteristics and immediate psy-
chological assessments influencing purchasing decisions, such as
price, convenience, perceived value, trust, attitude, and perceived risk.
Conversely, ultimate-level explanations focus on evolutionarily based
motives, including those associated with survival, reproduction, and
offspring protection.
Supplementary material summarizes representative studies that
exemplify these two research streams and highlight their primary
focal areas. These studies were chosen due to their direct investigation
of key motivational constructs, consumption contexts, or boundary
conditions relevant to the present research. The purpose of this selec-
tion is to contextualize the present study within the existing literature,
rather than to offer a comprehensive literature review in these
domains.
2.1 Evolutionary needs and parental
protection motivation
The evolutionary needs framework posits that human psychologi-
cal mechanisms have developed to address recurrent ancestral chal-
lenges related to survival and reproduction ( Confer et al., 2010 ;
Griskevicius and Kenrick, 2013). Fundamental evolutionary needs
include self-protection, disease avoidance, social affiliation, status
acquisition, mate acquisition, mate retention, and kin care.
Parental protection motivation, which emerges from the kin-care
system, refers to a motivational tendency that directs individuals to
protect children from potential harm (Buckels et al., 2015). This sys-
tem’s adaptive purpose is to promote the survival, health, and develop-
ment of offspring. Given that offspring survival is directly linked to
reproductive success, humans have evolved an increased sensitivity to
dangers that may compromise children’s safety, health, and nutritional
status (Taheran et al., 2024). Although this motivational system may
be more chronically activated among parents, it is not limited to
individuals who currently have children. Prior research indicates that
the parental care motivational system can also be activated among
nonparents, eliciting protective and nurturing behaviors (Buckels et
al., 2015; Schaller, 2018).
Broad parenting motivation provides a general framework for
understanding the influence of caregiving motivation on parental
behavior. It encompasses a wide range of child-oriented concerns and
goals, such as investing time and resources in children’s education
and long-term development. Existing research focuses primarily on
the broader concept of parenting motivation, demonstrating its
effects on consumer decisions related to safety assurance (Roy et al.,
2026), long-term developmental investment (Chen et al., 2024; Li et
al., 2019), and emotional bonding (Hofer et al., 2018). For example,
consumers with strong parenting motivation tend to avoid risk under
time pressure by choosing familiar and efficient products (Liang et
al., 2023).
Broad parenting motivation and parental protection motivation
are interconnected yet conceptually distinct. Broad parenting motiva-
tion refers to the overall drive to nurture and care for children across
various parental objectives and decision-making processes. In con-
trast, parental protection motivation represents the protective compo-
nent of the parental care motivational system, emphasizing the
identification of threats, prevention of harm, and safeguarding of off-
spring (Hofer et al., 2018). While pre-prepared foods provide conve-
nience, their industrial processing often raises issues related to food
safety, ingredient transparency, and nutritional quality ( He et al.,
2026). From an evolutionary needs perspective, stronger parental pro-
tection motivation should be associated with heightened vigilance
toward such concerns when children’s health is salient. Taken together,
existing research demonstrates that broad parenting-related motives
are relevant to consumer decision-making, but it remains unclear
whether the protection-specific component is associated with con-
sumers’ perceived risk and purchase intention toward pre-prepared
foods. Examining these relationships may therefore clarify how an
evolutionarily based parental motive is reflected in family-centered
food choices that balance convenience with health-related
considerations.
2.2 Antecedents of pre-prepared food
consumption
Prior research has identified a range of factors that influence con-
sumers’ purchase intention toward pre-prepared foods. The extant
literature predominantly focuses on intrinsic product characteristics,
including price, taste, packaging, and convenience (Liu et al., 2025;
Xiong et al., 2023; Xu and Sun, 2026), as well as psychological factors
including perceived value, trust, and attitude (Liu et al., 2025; Wong
et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). Additionally, perceived risk has been
demonstrated to negatively affect purchase intentions, particularly in
relation to concerns about food safety, nutritional quality, and additive
use (He et al., 2026; Xiong et al., 2023).
Moreover, research on food parenting and healthy eating behav-
iors suggests that parental motivations and practices significantly
shape children’s dietary patterns. Strategies such as restrictive feeding,
control over food availability, and parental modeling have been shown
to substantially influence children’s consumption of unhealthy or
energy-dense foods (Y ee et al., 2017). Further studies indicate that
parental caregiving motives increase sensitivity to potential threats to
children’s well-being (Fessler et al., 2014; Hofer et al., 2018), which

Studies on ready-to-eat and prepackaged foods also highlight the

Despite these insights, several limitations remain. First, most stud-

choices (Johnson et al., 2019; Liang et al., 2023), the specific role of
parental protection motivation as an evolutionary driver in pre-pre-
pared food consumption remains underexplored. Third, existing
investigations tend to focus on general food parenting practices or
healthy eating behaviors rather than specifically addressing pre-pre-
pared food consumption. Finally, limited attention has been paid to
the cognitive mechanisms underlying these relationships, particularly
the potential mediating role of perceived risk in the relationship
between parental protection motivation and purchase intention. These
gaps highlight the need to integrate evolutionary motivations into
research on consumer decision-making regarding pre-prepared foods.
3 Research framework and hypothesis
development
To address the gaps identified in previous research, this study
develops a conceptual framework (Figure 1) to examine: (a) the rela-
tionship between parental protection motivation and consumers’ pur-
chase intention toward pre-prepared foods; (b) whether perceived risk
mediates this relationship; and (c) whether health consciousness and
information sensitivity moderate this process.
3.1 Parental protection motivation and
purchase intention toward pre-prepared
foods
Pre-prepared foods, as a category of industrially processed prod-
ucts, frequently exhibit limited transparency concerning their
ingredients and manufacturing methods, which may conflict with
evolutionary tendencies toward disease avoidance and offspring pro-
tection. From the evolutionary needs perspective, when food choices
pertain to the well-being of children, threat-detection mechanisms are
activated, resulting in increased caution toward unfamiliar or exten-
sively processed foods (Kenrick et al., 2010). Moreover, the parental
care motivational system can be engaged not only in individuals who
are parents but also in nonparents (Buckels et al., 2015; Schaller, 2018).
Consequently, parental protection motivation may influence food
evaluations among a broad range of consumers, rather than being lim-
ited solely to those who currently have children.
Parental protection motivation should also influence how con-
sumers translate child-protection concerns into marketplace choices.
Research on parenting motivation shows that the salience of parenting
goals affects intertemporal preferences and diminishes variety-seeking
behavior, suggesting that such motivations can systematically modify
consumer decision-making strategies ( Li et al., 2019 ; Liang et al.,
2023). More specifically, evidence pertaining to protective motives
indicates that heightened parental-role salience leads to increased risk
aversion (Eibach and Mock, 2011), while a focus on safety prompts
parents to favor best-selling products that convey reassuring signals
of popularity and approval (Roy et al., 2026). Collectively, these find-
ings imply that parenting-related motivations tend to steer consumers
toward choices that provide greater reassurance and away from
options that are less aligned with protective concerns. In the context
of pre-prepared food consumption, it follows that stronger parental
protection motivation should correspond with a lower willingness to
purchase such products. Formally, the following hypothesis is
proposed:
Hypothesis 1: Parental protection motivation is negatively associ-
ated with consumers’ purchase intention toward pre-pre -
pared foods.
3.2 The mediating role of perceived risk
Protection motivation theory (Rogers, 1975) explains how threat
appraisal generates protective behavioral intentions. Rooted in the
fundamental drive for safety and harm avoidance, this theory posits
that individuals assess potential threats and subsequently adopt

Kenrick, 2013). Threat appraisal involves judgments of perceived
severity and vulnerability, providing a theoretical basis for under -
standing how concerns about potential harm are related to protective
responses (Floyd et al., 2000). In the present consumption context,
these concerns are relevant to consumers’ assessments of the poten-
tial negative consequences of purchasing pre-prepared foods.
Perceived risk refers to consumers’ subjective feelings of uncertainty
and anticipated negative outcomes during the purchasing process
(Ge, 2022; Stone and Grønhaug, 1993). Perceived risk is therefore
adopted as the mediating construct because it captures the overall
purchase-related evaluation central to the research question.
Perceived risk applies the theory’s threat-evaluation logic to consum-
ers’ judgments of the potential negative consequences of purchasing
pre-prepared foods.
Individuals with stronger parental protection motivation may be
more vigilant toward potential threats to children’s health and safety
(Fessler et al., 2014; Hofer et al., 2018). In the context of pre-prepared
foods, factors such as industrial processing, limited transparency
regarding ingredients, and ambiguous nutritional quality may amplify
perceived risk (He et al., 2026; Wong et al., 2025). Stronger parental
protection motivation may therefore be associated with greater uncer-
tainty and stronger expectations of potential negative consequences,
thereby resulting in higher perceived risk.
Higher perceived risk, in turn, is generally associated with risk-
avoidance responses and lower purchase intention. Previous research
has consistently shown that increased perceived risk negatively
impacts consumers’ willingness to buy food products (Castagna et al.,
2021). Meta-analytic evidence further confirms a significant negative
relationship between perceived risk and purchase intention (Pelaez et
al., 2019). Thus, although pre-prepared foods provide convenience
and efficiency, higher perceived risk may prompt consumers to engage
in risk-avoidance strategies, thereby decreasing their purchase
intention.
Taken together, stronger parental protection motivation is
expected to be associated with greater perceived risk, which is subse-
quently associated with lower purchase intention toward pre-prepared
foods. Consequently, perceived risk may serve as a mediating factor
explaining the negative relationship between parental protection moti-
vation and purchase intention. Therefore, the following hypothesis is
proposed:
Hypothesis 2: Perceived risk mediates the relationship between
parental protection motivation and purchase intention toward
pre-prepared foods, such that stronger parental protection moti-
vation is associated with greater perceived risk and, in turn,
lower purchase intention.
3.3 The moderated mediation effect of
health consciousness
Protection motivation theory further suggests that threat appraisal
varies according to individual cognitive characteristics and informa-
tion-processing tendencies (Milne et al., 2000). In this study, health
consciousness and information sensitivity are proposed as important
boundary conditions of the relationship between parental protection
motivation and perceived risk.
Health consciousness refers to the extent to which individuals are
concerned about and attentive to health issues ( Michaelidou and
Hassan, 2008). Prior research indicates that highly health-conscious
consumers possess greater health knowledge, scrutinize food informa-
tion more carefully, and adopt more health-oriented lifestyles (Klerck
and Sweeney, 2007; Siegrist et al., 2022). Health consciousness also
influences product evaluations and food purchasing decisions
(Nagaraj, 2021).
When consumers with high health consciousness are also
strongly motivated to protect their offspring, they are more likely to
apply stringent health standards to food products and focus on
potential risk cues, such as additives and nutritional quality ( Mäkelä
et al., 2023 ; Shimul et al., 2021 ). As a result, health consciousness
may strengthen the positive relationship between parental protec -
tion motivation and perceived risk. Given that greater perceived
risk is associated with lower purchase intention in food consump -
tion contexts (Ge, 2022), the negative indirect relationship between
parental protection motivation and purchase intention through
perceived risk should be stronger among consumers with higher
health consciousness. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is
proposed:
Hypothesis 3: Health consciousness moderates the indirect effect
of parental protection motivation on purchase intention through
perceived risk, such that this negative indirect effect is stronger
at higher than at lower levels of health consciousness.
3.4 The moderated mediation effect of
information sensitivity
Sensitivity refers to an individual’s tendency to respond strongly
to external stimuli and assign importance to them (Pluess, 2015). In
consumer research, this concept is often operationalized as consum-
ers’ attention to specific product attributes or informational cues
(Bansal et al., 2010; Gouthier et al., 2022; Vogrincic-Haselbacher et al.,
2021). Building on this perspective, the present study defines informa-
tion sensitivity as the degree to which consumers prioritize objective
product information, such as ingredient lists, processing methods, and
food-origin information.
According to the elaboration likelihood model, consumers pro-
cess information through different routes depending on their motiva-
tion and cognitive engagement (Shahab et al., 2021). Consumers with
high information sensitivity tend to rely on substantive information
rather than peripheral cues when evaluating products. For example,
Jin and Zhou (2014) found that consumers with strong food safety
concerns actively seek traceability information to reduce uncertainty.
Similarly, among consumers with stronger parental protection motiva-
tion, those with higher information sensitivity may place greater
weight on available product-specific information when evaluating
food safety. Such attention may make risk judgments more dependent
on product-specific information and less closely tied to general pro-
tective concerns (Liu et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2025).
In contrast, consumers with low information sensitivity pay lim-
ited attention to technical or safety-related information. When evalu-
ating product safety, these consumers may rely more on general
impressions or heuristics, allowing protection-related concerns to be
more closely associated with perceived risk (Dhar and Gorlin, 2013).
Information sensitivity is therefore expected to weaken the positive

Hypothesis 4: Information sensitivity moderates the indirect

4 Methods
4.1 Participants and data collection
This study employed a cross-sectional online survey of Chinese
consumers. Formal data collection was conducted from September 21
to September 27, 2025, following the controversy involving the
Chinese influencer Luo Y onghao and the restaurant chain Xibei.
Participants were recruited via Credamo.com, an open online research
platform comprising a sample pool exceeding three million registered
members from various provinces and regions across China. For paid
surveys, the platform randomly distributes survey invitations among
its registered panel members, providing access to consumers across
different regions of China (Wang et al., 2025). Credamo has also been
used in prior consumer behavior research conducted in China (e.g.,
Fu et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023). All participants provided informed
consent and received 2 RMB in compensation. The individual con-
sumer served as the unit of analysis.
A total of 1,000 responses were collected. After excluding 15
invalid responses due to failed attention checks or excessively short
completion times (less than 120 s), the final sample consisted of 985
participants (M age = 31.52 years, SD = 8.29), of whom 64.5%
were female.
No additional demographic screening criteria were specified for
the present study. Whether participants had children and the possibil-
ity of future parenting were not used as eligibility criteria. The sample
comprised adult consumers irrespective of whether they had children,
based on evidence suggesting that the parental care motivational
system can be activated in both parents and nonparents (Buckels et al.,
2015; Schaller, 2018). These two variables were recorded and included
as covariates in the primary analyses.
4.2 Measures
All constructs were measured using established scales adapted
from prior studies. The linguistic and contextual appropriateness of
these scales for Chinese participants was evaluated during the adapta-
tion process. The original items were translated into Chinese and sub-
sequently back-translated into English. Any inconsistencies identified
between the original and back-translated versions were reviewed and
resolved by two doctoral-level researchers familiar with consumer
psychology and food-consumption research. Subsequently, the ques-
tionnaire underwent further discussion and refinement by a panel of
seven experts, including two professors and five doctoral candidates
specializing in consumer psychology. Finally, a pilot study was con-
ducted with 30 Chinese consumers to evaluate the clarity, comprehen-
sibility, and contextual relevance of the items, leading to minor
revisions in wording based on participant feedback.
The five primary constructs were measured using 20 items,
with each construct measured by at least three items. Except for
the control variables, all measures used a seven-point scale
(1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). Specifically, purchase
intention was measured using three items adapted from Dodds et
al. (1991) . Parental protection motivation was assessed using five
items from Buckels et al. (2015) . Perceived risk was measured with
three items adapted from Stone and Grønhaug (1993)  overall risk
scale. Health consciousness was measured using four items
adapted from Michaelidou and Hassan (2008) , while information
sensitivity was measured using five items adapted from Bansal et
al. (2010) . The complete measurement items are provided in
Supplementary material.
Participants also reported demographic information, including
gender (male = 1, female = 2), age, whether they have children
(yes = 1, no = 0), the possibility of future parenting (0% = 0,
1–20% = 1, 21–40% = 2, 41–60% = 3, 61–80% = 4, 81–99% = 5, and
100% = 6), and awareness of the Luo Y onghao–Xibei controversy
(yes = 1, no = 0). Gender, age, and whether they have children were
included because demographic and household characteristics may be
associated with food-safety risk perception (Nardi et al., 2020). The
possibility of future parenting was included because parental care
motivation among nonparents is associated with the desire to have
children (Buckels et al., 2015). Controversy awareness was controlled
for, given that exposure to food-safety information through social
media may affect risk perception (Zhang et al., 2022).
4.3 Measurement assessment
Data were analyzed using SPSS 30.0 and AMOS 28.0. Reliability
was assessed using Cronbach’s α, with values above 0.70 indicating
acceptable internal consistency ( Nunnally and Bernstein, 1994 ).
Construct validity was evaluated through confirmatory factor analysis
(CFA). Following Hu and Bentler (1999), model fit was considered
acceptable when χ2/df < 5, comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker–
Lewis index (TLI) > 0.90, and root mean square error of approxima-
tion (RMSEA) < 0.08.
Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed according to
Hair et al. (2012). Composite reliability (CR) values above 0.70 and
average variance extracted (AVE) values above 0.50 indicated ade-
quate convergent validity. Discriminant validity was evaluated using
both the Fornell–Larcker criterion and the heterotrait–monotrait ratio
of correlations (HTMT). It was supported when the square root of
each construct’s AVE exceeded its correlations with the other con-
structs and when all HTMT values were below 0.85 ( Henseler et
al., 2015).
To assess common method bias, CFA was used to compare the
fit of a single-factor model with that of the proposed five-factor
model ( Podsakoff et al., 2012 ). A substantially poorer fit for the
single-factor model would indicate that common method bias was
less likely to threaten the validity of the findings. As an additional
diagnostic measure, full-collinearity variance inflation factors
(VIFs) were computed for the five primary constructs utilizing their
composite scores. Following Kock (2015) , each construct was

4.4 Hypothesis testing methods
The hypotheses were tested using Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Hayes,
2022). Model 4 was used to examine the mediating role of perceived
risk, whereas two Model 7 analyses were conducted to test the mod-
erating roles of health consciousness and information sensitivity in the
mediation process, respectively. The analyses employed 5,000 boot-
strap resamples and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CIs),
with gender, age, whether participants had children, the possibility of
future parenting, and controversy awareness entered simultaneously
as covariates in all primary models.
Furthermore, consistent with previous research employing
PROCESS Model 59 to investigate variations in mediation processes
across dichotomous demographic groups ( Tian et al., 2022), a sup-
plementary conditional process analysis was conducted. This analy-
sis examined whether the mediation process differed between
participants with and without children, with gender, age, the possi -
bility of future parenting, and controversy awareness included as
covariates.

such information may also shape the risk-evaluation process.

directly; rather, it appears to weaken the extent to which general
parental protection motivation corresponds to heightened risk per-
ceptions of pre-prepared foods.
7 Conclusion
This study examined the relationship between parental protection
motivation and consumers’ purchase intention toward pre-prepared
foods, focusing on the mediating role of perceived risk and the moder-
ated mediating roles of health consciousness and information sensitiv-
ity. Parental protection motivation was positively associated with
perceived risk, which was in turn negatively associated with purchase
intention. Separate moderated mediation analyses further showed that
this indirect relationship was stronger at higher levels of health con-
sciousness but weaker at higher levels of information sensitivity. These
findings deepen the protection-oriented explanation of parenting-
related motives in consumer behavior and extend research on pre-
prepared food consumption by complementing proximate
explanations with an ultimate-level motivational antecedent. The
study also provides practical implications for pre-prepared food com-
panies in designing differentiated risk communication and trust-
building strategies for distinct consumer groups.

First, this study introduces parental protection motivation into
research on pre-prepared food consumption as a protection-specific
and evolutionarily grounded motivational factor. Existing studies have
primarily explained consumers’ responses to pre-prepared foods

whereas consumer research on parenting-related motives has mainly
examined broader caregiving, nurturing, and investment tendencies
(Chen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2019; Roy et al., 2026). By focusing on the

protection-oriented account complements, rather than replaces, exist-
ing explanations based on product characteristics and immediate con-
sumer evaluations.
Second, this study integrates the evolutionary needs perspective
and protection motivation theory by identifying perceived risk as the
cognitive mechanism connecting an evolutionarily grounded motive
with product-specific purchase intention. The evolutionary needs per-
spective explains the origin and adaptive function of parental protec-
tion motivation, whereas protection motivation theory explains how
protection-related concerns are reflected in threat evaluation and
behavioral intention (Confer et al., 2010; Rogers, 1975). The mediating
role of perceived risk links these two theoretical levels by showing how
an ultimate-level motive is associated with purchase intention through
a proximate evaluation of a specific food category. This integration
clarifies the complementary roles of the two theories rather than treat-
ing them as parallel theoretical foundations.
Third, this study identifies differentiated boundary conditions in
the indirect relationship between parental protection motivation and
purchase intention. Prior research has shown that health conscious-
ness can shape how risk-related evaluations are translated into food
purchase intentions (Devi et al., 2023), while the importance consum-
ers attach to product information can alter their responses to product
uncertainty (Wu et al., 2021). Building on these studies, the present
study examines health consciousness and information sensitivity sepa-
rately as two boundary conditions of the mediated relationship and
reveals their contrasting roles. These findings suggest that consumers’
acceptance or rejection of pre-prepared foods depends not only on
their underlying protective motives and risk evaluations, but also on
how strongly they prioritize health-related concerns and rely on prod-
uct information when making purchase decisions. The findings there-
fore provide a more conditional account of how evolutionarily
grounded motives are associated with consumer responses in contem-
porary food-consumption contexts.
7.2 Practical implications
First, companies should develop trust-building strategies
grounded in evolutionary psychology to reduce the perceived
risks associated with parental protection motivation. Because
parental protection motivation is associated with greater per -
ceived risk, firms should present pre-prepared foods not merely
as convenient products but as safe, controllable, and reliable
options for family meals. This positioning should be supported by
standardized production, rigorous quality control, transparent
ingredient sourcing, clear disclosure of nutritional composition
and additive use, product traceability, and credible third-party
certification. Marketing communications should translate these
safeguards into specific and verifiable information about how
potential food safety and nutritional risks are controlled
throughout production and distribution. Such an approach may
reduce uncertainty surrounding pre-prepared foods and alleviate
consumers’ risk concerns.
Second, the contrasting moderating patterns of health conscious-
ness and information sensitivity highlight the value of considering
these characteristics in conjunction when developing product and
communication strategies. For consumers with greater health con-
sciousness, product development could emphasize balanced nutri -
tional composition, reduced additive use, and formulations suitable
for family or child-related consumption. The presentation of these
attributes should also reflect consumers’ information sensitivity.
Firms could assess consumers’ information preferences through brief
voluntary questions or privacy-respecting indicators, such as
whether they access detailed product pages or scan QR codes for
additional information. Consumers with high levels of both health
consciousness and information sensitivity may prefer detailed and
evidence-based information supporting nutritional and safety claims,
whereas highly health-conscious consumers with lower information
sensitivity may respond more favorably to concise nutritional sum-
maries, recognized certifications, and clearly organized visual labels.
This joint approach allows firms to coordinate health-oriented prod-
uct strategies with the depth and presentation of marketing
information.
7.3 Limitations and future research
First, this study employed a cross-sectional survey design,
which does not establish the temporal ordering or causal direction
of the relationships among parental protection motivation, per -
ceived risk, and purchase intention. Accordingly, the mediation
and moderated mediation results should be interpreted as rela -
tional patterns consistent with the proposed theoretical frame -
work. In addition, the self-report measure assesses current
individual differences in parental protection motivation, whose
evolutionary grounding is informed by prior theory and research
rather than directly tested in the present study. Such measures
remain susceptible to subjective bias and potential common-
method bias. Future research could use longitudinal designs,
adopt scenario-based experiments, or incorporate objective
behavioral data, such as actual purchase records or household
dietary logs, to improve methodological robustness and provide
stronger evidence concerning the proposed relationships.
Second, the composition and cultural scope of the sample may
limit the generalizability of the findings. All participants were
Chinese consumers, and women accounted for 64.5% of the
sample. Although gender was included as a covariate in the statis -
tical analyses, prior research suggests that gender and parenthood
may be associated with consumption decisions and food-parent -
ing practices ( Davison et al., 2020 ; Migheli, 2021 ). Future research
could recruit more gender-balanced samples and conduct studies
in other cultural contexts to examine gender differences and
assess the cross-cultural applicability of the proposed
relationships.
Third, this study did not examine other consumer- and informa-
tion-related factors that may provide alternative explanations. Prior
food-consumption research has linked cognitive fluency to perceived
risk and purchase intention (Li et al., 2022), traceability knowledge
and information quality to perceived risk and food purchase intention
(Ge, 2022), and consumer trust to purchase intention in risk-sensitive

Fourth, pre-prepared foods encompass diverse categories, includ-

products (He et al., 2026). These categories vary substantially in fresh-
ness, processing level, additive use, brand reputation, and price, which

differences. Future research should distinguish among product types
and examine how specific product characteristics moderate the influ-

Finally, this study focused primarily on individual-level moderators.
Although health consciousness and information sensitivity significantly

perceived risk, pre-prepared food consumption is also influenced by
broader social and contextual factors. Future research should incorpo-
rate contextual variables, such as food certification labels, brand origin,
or regulatory endorsement, to further examine how external cues miti-
gate perceived risk and influence purchase decisions.
