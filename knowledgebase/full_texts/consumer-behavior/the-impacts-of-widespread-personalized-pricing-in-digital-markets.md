Abstract. Digital technologies can help platforms to develop a personalised pricing model,
which involves gathering and analysing multidimensional data about consumers. This paper
explores the economic, ethical and social implications of the implementation of personalised
pricing in digital markets. The research is based on a number of examples of commercial
platforms, including Ctrip, Amazon, Target and Pinduoduo, and recent empirical and
theoretical research, to examine the theoretical efficiency gains from neoclassical economic
concepts and the actual market outcomes. The analysis is carried out on four aspects:
procedural unfairness because of price discrimination, risk of privacy violations because of
the intensive monitoring of consumers' data, psychological manipulation of consumer
preferences by algorithms, and allocative efficiency in theory versus reality. The results
show that while in theory personalised pricing can remove the deadweight loss and enhance
the allocation of resources, widespread information asymmetry and opaque algorithmic
systems and mass data extraction reduce the potential benefits. The widespread adoption
will further increase market inequality, reduce consumer privacy, affect consumer choice,
and allow for further concentration of market power by large digital platforms. Finally, such
pricing policies are at odds with the desire to promote general social welfare.
Keywords: personalised pricing, price discrimination, consumer privacy, algorithmic
manipulation, social welfare 　
1. Introduction
The collection and analysis of consumer data has picked up pace with the continuous evolution of
digital technology. The data collected and analyzed are well-established data on the consumption
habits, geographic location information and historical navigation data of people [1]. These data can
be leveraged for businesses to develop their own pricing strategy. The other approach is through
standardized price-setting procedures [2]. This issue has become crucial in the digital platform
economy, since enterprises need to formulate rational pricing strategies to ensure competitiveness.
This pricing policy is an example of price discrimination, a pricing policy that seeks to charge the
price that consumers are willing to pay. This process is significant for each and every consumer [3].
In more traditional markets, businesses don't have a lot of information about their customers, and
they focus mainly on the same pricing policies to draw in customers. Proponents of personalized
pricing argue that it can be more efficient in the use of resources, as it allows for prices to be

adjusted to reflect consumer willingness to pay. Such value-based pricing has been identified as very
effective to improve the efficiency of resource allocation through mechanisms. The so called
"ineffective cost" factors optimize the efficiency of resource utilization and reduce operation losses
and costs [4]. However, the processes that can undermine the effectiveness of such strategies are
more than procedural justice issues; they impact the process of collecting a significant amount of
data. This is a serious violation of consumers' privacy rights, since companies can subtly influence
consumers' behavior [5].
This article suggests that the personalized pricing mechanism has the potential to improve
efficiency, in theory. However, in the real market the effectiveness of this pricing method is often
less than satisfactory due to limited information resources and the information asymmetry in the real
market. This practice is not only hard to do, but often fails to produce good results. Furthermore, the
imbalance of fairness generated has a negative effect on data protection rights and contributes to
further strengthening the power imbalance between stakeholders. Consequently, this new way of
allocation is contrary to the overall goal of social welfare.
2. Personalised pricing and procedural unfairness
One of the main problems is the lack of market fairness because of personalised pricing [6]. The
price consumers pay for a product can vary depending on a number of factors. The biggest Chinese
online travel agency, Ctrip (Xiecheng), has been called out on its method. It was found that the same
room in the same hotel can be very different in cost to different users, and sometimes the difference
is quite large [7]. In this instance, one guest paid 899 yuan per person, while another paid 1,564
yuan, but the reasons for the difference are not clearly explained, so consumers cannot determine
whether they have been treated unfairly. A good allocation mechanism should be transparent in the
allocation process and should distribute the allocation in a fair manner, not an unequal distribution
as in this case, but this is not always the case [8].
Personalised pricing not only involves unfair treatment, but also allows for the exploitation of
vulnerable groups. Data can be used to find consumers who will pay more, and those who can be
charged more. This pricing is done on a basis other than supply and demand, namely, by individual
characteristics [9]. The Princeton Review case is a good example of how geographic data can be
used to create social inequality. ProPublica reports that the cost of online tutoring was based on the
student's ZIP code [10]. Consumer surveys corroborate these concerns. The Digital Economics Team
at Fudan University found that over 60% of online travel platform users felt they were discriminated
against by these platforms based on their "big data".
Similarly, Amazon has a recommendation system and pricing mechanism that changes prices for
each consumer to maximise consumer surplus [11]. It leverages its algorithms to understand
purchase history and price sensitivity, increasing prices or premium listings towards less price-
sensitive customers [12]. This has the effect of reducing consumer information and clarity to make it
more difficult for consumers to make informed and clear choices, which consequently leads to an
increase in social inequalities [13].
3. Data collection and consumer surveillance
Personalised pricing also relies on a lot of data collection and thus poses significant privacy
concerns. Personal information, including browsing history, transaction records, and other data, is
used in different ways by companies to estimate willingness to pay. This leaves a large information
gap. There are a lot of things companies need to know about consumers. Minimal information is

provided about data collection, analysis and pricing decisions by consumers [14]. For instance, an
independent security lab test found that it was collecting over 120 dimensions of user behaviour
data, more than necessary to complete a transaction.
This imbalance can make it challenging for consumers to make informed decisions. Thus,
consumers are no longer able to make rational consumption decisions [15]. This is not only a market
transaction, but a continuous monitoring and evaluation of consumers.
A good example of using consumer privacy for commercial purposes is the "Pregnancy
prediction" case at Target. Target's algorithm was able to make predictions about the pregnancy of a
customer based on the ordinary purchase information which was aggregated. Pregnant consumers
are less price sensitive and more likely to purchase high-priced products. The algorithm not only
takes advantage of the information gap but also consumers' "path dependence". The "convenience
illusion" is the effect of accurate targeting and personalised recommendations over years that
ensnare long-term users. In such a situation, the price is set according to systematic rent-extraction,
which is based on consumers' cognitive inertia and misplaced trust.
Companies can control purchase recommendations and maximize profits by violating personal
privacy [16]. Companies that have more consumer information and advanced algorithmic
technologies can anticipate consumer behavior and use effective pricing strategies [17]. On the other
hand, smaller companies with similar data resources may find it more difficult to compete, resulting
in growing market concentration and further strengthening the position of the large technology
firms.
4. Psychological manipulation in digital markets
The other issue is that in digital markets, personalized pricing is no longer a reactive phenomenon in
response to consumers' preferences. Platforms are increasingly not only observing behavior, but also
influencing and changing consumer behavior, which leads to a greater willingness to pay [18].
Personalised pricing is a violation of the rational individual hypothesis, which states that people
make rational decisions in a transparent information environment [19]. However, this is a standard
economic assumption, which is reversed with personalised pricing. The preferences of consumers
are no longer fixed, but are affected by the platform [20]. Consumer behaviour is not always
rational, as they are affected by emotions, framing effects and psychological pressure [21]. Digital
platforms take advantage of these biases to influence consumer choices and increase willingness to
pay.
This manipulation is usually done by 'loss aversion' and 'fear of missing out' [22]. For example, if
a customer is told "only one left" or shown a countdown timer, they will be forced to make a quick
decision without taking the time to think about other options or prices. In this context, it is not
consumers' willingness to pay for the utility of the product, but rather for passive consumption. The
pricing mechanism that Pinduoduo has taken is a good example of how behavioural pressure plays
into pricing. Social participation is structurally linked to discounts via group buying and "Cut a
Slice" mechanisms instead of market dynamics. Obligation and time pressure are not only the power
of the discount, but also of mechanisms like 'Cut a Slice '. Here, the consumer is not just comparing
the cost with the utility of the product. It means that personalised pricing might be employed as a
way of behavioural control, instead of being an efficient market coordination.

5. Allocative efficiency under personalized pricing
In the context of neoclassical economics, personalised pricing is an important price-setting policy,
and it is essentially price discrimination. This theory suggests that the purpose of a company is to
find out the price that each consumer is willing to pay and then set the price [23]. This is achieved
by charging more to less price-sensitive consumers and less to more price-sensitive consumers,
theoretically, maximising the extraction of the market and improving the overall effectiveness of the
marketing. Resources are planned to be and are used in a mathematically optimal way in this
idealised case. If the price discrimination is perfect or first degree, the total market output will be
expanded to the competitive level as illustrated in the left panel of Figure 1, where the price charged
to each individual is equal to his or her willingness to pay. The complete elimination of the
deadweight loss (DWL, Figure 1) is the basis of the neoclassical efficiency narrative that is
commonly linked to uniform monopoly pricing (right panel in Figure 1).

But the allocative efficiency that the neoclassical economists champion is mostly an algorithmic
mirage. There are plenty of real-world facts that systematically refute this rosy view. This efficiency
story is sold with a great deal of force as a means of hiding the psychological subversion of
consumer autonomy. Indeed, personalised pricing relies on monopolies over platform data and
black-box algorithms, and exacerbates information asymmetries and structural inequality. The
collection and use of data is frequently not done ethically, and informed consent is a formality.
Algorithms use cognitive biases in a systematic way. Thus, the willingness to pay becomes an
extremely controlled, endogenous variable, as the consumer's willingness to pay will be determined
by the product's characteristics.
6. Conclusion
A neoclassical view is that personalised pricing enables prices to be set according to the willingness
to pay of individual customers, and so the company can minimise losses, including losses that might

result in business failure. This is to attract more individuals to the system, and there is a certain
economic justification for this. But this initiative is based on a series of idealised assumptions. This
is not always the case in the digital platform economy. In many cases, there is no personalisation
when it comes to pricing. They rely on a vast amount of data gathering, complex algorithms and
technologies that are specific to their influence on consumer behaviour, rather than on its reflection.
The idea of widespread adoption of the different fee structures in all scenarios is difficult to
imagine in terms of economic benefits. These practices have a significant impact on the fairness of
the market and deprive users of all the advantages of data privacy. As far as access to data, there are
relevant regulations that effectively consolidate power in the hands of a few. The whole market has
been taken over by these platforms.
