Abstract
This study looks at how Millennials and Gen Z consumers in Gujarat understand and
act toward environmental issues, eco-friendly products, and sustainable practices. (White,
Hardisty, and Habib [17]) The main goals were to check the level of consumer awareness,
study environmentally responsible behavior, see how demographic factors relate to recycling
habits, and understand consumer opinions and attitudes toward sustainability. The research
used a descriptive design, and 157 respondents were engaged in this survey through an
online survey using convenience sampling. The Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was used
for analysis, descriptive statistics, and cross-tabulation in Wang and Young [34] and SPSS
software. The ECOSCALE framework by Stone, Barnes, and Montgomery [31], (Costello and
Osborne [7]) was used as the base, and most questions were measured on a 7-point Likert
scale to ensure accurate results.
The personal values were highlighted where, environmental knowledge, and positive
attitudes was significant in encouraging environmentally responsible behavior. Younger
respondents, especially those in their early twenties with higher education, showed
more awareness of eco-friendly products and practices. Recycling behavior was linked to
demographic factors such as education, age, and income. While people actively participated
in general and stationery recycling, the adoption of e-waste recycling was relatively low.
The importance of this study specifies in showing how values, knowledge, and
demographics influence sustainable consumer behavior. The results can help policymakers,

1. Introduction
Environmental problems such as depletion of resources, climate
change, biodiversity loss, and pollution have intensified calls for
behaviours that reduce individual and collective environmental impact
(Agyeman [1]). Understanding why individuals act—or fail to act—is
environmentally responsible ways remains a central challenge for
environmental science, policy, and sustainable consumption research. Two
well-established theoretical frameworks explain such behaviours: the
Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which links perceived behaviour,
attitudes, and subjective norms to control intentions and actions (Ajzen
[2]), and the Value–Belief–Norm (VBN) theory, which emphasises values,
ecological beliefs, and personal norms as drivers of pro-environmental
support and action. These models explain variance in environmental
intentions and behaviours but leave open empirical questions about
intergenerational differences and the persistent attitude–behaviour gap
Agyeman [1].
Recent scholarship highlights two interrelated empirical puzzles.
First, younger cohorts; particularly Millennials and Generation Z , report
relatively high levels of environmental concern, activism, and expectations
of corporate and governmental responsibility. Large-scale surveys likely
have shown that these cohorts are more engaging in climate change
activism and share environmental content on social media compared to
older generations (Tyson and Kennedy [3]). Second, however, repeated
evidence shows a gap between reported attitudes and actual behaviours
such as sustainable purchasing, energy use, and waste practices indicating
that favourable intentions do not always translate into consistent, everyday
environmentally responsible Agyeman [1]. Psychological, structural, and
contextual barriers such as cost, convenience, social influence, knowledge,
identity, and perceived efficacy can weaken the link between attitudes and
behaviour (Ajzen [2]).

2. Literature Review
The theory of planned behavior (TPB) (Ajzen [2]) and the beliefs,
values, and norms (VBN) theory (Revelle [26]) provide complementary
explanations for environmentally responsible behavior. The TPB explains
sustainable behavior through subjective norms, attitudes, and perceived
behavioral control,  Roy and Basu [28] While the VBN emphasizes
environmental values, beliefs, and personal moral commitment.
Collectively, these theories reveal the psychological procedures that
motivate people to respond in environmentally responsible behavior. The
Eco-Aware Consumer Behavior Scale (ECOSCALE) translates observable
behaviors such as recycling, energy conservation, responsible purchasing,
and waste reduction into practical metrics. Therefore, the TPB and VBN
form a theoretical foundation, while the ECOSCALE serves as an empirical
tool to assess environmentally responsible purchasing behavior.
Rajan [40] examined environmentally responsible purchase intention
and developed a predictive model linking sustainability-related factors
with consumers’ purchase intentions. Their findings highlight the
importance of understanding the factors that encourage consumers to
translate environmental concerns into responsible purchasing decisions.
Similarly, Tiwari et al. [39] examined sustainable consumer behaviour
among Indian millennials and highlighted the relevance of generational
characteristics in understanding sustainable consumption practices. These
studies provide a useful foundation for examining environmentally
responsible consumer behaviour among Gen Z and Millennials in the
present study.
Environmentally Responsible Behavior (ERB) refers to actions taken
by users to reduce negative environmental effects through conscious
choices such as recycling, energy conservation, sustainable purchasing,
and waste reduction. Over the years, researchers have examined the
psychological, social, and contextual determinants of ERB, emphasizing
generational, cultural, and situational variations in these behaviors (Ajzen

[2], Revelle [26]). Two important theoretical contexts have shaped much of
this research: the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and the Value-Belief-
Norm (VBN) model. According to TPB, subjective norms, attitudes, and
perceived behavioral control effect of behavioral intentions, which in turn
predict actions (Ajzen [2]). In contrast, the VBN model highlights how
personal values, beliefs, and moral obligations guide environmentally
responsible actions (Revelle [26]), (Mabkhot [19]).
Researchers have expanded these frameworks to consider factors like
green self-identity, trust, perceived consumer effectiveness, and conviction
in eco-labels, aiming to describe how individuals who express their
concern for the environment may still fail to act sustainably (Kaiser [16],
Theocharis and Tsekouropoulos [9]). This “intention-behavior gap”
remains a major issue in sustainable consumption research. Studies reveal
that young consumers, particularly Millennials and Generation Z, express
high environmental concern but do not always translate that awareness
into consistent behavior. Millennials often demonstrate greater tendencies
to purchase green products when affordability and convenience align,
whereas Generation Z tends to emphasize digital engagement and
transparency from brands (Verma, Tandon, and Tripathi [35], ( Nguyen,
Tran, and Lee [22], Bernardes et al. [5], Theocharis and Tsekouropoulos
[9]).
In the Indian context, Khan, Azhar, and Azeem [4] found that
Millennials actively engaged in recycling, energy conservation, and green
purchasing, primarily influenced by peer norms, perceived behavioral
control, and trust in eco-labels. However, both studies reported limited
rural and Gen Z participation and highlighted the requirement for
institutional support such as sustainability education and government-
backed labeling systems. Observed that Indian Gen Z consumers were
highly influenced by social medias like Instagram and YouTube, which
increased awareness but did not regulated the consistent eco-friendly
actions. These research guide suggest that while younger generations in
India are environmentally aware, barriers such as limited access,
convenience, and institutional incentives continue to restrict behavior
change.
Examined green banking awareness among Indian millennial
working women and found considerable awareness of several technology-
enabled and environmentally friendly banking services. The study
highlighted the importance of awareness and digital services in promoting
environmentally responsible practices among millennials.

influence ERB. Choudhary and Rani [6] reported that women, particularly
among Gen Z, were more proactive in adopting eco-friendly practices

energy conservation. Studies by Manley [20] and Cantillo, Astorino, and

universities and workplaces fosters long-term environmentally responsible
behavior. Similarly, Singh and Verma [29],  Singh and Verma [30] observed
that urban Gen Z in India showed stronger participation in public transport
use and compliance with plastic ban initiatives, reflecting how institutional
policies and public infrastructure support can strengthen sustainable
practices.
Across the reviewed studies, environmental concern consistently
emerges as an important antecedent of environmentally responsible
behaviour. However, there is less agreement regarding the role of
demographic variables. While several studies report stronger sustainable
behaviours among women and younger consumers, others suggest that
appropriate factors such as affordability, infrastructure, and institutional

2.1 Research Gap
The valuable insights were provided by current literature, the
behavior of environmentally conscious consumers; however, some gaps
still exist. Most studies rely on cross-sectional designs and self-reported
data, which limit our consideration of the causal relationships that
influence sustainable behavior. However, research focused primarily on
industrialized countries, while data from developing economies such as
India are scarce. Although theoretical concepts such as the Theory of
Planned Behavior (TPB) and the Theory of Values, Beliefs, and Norms
(VN) are widely used, their integration with behavioral measurement
tools such as ECOSCALE remains largely unexplored. Besides, there are
few case studies comparing Millennials and Generation Z in the same
cultural context, and standing research has focused chiefly on intentions
to purchase by environmental friendly products rather than broader
environmentally conscious practices such as recycling, energy conservation,
sustainable transportation, and waste reduction. The role of digital
engagement, trust in social media, and transparency in sustainability
communication also merit further exploration. Thus, a study of the
environmentally responsible behavior of the younger generation in
Gujarat, which is a rapidly growing state and actively implementing
sustainable initiatives, to provide valuable understandings into the factors
influencing sustainable lifestyles in the context of developing economies.
The existing study targets to address these gaps by conducting a
comparative analysis of Gen Z and Millennial consumers within India. It
investigates multiple dimensions of environmentally responsible behavior,
including recycling, energy use, sustainable transport, and green product
purchasing, while integrating psychological, social, and institutional
factors. Through this approach, the study seeks to offer a comprehensive
understanding of how awareness translates into sustainable action among
younger generations and provide applied suggestions for sellers,
policymakers, and educators striving to promote sustainable living Stone,
Barnes, and Montgomery [31].

3. Research Methodology and Analysis
This study adopted a quantitative, descriptive cross-sectional
research design to examine environmentally responsible buying behaviour
among consumers in India. A survey method was considered as it enables
the collection of standardized responses from a large participants at a one
point in time. The target population covered Indian consumers belonging
to Generation Z and Millennial cohorts , as more groups are involved in
sustainable consumption decisions.
The present research paper aims to describe the characteristics and
patterns of environmentally responsible behavior among different
generations. Primary data  for the study were collected with an online
survey using a structured questionnaire  managed via Google Forms . A
total of  157 respondents  belonging to the  Millennial and Generation Z
groups from the state of Gujarat participated in the study. The respondents
were selected using the convenience sampling method, as it allowed easy
access to participants within the target demographic. The structured
questionnaire consisted two sections; demographic details and ECOSCALE
model. Participation was voluntary. Respondents were informed about
the objectives of the research before participation. No personally
identifiable information was collected. Confidentiality and anonymity
were maintained throughout the research process, and all responses were
used solely for academic purposes.
Prior to statistical analysis, the collected data were sorted by
completeness, duplicate responses, inconsistent entries, and outliers.
Missing values were examined, Since the dataset contained no missing
values, no imputation procedures were applied.
To analyze the collected data, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA),
Descriptive Statistics, and Cross Tabulation were employed. The analyses
were conducted using Jamovi [33] and SPSS software. The study is
grounded on the ECOSCALE model developed by Stone, Barnes, and
Montgomery [31], which measures Environmentally Responsible
Consumer Behavior (ERB). Based on this framework, the paper interprets
respondents’ environmentally responsible attitudes and behaviors. Except
for demographic questions, all survey items were measured using a
7-point Likert scale , which helps in maintaining data normality and
capturing varying degrees of opinion.
To ensure the reliability of the measurement scales, Cronbach’s
Coefficient Alpha was calculated. This test checks the internal consistency
of the questionnaire items used in the research. A total of 31 constructs,
excluding demographic variables, were tested for reliability. According to

3.1 Result Analysis
Before conducting the statistical analyses, several diagnostic tests
were performed to evaluate the suitability of the dataset. The normality of
the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, skewness, and kurtosis.
Although the Shapiro–Wilk test was significant (p < .05), the skewness
and kurtosis values were within the acceptable range (±2), and visual
inspection of the histograms and Q–Q plots indicated that the data were
approximately normally distributed and suitable for subsequent analyses.
Factor analysis was employed to group the variables and identify
patterns in environmental responsible behavior. The KMO and Bartlett’s
(Table 2) test value (0.678) indicated acceptable sampling adequacy.
Although the KMO value (0.678) is categorized as “mediocre” according
to Kim and Choi [18], it exceeds the minimum acceptable threshold of 0.60
for conducting Exploratory Factor Analysis. Furthermore, Bartlett’s Test
was statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating that sufficient correlations
existed among the variables to justify factor analysis.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was employed because the
objective of the current research was to explore the dimensional structure
of ECOSCALE among Indian consumers. Although ECOSCALE has been
validated in previous international studies, limited empirical evidence
exists regarding its applicability in the Indian context. Therefore, EFA was
considered more appropriate than Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA),
which is typically employed when an established measurement model is
being tested. Future studies using larger samples should apply CFA or
Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) to confirm the factor structure
identified in this research.
Principal Axis Factoring (P AF) was employed to identify the latent
dimensions of environmentally responsible buying behaviour, as it
estimates the common variance among variables and is well suited to
behavioural research. To enhance interpretability, Varimax orthogonal
rotation was applied, producing a simpler and more interpretable factor
structure by maximizing the variance of factor loadings. Items with factor
loadings of 0.50 or above were retained for interpretation. Variables
exhibiting low communalities (below 0.30) or substantial cross-loadings
were evaluated for possible exclusion to improve construct validity. The
Results section now presents: Percentage of Variance Explained; and Final
Factor Loadings.
The analysis extracted four distinct factors that reflect environmentally
responsible behavior among respondents. Together, these four factors
accounted for 40.63% of the total variance, with individual contributions
of 15%, 10%, 8%, and 7%, respectively. Regarding the cumulative variance
explained (40.63%), previous methodological literature acknowledges that
exploratory studies involving behavioural and psychological constructs
often report moderate levels of explained variance. Since the basic objective
of this study was exploratory rather than confirmatory, the obtained
variance was considered acceptable.

6. Conclusion
The study concludes that Millennials and Generation Z in Gujarat
show a moderate but growing level of environmentally responsible
behavior. Their actions are mainly shaped by personal values, awareness,
and education. The findings confirm that moral beliefs and environmental
knowledge play a vital role in encouraging eco-friendly behavior. However,
there remains a gap between awareness and actual action, particularly in
areas like e-waste recycling.
Educational programs and awareness campaigns can help strengthen
environmental responsibility among young consumers. Integrating
sustainability education in academic courses and providing better access
to recycling facilities can improve participation in eco-friendly practices.
Financial incentives and social initiatives may also encourage consistent
sustainable behavior. Overall, the study highlights that young consumers’
values, education, and financial means collectively influence their
environmental behavior, providing useful insights for policymakers,
educators, and marketers to promote sustainability more effectively.
Future research should consider a larger and more diverse sample to
improve external validity. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and
Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) can further validate the measurement
model and examine causal relationships among the constructs.
