Abstract. From the last century to the present, whether consumer purchasing decisions are
related to unconsciousness has always been a core issue. This article, as a literature review
paper, systematically reviews the theoretical and empirical research on the relationship
between unconscious priming, cognitive load, and consumer decision-making. The results
of this review paper have found that unconscious priming mainly affects consumer decision-
making through three pathways. High cognitive load enhances this effect through
information presentation, product form, and the shopping platform environment, which
decreases the ability to analyze rationally and then changes consumers' decision-making
strategies. These findings indicate that the impact of unconscious priming on consumer
decision-making is not determined by fixed factors alone but is also influenced by consumer
cognitive load. This article not only represents theoretical contributions to the unconscious
cognition of psychology, helping consumers better understand their own motivation, but
also provides valuable strategic references for marketing practice, which helps to better
understand consumer behavior and formulate more ethical strategies to improve the overall
economy.
Keywords: Consumer Decision-Making, Unconscious Priming, Cognitive Load, Priming
Effect, Consumer Behavior
1. Introduction
Decision-making runs through almost everyday life. For example, students compare the functions of
different devices when buying them to determine which one is more suitable for their studies.
Consumers may compare different prices and brands when choosing food in supermarkets.
Similarly, consumers in shopping malls make their buying decisions based on their personal
preferences and actual needs, among whom some may prefer hip-hop style, while others may prefer
a gentle style. This seemingly simple behavior involves certain information processing and
judgement. According to research in cognitive psychology, most human behavior is driven by
unconscious priming in consumer contexts [1]. Researchers have observed this phenomenon in
several experiments. For example, participants who were unconsciously exposed to the Apple brand
logo performed better on the creativity task [2]. The metaphorical advertisements tend to stimulate

Although studies have found that cognitive load increases and changes people's behavior when

improving overall efficiency. Overall, the main structure of this article is roughly composed of five
parts, including introducing three main concepts, exploring the impact of unconscious priming on
consumer decision-making, analyzing how cognitive load amplifies this effect, discussion and
suggestions, and conclusion.
2. Core concepts
This research paper focuses on three core concepts: consumer decision-making, unconscious
priming, and cognitive load. Specifically, it studies how unconscious priming affects consumer
decision-making and how cognitive load strengthens or weakens this influence. According to the
Business Dictionary, consumer decision-making refers to the process including need recognition,
information search, product evaluation, purchase choice, and post-purchase response to satisfy
consumers' own needs and desires [5]. However, at the level of unconscious cognitive psychology,
many behaviors and decisions are influenced unconsciously, and unconscious priming may impact
consumers at different stages. Unconscious priming is the key concept that this article focuses on. It
refers to a procedure that environmental cues such as brand logos, product color, and online store
layout can activate psychological associations in the mind and influence behavior later without
individual awareness. This consists of four parts: lack of awareness, unintentional activation,
efficiency, and lack of personal control [1]. Similarly, cognitive load is crucial in consumer decision-
making. When consumers make choices, they often need to integrate different types of information,
such as comparing different options or being distracted by the shopping environment. Cognitive load
is defined as the burden that a task imposes on working memory, encompassing mental effort, time
pressure, and complexity of information processing [6]. These three concepts form the theoretical
basis for this research: consumer decision-making is the dependent variable, unconscious priming is
the independent variable, and cognitive load is the moderating variable.
3. The main influence of unconscious priming on consumer decision-making
The influence of unconscious activation on consumer decisions can be discussed in three aspects:
how consumers respond to sensory cues, how brand associations are activated, and how emotions
are transferred to the products. Based on the research, most human behavior is not driven by
conscious rational decision-making, but by unconscious psychological processes [1]. For example,
there is a neurophysiological experiment that provides empirical evidence for the impact of
advertising stimuli on consumer responses [3]. To be more specific, this experiment involved 43
participants, 22 females and 21 males, who were exposed to stimuli from 28 different levels of
complexity of advertisements. In addition, the researchers recorded the participants' reactions to

conductance response (SCR). These results indicate that metaphorical advertisements are more
likely than non-metaphorical advertisements to elicit positive neurophysiological responses, which
may further increase consumers' purchase intentions. Furthermore, unconscious priming may affect
consumers' decisions through three main pathways: semantic association, brand-related activation,
and emotional transfer. First, it illustrates the automatic semantic connection between physical
sensations (odor, temperature) and abstract concepts, which share the same brain region called the
insula [7]. Neuroscience research has found that this brain region is activated both when a person
holds something warm in the hand and when the person engages in warm social interactions with
family members. Secondly, in the 2008 experiment conducted by Fitzsimons et al., participants were
unconsciously presented with two different brand logos where the participants have no memory of
and then asked to come up with creative uses for the paper clips [2]. The result showed that
participants who were exposed to Apple came up with more creative uses than those who saw the
IBM mark. This is because the Apple brand is often associated with the word that related to
innovation; when consumers are exposed to the Apple brand logo in the shopping centre, their
unconscious impressions of creativity are activated, which makes people more inclined to choose
the Apple brand when buying electronic products [7]. Therefore, stimuli such as advertising slogans,
brand colors, and product packaging may first shape consumers' attention, familiarity, and product
preferences, which in turn affect subsequent purchasing decisions. Lastly, there is the emotional
transfer pathway, in which the environment can unconsciously induce the individual's emotional
state and then mistakenly attribute the evaluation of the product. For example, a classic study found
that male participants who had crossed a thrilling suspension bridge were more likely to mistake
their own anxiety and accelerated heartbeat for sexual attraction from female experimenters [8]. In
daily life, advertisements during entertainment programs directly affect viewers' consumption of
snacks increase by 45% [9]. People may experience certain types of emotion triggered by
advertisements and unconsciously transfer these emotions to consumption behaviour. Overall, these
three pathways may work together in actual consumption situations: semantic associations guide
interpretation, brand-related cues activate stored impressions, and emotional transfer shapes the final
evaluation of the product.
4. The amplification effect of cognitive load
Cognitive load can be regarded as a moderating factor that affects the degree to which unconscious
priming influences consumer purchasing decisions. According to the cognitive load theory, the
capacity of human working memory is limited. The more information an individual deals with
during the consumer decision-making process, the scarcer their cognitive resources become [10].
Increasing their sensitivity to environmental cues such as brand logos or promotional information.
For example, when consumers are under time pressure or have to process a large amount of product
information, they may rely on intuition and easily accessible cues, which can amplify the
unconscious priming effect. To better explain this mechanism, a non-hypothetical shopping
experiment was conducted to study how cognitive load changes consumer product choices in real-
life strategies [4]. In this study, participants completed a real food purchase task under high and low
cognitive load conditions. Some products were presented in the form of bundles or with discounts.
The results showed that under the influence of high cognitive load, consumers were more inclined to
choose bundled products, such as meal deals, even without discounts, rather than choosing
individual products. The probability of buying bundled goods is 3.98 times higher than for
consumers who are under no-load conditions. Bundled products can reduce the amount of

were randomly assigned to four different experimental groups. This experiment compared the
conditions between high- and low-cognitive-load movies to investigate how the task complexity of
these movies affects the participants' difficulty in decision-making. Each movie presents two forms
of comments; one is AI-generated bullet points, and the other is the original paragraph-based
comments. The result indicated that for the simpler movie, the two types of comments did not have a
significant impact on the cognitive load of participants. However, for movies that are more complex
and require more time for information processing, the key point of the comment can reduce
participants' cognitive load by approximately 11.8%. Afterwards, participants answered a survey on
deciding whether to watch the movie or not, and found that when cognitive load increased, people
were less willing to watch the movie. It showed that consumers experienced different levels of
cognitive load when using shopping platforms, especially on the non-native platform [10]. This is
because increased difficulty in understanding the content itself and unfamiliarity with navigating the
platform environment decrease the efficiency of processing the information, which affects their
satisfaction and willingness to repurchase products on the non-native platform. Therefore, when
consumers use non-native language platforms, both their internal and external load increase
significantly. In summary, high cognitive load can be influenced by external factors and internal
factors such as the form of information presentation and familiarity with shopping platforms, which
can weaken consumers' ability to analyze rationally. Therefore, cognitive load moderates the
relationship between unconscious priming and consumer decision-making.
5. Discussion & suggestions
As a review paper, this article is limited by the fact that most of the evidence comes from
laboratories or artificial environments, which may reduce the external validity of the research
results. This is because in the real shopping environment, there may be more complicated external
factors that are difficult to measure and control, such as cognitive load level before shopping and the
type of music played in the store, and these small environmental factors may affect the effectiveness
of this mechanism. Second, the references presented in this article do not adopt a unified method for
measuring cognitive load and lack scientific neurophysiological indicators. In addition, it is worth
noting that the unconscious priming effect remains controversial in academia, such as the well-
known intelligence priming experiment [12]. To be more specific, this experimental result overturns
a 1998 experiment, in which the people who thought about professors' characteristics before the
experiment started did not answer more questions correctly than those who thought about football
hooligans. Therefore, it triggers academic debate on the reliability of unconscious activation. Based
on the above findings, future research can consider verifying these findings in reality and
introducing more objective measurement methods for cognitive load, such as EEG and eye tracking.
More importantly, future research should examine individual differences in each person because
consumers in reality come from different age and cultural backgrounds because younger consumers
may be more suitable to go with digital platforms, while elderly consumers may prefer offline
consumption habits. In terms of cultural differences, some cultures have a higher acceptance of

6. Conclusion
Overall, through the analysis of existing literature, research has found that unconscious priming
mainly affects consumer decision-making through three pathways. Firstly, there is a semantic
association pathway where the brain automatically links physical touch and abstract concepts.
Secondly, the brand activation pathway, where brand identity and advertising can unconsciously
influence consumers' goals. Thirdly, there is emotional transmission, where consumers mistakenly
attribute their emotions to products or services. These three paths often interact with each other to
impact consumers' consumption behaviors. This review further found that cognitive load played a
crucial moderating role between unconscious priming and decision-making. To be more specific,
when cognitive load is high, consumers have low analytical ability and are more likely to rely on
obtainable environmental clues, thereby amplifying this priming effect. The research papers in this
review are mainly based on lab environments, with limited external control factors and there is a
lack of unified, digital standards of measurement for cognitive load. For future research, should
further confirm these findings in the real shopping scenarios, considering individual differences such
as age and culture
