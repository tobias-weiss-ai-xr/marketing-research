1. Introduction and Conceptual Gap
Historically, the banking sector was one of the first sectors to adopt information technology to deliver
services. In terms of e -banking channels adopted by banks in Jordan and similar banking markets,
these have traditionally included automated teller machines; online banking; electronic fund transfer
capabilities; SMS banking; card -based services; and basic customer guidance regarding customer
security responsibilities. Therefore, the early e -banking risk research conducted in Jordan viewed
information technology as a means of mitigating chann el-level risks including misuse of passwords,
unauthorized access to systems and/or cards; card theft or loss; risky customer behavior; and limited
understanding of the risks associated with using electronic services (Anouze & Alamro, 2019;
Yousafzai et al., 2009; Luo et al., 2010). That perspective is no longer sufficient.
Digital banking is now part of a larger financial technology ecosystem. Banks increasingly rely not only
on internally developed applications, but also on partnerships with third-party technology providers.
They also rely on mobile apps; instant payment platforms; APIs; electronic wallets; cloud computing;
other third -party technology vendors; AI -supported fraud detection programs; and data -sharing
agreements. As a result, the focus of r isk has moved from specific electronic channels to connected
digital service chains. The dependencies involved in a mobile banking transaction may include: the
customer's mobile device; authentication infrastructure that verifies customer identity and
authorizes access; API gateways that control access to various systems within the bank; payment
switches that process payments between two parties; cloud hosting that provides storage and
processing capacity to support multiple transactions at once; fraud anal ytics that monitor real-time
activity for suspicious behavior; telecommunications networks that transmit and receive messages
between devices; and third -party service providers that help manage the overall flow of data
(Gomber et al., 2017; Ozili, 2018; Arner et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2024).
The conceptual implication is significant. In the past, traditional e -banking security asked whether a
bank could secure an individual electronic channel. Today, with digital banking governance, banks
now ask whether they have sufficient control over a complex digital ecosystem of internal systems,
external vendors and service providers, customer behaviors, real -time payment transactions, data
exchanges, and service continuity. To govern the complexities of this new digital ecosystem requires
a stronger theoretical connection between cybersecurity governance, digital trust, third -party risk,
API security, operational resilience, and customer-level risk perceptions (Gomber et al., 2017; Wang
et al., 2024; Modesti et al., 2025).
In this paper, we develop that relationship. We argue that cybersecurity governance (CSG) is not
simply a technical control mechanism but rather a strategic governance capability that determines
how customers perceive trust in a bank's digital banki ng services, how banks manage third -party
dependencies, how API-based services are governed, how incidents are contained, and how critical

Digital banking has provided banks with speed, accessibility, convenience, cost efficiency, and
financial inclusion, but at the same time, it has exposed both banks and customers to cyber,
operational, behavioral, legal, data privacy, and third-party risks. As mobile banking, instant payment
systems, electronic wallets, fintech partnerships, API -based services, and cloud infrastructure
expand, banking risk is no longer confined to the internal information systems of the bank. Customer
devices and external providers are now potential sources of risk. Additionally, insecure application
interfaces, weak authentication methods, poor consent management processes, ransomware
attacks, social engineering tactics,  cloud concentration risks, automated fraud mechanisms, and
service outage risks all represent potential sources of digital banking risk (Gomber et al., 2017; Ozili,
2018; Wang et al., 2024; Modesti et al., 2025; Ojehomon et al., 2026).
This paper identifi es a conceptual gap in digital banking research, where security, trust, customer
adoption, and operational risk are treated as separate discussions by most researchers studying
digital banking. Cybersecurity studies have focused on controls and threats. Tr ust studies have
focused on customer beliefs and adoption. Operational resilience studies have focused on continuity
and recovery. Open banking studies have focused on API and third -party exposure. However, in
digital banking practice, these domains intera ct continuously. A cyber incident can reduce trust.
Weak API security controls can increase third-party exposure. Poor third-party oversight can weaken
operational resilience. Limited customer digital awareness can undermine strong technical controls.
Failures in resilience can transform a technical incident into a reputational crisis (Jafri et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2024; Modesti et al., 2025; Buttigieg & Zimmermann, 2024).
Therefore, this paper seeks to address the following conce ptual research question: How can
cybersecurity governance be theorized as a holistic mechanism to enhance digital trust, operational
resilience, and digital banking risk reduction in Jordanian banks?
This question is addressed through a theoretical framework connecting cybersecurity governance to
digital trust; to operational resilience; to API security; to third -party risk management; to customer
digital awareness; to incident response capability; and to reducing the overall risks in digital banking.

2. Conceptual Method and Framework
This paper adopts a structured conceptual synthesis method. A conceptual paper is not judged on
statistical results, but by the clarity of its constructs, the logic of its theoretical development, the
novelty of its conceptual integration, and the usefulness of its propositions for future research.
Accordingly, this paper does not present empirical hypotheses as though they had been tested. We
present resear ch propositions that can later be converted into hypotheses in survey -based,
interview-based, archival, or mixed -methods studies. Our synthesis follows a theory -building logic
rather than a systematic-review logic. We do not count every publication on cybe rsecurity or digital
banking, but we integrate the streams of literature that are theoretically necessary to understand
how digital banks can reduce risk. Four streams were selected because they represent the main
mechanisms through which digital banking risk is created and reduced: i) cybersecurity governance;
ii) trust-risk logic; iii) API and third-party risk; and iv) operational resilience (Gomber et al., 2017; Jafri
et al., 2024; Modesti et al., 2025; Buttigieg & Zimmermann, 2024).
We chose literature based on four criteria. First, we only included studies that contributed to one of
the core constructs of the paper. Second, the studies had to be relevant to banking, financial services,
fintech, open banking, digital platforms, cybersecurity, trust, or resilience. Third, the studies had to
support conceptual development as opposed to only providing generic technology commentary.
Fourth, we favoured recent journal studies where the topic has changed materially since 2020, while
retaining core studies that  define the core theoretical concepts of trust, perceived risk, and
technology acceptance. Our synthesis was carried out in five steps: i) reviewing earlier Jordanian e -
banking risk research to establish a historical baseline for the channel-based view of risk; ii) reviewing
the selected streams of literature to identify recurring constructs and mechanisms; iii) outlining
construct boundaries so that it is clear what constitutes governance and what does not, how trust,
awareness, API security, third-party risk, and incident response serve to mitigate or reduce risk, and
where the boundaries of these constructs lie; iv) developing theoretical pathways by asking how each
construct changes the probability, perception, or impact of digital banking risk; v) expressing those

2.1 Accounting Information Systems and Internal Control Perspective
Digital banking systems, viewed through the lens of Accounting Information Systems, represent more
than a platform for customers in their interactions with banks. The transaction processing and control
functions of these systems provide authorization, recording, validation, monitoring, and preservation
of all customer transaction data. Accordingly, Cybersecurity Governance supports the reliability of
recorded transaction data, the integrity of audit-trail data, the overall effectiveness of both IT General
Controls and Application Controls, and the quality of the internal reports used by management,
internal auditors, and audit committees. Accordingly, Cybersecurity Governance supports the
reliability of recorded transaction data, the integrity of audit -trail data, the overall effectiveness of
both IT General Controls and Application Controls, and the quality of the internal reports used by
management, internal auditors, and audit committees (DeLone & McLean, 2003; Masli et al., 2010).
Research has shown a link between Information Technology and Accounting Research in relation to
organizational decision usefulness (Hunton, 2002), as well as Information Systems Success Research,
which defines System Quality, Information Quality, Service Quality, and Net Benefits (DeLone &
McLean, 2003). Additionally, research on Internal Control Monitoring indicates that technology-
based monitoring can be an effective method for providing assurance over control processes (Masli
et al., 2010). Based on this rationale, the current model views cybersecurity governance, API security,
third-party risk management, customer awareness of digital banking, incident response capabilities,
and operational resilience as mechanisms for strengthening the digital internal control environment,
rather than strictly as banking technical issues.

Cybersecurity governance views cyber risk as being managed by the formal assignment of
responsibilities, through the oversight of Boards of Directors, a definition of acceptable levels of risk
("risk appetite"), the development of policies for managing cyber risk, regular monitoring of cyber -
risk exposures, holding individuals accountable for their actions related to cyber risk, reporting of
cyber-risk incidents, oversight of third-party providers' cybersecurity practices, and the incorporation
of cyber risk into enterprise -wide risk-management processes. Cybersecurity governance is a larger
concept than purely technical cybersecurity. Cybersecurity governance establishes how a bank will
allocate the responsibility for protecting against cyber threats,  prioritize which cyber threats should
be addressed first, monitor controls intended to prevent cyber threats, escalate the response to an
actual cyber incident, and learn from past failures. As  such, cybersecurity governance is a strategic
capability that connects technology-based risk to an organization's overall risk management strategy
(Wang et al., 2024; Bruno et al., 2025; Aldasoro et al., 2022).
Recent evidence supporting this strategic vi ew comes from banking research. Wang et al. (2024)
showed that challenges related to data privacy and cybersecurity resulting from banks engaging in
digital transformation were driven by legacy systems, regulatory compliance obligations, third-party
vendors, customer confidence, and emerging threats. Additionally, Bruno et al. (2025) demonstrated
that there was an association between banks' cybersecurity policies and their level of resilience and
performance. Lastly, Aldasoro et al. (2022, 2023) found that cyber and operational risks had financial
stability implications due to digital dependence creating shock transmission channels to institutions
beyond those originally impacted. Together, these studies support the conceptual argument that
effective cybersecurity governance can create both protective and strategic value for organizations.

The trust-risk framework provides an explanation for why customers continue to use online financial
services in spite of the uncertainty they feel. The  presence of trust diminishes customers' perceived
vulnerability. For example, customers cannot see the workings of encryption, authentication
mechanisms, ongoing monitoring of transactions for potential anomalies, API security controls to
limit unauthorized transaction activity, and incident response plans when a cyber event occurs in
digital banking. Therefore, customers rely upon the institutional credibility of the bank, their
perception of the security of their data at the bank, their perception of the privacy afforded to their
data at the bank, the reliability of services provided by the bank, and the observable behavior of the
bank during both normal operating conditions and when disruptions occur (Mayer et al., 1995;
McKnight et al., 2002; Yousafzai et al., 2009; Luo et al., 2010).
Trust has been extensively studied in organizational and digital environments. According to Mayer et
al. (1995), trust is defined as a willingness to be vulnerable to another party based on one's
expectations regarding that ot her party's behavior. McKnight et al. (2002) expanded upon this
understanding by proposing a theoretical model that incorporates measures of trusting beliefs and
trusting intentions for electronic commerce. Further study in digital banking indicated that trust and
perceived risk were key factors influencing customers' adoption and acceptance decisions (Yousafzai
et al., 2009; Luo et al., 2010). Building on previous research, the current  paper treats digital trust as
a mediator connecting cybersecurity governance with risk reduction.
The ecosystem approach is necessary because digital banking risk is spread among various connected
parties. Studies focused on digital finance and fintech demonstrate that financial services are
becoming increasingly dependent on platform interfaces, data flows, third -party suppliers, and
digitally facilitated customer interactions (Gomber et al., 2017; Ozili, 2018). Research on regulatory
technology demonstrates that digital transformation is changing the boundaries of financia l
regulation and supervision (Arner et al., 2016). Although the bank continues to be responsible for
ensuring customer trust and regulatory compliance, some or all of the sources of risk may exist
outside the bank's legal perimeter.
In addition to changing the focus of evaluation, the ecosystem perspective necessitates consideration
of additional dimensions. Instead of merely evaluating a bank based on the security of its internal
systems, this perspective evaluates a bank based on its ability to manage a d igital service chain
consisting of API gateways, cloud providers, fintech partners, payment processing companies, fraud
analytics suppliers, and end-customer devices. This is the reason why API security and third-party risk
management are essential concept s as core governance considerations rather than marginal
technical concerns (Gomber et al., 2017; Modesti et al., 2025; Saveljeva & Volkova, 2026; Ojehomon
et al., 2026).
Finally, the operational resilience perspective introduces a fundamental distinction:  preventing
events from occurring is different from managing events that do occur. Service availability matters in
digital banking because customers expect continuous access to accounts, fund transfers, debit and
credit cards, mobile wallets, and payment s ervices. When a cyber event causes a significant
disruption to critical digital services for customers, it rapidly becomes a trust failure, a reputational

Earlier e-banking risk research conducted in Jordan focused primarily on electronic channels and the
ability of information technology to provide a protective mechanism against potential customer -
related risks. That line of thought was appropriate for that period when the primary services offered
electronically included ATMs, online banking, SMS banking, card products, and electronic funds
transfer. Today’s digital banking ecosystem requires a different approach. Banks today must govern
their customer interfaces, internal systems, APIs, third -party vendors, fraud analytics, data privacy
controls, incident response processes, and service continuity (Ano uze & Alamro, 2019; Yousafzai et
al., 2009; Luo et al., 2010; Central Bank of Jordan, 2021).
This evolution brings about an important conceptual implication: the risks associated with digital
banking are no longer solely dependent upon how secure each indi vidual electronic channel is.
Rather, they depend upon how effectively the entire ecosystem is governed. For example, a bank
may have excellent controls around its mobile app but lack adequate oversight over third parties. It
may have robust encryption methods in place but lack adequate education for customers on how to
use digital services safely. It may have high levels of customer trust prior to an incident occurring but
fail to communicate clearly with those affected by a service interruption. Therefore, as presented
below, the model developed here views risk reduction as a multifaceted governance process
(Gomber et al., 2017; Ozili, 2018; Wang et al., 2024; Modesti et al., 2025).

Cybersecurity governance is the central construct in this study. Cybersecurity governance provides
the connection between the technical, organizational, and strategic aspects of managing cyber risk.
Effective governance identifies roles and responsibilities, allocates resources, establishes risk
tolerance, oversees third-party relationships, escalates and responds to incidents, supports internal
audits, and aligns cybersecurity controls with enterprise -wide risk management activities. As such,
cybersecurity governance is particularly significant in banking because cyber risks can potentially
impact a bank's financial performance, customer trust in the institution, regulatory compliance, and
overall service continuity (Wang et al., 2024; Bruno et al., 2025; Aldasoro et al., 2022).
Data privacy and cybersecurity challenges arising from the banking industry's digital transformation
efforts have been highlighted by Wang et al. (2024). Additionally, Bruno et al. (2025) demonstrated
that cybersecurity policies can positively influence financial resilienc e and performance at banks.
Further, Aldasoro et al. (2023) showed that operational and cyber risk events can produce significant
economic losses and create systemic concerns in the financial sector. Collectively, these studies
support the shift from viewi ng cybersecurity as a purely protective activity to viewing it as a
governance-enabled activity that protects business value.
As a result, the study views cybersecurity governance as an antecedent to both digital trust and digital
banking risk reduction (Wang et al., 2024; Bruno et al., 2025; Jafri et al., 2024).
Digital trust is central to the study since banking inherently involves some degree of financial
vulnerability. Banking customers must believe that the digital banking services provided will
safeguard their financial assets, personal information, identities, and transaction histories. Customer
reliance on mobile apps for remote transactions, instant payment systems, and data -sharing
interfaces heightens the importance of trust in digital banking s ervices compared to traditional
branch-based services. Although customers do not see what happens behind the scenes in digital
banking services, they remain exposed to perceived and actual risks (Mayer et al., 1995; McKnight et
al., 2002; Yousafzai et al., 2009; Luo et al., 2010).
There is evidence from existing research to support these arguments regarding the relationship
between digital trust and banking services. Multiple dimensions of trust exist in digital environments
according to McKnight et al. (2002). Similarly, Yousafzai et al. (2009) demonstrated that trust serves
a multidimensional role in the adoption of Internet banking. Luo et al. (2010) found that trust and
perceived risk act together to determine mobile banking acceptance. Recent studies by  Bajwa et al.
(2023), Jafri et al. (2024), and Aisyah et al. (2025) confirm that awareness of cyberattacks, perceptions
of security, and customer trust after an incident positively impact digital banking commitment and
usage.
Therefore, customer digital awareness is a prerequisite for achieving successful risk reduction in
digital banking since many incidents occur beyond the boundaries of a bank's internal systems.
Examples of how customer digital awareness can help mitigate incidents include phishing messages,
fake links, social engineering calls, sharing credentials, using insecure mobile apps, reusing
passwords, and using compromised devices, all of which can circumvent strong institutional controls.

Thus, customer digital awareness needs to be viewed as an expansion of the digital banking control

Research exists that demonstrates connections between online banking users' behavior and their

research has established a positive relationship between users' knowledge of cybersecurity, their

the effectiveness of governance practices related to cybersecurity in terms of reducing risk.
Additionally, open banking and API-based financial services expand the digital banking risk boundary.
Open banking enables banks to share data or services with other organizations through APIs, which
enhance innovation and customer experience; however, they increase the number of entry points
for attacks. Risks associated with open banking include inadequate authentication/authorization,
disclosure of too much sensitive data, inadequate consent management, poorly designed code,
transaction tampering, or weaknesses in third-party vendor systems (Modesti et al., 2025; Ojehomon
et al., 2026; Saveljeva & Volkova, 2026).
The necessity for formal models and security analyses of open banking API protocol requirements
has been demonstrated by Modesti et al. (2025). Research conducted by Saveljeva & Volkova (2026)
has shown that increased levels of trust among customers can lead to decreased assessments of
third-party risk across various categories; however, policy-based screening of third-party vendors for
cybersecurity-related deficiencies should not be replaced by customer-based trust assessments. The
negative impact of open banking on cybercrime, including API -enabled financial crimes, has been
noted by Ojehomon (2026). Overall, research supports viewing API security and thir d-party risk
management as distinct but interconnected areas within cybersecurity governance.
Finally, operational resilience rounds out the proposed framework as there are situations where
strong security alone is unable to prevent all types of incidents.  Banks must develop capabilities to
detect incidents, contain damage caused by incidents, recover quickly after an incident, communicate
adequately during the incident, and incorporate lessons learned into future development.
Operational resilience mitigates the severity of cyber events, while operational resilience limits the
impact of service interruptions on customers and critical systems. This is especially relevant given
that customers increasingly expect continuous access to their digital channels and  timely payment
processing infrastructure (Aldasoro et al., 2022; Buttigieg & Zimmermann, 2024; Bruno et al., 2025).
In addition to providing a platform for detecting cyber disruptions as they occur, Buttigieg &
Zimmerman (2024) indicated that regulating o perational resilience has emerged as a central
regulatory priority for financial supervisors due to the possibility that cyber and ICT disruptions could
disrupt supervisory coordination and create instability in markets. In contrast, in this proposed
framework, operational resilience acts as a loss mitigation factor. While stronger governance
improves operational resilience by translating effective governance into continuity under stress
through tested response plans, defined escalation procedures, coordinated responses from third

Customer digital awareness should help improve the effectiveness of governance by addressing
threats against customer endpoints (e.g., phishing, fake links, sharing  credentials, SIM-swap fraud,
using unsafe applications, etc.), which frequently undermine even strong institutional controls. As
such, customer digital awareness acts as a behavioral control domain that complements both
technical and organizational controls. The risk-reducing effects of cybersecurity governance should

Third-party risk management is closely related  to operational resilience. Banks rely on various third
parties, including cloud providers, fintech firms, software vendors, cybersecurity service providers,
payment processors, and data infrastructure suppliers. The bank could experience significant
operational and regulatory issues if one of those third parties fails. Due diligence, audit rights,
monitoring, service-level controls, incident reporting, and exit planning can all serve to reduce the
fragility associated with dependency on those third parties. Therefore, third-party risk management
should strengthen the operational resilience of a bank in digital banking (Saveljeva & Volkova, 2026;
Modesti et al., 2025; Buttigieg & Zimmermann, 2024).
Incident response capabilities represent the operational mechanism of resilience. The ability to
detect, escalate, contain, communicate during, and recover from events after an incident occurs will
ultimately determine whether a cyber event remains limited in scope or develops into a prolonged
disruption. Incident response is also important in enhancing trust because the speed and
transparency of the response to an incident are evaluated by customers and regulators alongside the
fact of an incident occurring. Thus, effective incident response should strengthen the operational
resilience of a bank in digital banking (Aisyah et al., 2025; Aldasoro et al., 2022; Buttigieg &
Zimmermann, 2024).
Operational resilience reduces the potential impact of cyber and technological disruptions. While
downtime resulting from a cyber or technological disruption may not result in a loss of revenue during
the initial period, if critical services are unable to be maintained or restored promptly, there is an
increased likelihood that any outage will evolve into a major fai lure for customers, compliance
purposes, or reputational reasons. In this way, operational resilience impacts the severity of risk
rather than simply its probability. Therefore, operational resilience should reduce the impact of cyber
incidents on digital banking risk (Aldasoro et al., 2022; Buttigieg & Zimmermann, 2024; Bruno et al.,
2025).
Cybersecurity governance produces risk reductions through two mechanisms that complement each
other. First, the belief-based mechanism represented by digital trust – customers who believe their
digital banking services are secure and reliable are less likely to perceive vulnerability in spite of
uncertainty. Second, the continuity-based mechanism represented by operational resilience – when
services continue to operate  or are restored quickly during incidents, then regardless of the
occurrence of an incident, the bank continues to maintain availability. Although these mechanisms
are interrelated, they are distinct; e.g., while customers may trust a bank’s services (digi tal trust),
that same bank may have operational fragilities (operational resilience). Therefore, digital trust and
operational resilience should jointly mediate the relationship between cybersecurity governance and
digital banking risk reduction. Digital t rust, reflected in perceived safety, reliability, and privacy,
represents a central mechanism through which cybersecurity governance may reduce digital banking
risk (Mayer et al., 1995; McKnight et al., 2002; Yousafzai et al., 2009; Luo et al., 2010; Buttigieg &
Zimmermann, 2024).

4. Jordanian Context, Contributions, and Implications
The Jordanian banking sector presents an appropriate context for examining this theoretical
framework due to the growth of digital ban king and digital payment services beyond traditional
electronic banking (e -banking) (Central Bank of Jordan, 2021; Jordan Payments and Clearing
Company, 2025).
The Central Bank of Jordan’s cybersecurity framework for financial institutions establishes cybe r-
resilience and risk-based controls as key elements in promoting cybersecurity at regulated financial
institutions. This position of the Central Bank of Jordan supports the central argument of the  paper;
namely, that cybersecurity is a governance issue, r ather than an IT issue (Central Bank of Jordan,
2021).
Additionally, national payments system initiatives reflect greater dependence upon digital payment
channels and financial technology infrastructure. Such increased dependency heightens the
significance of maintaining operational continuity, protecting customers, preventing fraud, and
communicating about incidents. As customers increasingly rely upon digital banking services and
transactions occur at higher volumes, the negative impacts resulting from a bank service outage, loss
of sensitive data via hacking or phishing, a third-party interruption of digital banking operations, or a
failure of payment processing platforms will become more severe (Jordan Payments and Clearing
Company, 2025; Aldasoro et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024).
Jordan serves as an illustrative context of why an ecosystem view of the digital banking service is
needed. A typical digital banking service may involve connecting banks, customers, mobile operators,
payment infrastructure, digital wallets, fintech suppliers, application programming interfaces (APIs),
cloud providers, and software suppliers. Therefore, risk mitigation cannot solely focus on enhancing
one particular banking channel. Instead, it necessitates governance of relationships and interactions
among these entities, including third parties, data flows, customer behavior, and recovery
capabilities (Gomber et al., 2017; Modesti et al., 2025; Saveljeva & Volkova, 2026).

Although the conceptual argument does not require treating Jordan as a singular example of all digital
banking ecosystems (i.e., an isolated instance), Jordan offers an opportunity to study digitally enabled
banking ecosystems within a developing country. Developing countries often rapidly embrace new
digital technologies (in this case digital banking services) while simultaneously confronting challenges
associated with customer knowledge about cybersecurity risks (customer digital awareness);
cybersecurity capacities of banks; incre asing technological dependency; and evolving regulatory
frameworks. The proposed model can facilitate empirical research on digital banking services in
Jordan and comparative research on other similarly structured banking ecosystems (Ozili, 2018;
Gomber et al., 2017; Al Doghan & Mirzaliev, 2024).
An initial contribution to prior literature lies in its conceptualization. Previous e -banking risk studies
primarily addressed e -banking risk related to individual banking channels (such as ATMs, internet
banking, credit/debit cards, and SMS banking). The current paper frames digital banking risk as a
systemic/governance problem. The reason this framing matters is that today's digital banking risk is
created through interdependencies among customers, banks, fintech companies providing APIs,
payment infrastructure, cloud services, and cyber threat actors. Consequently, the suggested model
extends the scope of analysis from the banking channel to the digital banking ecosystem (Gomber et
al., 2017; Ozili, 2018; Wang et al., 2024; Modesti et al., 2025).
A second contribution to prior literature lies in integrating cybersecurity governance into digital trust.
Typically, past research focusing on trust has relied on understanding customer perceptions, while
past research on  cybersecurity typically addresses controls and threats. The suggested model
integrates the two domains by positing that governance quality constitutes one foundation of digital
trust. Customers do not need to comprehend each control used technically; however, they do expect
to see signs of competence, reliability, transparency, and accountability (Mayer et al., 1995; McKnight
et al., 2002; Bajwa et al., 2023; Jafri et al., 2024).
A third contribution lies in connecting API security and third -party risk management to operational
resilience. Modern digital banking relies heavily on external providers and digital service chains.
Third-party risks are no longer merely procurement issues – they are resilience issues. Poor oversight
of third -party providers can magnify outage timeframes, delay recovery efforts, and impede
accountability during incidents. Thus, the proposed model views third -party risk management as a
prerequisite for achieving resilience (Modesti et al., 202 5; Saveljeva & Volkova, 2026; Buttigieg &
Zimmermann, 2024).
Fourth, this paper incorporates customer digital awareness as part of the control environment for
digital banking risk reduction. Traditionally, customers were viewed as users or adopters in vari ous
models. However, in digital banking risk governance, customers are also endpoints in the security
architecture. Additionally, their level of awareness regarding digital banking services influences their
susceptibility to phishing attacks, credential pr otection measures, transaction verification
mechanisms, and response to suspicious activity. The suggested model thus includes customer digital
awareness as an element of the framework for reducing digital banking risks - not outside it
(Alrababah et al., 2024; Al Doghan & Mirzaliev, 2024; Bajwa et al., 2023).

Lastly, the paper identifies a dual -mechanism logic underlying digital banking risk reduction.
Specifically, it differentiates between trust-based risk reduction and resilience-based risk reduction.
Trust-based risk reduction decreases perceived vulnerability and promotes continued use. Resilience-

digital banking risk reduction as a singular objective and highlights why banks need both confidence-

4.1 Accounting, Internal Control, and Assurance Implications
From an accounting perspective, the framework implies that audit committees,  internal auditors, IT
auditors, and risk committees should treat cybersecurity governance as part of internal control over
digital banking processes. API security relates to application controls, data integrity, authorization,
and transaction completeness; third -party risk management relates to outsourced service -provider
assurance and vendor -control monitoring; incident response capability r elates to exception
reporting, escalation, evidence preservation, and post -incident accountability; and operational
resilience relates to continuity of transaction processing and service reliability. Therefore, the
framework can guide assurance planning by  identifying control domains that should be subject to
walkthroughs, control testing, monitoring reports, and board-level reporting. This does not turn the
study into a financial accounting paper; rather, it provides an accounting information systems lens
for examining how digital banking risks can affect control effectiveness, auditability, accountability,
and operational risk reporting (DeLone & McLean, 2003; Masli et al., 2010).
Therefore, bank boards should view cybersecurity governance as a strategic r isk responsibility and
include reporting requirements covering cyber -risk appetite; incident trends; third -party
dependencies; API exposure; effectiveness of controls; resilience testing results, and indicators of
customer impact due to cyberattacks or vul nerabilities (Wang et al., 2024; Bruno et al., 2025;
Aldasoro et al., 2022).
Digital banking and risk management functions should develop digital banking risk maps covering
internal systems; customer interfaces; third -party services; APIs connecting system s; payment
platforms; cloud providers; fraud analytics functionality; data -sharing arrangements, etc. Internal
audit should evaluate whether governance responsibilities exist; escalation pathways are defined;
testing routines are established; and learning mechanisms post -incident are implemented and
effective (DeLone & McLean, 2003; Masli et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2024).
Digital banking and technical departments should implement multi -factor authentication; secure
development practices; testing for APIs us ed by banks; encryption methods for protecting sensitive
information; transaction monitoring for detecting anomalies; endpoint controls for preventing
malware installation; identity management solutions for ensuring authorized access only; regular
vulnerability scanning activities to identify potential entry points for attackers; and secure
communication channels for interacting with customers using mobile devices/applications (Wang et
al., 2024; Modesti et al., 2025; Ojehomon et al., 2026).

The paper proposes some causal explanations for why banks may achieve stronger cybersecurity
governance and higher digital trust in digital banking services. These ideas need to be tested
empirically at some point in order to support formal "causal" statements about how effective digital
banking practice really is. The paper also limits itself to a general description of the legal and
regulatory environment for digital banking and the financial sector in Jordan; it does not include any
detailed analysis of specific financial institutions' own primary data, customer surveys, incident
reports, etc., in order to focus its discussion more abstractly on what such a general framework would
look like. Therefore, the reader should consider the paper's proposed conceptual framework for
building theories about how banks might best manage the risks associated with cybersecurity as a
"theory-building" contribution to the literature, and as an initial starting -point for subsequent
empirical research, rather than as direct evidence that each of the proposed relationships  has been
shown to exist among Jordanian banks.
A second limitation of the paper is that it relies primarily upon secondary published sources in order
to describe the relevant laws, regulations, and standards applicable to the provision of digital banking
and payment services in Jordan. While relying upon published journal articles enhances the paper's
scholarly credibility and objectivity, it restricts the paper's ability to provide deeper insight into issues
related to compliance, regulation, and/or standardization in the banking and payments sectors in
Jordan. Future research could help to fill this gap by conducting interviews with relevant parties,
including government agencies responsible for regulating the financial sector, banks, payment
processors, and other participants in the financial system who are subject to regulation.
Third, the paper takes a deliberately high-level view of how banks manage their cybersecurity risk. In
particular, it concentrates on issues of governance, trust, and resilience (i.e. absorbing, responding
to, and recovering from disruption), and excludes technical details ab out how banks actually
implement cybersecurity controls and protocols using cryptography, secure authentication, network
controls, and related technical mechanisms. The author's decision to focus on these higher -level
concepts was based on the audience int ended for the paper - namely those interested in banking,
risk, information systems, and corporate governance. However, there is nothing to prevent future
researchers from writing a follow-up paper which combines this governance-oriented approach with
a more technically focused evaluation of the effectiveness of various types of security controls in
protecting against different types of cyberattacks.
