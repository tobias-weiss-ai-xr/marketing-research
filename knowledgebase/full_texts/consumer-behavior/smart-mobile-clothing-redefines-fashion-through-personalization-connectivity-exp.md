Abstract
The smart apparel industry in North America has expanded notably in recent years
and this trend is particularly due to increasing interest in wearable technology. These
garments are a combination of connectivity, personalization, as well as enhanced
functionality aimed at improving performance-related and experiential dimensions,
self-expression, mobility, safety, and everyday usability. Due to the pandemic, an
accelerated adoption of wearables in medical and safety applications has been
observed which has reshaped the connected apparel market. This study explores
the attitudes of the consumers toward smart and customized sportswear, focusing
on the gap between technological innovation and real-world demand. A survey
of 194 respondents was conducted using a mixed-methods research design. The
results revealed that there is strong consumer interest in advanced features such as
biometric tracking, thermal regulation, and effortless device integration. However,
concerns about data security, high costs, and product durability were found to
be the main barriers for the adoption. These findings indicate that technological
potential alone is insufficient to ensure widespread market acceptance. Although
early adopters have embraced these innovations, skepticism about their long-term
viability has grown. Furthermore, results showed that consumers have a preference
toward a tailored approach and value mobility, convenience, and ease of use
over mass-market solutions. They usually search for lifestyle elevation as well as
strong purpose-led products. This research shows the significance of mobility and
functionality in leading adoption.  By innovating with user expectations in mind,
smart clothing brands may help reshape parts of the industry and contribute to an
evolving relationship between fashion and trust-based personalization.

1. Introduction
The emergence of smart clothing is a significant development that reflects a convergence
of advances in technology and needs of the society. Beyond technological progress,
this emergence also raises critical questions related to adoption, usability, and value
creation for end users. In this study, the term “smart clothing” refers specifically to

textile-based garments integrating embedded sensors,
connectivity, and data-processing capabilities, and is used
interchangeably with “smart apparel” when referring to
consumer-facing wearables products in the sportswear
and lifestyle segments. The trend can be traced back to the
1980s. Early innovations, particularly in aerospace, played
an influential role in this evolution. Thermoregulating
textiles for spacesuits, as developed by NASA is an example
of the way functional fabrics were initially designed for the
purpose of specific environmental challenges. This laid the
groundwork for further innovations in the field of smart
textiles.1 These initial applications showed that textiles
have the potential to interact with their environment.
This concept has now been applied to several areas,
including healthcare and personal wellness. In the early
2000s, projects such as the Smart Shirt developed by
the Georgia Institute of Technology revolutionized the
integration of electronics with textiles. In this pioneering
work, biometric sensors were incorporated into fabrics.
This integration enabled real-time health monitoring and
demonstrated that the textiles have the potential to serve
as platforms for wearable technology.2 With advancements
in materials science, the integration of electronics into
textiles has further evolved. This has enabled the creation
of breathable, flexible, and stretchable fabrics that are
essential for wearable applications.3,4
This interdisciplinary collaboration between fields
such as medicine, engineering, and computer science
has been important for the transformation of clothing
into connected systems that not only improve the quality
of life while also tackling the challenges of mobility and
wellness.3−4 Today, smart clothing has the ability to provide
personalized experiences and meet varied user needs.
The synergy between advanced textile technologies and
electronic integration has resulted in garments that not
only perform traditional functions but also monitor
physiological parameters, in addition to adapting to
environmental changes.5,6
However, despite these technological advances, large-
scale consumer adoption remains limited and uneven
across application domains. This evolution shows a trend
in design thinking which is quite broad, where co-creation
and collaboration between end-users, designers, engineers
as well as healthcare professionals are increasingly
recognized as essential for the development of innovative
textiles that address growing needs of societies. 7 As the
industry is evolving, the focus on sustainability and end-
of-life solutions for electronics-based smart textiles will
become increasingly important. It needs to be ensured that
these innovations make a positive contribution to the user
experience as well as to the environment. 8 The potential
of smart clothing is particularly evident in areas such as
healthcare, workplace safety, marketing, and fashion.
Over a couple of decades, advances in mobile computing
and the miniaturization of electronic components have
made these innovations a tangible reality. Smart textiles,
particularly the ones that have thermally conductive
fabrics, are excellent examples of the functional capabilities
of clothing. These fabrics can respond to environmental
stimuli, such as temperature changes. This way, they can
enhance comfort and functionality of users.
Research has shown that textiles embedded with phase
change materials (PCMs) can regulate temperature by
absorbing, storing, and releasing thermal energy. This
functionality is particularly relevant in domains such as
healthcare and outdoor apparel, where thermal comfort
and physiological regulation are critical.9 The collaboration
among several stakeholders, such as researchers, end-
users, designers, and manufacturers, has been essential
for the creation of smart textiles that are lightweight and
comfortable. For example, the development of e-textiles
which have sensors and actuators can monitor vital signs,
detect falls, or provide feedback for rehabilitation.10 However,
despite excellent potential of smart textiles, the smart
clothing industry has a multitude of challenges. Complex
questions about manufacturing processes, sustainability,
and recyclability arise when advanced technologies are
integrated into textiles. In addition, expectations of
the consumer related to sustainable functionality, and
personalization are putting a lot of pressure on industry
processes for adaptation and innovation. Addressing these
challenges is important for ensuring that there is long-term
affordability and sustainability of smart clothing.
Furthermore, mobility solutions are currently a
particularly promising area of application. For cyclists, users
of alternative modes of transport such as skateboards as
well as scooters, and outdoor sports enthusiasts, intelligent
clothing provides the opportunity to combine safety, self-
expression, and comfort. Not to mention advanced features
such as reflective devices, motion sensors, or textiles with
heating or even cooling capabilities. The same goes for
clothing adapted for people with disabilities, which could
revolutionize the concept of inclusion in fashion.
The economic potential of smart clothing is expanding
steadily. Industry forecasts estimate that the global
smart clothing market was valued at approximately USD
2.47 billion in 2026 and is projected to reach USD 9.97
billion by 2034, representing a compound annual growth
rate (CAGR) of about 15.78%. 11 However, compared
to the broader wearable technology market 12, smart
clothing remains a niche segment, reflecting persistent
technological, manufacturing, and adoption barriers. This

cutting-edge technologies behind the development of
smart garments and their practical uses; (2) manufacturing,
personalization, and sustainability, examining the
challenges and opportunities which are associated with
production processes, customization strategies, and eco-
friendly practices; and (3) implications for users and
market adoption, namely as an assessment of the impact of
these advances on end users, particularly in areas such as
mobility, physical activity and adaptive fashion.
2. Literature review
The field of smart clothing has been the subject of extensive
academic and industrial research over the past two
decades. Early studies mainly focused on the integration
of sensors into fabrics. It paved the way for advances in
health monitoring, athletic performance enhancement,
and adaptive fashion. This section discusses the existing
literature and identifies research gaps in the domain of
smart clothing.
2.1. Evolution of smart clothing technologies
Research on smart textiles initially revolved around the
development of electronic textiles (e-textiles). These were
capable of embedding conductive fibers in traditional
fabric structures. Post et al .13 introduced the concept of
wearable computing by integrating microprocessors into
clothing. This highlighted the potential for real-time data
collection and user interaction. This foundational work
laid the groundwork for contemporary innovations in
sensor-embedded garments, including biometric tracking,
environmental adaptability, and connectivity features.
The history of smart clothing can be traced back to the
1990s due to significant technological advancements. The
Georgia Institute of Technology’s “Smart Shirt, ” developed
by Jayaraman and Park, 14 was a pioneering innovation. It
integrated biometric sensors which were capable of real-
time physiological monitoring. These early experiments
laid the foundation for modern smart textiles. They
demonstrated the potential applications of smart textiles
in healthcare, military, and consumer fashion. Advances
in nanotechnology and flexible electronics have since
enabled the miniaturization of components. This flexibility
has enhanced the durability, comfort, and functionality of
smart garments.15
Recent research by Fernández-Caramés and Fraga-
Lamas16 highlights how wearable technology is evolving
into a more integrated ecosystem, connecting smart
clothing with IoT-enabled devices. Companies like
Hexoskin and Myant have pioneered health-monitoring
garments, reinforcing the link between biometrics and
predictive healthcare solutions.
In parallel, the rapid adoption of non-textile wearables
such as smartwatches and fitness bands has shaped
consumer expectations regarding real-time data access,
usability, and device interoperability. However, unlike
wrist-worn devices, smart clothing offers continuous body
coverage, improved sensor stability, and closer proximity
to physiological signals, enabling more seamless and
embodied data collection.
This distinction positions smart clothing not as a
replacement for existing wearables, but as a complementary
and potentially more immersive interface for health,
performance, and mobility-related applications.
Research has shown that a purpose-driven approach
leading to emotional connection can have a massive
impact in shaping customer engagement and behavioral
outcomes. Recent empirical research demonstrates that
emotionally engaged customers exhibit higher repurchase
intentions, greater advocacy behaviors, and increased long-
term relationship value for firms .17 Emotional connection
therefore represents a strategic driver of customer lifetime
value and customer retention for retailers. Prior research
has shown that customer experiences across digital and
physical touchpoints influence customer engagement
and behavioral outcomes.18 According to Y ouGov, 65% of
Generation Z consumers report that they prefer brands

2.2. Applications in health and well-being
Smart clothing has been extensively studied in healthcare
applications. Medical research has focused on wearable
textiles that monitor vital signs, detect early symptoms of
diseases, and provide biofeedback for patients with chronic
conditions. Studies by Gao et al.20 discuss the integration
of sweat sensors in smart garments, allowing non-invasive
tracking of hydration levels, electrolyte balance, and stress
biomarkers.
Textiles capable of delivering medication transdermally
have been developed21, offering a potential breakthrough in
chronic disease management. Adaptive garments equipped
with embedded electrodes can assist in neuromuscular
rehabilitation, improving mobility for patients recovering
from injuries. Additionally, smart clothing designed
for elder care can monitor falls, detect cardiovascular
irregularities, and improve the overall quality of life for
aging populations. 22 These applications illustrate the
clinical and therapeutic potential of smart clothing, while
also highlighting the importance of reliability, comfort,
and long-term usability in real-world contexts.
2.3. Consumer adoption and market trends
While technological feasibility has been a focus,
consumer behavior remains an important determinant of
commercial success of smart clothing. 23 Studies by Ju and
Lee24 highlight skepticism surrounding data security and
privacy as major barriers to adoption. Consumers express
concerns over how biometric data is stored and used.
They emphasize the need for transparent data governance
policies. The demand for personalization is a growing
trend.25 Brands such as Sensoria and Myant, for instance,
have developed garments that adapt dynamically to users’
body morphology and movement patterns.
Athletes and fitness enthusiasts increasingly favor
smart garments which are capable of providing real-time
biomechanical feedback, optimizing training regimens
and reducing injury risks. Additionally, AI-based analytics
enhance predictive insights which allow users to tailor
their activities for optimal results. These trends reported in
both academic and industry-oriented studies highlight the
growing importance of personalization and data-driven
feedback in shaping consumer expectations toward smart
clothing.
2.4. Sustainability and manufacturing challenges
Despite its promising applications, smart clothing presents
sustainability challenges. Electronic waste is a growing
concern, as traditional textiles are not designed to
accommodate embedded circuits and sensors. Research by
Fobelets et al.26 calls for eco-friendly alternatives, including
biodegradable conductive materials and modular
electronic components that facilitate recycling. Prahl 27
discussed how cradle-to-cradle design principles may
help reduce waste and extend the usability of functional
garments. Recent research highlights that smart textiles
introduce complex sustainability issues related to material
selection, manufacturing processes, and end-of-life
management, requiring eco-design and circular design
strategies, including modular architectures, to mitigate
environmental impacts.28
Beyond material selection, sustainability challenges
also extend to the full lifecycle of smart clothing,
including manufacturing, use, maintenance, and end-
of-life management. Unlike conventional apparel, smart
garments integrate electronic components that complicate
disassembly, repair, and recycling processes. Lifecycle-
oriented approaches therefore emphasize modular
architectures, where electronic elements can be removed,
upgraded, or replaced independently of the textile
substrate, reducing premature obsolescence and waste
generation.29
Additionally, the development of energy-harvesting
textiles capable of generating power from movement or
ambient heat could revolutionize the sector, eliminating
dependence on external batteries.30 Such solutions not only
enhance user autonomy but also contribute to sustainability
objectives by reducing battery-related waste and extending
product lifespan.
2.5. Research gaps and positioning of this study
Although there is a lot of recent progress in the field of
smart clothing research, there are still several critical
gaps which exist. First, there is an urgent need for more
studies which combine textile engineering, data science,
and human-computer interaction. This could help to
improve predictive analytics and adaptability in wearable
technology. Second, not much has been done to address
the ethical concerns of consumers with respect to their
privacy and security. Several in-depth studies are required
to further investigate existing regulatory frameworks and
consumer protection laws along with the discussion of

Although the broader wearable technology market

Addressing these challenges requires a
multidimensional approach that focuses on improved
usability, affordability models, and specific marketing
efforts. Most importantly, a thorough understanding of
consumer behavior, preferences, and expectations is highly
essential because consumers are the end users and are the
primary stakeholders in the adoption of smart clothing.
Strategies for usability and affordability must be based on
consumer insights. This will ensure that innovations are
aligned with their needs, concerns, and lifestyles.
In response to these gaps, this study adopts a
consumer-centered empirical approach to examine
adoption drivers, perceived barriers, and expectations
related to personalization, connectivity, and usability in
smart clothing. By combining survey data with expert
insights, the study contributes to the literature by linking
technological innovation to real-world consumer demand
and market readiness.

consumer behavior to inform the development of more
user-centric and sustainable smart clothing solutions.
The existing literature shows that there is transformative
potential of smart clothing in multiple domains. However,
further research is necessary to align technological
advancements with user expectations and hence the need
for this particular study.
2.6. Research hypotheses
Based on consumer behavior trends and gaps from existing
literature, the following hypotheses were formulated to
examine preferences for smart clothing:
– Consumers prioritize comfort, fit, and ease of use
when considering the adoption of smart apparel over
purely technological features.
– Y ounger adult consumers (18–35) are more likely
to adopt smart clothing, driven by familiarity with
wearable technology and digital integration. This
hypothesis allows for variation within age subgroups
depending on perceived usefulness and purchasing
power.
– Consumers value personalization in smart clothing
and are more inclined to purchase products that allow
for customization in functionality, aesthetics, and
performance enhancement.
3. Methodology
This study employed a mixed-methods research design
to explore consumer adoption and personalization
preferences related to smart clothing, with a specific focus
on active sports space. A mixed-method approach was
selected to capture both measurable behavioral patterns
and deeper qualitative insights into consumer perceptions,
motivations, and barriers to adoption. Combining
quantitative survey data with expert interviews and
secondary market analysis allowed for triangulation of
findings and increased the robustness of the results.
3.1. Research design and approach
The research design integrated three complementary
data sources: a consumer survey, semi-structured expert
interviews, and secondary market analysis. The quantitative
component consisted of a structured questionnaire
comprising 25 items, including categorical questions,
ordinal scales, and Likert-scale measures. These items
assessed key dimensions such as perceived technological
appeal, price sensitivity, data security concerns, and
preferences for personalization and functionality in smart
clothing.
To complement the quantitative data, semi-structured
interviews were conducted with industry experts
specializing in textile engineering, wearable technology
development, and sportswear retail. These interviews
provided contextual insights into technological constraints,
production challenges, and emerging market trends. In
addition, secondary data from industry reports, market
research publications, and case studies were analyzed to
situate the empirical findings within broader economic and
technological developments in the smart apparel sector.
Prior to full deployment, the questionnaire was pre-tested
with a pilot sample of 20 participants to ensure clarity,
relevance, and consistency of the items. Feedback from
this preliminary phase led to minor revisions in wording
and scale alignment, improving the overall reliability and
usability of the survey instrument.
3.2. Data collection and administration
Data collection was conducted online over a 20-day period
using SurveyMonkey, selected for its accessibility and
data management capabilities. Participants were recruited
through social media platforms, primarily Facebook and
LinkedIn, to reach a diverse audience with varying levels
of engagement in active sports and wearable technology.
Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18

For the qualitative component, semi-structured

of their direct involvement in the smart-apparel value

deliberately combined distinct professional profiles: a
research-and-development director at a smart-textile

center, and experts in textile engineering and sportswear
retail. All participants possessed a minimum of five years
of professional experience in smart textiles, wearable
technology, or sportswear innovation. Each interview
lasted approximately 30–45 min and was conducted online
via videoconference during the same general period as
the survey data collection. With participant consent,
interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim
for analysis. The interview guide addressed adoption
barriers, technological feasibility, production and cost
constraints, sustainability considerations, and future
market trajectories.
3.3. Ethical considerations
This study was conducted in accordance with established
ethical research guidelines. Informed consent was
obtained digitally from all participants prior to data
collection. Participation was voluntary, and respondents
were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. All
survey responses were anonymized, and no personally
identifiable information was collected. Interview data were
treated confidentially and reported in aggregated form to
ensure participant privacy.
3.4. Data analysis
Quantitative data analysis was performed using SPSS
software. Descriptive statistics were first used to
summarize consumer characteristics and overall response
patterns. Chi-square (χ²) tests were conducted to examine
relationships between categorical variables, such as age
group and level of interest in smart clothing adoption.
ANOV A was employed to compare mean differences in
interest levels and willingness to pay across demographic
segments. Where significant effects were identified, post-
hoc comparisons were conducted to determine group-level
differences. A standard multiple linear regression, with all
five predictors entered simultaneously, was used to identify
the key predictors of interest in smart clothing. The
dependent variable was the respondent’s level of interest
in adopting smart clothing, measured on a 7-point Likert
scale. The predictors were operationalized as follows: age
(treated as an ordinal variable across the five age bands),
income band, prior familiarity with wearable technology,
preference for personalization, and level of data-security
concern. Prior to estimation, the standard assumptions
of linear regression were verified, including linearity,
normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, and the absence
of multicollinearity (assessed via variance inflation factors).
In addition, cluster analysis was applied to segment
respondents based on shared preferences, attitudes,
and adoption intentions. A k-means cluster analysis
was performed on five standardized variables central to
adoption: interest in smart clothing, data privacy concern,
preference for personalization, willingness to pay, and
prior familiarity with wearable technology. A three-

which were reviewed and refined against the full dataset.
Coding was carried out manually by the research team
through iterative comparison and discussion of emerging
themes. The analysis yielded four overarching themes
(adoption barriers, usability expectations, data privacy
concerns, and sustainability challenges) which were used
to contextualize and enrich the quantitative findings.
4. Results
This section presents the results of the consumer
survey and provides insights into purchasing behaviors,
preferences for smart textile features, concerns related
to connectivity and data privacy, and segmentation
patterns underlying distinct user profiles. The results are
organized to empirically test the three research hypotheses
formulated in section 2.6 using descriptive statistics,
inferential analyses, and segmentation techniques. The
results are structured to address comfort and usability
(H1), demographic differences in adoption (H2), and the
role of personalization in consumer preference (H3).
4.1. A gradual but promising adoption
The emergence of smart apparel reveals that there is both
curiosity and caution. While most respondents (96.39%)
already own active sportswear, purchasing habits are
still based on tradition. They favor specialized physical
stores over digital platforms. This inclination shows
the irreplaceable nature of the tactile experience that
consumers seek to feel the texture, assess the elasticity,
and ensure the perfect fit. These are the elements that
e-commerce has yet to fully replicate. When choosing
athletic apparel, Figure 1 shows that the priorities are clear:
comfort comes first (78.87%), followed by fit (53.37%)
and brand reputation (30.85%). The relatively lower
emphasis on brand reputation suggests that functional
attributes currently outweigh symbolic or brand-related
considerations in purchasing decisions for smart clothing.
Despite the convenience of online shopping, several
barriers continue to limit adoption. Uncertainty about
size and fit (72%), coupled with the inability to physically
interact with the product (68%), remains a significant
barrier. These findings indicate structural limitations in
current digital retail environments for smart apparel.
Technologies such as 3D fit simulators and augmented
reality interfaces could be employed to transform digital
storefronts into immersive and trusted domains.

highly sought after ( Figure 2 ). This shows a desire for
garments that seamlessly adapt to the needs of the body.
In contrast, features such as compression (43.23%) and
aerodynamics (42.48%) garner less enthusiasm. These

4.4. Statistical analysis of key variables
Quantitative analyses revealed the demographic dynamics
which impact the adoption of smart clothing. A chi-square
test of independence revealed a statistically significant
association between age group and high interest in smart
clothing (a score of 5 or higher on the 7-point scale), χ²(4, N
= 194) = 21.87, p < 0.001. The 25–35 age group showed the
highest proportion of elevated interest (51.4%), compared
with 16.7% among 18–24-year-olds and only 11.1% among
respondents aged 56 and over. Consistent with this, mean
interest was highest in the 25–35 group (M = 4.54) and
lowest among the youngest (M = 3.42) and oldest (M =
3.33) respondents (overall M = 3.98 on the 7-point scale).
This points to pronounced generational differences in
perception and readiness. Economic considerations also
influence adoption patterns.
A one-way ANOV A (F(4, 189) = 6.82, p < 0.001), with
Levene’s test confirming homogeneity of variances ( p =
0.227), revealed significant differences in willingness to
pay across age groups. Post-hoc Tukey tests indicated that
respondents aged 25–35 exhibited the highest willingness
to pay (mean = $145.35, SD = $51.21). It is significantly
higher than that of younger respondents aged 18–24 (mean
= $109.67, SD = $45.40), p < 0.001. These findings reveal
the importance of targeted pricing strategies that appeal to
value-conscious as well as premium-seeking consumers.
As illustrated in Figure 4, interest in smart clothing follows
a clear age gradient, peaking in the 25–35 group (M = 4.54)
and declining among both younger (18–24) and older
respondents. Considered together with the willingness-
to-pay results reported above, these findings provide
only partial support for Hypothesis H2: although age
significantly influences both interest in and willingness to
pay for smart clothing, the effect is not uniform across the
broad 18–35 range posited in the hypothesis. Engagement
is concentrated in the 25–35 cohort, whereas 18–24
respondents were comparatively less favorable. The 25–35
cohort therefore represents the most strategically relevant
segment for market engagement, while the comparatively
lower engagement among the youngest consumers
warrants further investigation.

4.6. The convergence of connectivity, expression,
personalization, and performance
Smart clothing is characterized by the integration of digital
technologies within textile structures, resulting in garments
that combine connectivity, personalization, and functional
performance. Survey results indicate that respondents do
not perceive these dimensions as isolated features, but
rather as interconnected attributes contributing to overall
garment value. Smart textiles embed sensors capable of
monitoring physiological data, while adaptive fabrics
respond to environmental conditions such as temperature
and movement. These combined functionalities were
consistently associated with enhanced usability and
perceived relevance across usage contexts.
Beyond functional performance, respondents reported
that smart clothing supports forms of self-expression
linked to lifestyle, activity level, and personal comfort
preferences. Customizable design elements and adaptive
features were frequently cited as enabling garments to
better align with individual needs, particularly in relation
to mobility, training intensity, and everyday wear. In
addition, sustainability-related attributes, such as durable
materials and responsible design, were mentioned as
reinforcing perceived product value.
Qualitative insights from expert interviews further
illustrate this convergence. According to an expert from a
technology transfer center,
“The future of smart clothing will soon be based on
the fusion of the physical and digital worlds. A major
transformation is underway! Thanks to augmented
reality and connected textiles, clothing will no longer
be passive objects but true interactive interfaces
that enhance the user experience. What we know
today will soon be obsolete. We are witnessing a
convergence that paves the way for a more immersive
fashion experience. ”
Taken together, these findings indicate that consumers
associate smart clothing with a combination of connectivity,
personalization, and performance-related benefits,
rather than with isolated technological functions. This
convergence emerged as a recurring pattern across survey
responses and expert perspectives, providing an empirical
foundation for the integrative framework discussed in the
following section.
5. Discussion
The results of this study highlight a fundamental paradox.
While consumers clearly acknowledge the strong potential
of smart clothing, concerns about usability, cost, and
privacy are the main factors behind non-adoption. These
observations directly reflect the empirical results presented
in section 4 and are discussed here in relation to hypotheses
H1–H3. Adoption therefore appears contingent on the
ability of brands to establish trust and support self-
expression. Brands with strong brand legitimacy and
established emotional connections with consumers—
particularly within the sportswear and technology
ecosystems—may benefit from a competitive advantage in
attracting early adopters. However, overcoming adoption
barriers requires a gradual and highly targeted approach
based on consumer education, affordable innovation,
and transparency, as suggested by findings related to
price sensitivity, usability expectations, and data privacy
concerns.

5.1. The gap between intention and adoption
There was found to be a significant gap between expressed
interest of consumers in smart clothing and its actual
adoption. This gap is consistent with the empirical results
presented in section 4, which highlight strong interest in
functionality and personalization alongside persistent
concerns related to price and trust. Although social image
and aesthetics are important and appealing reasons,
economic and practical considerations continue to prevent
adoption among masses. Consumers perceive the cost
ranging from $150 to $300 as prohibitive, compared to
their acceptable price range of $50 to $99. These findings
partially explain why hypotheses related to comfort and
personalization (H1 and H3) are positively perceived, yet
not fully translated into purchase behavior. This price gap
could be streamlined with the help of adapted business
models, such as rental systems, modular functionality,
subscription plans, or tiered offerings. These strategies are
presented as exploratory managerial implications rather
than empirically tested solutions. This could facilitate
broader market penetration. With respect to privacy
concerns, transparent communication and clear regulatory
frameworks are quite important to reassure consumers that
there would be strong security and ethical handling of their
data, a factor that emerged as a key barrier to adoption in
the results related to data protection and consumer trust.
5.2. The challenge of functionality and comfort
Our findings are consistent with previous research,
emphasizing that ease of use takes precedence over a lot
of technological sophistication. This observation directly
supports hypothesis H1, which posits that comfort and
usability are primary drivers of consumer interest in
smart clothing. Smart textiles must not just function as
technological gimmicks; rather, they should establish
themselves as practical tools that enhance daily life without
there being a steep learning curve. Gesture control, haptic
feedback, and voice interfaces could be employed as critical
breakthroughs for the purpose of adoption. These elements
are discussed as prospective design implications informed
by expert interviews and existing literature, rather than as
empirically validated adoption drivers measured in the
present study.
In addition, portability and comfort were found to
be at the forefront of consumer expectations. Lighter
electronic components, breathable materials, and
optimized flexibility are the main levers for convincing
users. Advances in miniaturization are an important way
to combine technological sophistication with portability
without compromising aesthetics, thereby addressing both
functional and experiential expectations identified among
respondents.
History shows that wearable innovation follows a
recurring pattern: initial skepticism leading to gradual
mainstream adoption. Seatbelts which were once
considered superfluous are now an important safety
standard. Smart clothing could follow a similar trajectory,
under certain conditions and over time. However, it needs
to be ensured that adoption is based on clear use cases
and measurable benefits, as suggested by the results of this
study. The current hype for artificial intelligence (AI) may
also speed up the adoption of smart clothing.
Furthermore, awareness campaigns should highlight
the tangible benefits of smart clothing, such as increased
safety for cyclists and motorcyclists, in line with the
mobility- (and safety-) related expectations identified
among respondents. It should provide evidence and
benefits of real-time physiological monitoring for
athletes or adaptation of thermal comfort to changing
environmental conditions. Initiatives such as free trials
and demonstrations which are highly interactive could be
important for overcoming initial skepticism, particularly
in the early phases of adoption.
5.3. Key levers for enabling the transition to smart
clothing
Finally, widespread adoption of smart clothing requires
more than technological advancements. It requires that
smart clothing be aligned with consumer expectations,
industry standards, and sustainability goals. These
dimensions emerge consistently from the quantitative and
qualitative results presented in  sections 4.1 to 4.5. Figure
5 shows several main levers which must be activated to
ensure a seamless and sustainable transition. Thus, success
in this evolving market relies on several fundamental
pillars, including miniaturization and aesthetics, durability
and ease of care, consumer education and awareness,
regulatory framework and privacy, interoperability
and connectivity, accessibility and affordability, and
sustainability and circular economy.
These levers directly reflect the primary adoption
barriers identified in the results, particularly those related
to comfort, cost, usability, trust, and long-term value.
The core of the diagram emphasizes “self-expression,
performance, and well-being, ” underscoring that smart
clothing should go beyond mere technology to provide
added value in terms of comfort, identity, and functionality.
Future research should quantitatively assess the full lifecycle
impacts of smart clothing, including energy consumption,
carbon footprint, and end-of-life management, in order
to better evaluate the environmental trade-offs associated
with embedded technologies.

6. Conclusion
Smart clothing represents an exciting frontier at the
intersection of fashion and technology as it combines
psychology, self-expression, lifestyle elevation, and
performance. However, despite its potential, there is
limited adoption. Drawing on the empirical findings of this
study, this limited adoption is particularly owing to pricing
constraints, privacy issues, inability for brands to build
trust, and consumer education gaps. These results confirm
that technological innovation alone is insufficient to
ensure market acceptance. By addressing these challenges
and employing transparency, shared values, and usability
improvements, the industry can accelerate adoption.
Further, the research suggests that a balance between
innovation, consumer expectations, and sustainability is
cardinal. This balance directly reflects the key drivers and
barriers identified across the quantitative and qualitative
analyses. For smart clothing to gain better mainstream
acceptance, manufacturers must focus on innovation as
well as practical consumer needs. Furthermore, durability,
comfort, and affordability should take precedence over
excessive complexity of technology, a finding that strongly
supports hypotheses H1 and H3. A phased introduction of
wearable features can help to enhance consumer confidence
and promote market expansion, particularly in early
adoption phases. Notably, targeting young demographics,
particularly those aged 25–35, would be highly effective.
This group is the most receptive and ready to engage,
especially through dynamic messaging that emphasizes the
technological advancements and performance-enhancing
benefits of smart sportswear, a result that partially supports
hypothesis H2. These findings should nevertheless be
interpreted with caution, as some age categories were
represented by relatively small subsamples, particularly
the 56-and-over group (n = 9), which limits the robustness
of generational comparisons and the generalizability of
age-specific conclusions. Further, future research should
explore industry-specific applications, such as medical
wearables and adaptive fashion, to further assess the
long-term viability of smart clothing solutions, as well as
longitudinal adoption patterns and lifecycle impacts.
