Abstract

 The development of technology in halal assurance systems has expanded the
ability to document, trace, verify, and provide information about the halal
status and processes of products. However, the existence of halal
information does not by itself explain how consumers use that information
to evaluate products. This study aims to integrate literature on halal
assurance technology with marketing management and consumer
information-processing literature to explain the us e of halal information in
product evaluation. The study employs a qualitative integrative literature
review based on structured library research. The main literature covers
research on halal technology, traceability, halal certification and information,
consumer behavior, information quality, information diagnosticity, and
product evaluation. The analysis was conducted through thematic analysis,
cross-study comparison, and integrative synthesis. The synthesis shows that
halal assurance technology has a mark eting function particularly when it
facilitates access to information that consumers can use. Information use
depends not only on availability, but also on information quality, usefulness,
relevance, credibility, and accessibility. In this process, informa tion
diagnosticity can serve as a conceptual mechanism for explaining the extent
to which information helps consumers form product judgments and evaluate
products. These findings are synthesized into the Halal Technology –
Marketing Information Framework (HT -MIF), which connects halal
assurance technology, halal information, consumer information evaluation,
information diagnosticity, product judgment, and marketing response. This
framework is conceptual and has not been empirically tested.

Keywords  Halal Technology; Halal Product Assurance ; Marketing Information ;
Information Diagnosticity; Consumer Evaluation; Product Judgment.

but also various types of information used to assess products before making
decisions. Information about ingredients, production processes, certification, product
origin, and quality assurance can form part of the consumer evaluation process. For

The development of halal assurance technology has expanded the ability of
industry players and relevant institutions to document, trace, verify, and provide
information about the halal status and processes of products. Research on online
traceability in Indonesia indicates that Muslim consumers consider the ability to
trace a product’s compliance with halal principles useful and important (Sayogo,
2018). More recent research also shows that system quality, information quality,
performance expectations, ease of use, social influence, and trust are associated with
consumer acceptance of halal traceability systems (Susanty et al., 2025).
The development of blockchain points in the same direction. Karyani et al.
(2024) discuss the use of blockchain to st ore and verify halal certificates and explore
consumer and regulator acceptance. However, these technical capabilities do not
automatically explain how available information is used in product evaluation.
This issue matters to marketing management because product information
creates value when consumers can use it to understand, compare, and assess a
product. Literature on information diagnosticity shows that consumers evaluate the
extent to which information helps them understand product quality and
performance. Filieri (2015) demonstrates a relationship between information quality,
information diagnosticity, and information adoption.
Literature on halal products also shows that halal information does not always
produce uniform responses. Maison et al. (201 8) found that the effect of halal labels
on product perceptions is limited when considered separately and becomes more
complex when interacting with country of origin and religion -based purchase
behavior. In contrast, Tarigan and Z. (2023) found a positive  effect of halal
certification on purchase intention in the context they examined.
This article brings together two streams of literature that are more often
discussed separately: literature on halal assurance technology and marketing
management literature  on consumer information evaluation. Its focus is not the
direct effect of technology on purchasing, but rather how technology facilitates
information that consumers can subsequently use.
To ensure traceability between the questions and the synthesis, the research
questions are explicitly formulated as follows: (RQ1) How is technology used in halal
assurance to generate and provide information to consumers? (RQ2) What
characteristics of technology -based halal information determine its use by
consumers? (RQ3 ) How do consumers use technology -based halal information to

LITERATURE REVIEW
1. Technology in Halal Product Assurance
Halal assurance technology includes tools and systems that support the
documentation, authentication, verification, and tracing of information about
products and production processes. Technology does not replace institutional halal
assurance processes, but supports the management and provision of evidence and
information.
Sayogo (2018) shows that online traceability can be perceived as useful
information for assessing a product’s compliance with halal principles. Vikaliana
(2024) positions tracking and tracing as important elements in maintaining halal
product integrity. Nugroho et al. (2024) discuss the urgency of technolog y adoption
for authentication and traceability across Indonesia’s halal value chain.
However, a system’s technical capabilities are not equivalent to successful
communication of information to consumers. A system may have advanced technical
capabilities ye t still produce information that is difficult to understand, difficult to
access, or irrelevant. Therefore, technology analysis needs to move beyond system
capabilities toward the use of system outputs as information.

2. Islamic Principles on Halal Information and Verification

tayyib. This principle makes halal status part of a Muslim’s consumption
considerations.
QS. Al -Hujurat [49]:6 provides the principle of tabayyun, namely the
importance of checking or verifying information before taking action based on it.
Hadith concerning halal, haram, and doubtful matters also establish the importance

3. Product Information in Marketing Management
From a marketing perspective, information is part of the consumer decision
environment. Consumers use various product cues to reduce uncertainty and form
judgments about products. Halal information can be one such cue when consumers
assess a product’s fit with their needs and preferences.
Maison et al. (2018) show that halal information does not operate in isolation.
Halal labels, country of origin, and religion -based purchase behavior can interact in
shaping product perceptions. Ibrahim et al. (2024) also show that product judgment
plays a role in the process connecting e -WOM with purchase intention for halal
products.
4. From Information Availability to Information Use
Available information is not necessarily used. Consumers need to assess
whether information is useful, relevant, credible, and sufficiently clear to support
evaluation. Sussman and Siegal (2003) identify perceived usefulness as an important
part of the information adoption process.
Filieri (2015) shows that information quality is an important antecedent of
information diagnosticity, which is subsequently associated with information
adoption. Studies of information quality on review platforms also show that conte nt
quality, expression quality, and utility quality can affect information adoption
through perceived diagnosticity and credibility (Effects of Information Quality...,
2021).
5. Information Diagnosticity and Product Judgment
Information diagnosticity refers to the extent to which information is perceived
to help consumers understand and evaluate the object being assessed. Filieri (2015)
demonstrates a relationship among information quality, diagnosticity, and
information adoption; Filieri et al. (2018) also s how the role of information relevance
and certain cues in diagnosticity over time.
In this article, information diagnosticity is not positioned as a proven mediator
in the relationship between halal assurance technology and purchase intention. The
concept is used as a conceptual mechanism to explain the stage at which halal

METHODS
This study uses a qualitative integrative literature review based on structured
library research. An integrative review was selected because it enables the
combination of empirical, conceptual, and methodological findings from several
streams of literature to build a cross -context synthesis (Whittemore & Knafl, 2005;
Torraco, 2005). T he literature review is treated as a research method, with attention
to source searching, relevance assessment, information extraction, cross -study
comparison, and argumentative synthesis (Snyder, 2019).
The search strategy was built around four keyword gr oups: (1) halal assurance,
halal certification, halal traceability, halal verification; (2) blockchain, digital
certification, digital technology, artificial intelligence, tracking and tracing; (3)
consumer information, information quality, information use fulness, information
credibility, information adoption, information diagnosticity; and (4) product
evaluation, product judgment, purchase intention, consumer response. Keyword
combinations were used flexibly according to the database and search engine, for
example: (halal AND traceability AND consumer AND information), (halal AND
certification AND blockchain AND consumer), and (information quality AND
diagnosticity AND product evaluation). The search focused on publications from
2021–2026, while foundationa l sources published before 2021 were retained when
needed to explain concepts, theories, or methodologies underlying the synthesis.
The inclusion criteria comprised: (a) peer -reviewed journal articles, academic
books or book chapters, and relevant normativ e or institutional documents; (b)
substantive discussion of halal assurance technology, halal information, traceability,
certification/verification, or consumer information evaluation processes; (c) verifiable
bibliographic metadata; and (d) findings or co ncepts that could be mapped to one of
the RQs. Sources were excluded if they discussed technology technically without a
connection to halal assurance or consumer information, lacked adequate
bibliographic traceability, were duplicates, or did not contribute to the article’s focus.
Evidence extraction recorded source identity, research context, type of
technology or assurance mechanism, form of information generated or facilitated,
consumer constructs examined, main findings, limitations, and the source’s fu nction
in relation to the RQs. Thus, sources were not treated merely as a reference list, but as
evidence units mapped from source → evidence → claim → RQ → synthesis.

Empirical sources were used to support findings about consumer behavior or
perceptions; conceptual sources explained mechanisms; and Islamic normative
sources provided a value foundation and interpretive boundaries.
The analysis was conducted in four stages. First, thematic analysis identified
themes relating to technology, information, consu mer evaluation, diagnosticity, and
marketing responses. Second, cross -study comparison examined similarities,
differences, and boundary conditions across studies. Third, integrative synthesis
connected findings on halal technology with marketing management  literature.
Fourth, conceptual framework development was used to construct the HT -MIF. The
synthesis was not used to claim new causal relationships; the relationships among
concepts in the HT-MIF are the result of conceptual integration and require empiri cal
testing in future research.
For reporting transparency, PRISMA 2020 was used as a guide for traceability
in the identification, screening, and reporting of sources. However, the article is not
described as a systematic review because it does not use me ta-analytic procedures or
claim to follow a complete systematic review protocol (Page et al., 2021). The article
uses no primary data and does not statistically test the HT -MIF. Therefore, no corpus
size or screening figures that could not be verified from  documented search
procedures are reported.

FINDINGS AND DISCUSSION
1. Halal Assurance Technology as Information Infrastructure
The synthesis indicates that technology’s function does not stop at managing internal
processes. When a system supports traceability, verification, documentation, and
information access, technology can become infrastructure connecting assurance processes
with consumers’ information needs. Sayogo (2018) shows that consumers perceive the ability
to trace halal compliance as useful. Karyani et al. (2024) also found that co nsumers expect an
easy-to-use application for checking halal products. These findings support positioning
technology as a means of facilitating access to and checking of information, rather than as a
direct determinant of product evaluation.
Technology doe s not directly determine consumer responses. It provides conditions
that allow halal assurance information to be better documented and made accessible. Its
marketing function emerges when these outputs are translated into information that can be
used in product evaluation.
2. Information Availability Is Not the Same as Information Use
Cross-literature findings highlight the importance of distinguishing information
availability from information utilization. A system may provide large amounts of data, but
consumers may not necessarily use it. Use requires the perception that information is useful,
relevant, credible, and easy to understand.
Susanty et al. (2025) show that information quality and several technology acceptance
factors are associated with the intent ion to use halal traceability systems. Information
adoption literature also links information quality with diagnosticity and credibility.
Therefore, the marketing success of technology should not be measured only by its ability to
store or display data, but also by whether the information helps meet consumers’ evaluation
needs.
3. Information Quality as a Marketing Bridge
Information quality serves as a bridge connecting technological infrastructure with
consumer evaluation. Information about certification sta tus, ingredient origin, processes, or
traceability evidence gains marketing value when it can be understood and is considered
relevant to consumers’ questions.
Filieri (2015) shows that information quality is an important antecedent of information
diagnosticity in the context of review information. This finding does not directly prove the
mechanism for halal products, but provides a conceptual basis for understanding why
technology-facilitated halal information does not automatically have equal evaluative v alue.
Other studies show that information quality can affect perceived diagnosticity and
information credibility during information adoption (Effects of Information Quality..., 2021).

4. Halal Information as an Input in Product Judgment
Halal information can function as one of the product cues used to form consumer
judgments. Maison et al. (2018) show that halal labels interact with country of origin and
religion-based purchase behavior. Ibrahim et al. (2024) show that product judgment is an
important part of the process connecting e-WOM with purchase intention for halal products.
Halal information is better understood as one input in evaluation rather than the sole
cause of a purchase decision. Tarigan and Z. (2023) found a positive effect of halal
certification on purchase intention in the context they examined, while other studies show a
more limited effect. These differences support the inclusion of boundary conditions.
5. Information Diagnosticity as a Linking Mechanism
Information diagnosticity provides a link between information and evaluation.
Information becomes diagnostic when consumers consider it capable of helping them
understand a product’s quality, suitability, or characteristics. Filieri (2015) links diagnosticity
with information adoption, while Filieri et al. (2018) demonstrate the role of information
relevance and certain cues.
In the halal context, diagnosticity helps explain why two pieces of information that
both relate to halal status may not have the same evaluative value. Existing literatu re
supports the importance of information quality, relevance, and usefulness for diagnosticity,
while verification capability and accessibility are positioned in the HT -MIF as information -
design characteristics that may conceptually support use. Accordingl y, the HT -MIF places
diagnosticity after consumer information evaluation without claiming that all these
characteristics have been tested simultaneously in halal assurance technology.
6. Boundary Conditions in the Use of Halal Information
The use of halal information does not occur under uniform conditions. The reviewed
literature indicates that consumer characteristics and context can change how information is
processed or used. Consumer knowledge, involvement, religiosity, product category,
perceived risk , and digital context are therefore positioned in the HT -MIF as candidate
boundary conditions requiring further testing, rather than as moderators already validated
specifically for all relationships in the model. Maison et al. (2018) show the role of reli gion-
based purchase behavior in responses to the combination of halal cues and country of origin,
while Ibrahim et al. (2024) demonstrate the importance of source credibility.
Maison et al. (2018) demonstrate the role of religion -based purchase behavior in
responses to the combination of halal cues and country of origin. Ibrahim et al. (2024) show
the importance of source credibility in information processing and product judgment. The
HT-MIF therefore does not assume a universal response pattern.
7. Halal Technology–Marketing Information Framework (HT-MIF)
The synthesis distinguishes two functions of technology. First, the assurance function
supports documentation, traceability, verification, and information integrity. Second, the
information function facilitates access to assurance outputs so that consumers can use them.
The second function explains how technology enters marketing management.

8. Contribution to Marketing Management
The HT-MIF shifts attention from a technology -centered view to an information -
centered view. Technology remains important, but its marketing value depends on how
technological outputs become information that consumers can access, understand, and use.
This position is supported by research on traceable food, which shows relationships between
product diagnosticity, informativeness, and trustworthiness and perceived blockchain
transparency, although that study did not focus specifically on halal products (Shukla e t al.,
2025).
This perspective helps explain why transparency does not always produce the same
response. Abundant information that is irrelevant or difficult to understand may have lower
evaluative value than information that is concise, credible, relevant, and easy to verify.
9. Managerial Implications
Companies and managers of halal assurance systems need to pay attention to
consumer-facing information, not only to the quality of internal systems. Information must
be findable and understandable to consumers.
Traceability needs to be translated into information with evaluative value. Providing a
code or digital access alone is insufficient if consumers do not know what information can be
checked.
Information quality needs to be maintained through accuracy, rel evance, credibility,
currency, and ease of access. In this article, these principles are managerial implications
derived from information quality and diagnosticity literature, rather than a set of
characteristics tested simultaneously in halal assurance sy stems. Halal assurance technology
can therefore be developed as part of a product information architecture.

10. Theoretical Implications, Limitations, and Research Agenda
The first contribution is to connect halal assurance technology literature with consumer
information processing through technology -facilitated information. The second is to
distinguish information availability from information utilization. The third is to position
information diagnosticity as a theoretical bridge. This position is a conceptual synthesis, not
a claim of empirical validation.
11. Limitations
This study is an integrative literature review and therefore does not empirically test
relationships among constructs. The HT-MIF cannot yet be treated as a validated model.
The literature differs in product, consumer, technology, and marketing contexts, so
direct generalization should be undertaken cautiously.
12. Future Research Agenda
Future research can test the pathway Information Quality → Information Diagnosticity
→ Product Judgment → Product Evaluation → Marketing Response, with hal al knowledge,
religiosity, involvement, product category, perceived risk, and digital context as candidate
boundary conditions. This pathway is a research proposition derived from the synthesis, not
an empirical relationship established in this article.
Empirical research can also compare digital certification, QR -based verification,
traceability platforms, and blockchain -based certification, while considering differences in
information quality and diagnosticity from the consumer perspective.

CONCLUSION
Halal assurance technology can function as marketing information infrastructure when
the information it facilitates is accessible to and used by consumers. The availability of halal
information does not automatically mean that the information is used. Its value is influenced
by information quality and characteristics, as well as consumer conditions and the context of
use.
Information diagnosticity provides a conceptual perspective for explaining how
information helps shape product judgment and product evalu ation. The HT -MIF connects
halal assurance technology, halal information, consumer information evaluation, information
diagnosticity, product judgment, product evaluation, and marketing response.
The HT-MIF is not a new theory and has not been empirically tested. From an Islamic
perspective, halal and tayyib, tabayyun, and clarity regarding halal, haram, and syubhat
provide a normative foundation for the importance of information that can be verified and
used responsibly. The article’s main contribution is a conceptual explanation of the
marketing function of halal assurance technology through information, rather than the direct
effect of technology on consumer decisions.
