A B S T R A C T
Electric kettles are the most popular miniature household appliances, valued both for
their convenience and effectiveness. Despite being built with safety features that will
enable automatic shutdown and boil-dry protection, they have been shown in some tests
to be frequently abused. The present paper theoretically investigates the most common
abuses of electric kettles and associated safety hazards. Such risks include overfilling,
boil-dry, warming of non -aqueous foods such as soup, milk, or thick substances, and
improper cleaning practices. All these mishandlings could increase the potential risk of
thermal burns, electric shock, fire, and appliance destruction. For systematic hazard
identification purposes, the research utilizes Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA)
and Risk Matrix methodologies to c ategorize and prioritize hazards based on severity,
frequency, and detectability. The research reveals a significant gap between consumer
behavior and producer design assumptions that lead to unnecessary accidents. The
research also evaluates current safety standards and certifications, such as IEC 60335-
2-15 and EN 50564, and national eco -design regulations and new trends, such as AI -
powered smart kettles, wireless charging systems, and fireproof and eco -friendly
materials. The findings accentuate the impo rtance of enhancing safety design,
regulation adherence, and consumer education. The study recommends a multilateral
strategy that encompasses pioneering engineering, consumer -oriented product design,
and behavior change to counteract safety risks and sync hronize home appliance use
with emerging requirements of safety for modern homes.

present in homes due to their ease, greater heat capacity
for water use for drinking, cooking, and other household
purposes. The history of kettle manufacturing began with
cast iron kettles in the pre -18th century, prized for their
strength and low cost, and manufactured by sand casting

made them an attractive household appliance worldwide

urbanization, time poverty in modern life, and world
demand for convenient appliances. Electric kettles are
one of the most power -consuming small domestic

dominance of their usage (Durand et al., 2022). Their
dominance is a significant aspect to render them safe
because any minute design defect or consumers' mistake
can lead to highest risk to cause in daily life.

The primary motivating stimulus for electric kettle safety
assessment is based on instances of their abuse in spite of
them having international safety features like auto shut -
off and boil -dry protection (Durand et al., 2022).
Common abuse conditions like overfilling, boiling a
material with non-water content, or with defective safety
features can lead to devastating hazards like thermal
scalds, electrical shock, steam explosion, or fire (Murray
et al., 2016; James, 2016). For instance, the U.S.
Consumer Pr oduct Safety Commission reported
incidents of home appliances, such as electric kettles,
caused by consumer misuse or product defect (Sandez et

such as smart sensors and Internet of Things (IoT)
connectivity brings new risks, such as potential
cybersecurity risks or malfunction of automated systems
(Kołaczek et al., 2025). These are the reasons why it is a
necessity that there be ongoing testing of safety with
particular focus on new and old hazards  under real
conditions of use in an effort to safeguard consumers and
inform manufacturer innovation.

The aim of the current research is two -fold in a bid to
develop a general review of the safety of electric kettles
both practically and theoretically. The review intends to
find typical abuse patterns by analyzing consumer
consumption and real consumption, assess related risk in
systematic manners like Failure Modes and Effects
Analysis (FMEA), and analyze the sufficiency of safety
features installed. The review also talks about the
potential of new technology, such as smart sensors for
temperature and water level monitoring, IoT-based alarm
systems, and fire -resistant materials, in minimizing
identified risk. The study also aims to explore
compliance to global safety standards, such as IEC
60335-2-15 for household appliances, and recommend
enhanced regulato ry standards (Ma & Li, 2022). In
bridging the disconnect between consumer behavior and
technology innovation in developing guidelines toward
safer use through enhanced design and consumer
awareness, the study aims to reduce accidents and
promote safety in the home.

2. OPERATING AND DESIGN PRINCIPLES
OF DOMESTIC ELECTRIC KETTLES

Domestic electric kettles are made to provide a fast, easy
method of water boiling, depending on the operation of
electricity and heat combined together to effectively boil
water quickly (Nicole, 2021). The key design elements
would include a source of heating, water reservoir,
thermostat, and bottom attached to an electric supply
(Figure 1) (Shah et al., 2019). The heating component,
usually nichrome or similar conductive material converts
electricity into heat due to resistance and heats the water
to boiling point (Durand et al., 2022). The majority of
kettles in use now have a power rat ing of 2000 -2500
watts, allowing for quick heating, as observed through
research on household a ppliance efficiency (Laviron et
al., 2021). The thermostat controls water temperature and
has an automatic cut -off system on boiling detection, in
the case of most systems, through steam detection, to
prevent over-heating (Greene & Lewis, 1996). The kettle
body, usually made of stainless steel, plastic, or glass, is
built to endure high temperatures while keeping the user
safe from injury through insulation (Yu et al., 2024). The
effect of material choice on the energy consumption of
electric kettles when b oiling one liter of water. Tests
comparing plastic, steel, and glass kettles showed that
plastic kettles are the most energy efficient, consuming
between 0.097 and 0.109 kWh and taking 2 min 2 s – 2
min 14 s to boil. This is due to their low thermal
conductivity, which reduces heat loss. Steel kettles
consume more energy (0.107 and 0.117 kWh) and require
longer boiling times (3 min 6 s – 3 min 22 s), while glass
kettles show similar energy consumption (0.105 and
0.118 kWh) and longer boiling times (3 min 1 s – 3 min
9 s), both of which are influenced by their high thermal
conductivity. Choosing plastic kettles could save
approximately 80 GWh per year if each household boils
one liter per day, highlighting the importance of material
selection for sustainable kettle design despite the low
energy savings per use (Yu et al., 2024). All of these
elements are integrated to provide functionality and
efficiency, therefore electric kettles are an easy option for
usage in day-to-day life in homes around the globe.

The materials employed in the making of electric kettles
are selected on the basis of durability, heat resistance, and
safety standards compliance. Stainless steel is the
material of choice for both heating element and inner
reservoir due to corrosion resi stance and thermal shock
resistance, as required by standards like IEC 60335-2-15
(Chen et al., 2022). Plastic components, occasionally
BPA-free in a safety -conscious interest, are utilized in
handles and outer shrouding to reduce heat conduction
and avoid burning people (Bassett & Arild, 2002). Glass
is employed to a greater extent in upscale models for
chemical inertia and aesthetics but has to be engineered
with great care so that it does not crack from thermal
stress (Wang et al., 2013). The material ch oice has
obvious ramifications on the safety record of the kettle,
as flimsy materials tend to degrade over time and harm
via electrical failure or leakage (Gallego -Schmid et al.,
2018). There are regulations to meet safety certification
by organizations s uch as UL or CE, which necessitate
extensive testing of components and materials before
successful conformity with compliance tests against
international safety standards (IEC, 2006; Kee et al.,
2017).

Typical safety features in standard electric kettles are
designed to respond to normal conditions of hazard when
in use (Durand et al., 2022). Auto shut-off units, triggered
by thermistor probes or by steam sensing, turn off the
kettle from drying out or o verheating, a safety measure
against fire. Boil-dry protection, which is in most kettles
inbuilt, turns off the heating element the moment the
water level goes below a certain point, to avoid damage
or risk of electrical burnout (Sidharta et al., 2000; Berg et
al., 2000; Shah et al., 2019). Insulated handles, non -slip
bases, and locking lids to avoid spilling or scalding are
usual features in most kettles. Such features are grounded
on years of appliance safety research and governed by
agencies to ensure co nsumer safety (Berg et al., 2000;
Shah et al., 2019). Yet such efficacy can be broken by
abuse in the form of tampering or maintenance
negligence, thereby establishing the necessity for safe
design and consumer education to safeguard safety
standards during use.

3. MISUSE PATTERNS UNDER REAL-
WORLD CONDITIONS AND
ASSOCIATED HAZARDS

Electric kettles, being uncomplicated appliances, are
prone to a wide variety of abuse under normal domestic
usage. A most typical form of abuse includes overfilling
the kettle above its capacity. This results in spilling
boiling water, which maximizes the risk of burn injuries,
while also damaging internal electrical cabling (Murray
et al., 2016; James, 2016; Oğur, 2014). Another prevalent
form of abuse is dry boiling, where one boils the kettle
with insufficient water. This can cause overheating of the
heating device, posing the danger of fires and electrical
damage (Table 1) (Murray et al., 2016; Ojetoye et al.,
2025).

Choosing a kettle on its beauty instead of its efficiency is
an improper approach as it creates models that lack
energy-saving features such as thermal insulation, low
minimum fill levels, or variable heat controls (70-95°C),
which conserve energy and enhance the quality of drinks.
The referred study verifies that 86% of consumers
prioritize design to accommodate kitchen design, which
results in higher energy consumption through heating
loss and overfilling of inefficient kettles. This overlooks
modern "smart" or green kettles (e.g., 1350W insulated
kettles) with minimal wastage of energy. To address this,
consumers should seek kettles that combine aesthetic
appeal with energy -efficient features to balance
performance and design (Murray et al., 2015).

Another significant misuse involves boiling non -water
liquids such as milk, soup, or sugary beverages. These
substances differ in boiling points and viscosity, resulting
in residue buildup, spout clogging, overheating, and even
pressure-induced mini -explosions, especially in kettles
that are not regularly descaled (Carleton, 1929). Standard
electric kettles are typically not equipped with sensors or
materials designed to safely handle viscous or sugary
contents. Furthermore, tampering with safety devices,
such as disabling the auto shut off or thermostat, poses
severe risks (Pop & Popper, 2016). Though less common,
such modifications can disable critical safety functions,
sometimes leading to fatal appliance failures.

Poor maintenance practices also contribute significantly
to safety hazards (Okoye & Gangkwi, 2024). Users may
mistakenly immerse the entire kettle, including the
electrical base, in water during cleaning, leading to short
circuits and electric shock risks.  Failure to descale
regularly results in limescale buildup, reducing heating
efficiency and increasing overheating risk. Safety
agencies have repeatedly emphasized the dangers of
neglecting maintenance, but a large portion of users
remain unaware of the correct procedures (Ojetoye et al.,
2025).

These misuse patterns underscore a persistent gap
between manufacturer design assumptions and real -
world user behavior. They suggest a need for improved
consumer education and enhanced design features to
mitigate risk and ensure user safety.
4. HAZARD SCENARIOS AND RISKS
INVOLVED

The most frequent hazards associated with electric kettle
misuse are thermal injuries, where contact with hot
surfaces or boiling water leads to burns. Overfilling, for
instance, increases the likelihood of boiling water spilling
onto the user or surrounding  areas, potentially causing
serious scalds (Oğur, 2014; Szymanski & Tannan, 2025).
According to the U.S. Consumer Product Safety
Commission, electric kettles are a notable cause of burn
injuries, particularly when users ignore safety
instructions (Table 1) (Deljavan et al., 2012).

Overheating may occur when the kettle operates without
sufficient water (Table 1). This can damage components,
ignite nearby flammable materials, or cause the appliance
itself to fail (Ojetoye et al., 2024; Ojetoye et al., 2025).
Electrical hazards also ar ise from improper cleaning
methods, such as submerging the base in water, which
can short circuit internal systems and cause electric
shocks—posing risks of injury or even death. Other risks
come from disabled safety functions like the auto shut -
off, resulting in higher risk of insulation failure, arcing,
and ensuing fires (Durand et al., 2022).

In extreme cases, there are scalding explosions and steam
releases from misuse of kettles to heat substances like
milk or sweet liquids. The materials have the potential to
create excessive pressure, leading to potentially unsafe
ejections of hot liquid or  steam. Incidents are also noted
in various appliance safety studies, especially when
consumers forgo intended use scenarios (Durand et al.,
2021). Furthermore, fires are still a primary cause,
especially when overheating is near combustible
materials or when safety features are bypassed (Ojetoye
et al., 2025). Underwriters Laboratories and regulatory
bodies suggest better design and mandatory testing
requirements, as outlined in IEC 60335 -2-15. User
failure, negligence, or misapplication may compromise
these safety features. Therefore, enhancing the safety of
electric kettles also depends on engineering resilience but
requires bridging the information gap with focused user
education and clear consumer warnings (Durand et al.,
2021).
5. FAILURE MODES AND RISK
ASSESSMENT METHODS

Some failure modes that are common include failure of
the auto shut -off system, kettle overheating due to boil -
dry conditions , and electrical failure due to water
intrusion or loss of insulation. For instance, a failure in
the thermostat or blockage of the steam sensor can lead
to on -going heating of the kettle, which can lead to
overheating or fires (Ojetoye et al., 2025). FMEA , the
standard risk assessment process applied for appliance
design, is how the potential failures, failure modes, and
their impact  are determined (Damanab et al., 20 14).
FMEA considers every component, i.e., heating element
or base, separately to ascertain how improper use as
overfilling or filling with something other than water will
lead to failures such as short circuits or thermal damage
(Alshboul & Petriu, 2018). T o aid appliance safety
studies, FMEA makes it possible for design
improvements to be ordered by measurable failure
severity, occurrence, and detectability (Salah et al.,
2023). Risk analysis methods, such as creation of  risk

Risk matrix places the potential hazards, say burns,
electric shock, or fire, in ascending order of likelihood
and severity so that manufacturers and regulators identify
high-risk hazards (Hughes & Hughes, 2008). Dry -
boiling, for example, will be rated as a high -risk
occurrence since it has a tendency to trigger fire s reports
of injury (Roosen, 2023). These matrices include usage
information, e.g., misusing patterns like wrong cleaning
or tampering with safety devices, in the definition of
contribution to failure modes by user behavior (Hughes
& Hughes, 2008). Recorde d accident case histories
provide an excellent quantity of data pertaining to
genuine failure modes and their consequences under true
conditions, justifying the need for proper risk assessment
(Burd & Burd, 1985).

Kettle fires supposedly occurring as a result of boil -dry
accidents exemplify the susceptibility of early safety
designs without any boil -dry protection. Similarly,
electrical shock as a result of water invasion into the
bottom of the kettle during faulty cleaning highlights the
importance of water resistance and consumer training.

By including FMEA, risk matrices, and experiential
accident data, this section exemplifies a forward-looking
approach in risk avoidance and making electric kettles
safe in spite of abuse. A study performed by Burd & Burd
(1985), conducted at Leicester Roya l Infirmary’s Burns
Unit from 1981 to 1984, examined scald injuries in 153
preschool children (aged 60 months or less). Scalding
was the primary cause of admission, with 23 of 27 kettle-
related injuries resulting from children pulling on
dangling kettle fl exes. Boys were more frequently
affected. The study highlights the persistent danger of
conventional straight kettle leads, despite awareness and
passive prevention efforts like education. It proposes
active prevention through legislation mandating curly
cables, which retract and eliminate dangling hazards, as
a cost-effective solution to reduce severe scald injuries in
children. A few children were required to have skin grafts
and extended hospital stays, demonstrating the
seriousness of these accidents (B urd & Burd, 1985).
Holmes et al's 2012 research explores the severity of
scald burns caused by children pulling on the exposed
cords of electric kettles at Red Cross War Memorial
Children's Hospital in Chapel Hill, NC's Pediatric
Regional Burn Unit. The re search notes that such scalds
typically affect most frequently the cheek, shoulder, and
chest, often requiring skin grafting due to children's
instinct to flinch and recoil during the incident. Hospital
stay for the affected children averaged at 10.4 days, with
a range of 1 to 124 days, significantly shorter compared
to previous studies, likely due to excessive bed utilization
and need for timely discharge. Patients required a number
of follow-up visits ranging from 1 to 22 visits (Holmes et
al., 2012).
6. REGULATORY STANDARDS AND
SAFETY CERTIFICATIONS

6.1. European Standards and Regulations

Electric kettles in the EU are controlled by a series
of standards and directives dealing with safety,
energy efficiency, and the environment. European
Standard EN 50564:2011 addresses energy
consumption in standby and off modes for
household electrical and electronic equipment,
including electric kettles, but does not fully tackle
energy efficiency during operation  (CENELEC,
2011). Safety standards are covered by BS EN
60335-1:2012+A15:2021, which deals with the
general safety of household appliances, and BS EN
60335-2-15:2016+A11:2018, which provides
specific safety requirements for appliances used for
heating liquids, such as electric kettles  (BSI, 2020;
CENELEC, 2016). These standards are formulated
by technical committees such as CLC/TC 59X
(Performance of household and similar electrical
appliances) and CLC/TC 61 (Safety of household
and similar electrical appliances). Additionally, IEC
60530:1975 outlines procedures for measuring the
performance of electric kettles, particularly the time
taken to heat 1 liter of water from 15°C to 95°C.
However, it still does not prescribe specific methods
for measuring electricity consumption. The essay
suggests this requirement could be updated to
incorporate energy efficiency ratings (Durand et al.,
2021).

6.2. Ecodesign and Energy Labelling
Frameworks

The Ecodesign Directive (2009/125/EC) and Energy
Labelling Regulation (EU 2017/1369) establish schemes
for energy efficiency and energy-related product labeling
(ErP). Even though there is no individual Ecodesign
legislation that directly regulates electri c kettles, the
paper determines whether they qualify to be covered
under these regimes. The Energy Labelling Regulation
creates energy classes A to G and demands a product
database for ensuring transparency and monitoring
within the market. The regimes aim at increasing energy
efficiency and helping consumers make a better
judgment about kettle quality (Durand et al., 2021).

7. DIRECTIONS FOR ELECTRIC KETTLE
SAFETY

In the Future With  continued development electric
kettles, advances  in the field of material science will
radically enhance their durability and safety, fighting
risks of abuse conditions like over-heating or dry boiling.
New flame-retardant materials are being developed, like
new ceramic materials and second -generation f lame-
retardant polymers, that are not undermined by very high
heat, with less risk of fire or chemical leaching with
prolonged use (Li, 2010; Li et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022).

A study published by Sezer & Altintas, (2023) developed
a wireless power transfer (IPT) system to operate an
electric kettle with over 90% efficiency. The system
utilizes high-coupling-coefficient coils and a Half-Bridge
resonant converter, incorporating control algorithms that
ensure safety by halting power transfer when the kettle is
removed, minimizing electrical risks. Compatible with
existing induction cooking systems, this design provides
a practical and safe solution for household use ( figure 2)
(Sezer & Altintas, 2023).

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in electric kettles is
of great potential in terms of real-time safety monitoring
and reducing risks. A study by Mokhsin et al. (2022)
introduced a smart kettle, an advanced, wirelessly
operated version of the tradit ional kettle, controlled
remotely via a user-friendly mobile application designed
to save energy, reduce costs, and enhance convenience.
The app allows users to select from four preset
temperature settings or customize their own, set timers,
monitor water levels, and check kettle connectivity, with
a discovery page providing additional information and
user guides. The design followed the Mobile Application
Development Lifecycle Model (MADLC) with seven
phases: identification, design, development, prototyping,
testing, deployment, and maintenance. Smart kettle uses
an Arduino Uno board with three nodes—Base Node (for
TCP/IP and HTTP protocol communications), Logger
Node, and Kettle Node —as well as a radio module and
an extensometer for water weight measurement. The app
prototype has five screens: a pair screen, a main page for
temperature and timer control, a water level page, a list
page for the kettle, and a discover page. The app has
command inputs, tap buttons, and option choices, with
error and success me ssages that guarantee simplicity,
particularly when dealing with multitasking and senior
citizens. The click -sensitive interface with links to
external pages is intuitively designed and continually
enhanced on the basis of users' feedback in order to
address the demands of the times and ensure customer
protection and trust (figure 3) (Mokhsin et al., 2022).

Krongtripop & Kirdpipat (2016) in a study sought to use
a neural network based on an Arduino microcontroller to
reduce energy consumption in an electric kettle. The
neural network employed the backpropagation learning
rule and Levenberg -Marquardt algorithm  in order to
learn from previous usage histories, which included the
hour and day of usage of the kettle and weather
temperatures. This allowed the system to control and
manage kettle working hours predictively in an efficient
manner.

The design of the system included relay circuitry for
kettle control and LCD display for showing the state of
operation. Some parameters, i.e., learning rate = 0.1,
learning rate decrement = 0.7, learning rate increment =
1.05, 30,000 epochs, mean squared error performance
function, etc., were used in conducting the experiment.
The outcomes, as elaborated in the paper, illustrated the
ability of the neural network to process information at
high lev els of speed and precision and continuously
control the electricity kettle to realize extensive energy
saving. The design of the hardware circuit, shown in the
paper, also aided in ensuring the safe control of the
electric kettle, validating the accuracy o f the system in
forecasting and controlling operating times ( figure 4)
(Krongtripop & Kirdpipat, 2016).

Moreover, the product based on eco -friendly and non -
toxic materials is also in accordance with regulations like
the RoHS directive, minimizing health risks after
exposure to dangerous chemicals (Gallego-Schmid et al.,
2018; Jefferson Andrew et al., 2024). A report published
by Murray et al. (2015) examines kettle usage in 14 UK
households across a three -month period  to identify
possible means of saving energy. It highlights the fact
that kettle overfilling, a common habit in three -quarters
of UK households, leads to significant wastage of energy
and costs an estimated £66 million each year. Using
monitoring of energy use and behavioral observation, the
study examines the possibility of feedback and take-up of
energy-saving "eco" kettles such as vacuum -insulated
kettles to reduce consumption. Semi -regular patterns of
use based on occupancy and lifestyle are found by the
study, with eco kettles reducing consumption by as much
as 30% in certain cases, a finding seen in a case study of

8. CONCLUSION

This study proved that electric kettles' safety is
vulnerable to  several crucial factors, including their
intrinsic safety properties and the consumer -device
interaction under genuine usage conditions. Despite
significant advances in safety design and regulatory
compliance, persistence in kettle -related accidents
suggests an unconscious discord between product
engineering and real-world consumer habits of use.

Misuse patterns such as overfilling, dry boiling, and
heating non -water substances still create extremes of
electrical and thermal hazards. Ignoring maintenance
such as lack of descaling or inadequate cleaning just fuels
the flames. Worse, there is evidence that some users
actually disable safety features —actions that lead to
catastrophic failure. These real usage patterns rev eal the
inadequacies of current design paradigms and regulatory
models for avoiding misuse-induced accidents.

By using FMEA and risk matrix tools, the study clearly
spelled out and ranked up to typical failure modes,
offering practical advice on where safety interventions
are most necessary. The study also explored the potential
of new technologies —such as smart k ettles with AI -
based monitoring, induction -coil wireless heating
systems, and heat -resistant, eco -friendly materials —to
serve as game -changing solutions to enhance appliance
safety. Technological innovation is not enough, however,
without the awareness of the user and the habit of the
right behavior. To move forward, an integrated safety
strategy is essential —one that encompasses stringent
regulatory compliance, thoughtful user -centered design,
robust consumer education, and the continued
development of adaptive safety technologies. Ultimately,
full electric kettle safety will be brought about by

9. RECOMMENDATIONS

Based on the findings of this study, the following
recommendations are proposed to enhance the safety,
efficiency, and user awareness regarding electric kettle
usage:
• User-Centric Design:  Manufacturers should
prioritize intuitive and foolproof designs that
mitigate risks even under misuse, such as
overflow detection and smart shut -off for non -
water substances.
• Material Improvements: Use of flame-retardant,
eco-friendly, and chemically inert materials
should be standardized to resist overheating,
corrosion, and contamination.
• Integrated Smart Technology:  Incorporate AI-
powered controls, IoT connectivity, and sensor -
based safety features for real-time monitoring and
automatic failure prevention.
• Enhanced Regulatory Measures:  Update
existing standards to reflect modern hazards,
including misuse scenarios and cybersecurity
threats posed by smart kettles.
• Mandatory Safety Education:  Appliance
packaging should include illustrated misuse
scenarios and maintenance guidelines. National
campaigns should raise awareness about electric
kettle risks.
• Legislative Action:  Enforce design regulations
such as retractable cords to prevent child scalding
injuries, as supported by burn injury data.
• Data-Driven Monitoring:  Encourage real -time
usage tracking and feedback to inform safer
consumer habits and guide future regulatory
decisions.
