Abstract
Purpose – The prevalence of uncivil consumer behavior on social media presents challenges for brands
managing publicly visible complaint interactions, where bystanders observe and evaluate responses. While prior
research has examined accommodative brand responses and online incivility, few studies have examined how
complaint tone shapes bystanders’ perceptions of accommodative responses in publicly visible interactions.
Design/methodology/approach – Two online scenario experiments were conducted with US consumers, using
the airline (N 5 679) and hotel (N 5 553) industries as contexts. Both studies utilized a 2 3 2 design to test the
proposed model.
Findings – Accommodative responses significantly increased bystanders’ purchase intentions, and this effect
was fully mediated by brand attitude. Complaint tone moderated this indirect effect such that the positive impact
of accommodative responses was stronger for civil complaints and weaker for uncivil complaints.
Practical implications – The findings suggest that brand managers should adopt tone-sensitive response
strategies when managing public complaints, while platforms and policymakers should support tools and
guidelines that reduce online incivility and promote constructive communication environments.
Originality/value – This study examined accommodative responses, complaint tone, and observing bystanders
together in public social media complaint interactions and found that bystanders interpret brand responses in
relation to the complaint tone that precedes them. The findings demonstrate that accommodative responses
improve brand attitude and purchase intention more strongly for civil complaints than for uncivil complaints.
The study also extends social learning theory by showing that bystanders learn from both brand behavior and
consumer tone.

A defining characteristic of contemporary online complaints is the variation in
communicative tone. Complaints range from civil (polite and respectful) to uncivil (rude,
aggressive, or profane). Prior research has documented the rise of online incivility (Bacile
et al., 2018; Bhutto et al., 2022; Wolter et al., 2023). In the event of a service failure, consumers

negatively affect a brand’s reputation and escalate the negative impact of the situation. Several
swear words are commonly used in uncivil online complaints, including “f*ing,” “jack shit,”
and “bastard” (B�eal et al., 2023; Dineva and Daunt, 2023; Lafreniere et al., 2022). Such uncivil
complaints often contain emotionally charged language reflecting frustration or anger, which
can intensify interactions and shape how they are perceived by others (Bhutto et al., 2026).
Due to the public visibility of social media complaints, other users observe how brands
respond. These users, often referred to as bystanders or silent observers, form judgments about
the fairness and professionalism of the brand based on what they witness (Huang and Ha, 2024;
Johnen and Schnittka, 2019). Uncivil complaints escalate negative sentiment (Bacile et al.,
2025; Bacile, 2020; Fujii, 2025; Huang and Ha, 2024) and increase reputational risks when
poorly managed (Marlow, 2024). Poor complaint handling carries significant financial
consequences, with estimates suggesting annual losses of up to $494 billion (Customer Care
Measurement and Consulting, 2020). Accommodative responses, a common strategy in the
service sector, involve accepting responsibility, apologizing, taking corrective action,
expressing regret, and seeking additional information (Esmark Jones et al., 2018; Lopes
et al., 2023).
Prior research on online complaints has expanded rapidly, and a number of existing studies
have examined response strategies, complaint tones, and bystander evaluations in different
ways. Several studies (e.g. Dens et al., 2015; Chang et al., 2015; Weitzl and Hutzinger, 2017;
Piehler et al., 2019; Herhausen et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024) have focused
on brand response strategies, such as apology, compensation, explanation, accommodative
response, defensive response, and active listening. These studies indicate that accommodative
brand responses can improve consumer outcomes, including fairness perceptions, brand
attitudes, word-of-mouth intentions, trust, and purchase intentions. While they establish that
response strategy matters, the aforementioned studies do not clarify whether accommodative
responses remain equally effective when the complaint on social media (e.g. Facebook) is
expressed in a civil versus an uncivil tone.
A number of researchers have explored online incivility, harsh language, trolling, and
consumer-to-consumer (C2C) conflict (Bacile et al., 2018; Batista et al., 2022; Dineva and
Daunt, 2023; Wolter et al., 2023; Riedel et al., 2025; Jeseo and Tatara, 2025), and have
provided important insights into harsh reviews, C2C incivility, uncivil comments, trolling, and
illegitimate reviews. However, these studies focus on related but distinct forms of incivility
and do not directly examine whether a civil versus an uncivil complaint changes the
effectiveness of an accommodative response.
Bystander-focused studies have also shown that observers evaluate brands after witnessing
online complaint interactions (Weitzl and Hutzinger, 2017; Hutzinger and Weitzl, 2021; Ku
et al., 2021; Liao et al., 2025; Floriano, 2025). These studies demonstrate that observing
audiences form brand attitudes, trust, satisfaction, and purchase intentions after witnessing
complaint handling. However, these studies do not fully explain how bystanders interpret an
accommodative response when the original complaint differs in tone. This is important
because bystanders do not evaluate the brand response in isolation; they observe the complaint
and the response as a single interaction.

design by (1) focusing on accommodative response versus no response, (2) examining
complaint tone as civil versus uncivil, and (3) adopting a bystander perspective to test how

by both the complainant’s tone and the brand’s response; bystanders learn not only from how
the brand behaves but also from the tone of the consumer behavior that precedes the response.
2. Literature review
2.1 The effect of online complaints on brands
Online complaints on social media have become a primary channel through which consumers
express dissatisfaction, seek remedies, and publicly evaluate brands (Kim et al., 2025; Kim
and Kim, 2019; Kowalewicz, 2024; Sharma et al., 2023; Zhang and Zhang, 2025). Unlike
traditional complaint channels, these interactions are visible to a wide audience, transforming
complaint handling into a public process that extends beyond the complainant–firm
relationship.
Complaints are behavioral expressions of dissatisfaction that may serve multiple purposes,
including emotional release, problem resolution, and interpersonal communication (Fornell
and Westbrook, 1984; Hogreve et al., 2019; Kowalski, 1996). In digital environments, the tone
in which complaints are expressed has become increasingly important. Prior research
distinguishes between civil complaints, which are polite and respectful, and uncivil
complaints, which involve rude, aggressive, or profane language (Anderson et al., 2014;
Porath et al., 2011; Wolter et al., 2023). Synthesizing these insights, recent work has defined
consumer incivility as rude, disrespectful, and emotionally charged customer behavior
directed at service employees in online or offline settings, often characterized by ambiguous
intent and broader negative consequences for service environments (Bhutto et al., 2026). Such
uncivil communication has been associated with increased anxiety and negative observer
reactions (Porath et al., 2011), polarization of attitudes in online settings (Anderson et al.,
2014), and social discomfort or reduced engagement among audiences exposed to hostile
interactions (Bacile et al., 2018; Wolter et al., 2023).
Consequently, online complaints, particularly those with an uncivil tone, can negatively
affect consumers’ brand perceptions (Wolter et al., 2023). A study of uncivil words used by
consumers provides evidence that aggressive language expressions, such as “f*ing,” “you
bastard,” and “shit” (B�eal et al., 2023; Dineva and Daunt, 2023; Lafreniere et al., 2022), as
well as emotional slang expressions, such as “i h8 ur brand” (Bacile, 2024), can be used to
analyze consumer behavior. In service contexts, this lack of civility not only disrupts the
relationship between complainants and brands but also influences how observing users
evaluate brand performance (B�eal et al., 2023; Huang and Ha, 2024).
Due to the high public visibility of these interactions, their effects extend beyond the
complainant. Evidence suggests that uncivil complaints reduce engagement, polarize audience

2.2 Extant research on accommodative responses to online complaints
Accommodative  responses are widely used by firms to address customer complaints. These
responses typically involve acknowledging  the issue, apologizing,  explaining the situation,
and offering corrective actions (Esmark Jones et al., 2018; Lopes et al., 2023). As
summarized in Table 1, a substantial body of research demonstrates  that accommodative
responses improve consumer evaluations.  Prior studies have shown that responses
characterized  by apology, empathy, and corrective action enhance satisfaction,  brand
attitudes, and behavioral outcomes such as word-of-mouth  and purchase intentions (Dens
et al., 2015; Chang et al., 2015; Weitzl and Hutzinger, 2017; Piehler et al., 2019). These
positive effects extend beyond complaining customers to include observing audiences.
Bystanders who witness accommodative  responses are more likely to form favorable
impressions of the brand and update their evaluations accordingly (Hutzinger and Weitzl,
2021; Ku et al., 2021).
Table 1 also highlights that the effectiveness of brand responses varies across contexts.
Defensive  responses  may outperform  accommodative  responses  depending  on
communication style and context (Johnen and Schnittka, 2019), and combining an
explanation with compensation may produce stronger behavioral outcomes than single
strategies (Piehler et al., 2019). In addition, recent work indicates that consumers may react
less positively to AI-generated responses than to human-crafted replies due to concerns about
authenticity and empathy (Tan et al., 2025). Other studies have shown that response
effectiveness may depend on the nature of the complaint or interaction context, such as trolling
or illegitimate complaints (Jeseo and Tatara, 2025; Mulcahy et al., 2023; Riedel et al., 2025).
These findings suggest that accommodative responses are generally effective, but their impact
depends on the context in which they are delivered.
2.3 Bystanders’ perceptions of social media
Due to the public visibility of social media complaint interactions, they are frequently
observed by bystanders who are not directly involved in the interaction but may still form
evaluations of the brand (Batista et al., 2022; Singh and Mishra, 2023; Wolter et al., 2023;
Huang and Ha, 2024). These observers play an important role in shaping brand reputation, as
their attitudes and behavioral intentions are influenced by what they witness.
Prior research has shown that specific characteristics of brand responses influence
bystander evaluations. Empathetic and active listening responses reduce negative reactions
(Herhausen et al., 2023), relational commitment enhances trust (Kim et al., 2025),
personalized or timely responses improve satisfaction and purchase intentions (Floriano,
2025), and public responses, compared to private ones, strengthen positive brand evaluations
(Liao et al., 2025). These findings indicate that bystanders respond not only to whether a brand
responds but also to how the response is delivered.
However, bystanders do not interpret brand responses separately from the complaints that
trigger them. In publicly visible online complaint interactions, the complaint and the response
are observed together. Prior studies have shown that accommodative responses can improve
consumer evaluations (Dens et al., 2015; Hutzinger and Weitzl, 2021; Liao et al., 2025), while
uncivil communication can negatively affect brand-related evaluations and audience
perceptions (Bacile et al., 2018; Wolter et al., 2023; Huang and Ha, 2024). What remains
unclear is whether bystanders interpret the same accommodative response differently when
the original complaint is civil rather than uncivil.

This study builds on social learning theory (Bandura, 1977), which explains how individuals
learn attitudes and behaviors by observing others’ behaviors and their consequences. During
online complaint interactions, bystanders observe two cues: the tone of the complaint and the
response provided by the brand. A civil complaint signals respectful communication, whereas
an uncivil complaint indicates rude or norm-violating behavior. Accommodative responses
indicate responsiveness, accountability, and concern for the customer.
Bystanders do not interpret brand responses in isolation. They observe both the complaint and
the response as parts of the same public interaction. Thus, the tone of a complaint may influence
the interpretation of an accommodative response. When the complaint is civil, an accommodative
response may appear appropriate and strengthen brand evaluations. The tone of the complainant
may undermine the positive effect of an accommodative response when the complaint is uncivil.
This study examined whether accommodative responses influence bystanders’ purchase
intentions through brand attitudes and whether the process differs for civil and uncivil
complaints.
3.1 The relationship between accommodative responses and bystanders’ purchase
intentions
In social media environments, complaint handling is publicly visible, allowing bystanders to
observe how brands respond to dissatisfied customers. These observations serve as
informational cues that influence how individuals learn by evaluating the brand and form
behavioral intentions (Hutzinger and Weitzl, 2021; Ku et al., 2021).
Accommodative responses, such as apologies, explanations, corrective actions, and
expressions of concern, signal that the brand is responsive, accountable, and customer-
oriented (Gelbrich and Roschk, 2011; Lopes et al., 2023). When bystanders observe such
responses, they may infer that the brand takes customer concerns seriously and is willing to
address service problems. Prior research has shown that accommodative responses improve
consumer evaluations and behavioral outcomes, including brand attitudes, word-of-mouth
intentions, and purchase intentions (Dens et al., 2015; Weitzl and Hutzinger, 2017; Piehler et al.,
2019). More recent studies have demonstrated that observers update their trust and behavioral
intentions based on publicly visible complaint handling (Liao et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025;
Zhang and Zhang, 2025). Thus, when bystanders observe an accommodative response rather
than no response, they are more likely to evaluate the brand favorably and develop stronger
purchase intentions. In light of this reasoning, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H1. Compared to no response, accommodative responses to online complaints have a
positive effect on bystanders’ purchase intentions.
3.2 The mediating role of brand attitude
Although accommodative responses may influence behavioral intentions, this effect is likely to
operate through bystanders’ evaluations of the brand. Brand attitude reflects an individual’s
overall evaluation of a brand and is a key predictor of behavioral outcomes (Hagtvedt, 2011).
From a social learning theory perspective, bystanders observe how a brand behaves in response
to a complaint and use this observed behavior to form an evaluative judgment about the brand.
When brands respond in an accommodative manner, bystanders may interpret the response
as a sign of professionalism, responsibility, empathy, and customer orientation. These
observed cues can improve bystanders’ attitudes toward the brand because the brand appears
willing to listen, acknowledge concerns, and take corrective action. Prior studies have shown
that accommodative and empathetic responses improve brand attitudes among consumers and
observers (Dens et al., 2015; Esmark Jones et al., 2018; Hutzinger and Weitzl, 2021). Related
research also indicates that response characteristics such as empathy, responsiveness,
personalization, and relational commitment strengthen brand evaluations and trust (Chang
et al., 2015; Lee and Song, 2010; Herhausen et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025; Floriano, 2025).

Positive brand attitudes, in turn, are expected to increase purchase intentions. If bystanders
form favorable attitudes after observing an accommodative response, they are more likely to
consider purchasing from the brand. Conversely, when a brand does not respond, bystanders
may lack positive cues about the brand’s responsiveness, which can weaken brand attitudes

purchase intentions indirectly through brand attitude. Based on this reasoning, the following
hypothesis is proposed:
H2. Brand attitude mediates the effect of the brand’s accommodative response on
bystanders’ purchase intentions.
3.3 The moderating role of online complaint tone (civil vs. uncivil)
Bystanders  observe not only the brand’s accommodative  response but also the tone of the
complaint that triggered the response. Drawing on social learning theory (Bandura, 1977),
this study argues that bystanders  learn from the full public interaction  by observing two
connected behaviors:  the complainant’s tone and the brand’s response. Complaint tone
provides contextual  information  about the interaction,  while the brand response provides
information  about the brand’s behavior. When the complaint is civil, it is expressed in a
respectful  and socially acceptable  manner. In such cases, bystanders  may perceive the
complaint as legitimate  and the brand’s accommodative  response as appropriate.  The
response may therefore strengthen  brand attitude because the brand appears responsive  to a
reasonable  customer concern. Prior research has shown that accommodative  responses can
improve brand evaluations  and behavioral  intentions  when observers perceive the brand as
responsive  and customer-oriented (Dens et al., 2015; Weitzl and Hutzinger, 2017; Zhang
and Zhang, 2025).
In contrast, when the complaint is uncivil, using rude, hostile, or aggressive language,
bystanders may perceive the complainant as violating social norms (B�eal et al., 2023; Dineva
and Daunt, 2023; Wolter et al., 2023). In such cases, the uncivil tone may change how
bystanders interpret the brand’s accommodative response. Although an accommodative
response still signals responsiveness, its positive effect may be weaker because bystanders
evaluate the response within a more negative and norm-violating interaction context. A rude
tone may draw attention away from the brand’s positive behavior, reduce the perceived
appropriateness of the accommodative response, or make the overall interaction less
favorable. Therefore, the indirect effect of accommodative responses on purchase intention
through brand attitude is expected to be stronger when complaints are civil and weaker when
complaints are uncivil. This reasoning leads to the following hypothesis:
H3. Complaint tone moderates the indirect effect of the brand’s accommodative response
on bystanders’ purchase intentions through brand attitude such that the effect is
stronger when the complaint is civil (vs. uncivil).
4. Research methodology
4.1 Experimental design, sampling strategy, and scenario development
This paper details two experimental studies conducted at different times. Study 1, focusing on
the airline context, was conducted first, in May 2024. Study 2 was conducted in the hotel
context in July 2025, after the data for Study 1 had been collected and analyzed. Study 2 was
designed as a conceptual replication to assess whether the hypothesized relationships hold
across distinct service settings rather than being context-specific.
The airline and hotel sectors were selected because they are prominent high-contact service
settings in which service failures, customer complaints, and brand responses are frequently
visible to other consumers on social media. Both sectors involve experiential and relatively

incomplete responses. The participants in this study received a small compensation for
participation, and the average completion time was approximately seven minutes.

4.4 Pre-testing
Pre-tests were carried out to ensure that the online survey functioned as designed, to validate
manipulations before the main data collection, and to confirm that the scenarios were perceived as
sufficiently realistic. In terms of validating manipulations, two identical manipulation checks
were used in both Study 1 and Study 2. Manipulation check 1 (MC1) tested whether the
respondents perceived the complaint tone as intended. This was operationalized via the following
question: “To what extent did the customer-generated comments include uncivil language [rude
or inappropriate language]?” Manipulation check 2 (MC2) tested whether the respondents
considered the brand’s response to be accommodative. This was operationalized via the question,
“How accommodative was the response from the brand?” Scenario realism was operationalized
through the question, “How would you rate the realism of the presented content?” In the
manipulation and realism checks, the response scale ranged from 1 (“not at all”) to 7 (“very
much”) and 1 (“very unrealistic”) to 7 (“very realistic”), respectively. Both the realism check and
the manipulation checks confirmed that the manipulations worked as intended.

significantly more accommodative (M 5 4.73, SD 5 1.033, n 5 15) than the no response
(M 5 1.87, SD 5 0.915, n 5 15) (p < 0.001). Scenario realism was above the midpoint
(M 5 5.10, SD 5 1.24, N 5 30) (p < 0.001).
In Study 2 (the hotel context, N 5 60), uncivil complaints were perceived as more uncivil
(M 5 5.50, SD 5 1.17, n 5 30) than civil complaints (M 5 1.40, SD 5 0.89, n 5 30)
(p < 0.001). Furthermore, the accommodative response was viewed as more accommodative
(M 5 5.06, SD 5 1.93, n 5 34) than no response (M 5 1.42, SD 5 0.504, n 5 26) (p < 0.001).
Again, the scenario realism was above the mid-point (M 5 5.42, SD 5 1.49, N 5 60).

5.2 Hypothesis testing for studies 1 and 2
To test H1, a PROCESS macro [Model 1] (Hayes, 2013) was used to examine the effects of an
accommodative response to online complaints on bystanders’ purchase intentions compared to
no response. The results supported H1 (Airline: β 5 0.93, standard error [SE] 5 0.16, t 5 5.88,
p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.62, 1.24]; Hotel: β 5 1.26, SE 5 0.17, t 5 7.34, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.92,
1.60]), revealing that social media users who observed an accommodative brand response
reported significantly higher purchase intentions than those who observed no response.
Using a PROCESS macro [Model 4] to evaluate H2, both studies found that the
accommodative response had a significant indirect influence on purchase intention as a
function of brand attitudes in both contexts (airline: β 5 0.51, 95% CI [0.34, 0.69]); hotel:
β 5 1.20, 95% CI [1.01, 1.39]). Direct effects were not significant, indicating full mediation.
To test H3, a PROCESS macro [Model 7] was utilized in both studies to examine whether
complaint tone moderated the indirect effect of accommodative brand responses on
bystanders’ purchase intentions via their perceptions of brand attitude. Both the hotel and
airline contexts had significant indexes of moderated mediation (hotel: Index 5 �0.37,
SE 5 0.16, 95% CI [–0.69, �0.05]; airline: Index 5 �0.39, SE 5 0.18, 95% CI [–0.74,
�0.04]), indicating that the indirect effects were stronger when civil complaints were
involved.
As shown in Figure 2a (the airline context) and b (the hotel context), bystanders’ brand
attitudes were significantly affected by complaint tone and accommodative response across
both studies. Although accommodative responses generally improved brand attitudes, this
effect was significantly stronger for civil complaints than for uncivil complaints, indicating a
moderating role of complaint tone. Specifically, the positive influence of accommodative
responses was attenuated when complaints were expressed using uncivil language.
A significant indirect effect was found for civil complaints in the hotel industry
(Effect 5 1.40, SE 5 0.13, 95% CI [1.15, 1.66]) compared to uncivil complaints
(Effect 5 1.03, SE 5 0.12, 95% CI [0.79, 1.28]). A similar indirect effect was observed in
the airline sector for civil complaint tone (Effect 5 0.70, SE 5 0.12, 95% CI [0.46, 0.96])
compared with uncivil complaints (Effect 5 0.32, SE 5 0.13, 95% CI [0.06, 0.58]). In both
contexts, significant interactions were found between response type and complaint tone (hotel:
β 5 �0.49, p 5 0.026; airline: β 5 �0.45, p 5 0.032).
In both the airline and hotel contexts, the direct effects of an accommodative response on
purchase intention were not significant when brand attitude was taken into account (hotel:
0.13, p 5 0.203; airline: 0.12, p 5 0.108), indicating full mediation. These results support H3,
namely that accommodative responses increase purchase intentions, especially in cases of civil
complaints, by enhancing brand attitudes.
Among the control variables, gender, Facebook interaction, and employment status
significantly influenced brand attitudes across both studies. Purchase intentions were also
affected by age and education. Specifically, females and those with higher Facebook
interactions demonstrated more positive brand attitudes, while younger and more educated
bystanders showed higher purchase intentions.

bystanders’ evaluations, the findings show that accommodative responses positively influence
bystanders’ purchase intentions through brand attitude. However, this effect is weaker when

mechanism underlying bystander responses: observers first evaluate whether the brand
behaves fairly and professionally, and these evaluations then shape purchase intentions. This is

stronger positive effects on brand attitudes and purchase intentions, whereas these effects are
weaker for uncivil complaints. While prior research has examined the negative consequences
of uncivil communication (Bacile et al., 2018; Wolter et al., 2023), the present findings show
that complaint tone influences how brand responses are interpreted, highlighting the
importance of considering both elements jointly in public complaint settings.

evaluations by observing interactions between complainants and brands (Bandura, 1977).

behaviors but also the context in which these behaviors occur. In particular, complaint tone
influences how brand responses are interpreted, suggesting that observational learning in
digital complaint environments is shaped by both brand behavior and interaction context. The
consistency of findings across airline and hotel contexts further suggests that these effects are
not limited to a single service setting. Although effect sizes vary, the overall pattern remains
stable, indicating that publicly visible complaint handling influences bystander evaluations
across different service environments.
6.1 Theoretical implications
This research contributes to the online complaint and brand response literature in three ways.
First, this study contributes by examining the combined role of accommodative brand
responses, complaint tone, and observing bystanders in publicly visible online complaint
interactions. Prior studies have examined accommodative or related brand responses (Dens
et al., 2015; Weitzl and Hutzinger, 2017; Piehler et al., 2019), online incivility or complaint
tone (Bacile et al., 2018; Wolter et al., 2023; Huang and Ha, 2024), and bystander evaluations
(Hutzinger and Weitzl, 2021; Ku et al., 2021; Liao et al., 2025). However, they have not clearly
shown how bystanders evaluate accommodative responses when the triggering complaint is
civil versus uncivil. By testing these elements together, this study shows that bystanders form
brand attitudes and purchase intentions by interpreting the complaint and the brand response as
part of the same public interaction.
Second, this study contributes by showing that complaint tone changes the effectiveness
of accommodative  responses. Across the airline and hotel contexts, accommodative
responses improved bystanders’ brand attitudes and purchase intentions, but these positive
effects were weaker when complaints were uncivil rather than civil. This finding refines
prior research showing that accommodative  responses generally improve consumer

6.2 Practical implications
The findings provide actionable implications for three distinct stakeholder groups involved in
managing public digital complaint environments: brand managers, social media platform
managers, and policymakers. Each group plays a different role in shaping how complaint
interactions unfold and are interpreted by observing audiences.
6.2.1 Implications for brand managers. For brand managers (e.g. airline and hotel brands),
the results highlight that accommodative responses remain an effective strategy for improving
bystanders’ brand attitudes and purchase intentions. However, their effectiveness is not
unconditional. When complaints are expressed in uncivil tones, the positive impact of such
responses is significantly weakened. This suggests that managers should move beyond generic
response approaches and adopt tone-sensitive communication strategies.
In practice, this means that brand managers should develop structured response protocols
that account for the tone and context of complaints. While maintaining professionalism and
empathy is essential, immediate accommodative responses to highly aggressive or abusive
complaints may not always yield the intended positive outcomes. Instead, managers may
consider delayed, reframed, or moderated responses that maintain brand dignity while
avoiding the reinforcement of norm-violating behavior. Training frontline social media teams
to recognize complaint tone and adjust response strategies accordingly is therefore crucial. In
general, managers should recognize that the handling of complaints on social media is not
limited to resolving individual issues but is a public performance that is observed by a wider
audience. A small improvement in the way responses are framed can have a significant impact
on the perception of a brand and on observers’ purchase intentions.
6.2.2 Implications for social media platform managers. For social media platform
managers (i.e. those responsible for designing and governing platforms such as Facebook), the
findings highlight their central role in shaping the communication environment in which
brand–consumer interactions occur. Since uncivil communication weakens the effectiveness
of otherwise appropriate brand responses, platforms directly influence whether complaint
interactions remain constructive or become harmful.
Platform managers should therefore invest in proactive moderation systems, including
automated tone detection, sentiment monitoring, and early intervention mechanisms, to
identify and manage uncivil or abusive content. Such tools can help prevent escalation and
maintain communication environments that support constructive engagement between brands
and users. In addition, clearer community standards and enforcement policies are needed to
discourage aggressive language and promote respectful interaction norms.
Importantly, platform design choices, such as the visibility of responses, comment ranking
algorithms, and reporting mechanisms, can shape how interactions are interpreted by
bystanders. By prioritizing respectful and informative exchanges, platforms can enhance the

6.3 Limitations and future research
This paper has several limitations that should be acknowledged and addressed in future
research. First, the experimental design tested only accommodative versus no-response
strategies. The literature indicates that accommodative responses build bystander trust better
than defensive or minimal actions do (Chang et al., 2015; Johnen and Schnittka, 2019). Future
research should expand the experimental design by applying multiple response styles, such as
the use of humor or sarcasm, which may yield different insights.
Second, complaint tone was manipulated at the message-thread level: both the focal
complaint and the two contextual complaints were written in either civil or uncivil language.
This approach reflected the way bystanders encounter complete conversational streams on
social media. However, the uncivil contextual comments contained stronger language than the
focal complaint, which may have amplified perceived incivility and influenced the
manipulation checks. Consequently, the findings identify the effect of the overall
interaction tone rather than isolating the causal effect of the focal complaint alone. Future
studies should manipulate only the focal complaint while holding contextual comments
constant.
Third, the use of scenario-based experiments may have limited external validity, as the
participants evaluated hypothetical situations rather than real consumption decisions.
Although such designs are common in service research, future studies could complement
these findings using field data, archival social media interactions, or behavioral experiments
involving actual consumers to enhance ecological validity.
Fourth, the empirical context was limited to the airline and hotel sectors, which represent
high-contact service environments. While this improved internal consistency, it may constrain
generalizability. Future research should examine whether the observed effects hold across
other contexts, such as healthcare, public services, or e-commerce, where expectations,
emotional intensity, and interaction norms may differ.
Finally, this study focused on situational factors (i.e. complaint tone and response strategy)
but did not account for individual differences among bystanders, such as prior brand attitudes,
empathy, or digital literacy. These factors may influence how observers interpret complaint
interactions and evaluate brand responses. Incorporating such variables could provide a more
nuanced understanding of heterogeneity in bystander reactions.

This paper concludes with the importance of context-sensitive accommodative responses

findings underscore the need for strategically managed responses that consider both brand
actions and consumer behavior. Future research should build on these insights to better
understand how firms manage public complaints and strengthen customer trust.
