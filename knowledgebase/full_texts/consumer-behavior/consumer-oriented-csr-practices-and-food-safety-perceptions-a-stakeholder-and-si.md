Abstract
Consumer concerns about food safety have intensified in recent years, as
transparency and accountability in the food sector have become essential to
maintaining public trust. This study examines how consumer-oriented
corporate social responsibility (CSR) practices influence food safety
perceptions, emphasizing the mediating role of customer satisfaction and the
moderating role of loyalty. Drawing on stakeholder theory and signaling
theory, we developed and tested a model using survey data from 498 chain
restaurant consumers in China. Partial least squares structural equation
modeling (PLS-SEM) results show that consumer/product safety CSR, ethical
procurement and supply chain CSR, and environmental/social CSR all
positively affect perceived food safety, although the effect of
environmental/social CSR is relatively weaker. Moreover, customer
satisfaction mediates the relationships between consumer/product safety
CSR and perceived food safety, and between ethical procurement and supply
chain CSR and perceived food safety, while customer loyalty strengthens the
impact of satisfaction on safety perceptions. These findings contribute to
consumer behavior and CSR literature by uncovering the psychological
mechanisms through which CSR enhances food safety perceptions. For
practitioners, the study highlights how CSR strategies can simultaneously
improve satisfaction, strengthen loyalty, and build sustainable consumer
trust in food safety.
Keywords: corporate social responsibility (csr); perceived food safety;
customer satisfaction; customer loyalty; stakeholder theory; signaling theory
1. Introduction
Food safety has become a central concern for consumers, policymakers, and
enterprises worldwide. Frequent incidents of contamination, fraudulent
labeling, and supply chain failures have eroded public trust in the food
industry. According to the latest data from the World Health Organization
(WHO), approximately 600 million people fall ill each year from unsafe food,

resulting in 420,000 deaths and a global loss of around 33 million disability-
adjusted life years (DALYs) [1]. These figures indicate that food safety is not
only a major public health issue but also a significant challenge affecting
social stability and economic development.
The risks associated with food safety are evident across countries and regions.
For example, estimates from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention (CDC) suggest that around 9.9 million cases of foodborne illness
occur annually in the United States, leading to 53,300 hospitalizations and
931 deaths [2]. This highlights that even in developed countries with relatively
mature regulatory systems, food safety risks remain and consumer trust in
the supply chain is fragile. In China, studies have pointed out that although
national food safety risk assessment and regulatory mechanisms have
improved in recent years, challenges persist at the local enforcement and
distribution levels, leaving significant vulnerabilities in grassroots food
supply chains [3,4]. Collectively, these findings underscore the complexity and
persistence of food safety problems.
As consumers increasingly demand transparency and accountability in food
production and distribution, enterprises face growing pressure not only to
comply with legal requirements but also to ensure the safety and reliability
of food through responsible business practices. In this context, corporate
social responsibility (CSR) is regarded as a key strategic tool for
strengthening consumer trust. CSR is generally defined as firms’ voluntary
actions that go beyond legal compliance to meet stakeholder expectations
and promote social well-being [5]. In the food industry, CSR practices may
include implementing strict safety standards, ensuring supply chain
transparency, adopting ethical procurement, and providing accurate product
information. Such practices can directly shape consumer perceptions of a
company’s commitment to food safety. While prior studies have extensively
examined CSR in relation to consumer trust [6,7], corporate reputation, and
purchase intention, relatively limited research has explicitly linked CSR
practices to consumers’ perceptions of food safety. In particular, existing
studies tend to treat CSR as a general signal of corporate responsibility,
without examining how specific CSR dimensions translate into domain-
specific evaluations such as food safety perception.
At the same time, existing research on food safety has primarily focused on
regulatory frameworks, risk governance, and technical control mechanisms,
with relatively limited attention given to firm-driven factors such as CSR. As
a result, the role of corporate responsibility as a strategic tool in shaping
consumer perceptions of food safety remains underexplored, particularly in
terms of how different CSR dimensions translate into consumer-level
evaluations [8]. From a signaling perspective, customer satisfaction can be
interpreted as a psychological response to positive corporate signals, which
subsequently shapes consumer evaluations. When companies actively engage
in CSR initiatives related to food safety, consumers may view them as more
responsible and reliable, resulting in higher satisfaction that translates into
stronger perceptions of food safety.

Moreover, customer loyalty may moderate the relationship between CSR and
food safety perceptions. Loyalty reflects the degree of commitment
consumers feel toward a brand or firm and shapes how they interpret CSR
initiatives [9]. Highly loyal customers are more likely to respond positively to
CSR practices, thereby strengthening their effect on food safety perceptions.
In contrast, less loyal customers may remain skeptical, weakening the
effectiveness of CSR. Examining this moderating role thus provides a more
comprehensive understanding of how CSR influences consumer perceptions
of food safety.
Drawing on stakeholder theory and signaling theory, this study develops an
integrated theoretical framework to explain both the motivations behind CSR
engagement and the mechanisms through which CSR shapes consumer
perceptions of food safety. From a stakeholder perspective, CSR represents
firms’ responses to consumers’ expectations regarding food safety, while
from a signaling perspective, CSR serves as a credible signal that reduces
information asymmetry and enhances consumer trust. Based on this
framework, the present study proposes and empirically tests a model
examining how CSR influences consumer perceptions of food safety through
the mediating role of customer satisfaction and the moderating role of
customer loyalty. Specifically, it addresses three research questions: (1) Does
CSR positively influence consumers’ perceptions of food safety? (2) Does
customer satisfaction mediate the relationship between CSR and food safety
perceptions? (3) Does customer loyalty moderate the relationship between
CSR and food safety perceptions? These questions are examined using
quantitative methods and validated measurement scales in the context of the
food industry.
This study makes three key contributions. First, it extends CSR research by
linking CSR practices to a domain-specific outcome—consumer perceptions
of food safety—thereby bridging the gap between CSR and food safety
literature. Second, it conceptualizes CSR into three theoretically grounded
dimensions based on prior CSR and food industry research (e.g., Turker, 2009;
Maignan & Ferrell, 2000), and examines their heterogeneous effects,
providing a more nuanced understanding of how specific CSR practices
influence consumer evaluations in the food context. Third, it develops an
integrated analytical framework incorporating customer satisfaction and
customer loyalty, revealing both mediating and moderating mechanisms
through which CSR influences food safety perceptions.
2. Literature Review and Hypothesis
2.1Theoretical Foundation
The theoretical foundation of this study integrates stakeholder theory and
signaling theory to provide a unified framework explaining both why firms
engage in CSR and how CSR influences consumers’ perceptions of food safety.

Specifically, stakeholder theory explains CSR as a response to stakeholder
expectations, while signaling theory explains how CSR functions as a credible
signal that shapes consumer perceptions under conditions of information
asymmetry. These two perspectives jointly explain both the motivations
behind CSR engagement and the mechanisms through which CSR influences
consumer perceptions of food safety via customer satisfaction and loyalty.
2.1.1 Stakeholder Theory
Stakeholder theory, proposed by Freeman (1984) [10], emphasises that while
creating economic value, firms must also address the interests of multiple
stakeholders, including consumers, employees, suppliers, governments, and
the broader public. The theory posits that long-term competitive advantage
arises from building stable and mutually beneficial relationships with key
stakeholders [10,11].
In this study, CSR is understood as a primary means through which firms
respond to stakeholder expectations [12]. For consumers—the most immediate
stakeholders in the food industry—the core concern is food safety. When
companies actively engage in CSR practices, such as enhancing food safety
standards, disclosing supply chain information, and strengthening internal
monitoring and training, they directly address consumer concerns and
thereby earn their trust. CSR not only enhances consumers’ perceptions of
food safety but also improves their overall satisfaction by meeting their needs
and expectations. Customer satisfaction, in this sense, represents a concrete
manifestation of the “stakeholder responsiveness” effect within stakeholder
theory. Moreover, long-term and stable stakeholder relationships are
reflected in customer loyalty. Highly loyal customers are more likely to accept
and endorse corporate CSR practices, thereby reinforcing the positive impact
of CSR on perceptions of food safety. Consequently, stakeholder theory
provides the theoretical foundation for the mechanisms examined in this
study.
2.1.2 Signaling Theory
While stakeholder theory explains the motivation behind CSR engagement, it
does not fully clarify how consumers interpret CSR activities. To address this,
signaling theory provides a complementary perspective by explaining how
CSR is translated into consumer perceptions. Signaling theory, first
introduced by Spence (1973) [13], emphasises that in contexts of information
asymmetry, organisations transmit observable signals to shape the
perceptions and decisions of external audiences [14]. In the food industry,
consumers often lack the professional expertise needed to directly assess
food safety and therefore rely on the information and cues provided by firms
[15]. CSR initiatives serve as important signals of a company’s commitment to
food safety. Actions such as obtaining recognised food safety certifications,

disclosing testing results, and promoting food safety awareness function as
positive signals that reduce information asymmetry and strengthen consumer
trust in food safety [16].
Within the framework of this study, signaling theory offers an explanation for
the relationships among the variables. First, CSR, as a positive signal from
firms, directly enhances consumers’ perceptions of food safety. Second, when
consumers interpret these signals as evidence of corporate trustworthiness,
their satisfaction increases, which in turn reinforces their perception of food
safety. Finally, the effect of signals is not uniform across all consumers.
Highly loyal customers are more inclined to interpret corporate signals
positively, thereby amplifying the influence of CSR on food safety perceptions,
whereas less loyal consumers may respond weakly or even with scepticism,
diminishing the effect [17].
Taken together, stakeholder theory and signaling theory form an integrated
explanatory framework for this study. CSR can be understood as a response
to stakeholder expectations (stakeholder theory) and as a signal that reduces
information asymmetry and shapes consumer perceptions (signaling theory).
Through this dual mechanism, CSR influences consumers’ perceptions of
food safety both directly and indirectly via customer satisfaction, while
customer loyalty further conditions how these signals are interpreted. This
integrated perspective provides a coherent theoretical foundation for the
proposed research model.
2.2 Selection of CSR Dimensions
Corporate social responsibility (CSR), as a multidimensional concept, is
typically measured through different dimensions depending on the research
context. Carroll’s (1991) [18] CSR pyramid proposed four levels—economic,
legal, ethical, and philanthropic responsibilities. Turker (2009) [19], on the
other hand, developed a scale that captures firms’ responsibilities toward
customers, employees, society, and the environment. In the food industry,
scholars in recent years have placed greater emphasis on CSR dimensions
that directly relate to consumer experience and food safety. For example,
Yang et al. (2023) [3] highlighted in their study on food risk governance in
China that consumers are particularly concerned with corporate practices
related to food quality and safety. Wu et al. (2024) [20], drawing on an
empirical investigation of street food vendors, found that product safety and
supply chain transparency are critical to building consumer trust. Similarly,
Lu et al. (2024) [4] stressed in their research on food distribution oversight
that firms’ performance in supply chain safety and social responsibility
significantly influences consumers’ overall perceptions of food safety.
Building on the multidimensional nature of CSR and integrating insights from
food safety research, this study conceptualises CSR dimensions according to

their proximity to food safety risk, supply chain transparency, and broader
social responsibility. Accordingly, three CSR dimensions are identified:
(1) Consumer/Product Safety CSR, which includes quality control,
transparent labelling, and food safety certification. This dimension directly
addresses consumers’ primary concerns regarding product safety and health
risks, making it the most immediate and salient driver of food safety
perception;
(2) Ethical Procurement and Supply Chain CSR, which involves legal and
compliant sourcing of raw materials, traceability, and monitoring of food
safety throughout the supply chain. This dimension reduces uncertainty in
the production and distribution process, thereby strengthening consumer
trust in food safety.;
(3) Environmental and Social CSR, which covers reducing environmental
pollution, promoting sustainable development, and engaging in community
initiatives related to food safety. Although more indirect, this dimension
signals firms’ long-term responsibility and commitment, which may influence
consumers’ broader evaluations of food safety.
Together, these three dimensions are closely aligned with food safety and
provide a comprehensive reflection of CSR practices in protecting consumer
interests within the food sector.
2.3 The Direct Relationship Between CSR and Perceived Food Safety
2.3.1 Consumer/Product safety CSR and Food Safety
Recent studies have shown that consumer/product safety CSR are key factors
in enhancing consumers’ perceptions of food safety. Treiblmaier et al. (2022)
[21] found that when consumers are exposed to safety signals such as food
safety certifications, transparent labelling, and quality assurance, their
perceptions of food safety increase significantly. Similarly, Czernyszewicz et
al. (2024) [22] reported that firms’ demonstration of responsibility in
processing and testing directly translates into higher consumer recognition
of food safety. Kasetty et al. (2024) [23] further revealed that food handling
and hygiene practices remain critical for building consumer trust in food
safety. From a signaling perspective, these practices constitute direct and
observable signals of a firm’s commitment to food safety, thereby reducing
perceived risk and enhancing consumer confidence. Because product safety
CSR is closely related to consumers’ immediate consumption experience, it
represents the most proximal dimension to food safety risk and thus exerts a
strong influence on perceived food safety.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the more actively firms engage in
CSR practices directly related to product safety, the higher consumers’

H1. There is a positive relationship between Consumer/Product safety CSR and
Perceived Food Safety
2.3.2 Ethical Procurement and Supply Chain CSR and Food Safety
Recent research highlights that supply chain management and procurement
processes play a decisive role in shaping consumers’ perceptions of food
safety. Hema et al. (2024) [24] reported that regulatory compliance and supply
chain quality influence food safety incidents and consumer trust. Similarly,
Isanovic et al. (2022) [25] found that a lack of transparency and oversight
weakens consumers’ perceptions of food safety. Djekic et al. (2022) [26] also
showed that failures in procurement and handling practices lead to a decline
in perceived food safety.Compared with product safety CSR, supply chain
responsibility is less directly observable by consumers and operates through
reducing uncertainty in the production and distribution process. From a
signaling perspective, practices such as traceability systems and ethical
sourcing serve as indirect signals that help consumers infer the safety and
reliability of food products, thereby mitigating information asymmetry.
Taken together, these findings suggest that supply chain-related CSR
influences perceived food safety primarily by reducing uncertainty in
production and distribution processes. Based on this, the present study
proposes the following hypothesis:
H2. There is a positive relationship between Ethical Procurement and Supply
Chain CSR and Perceived Food Safety
2.3.3 Environmental/Social CSR and Food Safety
In recent years, increasing attention has been given to the indirect effects of
environmental and social responsibility on consumers’ perceptions of food
safety. He et al. (2022) [16] observed that firms engaging in environmental
and social practices influence public evaluations of corporate accountability
and trust. Similarly, Petrescu et al. (2022) [27] found that environmental
protection and social welfare investments are interpreted as signals of
corporate reliability. Paliwoda et al. (2024) [28] also emphasized that
environmental responsibility contributes to perceptions of food safety.
However, compared with product safety and supply chain CSR,
environmental and social CSR is relatively distal from consumers’ immediate
concerns about food safety. These practices primarily function as general
signals of corporate responsibility rather than direct indicators of food safety
performance. As a result, the translation of such signals into food safety

perceptions depends more on general trust rather than direct consumption

Therefore, environmental and social CSR is expected to contribute positively

H3. There is a positive relationship between Environmental/Social CSR and

2.4 Customer Satisfaction and Perceived Food Safety
In recent years, the role of customer satisfaction in food consumption and
safety trust research has attracted increasing attention. Empirical studies
have shown that satisfaction formed during food purchasing and consumption
not only shapes the overall consumer experience but also reinforces
perceptions of food safety [29]. Berybe et al. (2023) [30] found that when
consumers report higher satisfaction with food products and services, their
perceived risk of food safety decreases significantly, leading to greater trust
in food safety. Similarly, Shafieizadeh et al. (2023) [31] demonstrated that
when customers are satisfied with hygiene and service quality, they are more
likely to believe that firms can effectively ensure food safety. Sarmiento et al.
(2022) [32] further emphasized that consumer satisfaction can offset feelings
of insecurity, thereby playing a critical role in shaping perceptions of food
safety. From a signaling perspective, customer satisfaction reflects
consumers’ positive evaluation of corporate signals, such as food safety
practices, service quality, and transparency. When consumers experience
high satisfaction, they are more likely to interpret these signals as credible
and reliable, which in turn strengthens their perception of food safety.
Taken together, these findings indicate that customer satisfaction is not only
an important outcome of consumption experiences but also a key
psychological mechanism through which consumers form food safety
perceptions. Based on this, the present study proposes the following
hypothesis:
H4. There is a positive relationship between Customer Satisfaction and
Perceived Food Safety
2.5 The mediating role of Customer Satisfaction Between CSR and Perceived
Food Safety
2.5.1 Customer Satisfaction mediates the relationship between
Consumer/Product safety CSR and Perceived Food Safety
A growing body of research has confirmed that customer satisfaction serves
as an important bridge between firms’ product safety responsibilities and
consumers’ perceptions of food safety. When consumers are exposed to

reliable product safety information—such as quality assurances and safety
certifications—they tend to report higher satisfaction [33,34], which in turn
strengthens their perceptions of food safety [35,36]. In addition, product safety
assurance measures have been shown to influence food safety perceptions
indirectly through increased satisfaction [37].
From a signaling perspective, product safety-related CSR provides direct and
observable signals of food safety performance. Because these signals are
closely tied to consumers’ immediate consumption experience and perceived
risk reduction, they generate strong satisfaction responses that are more
readily and directly translated into perceptions of food safety, making
satisfaction a particularly effective mediator in this context. Based on this,
the present study proposes the following hypothesis:
H5. Customer Satisfaction mediates the relationship between
Consumer/Product safety CSR and Perceived Food Safety
2.5.2 Customer Satisfaction mediates the relationship between Ethical
Procurement and Supply Chain CSR and Perceived Food Safety
In the food industry, the fulfillment of responsibilities within the supply chain
not only determines the actual level of food safety but also influences
consumer perceptions through customer satisfaction. Research has shown
that supply chain transparency and compliance enhance both consumers’
evaluations of food safety and their satisfaction with the company [38].
Similarly, responsible supply chain practices increase satisfaction, which in
turn strengthens perceptions of food safety [39,40].
From a signaling perspective, supply chain CSR operates by reducing
uncertainty in production and distribution processes. Because these
processes are not directly observable, consumers rely on signals such as
traceability and transparency to form their evaluations. When perceived as
credible, these signals enhance satisfaction, which then translates into
stronger perceptions of food safety. However, because such signals are
indirectly inferred rather than directly experienced, the mediating role of
satisfaction is less immediate than in product-related CSR. Based on this, the
present study proposes the following hypothesis:
H6. Customer Satisfaction mediates the relationship between Ethical
Procurement and Supply Chain CSR and Perceived Food Safety
2.5.3 Customer Satisfaction mediates the relationship between
Environmental/Social CSR and Perceived Food Safety
Environmental and social responsibility not only shapes the overall image of
a company but may also influence consumers’ perceptions of food safety
through customer satisfaction. Prior studies suggest that environmental

initiatives can enhance consumers’ satisfaction with firms [41], while social
responsibility practices contribute to trust and positive evaluations [42]. Such
improvements in satisfaction and trust may, in turn, lead to more favourable

From a signaling perspective, environmental and social CSR functions as an
indirect signal of corporate responsibility rather than a direct indicator of
food safety performance. These signals primarily influence consumers’ value-
based and emotional evaluations, rather than their immediate assessment of
food safety. As a result, while such initiatives may generate satisfaction, their
ability to translate into food safety perceptions is likely to be weaker and less

2.6 The moderating role of Customer loyalty Between Customer Satisfaction
and Perceived Food Safety
Customer loyalty is generally regarded as a key factor in maintaining long-
term relationships between firms and consumers, and it also shapes how
customers interpret the link between satisfaction and perceptions of food
safety. Recent studies provide supporting evidence. Hoyos et al. (2024) [44]
reported that when consumers display high levels of loyalty toward a
company, they are more inclined to translate satisfactory experiences into
trust in food safety, whereas this effect is notably weaker among less loyal
groups. Similarly, Barbosa et al. (2023) [45] found that loyal customers tend
to interpret firms’ responsibility practices more positively, thereby
strengthening the role of satisfaction in enhancing food safety perceptions.
Teangsompong et al. (2024) [46] further highlighted that loyalty acts as an
“amplifier” in the consumer–firm relationship.
From a signaling perspective, customer loyalty influences how consumers
interpret and respond to corporate signals. Highly loyal customers are more
likely to perceive firms’ signals as credible and trustworthy, which
strengthens the impact of satisfaction on food safety perceptions. In contrast,
less loyal customers may interpret the same signals with greater skepticism,
thereby weakening the relationship between satisfaction and perceived food
safety.
Collectively, these findings suggest that customer loyalty does not directly
determine food safety perceptions but instead moderates the relationship

between satisfaction and food safety perception. In other words, within highly
loyal customer groups, satisfaction is more likely to translate into strong trust

In summary, existing literature has highlighted the roles of CSR practices,
customer satisfaction, and customer loyalty in shaping consumers’
perceptions of food safety from different perspectives. However, several
important gaps remain. First, prior research has predominantly examined
CSR as a unified construct, with limited attention to how different CSR
dimensions may exert heterogeneous effects on food safety perceptions.
Second, although the mediating role of customer satisfaction and the
moderating role of customer loyalty have been explored in service and
marketing contexts, their joint mechanisms have not been systematically
examined within the domain of food safety.
Drawing on stakeholder theory and signaling theory, this study addresses
these gaps at both theoretical and empirical levels. Specifically, CSR is
disaggregated into three theoretically grounded and contextually relevant
dimensions—consumer/product safety responsibility, ethical procurement
and supply chain responsibility, and environmental and social responsibility—
to examine their differentiated effects on food safety perceptions.
Furthermore, customer satisfaction and customer loyalty are incorporated
into an integrated framework as a mediator and a moderator, respectively, to
uncover the underlying mechanisms through which CSR influences consumer
perceptions.

3. Methodology
3.1 Population and Sampling
This study targeted consumers aged 18 and above who had dined at chain
restaurants at least five times in the past six months. Using purposive
sampling, data were collected through a structured questionnaire from
respondents in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou. The research protocol was
reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of
Management, Zhengzhou University, China. Informed consent was obtained
from all participants prior to their participation in the study. Participation
was entirely voluntary and anonymous, and all respondents were assured that
their data would be used solely for academic research purposes.
A total of 600 questionnaires were distributed, of which 523 were returned.
After data screening, 25 responses were excluded due to incomplete or
invalid answers (e.g., excessively short response times or uniform responses).
A final sample of 498 valid questionnaires was retained, yielding an effective
response rate of 83.0%.
Regarding sample characteristics, 45.4% of respondents were male and 54.6%
were female. The majority of respondents were aged between 25 and 44 years.
In terms of education, most held a bachelor’s degree (40.8%), followed by
associate degrees (23.5%) and master’s degrees (16.9%). Monthly income
levels were relatively evenly distributed across categories (see Table 1).

3.2 Measurement Tools
Data were collected using a structured self-administered questionnaire
consisting of two sections. The first section gathered demographic
information, including gender, age, education, and monthly income, and
included screening questions to ensure respondents were aged 18 or above
and had dined at chain restaurants at least five times in the past six months.
The second section measured the core constructs of the study, including
Consumer/Product Safety CSR (C/PS CSR), Ethical Procurement and Supply
Chain CSR (EPASC CSR), Environmental and Social CSR (E/S CSR),
Perceived Food Safety (PFS), Customer Satisfaction (CS), and Customer
Loyalty (CL). All constructs were measured using established scales from
prior studies. Specifically, C/PS CSR was adapted from Turker (2009) [19];
EPASC CSR from Maignan and Ferrell (2000) [47] and Lindgreen et al. (2009)
[48]; E/S CSR from Pérez and del Bosque (2013) [49]; PFS from the U.S. FDA
(2021) [50]; CS from Homburg and Stock (2005) [51]; and CL from Zeithaml et
al. (1996) [52]. The full questionnaire items are presented in the
Supplementary Materials.
Although these scales were originally developed in broader contexts, they are
conceptually applicable to the food industry, as CSR practices related to
product quality, supply chain management, and environmental responsibility
are closely associated with food safety concerns. To ensure contextual
relevance, the items were reviewed and slightly adapted in wording where
necessary, and respondents were instructed to evaluate them based on their
food consumption experiences. For example, items related to product safety
were explicitly framed in terms of food quality, safety assurance, and risk
reduction, while supply chain CSR items were interpreted in terms of
traceability and transparency in food sourcing and distribution.

To ensure content validity, the measurement items were translated using a

3.3 Data Analysis
The data were initially analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 28.0 (IBM
Corp., Armonk, NY, USA; https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics) and
SmartPLS Version 4.0 (SmartPLS GmbH, Oststeinbek, Germany;
https://www.smartpls.com) to assess common method variance. Both
measurement model testing and structural model testing were conducted
using SmartPLS Version 4.0.
A two-step approach was employed, including the assessment of the
measurement model and the structural model. The measurement model was
evaluated in terms of reliability and validity using indicator loadings,
Cronbach’s α, composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE),
the Fornell–Larcker criterion, and the heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT),
following established guidelines in the PLS-SEM literature [53–55].
The structural model was assessed by examining path coefficients and their
statistical significance using a bootstrapping procedure. The coefficient of
determination (R²) and predictive relevance (Q²) were used to evaluate the
model’s explanatory and predictive power, while model fit was assessed using
the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).
Mediation effects were tested using bootstrapping to estimate indirect effects
and their confidence intervals, and moderation effects were examined by
including the interaction term between customer satisfaction and customer
loyalty.
To address potential common method bias, both Harman’s single-factor test
and the full collinearity assessment (VIF) approach were applied.

5. Discussion
This study examines how different dimensions of corporate social
responsibility (CSR) influence consumers’ perceptions of food safety, while
also exploring the mediating role of customer satisfaction and the moderating
role of customer loyalty. Overall, the findings indicate that CSR should not be
viewed as a uniform construct. Instead, its effects appear to vary depending
on how closely each dimension is related to consumers’ immediate concerns.
To begin with, the results provide strong support for H1. Consumer/Product
Safety CSR (C/PS CSR) shows a significant positive effect on perceived food
safety (PFS). This is not entirely surprising, as practices such as transparent
labelling, product quality assurance, and the protection of consumer rights
are directly linked to what consumers care most about. Prior studies have
similarly shown that these practices can enhance trust in food safety [63,64].
From a signaling perspective, such activities provide relatively clear and
credible cues that help reduce information asymmetry in food markets [65].
Since consumers are often unable to directly evaluate food safety, they tend
to rely on these observable signals when forming their judgments.
A similar pattern can be observed for H2. Ethical Procurement and Supply
Chain CSR (EPASC CSR) also has a significant positive effect on PFS. This

ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS

suggests that consumers do pay attention to how firms manage their
upstream processes, even if these processes are not directly visible. Existing
research indicates that supply chain transparency and traceability can
strengthen perceptions of safety [66,25]. One possible explanation is that
ethical sourcing and transparent procurement practices serve as indirect but
still meaningful signals of product reliability [67]. Compared with more
general CSR activities, these practices seem to be more closely associated
with food safety in consumers’ minds.
By contrast, although H3 is supported, the effect of Environmental and Social
CSR (E/S CSR) is noticeably weaker. This difference is worth noting. While
environmental protection and social responsibility are generally viewed
positively, they may not be strongly associated with food safety at the
individual level. As suggested in previous research, such initiatives tend to
improve overall corporate image rather than specific product evaluations [16].
From a stakeholder perspective, it appears that these activities address
broader societal expectations, whereas consumers may prioritise issues that
are more directly linked to their personal health and risk [28]. In other words,
although consumers recognise these efforts, they may not readily translate
them into perceptions of food safety.
Turning to the mediating effects, the results confirm that customer
satisfaction plays an important role in linking CSR to perceived food safety.
In the case of C/PS CSR (H5) and EPASC CSR (H6), the indirect effects
through satisfaction are both significant. This suggests that consumers do not
respond to CSR practices in a purely cognitive way; instead, their evaluations
are shaped by their overall experience. Prior studies have also highlighted
satisfaction as a key mechanism connecting CSR and trust [33,34]. It seems
reasonable to assume that when firms consistently deliver safe products and
maintain reliable supply chains, consumers develop more positive
experiences, which in turn strengthen their perceptions of food safety [35,36].
However, H7 is not supported, and this finding merits closer examination.
Customer satisfaction does not significantly mediate the relationship
between Environmental and Social CSR and perceived food safety. This may
be explained by the nature of these activities. Compared with product- or
supply chain-related CSR, environmental and social initiatives are less
directly linked to consumers’ immediate food safety concerns and are less
observable in consumption contexts. As a result, consumers may find it
difficult to associate these initiatives with their own experiences, limiting
their ability to translate such CSR into satisfaction. Instead, these activities
may influence food safety perceptions through more indirect evaluations.
From another angle, this finding may also reflect differences in stakeholder
relevance. Environmental and social CSR often targets broader stakeholder
groups, such as communities or regulators, rather than consumers’

ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS

immediate concerns related to product safety [42]. Consequently, although
such initiatives may enhance corporate reputation, they do not necessarily
influence satisfaction in a way that translates into food safety perceptions.
This also suggests that E/S CSR may operate through other mechanisms, such
as overall brand evaluation or perceived corporate integrity [68], rather than
through satisfaction.
The moderating results further indicate that customer loyalty plays an
important role in shaping how satisfaction influences perceived food safety.
As expected, the interaction between customer satisfaction and customer
loyalty is positive and significant (H8). More specifically, the effect of
satisfaction on perceived food safety appears to be stronger among
consumers with higher levels of loyalty. This pattern becomes clearer when
examining the simple slope analysis, which shows a steeper relationship for
highly loyal consumers compared to those with lower loyalty.
This finding is broadly consistent with earlier research suggesting that loyalty
can strengthen the impact of satisfaction on consumer evaluations [44,46]. One
possible explanation is that loyal customers tend to have more stable
relationships with firms and are therefore more inclined to interpret their
experiences positively. From a stakeholder perspective, such customers may
be more committed and less sensitive to perceived risks [69]. At the same time,
repeated positive experiences may make them more responsive to signals
related to product quality and safety, thereby reinforcing the satisfaction–
perception link.
Taken together, these findings contribute to the existing literature in several
ways. In particular, this study proposes a proximity-based explanation of CSR
effectiveness in the food safety context, showing that CSR dimensions differ
systematically in how they translate into consumer perceptions. First, they
suggest that CSR should be examined at a more disaggregated level, as
different dimensions do not have the same impact on perceived food safety.
In particular, CSR activities that are more directly related to consumers’
immediate concerns appear to be more influential. Second, by combining
stakeholder theory and signaling theory, this study offers a more nuanced
explanation of how CSR affects consumer perceptions under conditions of
information asymmetry. Third, the results highlight a conditional mechanism
in which customer satisfaction serves as a mediator, while customer loyalty
strengthens this relationship.
From a managerial perspective, several practical implications can be drawn.
For large chain restaurants, investing in supply chain transparency and
traceability systems may be especially important, as these directly influence
consumer trust in food safety. In contrast, smaller restaurants may benefit
more from focusing on visible product-related practices, such as hygiene
standards and clear labelling. In addition, firms should explicitly link

environmental and social initiatives to food safety outcomes in their
communication strategies; otherwise, these efforts may not translate into
consumer perceptions.
Finally, it may also be useful for firms to adopt different strategies for
different customer groups. For highly loyal customers, relationship-oriented
approaches such as membership programmes and personalised services
could help reinforce the positive effects of satisfaction. For less loyal or new
customers, however, stronger emphasis on direct safety signals—such as
certifications or quality guarantees—may be necessary to build initial trust.
In this way, firms can better align their CSR practices with consumer
expectations and enhance perceived food safety.
6. Conclusions
This study examines how different dimensions of corporate social
responsibility (CSR) shape consumers’ perceptions of food safety, while also
considering the mediating role of customer satisfaction and the moderating
role of customer loyalty. The findings indicate that CSR should not be treated
as a uniform construct, as its effectiveness depends on how closely it relates
to consumers’ immediate concerns.
The results show that CSR practices directly linked to food safety—such as
consumer/product safety CSR and supply chain CSR—exert stronger effects
on perceived food safety, whereas environmental and social CSR has a
weaker and more indirect influence. Customer satisfaction plays a central
mediating role, particularly for CSR activities closely tied to consumption
experiences. In contrast, environmental and social CSR does not significantly
influence food safety perceptions through satisfaction, suggesting that its
effects are more likely to operate through broader evaluative mechanisms
such as trust or corporate reputation [42,68]. In addition, customer loyalty
strengthens the relationship between satisfaction and perceived food safety,
indicating that loyal consumers are more likely to translate positive
experiences into stronger safety perceptions [44,46].
This study contributes to the literature in several respects. First, it shows that
CSR dimensions differ in their effectiveness, underscoring the need to
distinguish between consumer-proximal and more distal CSR activities.
Second, by integrating stakeholder theory and signaling theory, it offers a
clearer account of how CSR both responds to stakeholder expectations and
functions as a signal under conditions of information asymmetry. Third, it
introduces a proximity-based perspective to explain why CSR dimensions
vary in their influence on food safety perceptions, providing a more refined
understanding of CSR effectiveness in this context.

From a managerial perspective, several implications emerge. For large chain
restaurants, investment in supply chain transparency and traceability
systems is particularly important, as these directly influence consumer trust
in food safety. Smaller restaurants may benefit more from focusing on visible
product-related practices, such as hygiene standards and clear labelling.
Firms should also make explicit connections between environmental and
social initiatives and food safety outcomes, as weak linkage may limit their
impact on consumer perceptions. Finally, differentiated strategies are
needed for different customer groups: relationship-oriented approaches can
reinforce the effects of satisfaction among highly loyal customers, whereas
stronger and more explicit safety signals are essential for building trust
among less loyal customers.
7. Limitations And Recommendations
This study has several limitations. First, the use of cross-sectional data limits
causal inference. Future research could adopt longitudinal or experimental
designs.
Second, the sample is restricted to chain restaurant consumers in major
Chinese cities, which may limit generalisability. Future research should
replicate the study across different food sectors (e.g., packaged food, fresh
produce) and cultural contexts to validate the broader applicability of the
model.
Third, the study relies on self-reported data, and perceived food safety may
not fully reflect objective conditions. Future research could incorporate
behavioural or external data.
Finally, only customer satisfaction is examined as a mediator, while other
mechanisms (e.g., corporate reputation or brand trust) remain to be explored.
Future research may also consider integrating additional theoretical
perspectives to better explain these alternative pathways.
