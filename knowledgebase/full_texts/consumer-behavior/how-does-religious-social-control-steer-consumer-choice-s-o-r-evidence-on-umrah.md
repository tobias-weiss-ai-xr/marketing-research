Abstract
This study examines how religious social control and religious guilt shape Umrah intention by employing the
Stimulus–Organism–Response (S -O-R) framework. Religious social control is conceptualized as the stimulus,
religious guilt as the organismic state and Umrah intention as the behavioral response. Data were collected from
266 Muslim consumers residing in Türk iye who declared an intention to perform Umrah using purposive and
convenience sampling. The measurement model was evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and the
research hypotheses were subsequently tested by analyzing the structural model usi ng AMOS. The measurement
model was validated through confirmatory factor analysis and hypotheses were tested via structural equation
modeling (SEM). The findings reveal that religious social control significantly and positively influences religious
guilt which in turn increases Umrah intention. Moreover, the results demonstrate that socially reinforced religious
expectations translate into internal moral emotions that guide pilgrimage -related decision-making. By integrating
social and psychological dimensio ns within a single theoretical framework, this study contributes to religious
tourism and consumer behavior literature.

INTRODUCTION
As Umrah is a faith-centered pilgrimage, theory-driven research aimed at understanding visitors’
decision-making processes has been steadily increasing, and this heterogeneous market segment is
gradually being explored (Atikah et al., 2022; Khadijah et al., 2022; Othman et al., 2021). Saudi Arabia’s
announcement that more than 15 million Umrah visitors were recorded in early 2025, following post -
pandemic recovery and capacity expansions (Gulf News, 2025), demonstrates the magnitude of this
market. The diversity among Umrah visitors is not limited to their countries of origin; visitors have also
been segmented according to their intentions, behavioral differences, and post-ritual evaluative content.
This diversity indicates that not only intentions and behaviors related to Umrah, but also these intentions
and behaviors themselves, are associated with different antecedents (Elgammal et al., 2023). Despite the
theoretical limitations in Umrah research regarding the psychosocial mechanisms through which
religious context translates into concrete preferences (Sa’dom et al., 2023), existing studies have tended
to emphasize pilgrims’ practical needs and highlight their emotional support requirements (Mohamed
et al., 2023).  Addressing this gap requires a theoret ical framework that links external religious
influences, internal psychological states, and behavioral intentions. The S –O–R framework is
particularly suitable for this purpose.
The S -O-R theory, which conceptualizes behavior as a chain of relationships in  which an
environmental stimulus (S) is theorized to be associated with changes in an individual’s internal state
(O), leading to approach or avoidance responses (R) (Jacoby, 2002), has been shown to play a functional
role in the tourism literature for und erstanding consumer behavior (Erul et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2023;
Wu & Lai, 2022). Although a limited number of studies have examined Umrah visits within the S-O-R
framework (Bano et al., 2024; Othman et al., 2021), these studies do not consider religious social control
as a stimulus nor religious guilt as an organismic state. However, considering consumers independently
of religious social control as social beings, and guilt potential, as individual beings, would indicate a
limited analytical perspective.
In this study, consumers' intentions for performing Umrah are examined using the SOR model as
a central framework. The study attempts to fill a gap in literature by examining the factors influencing
Umrah pilgrimage within a social context using the religious social control variable, and within an
individual context using the religious guilt variable. By examining the relational aspects of both social
and individual factors within a single model, the study aims to offer a new perspective.

Religious Guilt
Individuals experience religious guilt as the gap between their overt behaviors and internalized
religious boundaries widens. The intensity of this guilt is expected to be stronger in societies where
religious rules are explicitly stated (Khosravi, 2018). The intensit y of the relationship with religion
permeates the entirety of a person's daily life and actions perceived as inconsistent with religious rules
may be associated with feelings of guilt, inadequacy, and even failure (Syahrivar & Pratiwi, 2018).
Studies (Bulakhova, 2022; Carroll, 2020; Nachman, 1981; Piers & Singer, 1971; Rohan et al., 2014)
emphasize that the source of this guilt transcends individuality. The tension between the concepts of sin
and obedience largely functions as a control mechanism within the  social structure, triggering the
individual's guilt (Martínez de Pisón, 2023).
From the perspective of the psychology of religion, religious guilt should be distinguished from
religious shame. Religious guilt generally concerns the negative evaluation of  a particular behavior
perceived as inconsistent with internalized religious or moral standards, whereas shame involves a
broader negative evaluation of the self. Religious orientation may also shape these emotional responses.
Previous research has shown that intrinsic religiosity is positively associated with guilt and confession,
whereas extrinsic religiosity may be more closely related to shame and unhealthy forms of guilt (Chau
et al., 1990; Meek et al., 1995; Maltby, 2005). Studies conducted in Türkiye further indicate that intrinsic
religious motivation and religious orientation are associated with individuals’ cognitive and
psychological functioning (Şahin et al., 2023; Kurnaz & Şentürk, 2023). Accordingly, religious guilt in
the present study is conc eptualized as a moral -emotional state associated with internalized religious
standards, religious motivation, and socially reinforced religious expectations.
While guilt plays a role in regulating life by ensuring the sustainability of sociality to a cert ain
extent, advanced guilt makes it difficult for an individual to live in harmony with society. This is because
persistent guilt can paralyze and make a person feel impure. Regardless of its intensity, guilt manifests
itself in purchasing behavior. The cl earest indication of this is that it prevents unethical consumption
patterns while channeling consumers towards ethically compliant products and services (Syahrivar &
Pratiwi, 2018; Adil, 2022). Within the SOR model, religious guilt is likely to be present  in the
consumer's intention to perform Umrah, which can be expressed within the scope of religious tourism.

Stimulus – Organism – Response Theory and Umrah Intention
When religious tourism and Islam are considered together, Umrah can be described as a type of
pilgrimage th at can be undertaken at any time of the year. Although this visit to the Kaaba is not
classified as a religious obligation like Hajj, it is among the acts of worship approved by Islam (Gannon
et al., 2017).Although individuals' religious affiliations motiv ate them to undertake the Umrah
pilgrimage, previous research indicates that pilgrims' intentions are not independent of the experiential
and psychological evaluations they experience during and after the pilgrimage. Therefore, the intention
for Umrah includes both the performance of religious duties and post-travel evaluations, including future
behavioral outcomes such as satisfaction, perceived value, intention to revisit, and positive word -of-
mouth (WOM) (Elgammal et al., 2023). Naturally, integrating fa ith-centered actions with behavioral
theory is considered necessary to explain the outcomes that constitute intention in the current context of
religious tourism (Çıkı & Tanrıverdi, 2025). Based on the Stimulus-Organism-Response (SOR) theory,
it can be sai d that the intention for Umrah arises as a result of a constructed psychological process in
which external religious and social factors are theoretically associated with internal emotional states and
subsequent behavioral intention (Mehrabian & Russell, 19 74). In line with tourism research that uses
SOR as a theoretical background, stimulus-organism and organism-response connections theoretically
represent sequential but analytically distinct relationships. In other words, the flow of inputs and outputs
of a psychological function is revealed. (Çıkı & Tanrıverdi, 2025).
 In the proposed research model, religious social control acts as a stimulus that aligns with
religious expectations and reflects this in perceived social norms. This set of rules activates the organism
conceptualized as religious guilt when individuals perceive a discrepancy between their religious
standards and their actual behavior. In this context, the constructs are theoretically classified according
to the S–O–R framework, while the two hypothesized relationships—religious social control to religious
guilt and religious guilt to Umrah intention—are tested separately, as shown in Figure 1.

METHODOLOGY
Population and Sample
The study population is defined as consumers living in Türkiye who have an umrah intention.
Purposeful sampling (Bourgeault et al., 2010, p. 317) was used to identify individuals  who met the
characteristics of the target population. In this context, the survey form was distributed to participants
who defined themselves as Muslim and declared that they had an intention to perform umrah. A total of
266 participants meeting these criteria were in cluded in the study. Data were collected online in
December 2025 through a structured questionnaire prepared using Google Forms. The survey link was
distributed via social media platforms and messaging groups. Since the study focused on individuals
with a specific religious travel intention, purposive sampling was considered appropriate for identifying
participants who met the theoretically relevant inclusion criteria. Accordingly, participants were
required to identify themselves as Muslim, be at least 18 years old and declare an intention to perform
Umrah within the next 12 months. The accessible population therefore consisted of eligible individuals
who could be reached through the online channels used in the study. However, because the participants
were recruited through non-probability online sampling, the sample cannot be considered statistically
representative of all Muslim consumers in Türkiye who intend to perform Umrah. Therefore, the
findings should be interpreted within the characteristics of the present sample and their generalizability
is limited.

Ethical Permissions for the Research and Ethical Principles
The purpose of data collection in this study is academically focused, and data is securely stored
to protect the privacy of participants. Participation was voluntary, and no participant was included in the

Research and Publication Ethics Committee at its meeting no. 13, held on November 7, 2025.
Data Collection
This study collected data exclusively online from Muslim individuals aged 18 and above residing
in Türkiye using non -probability purposive and convenience  sampling. The survey was administered
via a Google Form link disseminated through social media and messaging groups. Eligibility screening
comprised the items: “I plan to perform Umrah within the next 12 months” (5 -point Likert; inclusion
threshold ≥ 4) and “There is no absolute barrier preventing me from performing Umrah within the next
12 months” (Yes/No; Yes required). Individuals who had performed Umrah within the past 12 months
were excluded. Respondents who passed the screening were directed to the s cale section covering
religious social control, religious guilt, Umrah intention, and control variables. Data were collected in
December 2025, and N = 266 valid responses were analyzed. Informed consent was obtained from all
participants, and the survey wa s conducted anonymously. Owing to the non -probability sampling
design, the statistical generalizability of the findings is limited; this issue is further addressed in the
discussion section.
The scale questions were first translated from English into Turkish and then back-translated into
English to assess semantic equivalence before being included in the questionnaire. The survey form
employed validated measurement scales adapted from prior studies. The religious social control and
religious guilt items wer e adapted from Syahrivar et al. (2022) while the Umrah intention items were
developed based on the behavioral intention framework proposed by Ajzen (1991).  Accordingly,
religious social control was measured with four items, religious guilt with five items,  and Umrah
intention with five items. All item statements were rated on a five-point Likert-type scale, with endpoints
labeled “strongly disagree” and “strongly agree,” and scale points numbered from 1 to 5. Additionally,
the survey form included questions regarding demographic characteristics such as age and gender.

DISCUSSION
The structural results support the two hypothesized relationships within the S –O–R framework.
Religious social control positively predicts religious guilt (β = 0.33, p ≤ .001), whereas religious guilt
positively predicts Umrah intention (β = 0.21, p ≤ .001).  Overall model fit was within acceptable
thresholds (χ²/df = 3.41, CFI = .968, GFI = .958, RMSEA = .057). Given the cross-sectional design, the
estimated structural paths should be interpreted as theoretically directed associations rather than
definitive evidence of temporal or causal relationships.  Interpreted through the S –O–R lens,  these
findings suggest that community -based normative pressures are associated with internal moral -
emotional evaluations, while religious guilt is also associated with Umrah intention.
When contrasted with the broader Umrah literature, the present pattern complemen ts dominant
service- and destination-centred explanations. For example, Gannon et al. (2017) show that destination
attributes and perceived destination image are central antecedents of post -travel behavioral intentions
and that social interaction motives o perate partly through destination image. Elgammal et al. (2023),
using a mixed-method design and cluster analysis, segment Umrah performers into distinct groups based
on services cape perceptions, hedonic/convenience value, and outcome behaviors (e.g., int ention to
return and positive word-of-mouth). Likewise, the systematic review by Harun et al. (2024) synthesizes
evidence that attitudes, motivation, religiosity, satisfaction and wellness -related factors are repeatedly
linked to Umrah travel intentions. A gainst this backdrop, the present study extends prior accounts by
examining religious social control as an antecedent of religious guilt and religious guilt as an antecedent
of Umrah intention
Theoretical and Managerial Implications
In a theoretical context, this study establishes an S-O-R model in religious tourism by defining a
specific normative stimulus and an emotional/moral organism. Instead of focusing on the physical or
marketing environment as the 'stimulus,' religious social control is framed as a  socially ingrained
normative system that may be associated with behavioral intentions. This framework includes the
expectations and sanctions of reference groups. It aligns with calls to integrate psychology -centered
mechanisms into the literature, moving beyond descriptive studies in the Umrah literature (Harun et al.,
2024). Furthermore, by including religious guilt at the organismic level, the model distinguishes external

through trusted networks, partnerships with community intermediaries should be designed to safeguard

Limitations and Directions for Future Research
To summarize the limitations of this study, firstly, the cross-sectional reporting design, collected
online via non -probability purposeful/convenience sampling, restricts causal inference and reveals
social acceptability biases. Secondly, the response stru cture is defined as intention. While appropriate
as a close cognitive outcome within the S -O-R framework, it should not be overlooked that intention
may not translate into behavior under constraints such as financial and health status or quota availability.
Thirdly, the single-country setting (Türkiye) may include context-specific normative climates and limit
transferability. Similar limitations have emerged in previous studies (Elgammal et al., 2023). It is
explicitly stated that convenience sampling limits generalizability and that a limited observation window
may only provide a partial picture of Hajj behavior, highlighting broader methodological challenges in
Umrah research.
In addition, the eligibility criterion required participants to report a relativ ely strong intention to
perform Umrah. This screening procedure may have restricted the variability of the dependent variable
and reduced the representation of individuals with low or uncertain Umrah intentions. Consequently,
the observed relationships sho uld be interpreted within this restricted range of intention and future
research should include participants representing the full spectrum of Umrah intention.

samples. Reducing the gap between intention and behavior through longitudinal designs or, where

APPENDIX
Measurement Items
Religious social control (Syahrivar et al., 2022):
• People check whether you have fulfilled your religious duties.
• People remind you to fulfil your religious duties.
• People will criticize or speak ill words about you if you fail to fulfil your religious duties.
• People will stay away from you if you fail to fulfil your religious duties.
Religious guilt (Syahrivar et al., 2022):
• Belief that you have committed a sin too big to be forgiven.
• Belief that sin has caused your problems.
• Fear of evil or of the devil.
• Fear of God’s punishment.
• Feeling regretful of what you did in the past that were not coherent with your religious teachings.
Umrah Intention (Ajzen, 1991):
• I intend to perform Umrah within the next 12 months.
• I plan to perform Umrah within the next 12 months.
• I will make an effort to perform Umrah within the next 12 months.
• The likelihood that I perform Umrah within the next 12 months is high.
• I expect to make the necessary preparations to perform Umrah within the next 12 months.
