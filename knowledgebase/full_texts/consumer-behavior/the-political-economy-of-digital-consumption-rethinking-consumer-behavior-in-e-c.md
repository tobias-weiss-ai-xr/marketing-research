ABSTRACT
Consumer behavior in e -commerce carries a complexity that surpasses the explanatory reach of prevailing
theoretical frameworks. Drawing on a critical integrative review methodology, this study compares classical and
neoclassical rational consumer assumptions with the realities of digital shopping environments. The Keynesian
approach, behavioral economics, transaction cost theory, signaling theory, and network economics frameworks
are each subjected to direct evaluative scrutiny. Building on global e -commerce data and empirical literature, the
study argues that while price retains its weight in consumer decisions, it is no longer the sole determinant. Delivery
speed, payment convenience, platform trust, and algorithm-driven personalization play increasingly decisive roles
across different product categories and consumer profiles. The study then interrogates the explanatory gaps in
existing theoretical frameworks and proposes an original conceptual model organized around the concept of
convenience economy. The model holds that consumers in digital environments organize their decisions around
minimizing total effort cost, a construct encompassing price, time, cognitive load, physical friction, and emotional
burden. The convenience economy model diverges from existing theoretical traditions in several fundamental
respects: it positions price as one of five components of total effort cost rather than the sole determinant,
incorporates artificial costs generated by algorithmic design, and theoretically frames the tension between platform
architectures that narrow the consumer's choice set and consumer autonomy. The absence of empirical testing and
the predominantly developed-economy orientation of the existing literature constitute the primary limitations of
this study.
Keywords: Consumer Behavior, E-Commerce, Convenience Economy, Total Effort Cost, Digital Consumption,
Platform Economics, Decision Architecture.

INTRODUCTION
For most of its history, economic thought has pictured the consumer as a well -ordered,
purposeful actor, one who holds perfect information, maintains stable preferences, and
maximises utility subject to a budget constraint. From Smith's (1776) account of markets
settling around natural prices to Marshall's (1890) demand curves, this tradition explained
consumer behavior almost entirely through the price mechanism. Under certain conditions the
approach generated reliable predictions, yet the empirical work that accumulated from the
second half of the twentieth century onwards documented systematic patterns in human
decision-making that sit uncomfortably alongside the model's abstract rationality.
That gap has grown harder to ignore since consumption moved onto digital platforms.
E-commerce is not simply physical retailing transposed onto a screen; it is a structurally distinct
environment that shapes consumer behavior under radically different cond itions. In this
environment market friction has been compressed toward zero, personalised recommendations
reach the screen before the consumer has formulated a search query, and payment  has been
reduced to a single touch. According to Statista Market Insights (2024a), global e -commerce
revenue exceeded 3.6 trillion US dollars in 2024, and eMarketer (2024) projects that the
channel's share of global retail sales will rise from 19.4 per ce nt in 2023 to 22.6 per cent by
2027. Growth at this pace and scale poses a direct challenge to received theory, and the question
of how well frameworks developed for a pre -digital world can account for what is happening
in contemporary retail has become unavoidable alongside this transformation.
Each of the prevailing theoretical frameworks accounts for certain dimensions of the
digital consumption environment, yet none captures it in full. Classical and neoclassical

This study aims to close those gaps through a critical integrative review methodology.
Drawing on the methodological frameworks of Torraco (2005) and Whittemore and Knafl
(2005), this approach takes theoretical comparison and conceptual model development as its
core rather than statistical synthesis. The core assumptions of the principal schools of economic
thought are mapped, compared against the behavioral patterns observable in e -commerce, and
used as the foundation for a new conceptual framework: the convenience economy model. The
model holds that consumers in digital environments organize their decisions not around price
as the sole determinant but around minimising total effort cost, a construct that encompasses
price, time, cognitive load, physical friction, and emotional burden. The theoretical foundations
behind this claim, the empirical evidence that supports it, and the propositions it generates for
future research are examined in the sections that follow.
Theoretical Approaches to Consumer Behavior
The economics literature approaches consumer behavior from several analytically distinct
perspectives. This section examines the core assumptions of each perspective and assesses how
well they hold up against the conditions actually prevailing in e -commerce. Foundational
theoretical texts and recent empirical studies serve different functions in this assessment: the
classical texts are used to establish the analytical core and key assumptions of each approach,
while the empirical studies form the evidence base for testing how those assumptions hold in
digital consumption environments.
Adam Smith (1776) cast the consumer as a self -interested rational actor and assigned
the coordinating role of markets to the invisible hand. In this framework, price is the primary
information carrier between buyers and sellers, and the consumer's decision reduces essentially
to a price -value comparison. The classical approach is adequate for explaining broad market
regularities, yet it systematically excludes the psychological, social, and contextual dimensions

of individual choice. In an e-commerce environment, price has not lost its importance, but it is
no longer the sole determinant. Dynamic pricing algorithms produce user -specific prices,
replacing the single transparent market price Smith envisaged with layered, individually
calibrated pricing structures. This limitation of the classical approach in accounting for that
transformation points toward the need for an integrative framework that  captures variables
beyond price.
Alfred Marshall (1890) brought supply and demand together within a partial equilibrium
framework and gave modern microeconomics its analytical architecture. The utility
maximisation premise rests on strong assumptions: preferences are stable and transitive, prices
are known, and decisions are made optimally in light of that knowledge. These assumptions
face serious difficulties in digital environments. While price comparison is technically easier
on online platforms, in practice the great majority of users do not venture beyond the first few
positions in algorithmic search rankings. Interface features such as flash discount alerts,
countdown timers, and low -stock notifications trigger emotional responses before any utility
calculation has been performed. Tang et al. (2021), examining e-shopping behavior through the
theory of planned behavior, found that attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral
control outperformed price as predictors of purchase decisions.  The neoclassical model's
exclusion of time and effort from the utility function is the fundamental reason it struggles to
account for how digital consumption actually unfolds, and that gap is one of the starting points
for the convenience economy model.
John Maynard Keynes (1936) treated the propensity to consume as a function of income
while insisting that expectations and uncertainty fundamentally shape the behavior of economic
actors. Post -Keynesian scholars, rejecting the rationality assumptions of ne oclassical
economics, argued that uncertainty and institutional context must be placed at the centre of
economic analysis (Pressman, 2011; Hein, 2017; Stockhammer, 2022). This extension requires
a deliberate departure from Keynes's original scope. Keynes's  uncertainty analysis addresses
macroeconomic fluctuations and investors' long -run expectations, not individual purchase
decisions directly. The framework's most direct contribution to e -commerce analysis is the
coherent account it offers of how uncertainty shapes the trust calculation behind every purchase
decision. A consumer who abandons a shopping basket mid-checkout is usually responding to
an expectation mismatch. The product may not match the photographs, delivery may arrive late,
or the returns process may prove more laborious than anticipated. Keynesian analysis proves

insufficient, however, in explaining the consumption volatility generated by platform
algorithms and the mechanisms behind impulse purchases.
Herbert Simon (1955) demonstrated that human decision -making proceeds through
bounded rationality. People act on the basis of whatever information their cognitive capacity
makes accessible, not on the basis of complete information. Simon's framework forms an
analytically complementary structure with Kahneman and Tversky's (1979) prospect theory.
Prospect theory shows that individuals tend to weight losses more heavily than equivalent gains,
and that probability judgements depart systematically from objective calculation. Richard
Thaler's (1985) mental accounting theory shows that consumers maintain separate
psychological accounts for different expenditure categories and respond very differently to
objectively identical amounts. The disproportionate irritation that delivery charges cause
relative to product price, the reliable increase in basket size to reach a free -shipping threshold,
and the enduring appeal of instalment payment options are the tangible reflections of this
mechanism. Thaler and Sunstein (2008) showed that decision archit ecture shapes preferences
in predictable directions, and default payment methods, recommendation rankings, and
checkout flow design on e -commerce platforms fulfil precisely that function. Behavioral
economics stands out as the framework with the greatest explanatory power in the digital
consumption environment, and the convenience economy model takes it as one of its primary
reference points.
Coase (1937) and Williamson (1981) built transaction cost theory on the premise that
search, negotiation, and enforcement costs determine whether economic activity is organised
through markets or firms. The theory's core prediction holds in e -commerce: platforms that
reduce transaction costs attract large user bases and consolidate dominant market positions.
Where the theory is strained is in the artificial transaction costs generated by algorithmic design.
Platform architecture makes some options visible and frictionless while rendering others
difficult to reach or effectively invisible. This gives rise to an important theoretical tension: the
algorithm that reduces effort cost from the user's perspective simultaneously eliminates
alternatives the consumer never sees and narrows the choice set. The tension between the
impression of free choice and the reality  of platform-determined option sets is a problem that
Coase and Williamson did not anticipate and that transaction cost theory alone cannot resolve.
George Akerlof (1970) used the market for lemons model to show how quality
uncertainty distorts market outcomes. In markets where information asymmetry runs deep, low-

quality products displace good ones. Online shopping operates structurally in the information-
asymmetry environment Akerlof described. User reviews, seller ratings, return guarantees, and
platform certification schemes have emerged to close this gap. Kim and Krishnan (2015)
showed that in product categories carrying high uncertainty, user reviews carry significantly
greater weight in purchase decisions. Handoyo (2024) synthesised trust, ri sk perception,
security, and electronic word -of-mouth within a meta -analytic framework and confirmed that
these variables carry real explanatory weight in e-commerce environments. Although signaling
theory is powerful in explaining trust mechanisms, it is known that these signals carry different
weights across cultural and institutional contexts, and that variation limits its application as a
universal framework.
Katz and Shapiro (1985) demonstrated that as the number of people using a product or
platform grows, existing users derive increasing value from it, and in this way defined network
externalities. Rochet and Tirole (2003) carried this logic into two -sided m arket theory and
identified a fundamental dynamic of platform economics: a successful platform must grow both
the buyer and seller side simultaneously, and once that growth gains momentum it becomes a
self-reinforcing cycle. The global expansion of platforms such as Amazon, Temu, or Alibaba
becomes intelligible only through this framework. Growing u ser numbers attract more sellers
and expand product variety, drawing in new users and sustaining the cycle. Lee and Choeh
(2020) showed that the effect of review helpfulness and reviewer credibility on product sales
varies by product type, illustrating  how social influence operates within these networks. Li et
al. (2025) examined the relationship between consumer attention and market concentration in
e-commerce through an agent -based modelling approach and demonstrated how platform
dynamics translate in to structural market outcomes. Network economics is powerful in
explaining market structure, yet consumer psychology remains an area where this framework
does not reach sufficient depth. How network effects translate into consumer commitment, and
how platforms exploit that commitment while reducing service quality, are questions that
behavioral economics and signaling theory must answer.
Singh and Basu (2023) reviewed two decades of research on the subject and argued that
different theoretical traditions need to be brought into dialogue rather than applied
independently of one another. Zhang et al. (2021), drawing on post -pandemic consumption
data, reached a comparable conclusion: if genuine understanding is the goal, the social,
psychological, and technological dimensions of online behavior cannot be evaluated in
isolation. Each of the approaches summarised in Table 1 offers explanatory power in certain

Method
This study adopts a critical integrative review methodology. In the sense defined by Torraco
(2005), an integrative review is a distinctive form of inquiry that synthesises existing literature
with the aim of generating new knowledge about a topic. This ap proach differs structurally
from systematic reviews grounded in the PRISMA protocol. Whereas systematic reviews
subject homogeneous studies to statistical synthesis through pre -specified protocols, an
integrative review allows for the simultaneous assessment of studies d rawn from different
methods and theoretical traditions, and thereby enables conceptual model development
(Whittemore & Knafl, 2005). Since the aim is to analyse the principal schools of economic
thought comparatively and to develop an original conceptual  model suited to the digital
consumption environment, an integrative review constitutes the most appropriate
methodological option, given that it takes theoretical comparison rather than statistical
synthesis as its foundation.
The literature search was conducted across the Web of Science, Scopus, and Google
Scholar databases during the September -December 2025 period. Within each database, four
keyword combinations were applied using the AND operator: “e -commerce AND consumer
behaviour AND platform economy ”, “online shopping AND behavioral economics AND
digital consumption”, “convenience economy AND e -commerce”, and “algorithmic
personalization AND purchase decision”. The OR operator was used between synonymous
terms. These combinations yielded a total of 4 35 raw records: 180 from Web of Science, 165
from Scopus, and 90 from Google Scholar. The distribution of search results by database is
presented in Table 2.

The literature synthesis was conducted through thematic coding. Each source was
examined along three core questions: what are the fundamental assumptions of the relevant
theoretical approach; to what extent do those assumptions hold in the e-commerce context; and
what explanatory gaps emerge? Foundational theoretical texts were used to establish the
analytical core of each approach, while empirical studies were assessed with a view to testing
whether that core holds in digital consumption environments. These two source groups were
deliberately assigned different analytical functions: theoretical texts serve a framework -
constructing role while empirical studies occupy a framework-testing position.
The most significant methodological limitation of the study is the absence of original
field data. The convenience economy model is a theoretical synthesis consistent with the
existing empirical literature; it has not been subjected to scale development, confirmatory factor
analysis, or experimental testing, and is presented as a framework on which future empirical
work can build. The second limitation is that the empirical sources drawn upon are
predominantly oriented toward developed economies. This means that consumer behavior in
the rapidly growing markets of Africa, Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and Latin America is
underrepresented, which constrains the generalisability of the findings.
The convenience economy model is presented as a theoretical synthesis and is intended
to serve  as a framework that future empirical studies can test. Operationalising the five
components of total effort cost (price, time, cognitive load, physical friction, emotional burden)
through measurable indicators lies at the heart of the research agenda this study opens. Research
designs aimed at testing the model can be configured in several different ways. In a scale
development approach, separate measurement items can be developed for each component and
subjected to factor analysis. In a structural equation modelling approach, causal relationships
among the components can be tested comparatively across different product categories. In
experimental designs, the causal effects of platform interface manipulations on effort perception
can be measured in controlled settings. In a regional comparative analysis, the question of how
the model's component weightings vary across developing e -commerce markets can be
investigated.

Consumer Behavior in The Light of Global Data
The Global Spread of E-Commerce and the Transformation of Consumer Preferences
Over the past fifteen years, e-commerce has led one of the most rapid structural transformations
in the history of global retail. According to projections from Statista Market Insights (2024b)
and eMarketer (2024), the channel's share of global retail sale s will rise from 19.4 per cent in
2023 to 22.6 per cent by 2027. Several mutually reinforcing factors underlie this expansion.
Broadband connectivity has become widespread, smartphone use has deepened, and digital
payment infrastructure has matured. These features stand at the head of the key drivers. The
OECD's (2024a; 2024b) reports show that advances in digital connectivity and trust
infrastructure have meaningfully reduced market friction on the consumer side.
This growth has not followed a balanced distribution; while expansion has been
relatively moderate in most OECD member countries, the Asia-Pacific region has absorbed the
bulk of global growth. UNCTAD (2024a; 2024b) adds a critical dimension to the picture by
stressing that digital economy growth in developing countries must be assessed alongside
questions of sustainability and inclusive access.
The shift in consumer preferences accompanying this expansion reaches well beyond a
simple change of shopping channel, and expectation profiles have been fundamentally redrawn.
Masuch et al. (2024), working with large -scale transaction data, found that fas t deliveries
arriving below the average delivery time significantly increase the probability of product
returns. The researchers attributed this counterintuitive finding to the shortening of the time
available for post-purchase cognitive dissonance to resolve. Oyama et al. (2024), in a stated -
preference study conducted with 4,062 participants across Japan's three largest metropolitan
areas, found that the great majority of consumers were unwilling to pay even ten per cent of the
delivery fee for faster delivery, and demonstrated that delivery preferences vary markedly by
product category, price level, and purchase frequency. Puengwattanapong and Leelasantitham
(2022) documented that convenience perception, trust, and social influence operate not as
separate forces but as components of an integrated decision process. Jerab (2025) reached a
complementary conc lusion: convenience, price, trust, and user experience influence one
another mutually, and isolating any one of them from the rest makes it harder to understand
how they actually work.

The Conceptual Framework of The Convenıence Economy
E-Commerce as a Structural Force: From Effort Cost to Frictionless Consumption
E-commerce does more than reshape consumption patterns; it restructures the market itself and
in doing so transforms consumer expectations. Traditional retailing required the consumer to
complete a series of steps in person: travelling to the store, selecting a product, making payment,
and carrying purchases home. These steps created an effort cost embedded within the act of
consumption, a cost that economists long ignored analytically. Berry, Seider s, and Grewal
(2002) defined service convenience through a five -dimensional model: decision convenience,
access convenience, transaction convenience, benefit convenience, and post -purchase
convenience. E -commerce platforms outperform traditional retail decisively across all five
dimensions. The OECD's (2024a; 2024b) reports indicate that advances in digital connectivity
infrastructure have meaningfully reduced market friction on the consumer side.
The technological components underlying the convenience economy reinforce one
another. Smartphone applications liberate shopping access from the constraints of time and
geography, eliminating the limits that physical retailing imposed. Personalisation algo rithms
surface recommendations for products the consumer would not have actively searched for, and
recommendation systems begin shaping the decision process before the consumer has formed
a clear intention. One-click purchasing systems, drawing on pre-stored card or account details,
reduce the act of purchase to a single gesture. Di Iorio, Kosse, and Szemere (2024), in a digital
payment adoption study conducted for the Bank for International Settlements, found that the

Frictionless consumption describes a purchasing environment in which physical,
cognitive, and temporal obstacles have  been reduced to a minimum, and three mutually
reinforcing mechanisms produce that environment. The first mechanism is the compression of
the decision process. Algorithm -driven recommendation systems significantly shorten the
consumer's search and in some cases begin shaping the purchase decision before the consumer
has consciously formed one. The second mechanism is the removal of payment friction, which
operates through two distinct technological channels. Tokenisation converts card or account
data into an encrypted token, eliminating the need to enter credentials at each transaction, while
biometric authentication completes identity verification in seconds through fingerprint or facial
recognition. The third mechanism is the elimination of delivery unce rtainty. Real-time parcel
tracking and guaranteed delivery windows minimise the anxiety that typically follows a
purchase. Digital payment infrastructure also independently expands consumption volume.
Contactless payment, mobile wallets, and buy -now-pay-later arrangements reduce the
psychological weight of spending decisions, an effect that Thaler's (1985) mental accounting
framework directly predicts.

Recommendation systems and personalisation algorithms are among the most consequential

shorten search, lighten decision load, and streamline the payment process. T he same

set. Ribeiro et al. (2025) systematically documented this tension, finding that algorithms both
facilitate and structure the decision process. The tension between the impression of free choice

The Convenience Economy Model: Theoretical Comparison and Propositions
Among the frameworks examined, behavioral economics comes closest to accounting for what
takes place in digital consumption environments. Simon's (1955) concept of bounded rationality
translates directly into a narrative about how consumers navigate algorithm -driven
recommendation streams. Kahneman and Tversky's (1979) pros pect theory illuminates
recurring asymmetric valuation patterns in online shopping: adding items to a basket to reach a
free-shipping threshold, the urgency created by discount countdowns, and the disproportionate
weight assigned to negative reviews are the most visible reflections of these patterns. Thaler's
(1985) mental accounting framework offers a particularly powerful analytical lever on the
behavioral effects of the spread of digital payment. Thaler and Sunstein's (2008) decision
architecture approach shows that platform design can steer consumer preferences in predictable
directions, often more decisively than price differentials. Redine et al. (2023), systematically
reviewing the impulse buying literature, confirmed the central role of behavioral mechanisms
across a wide range of e-commerce contexts.

Transaction cost theory provides a strong foundation for explaining e -commerce
dynamics but requires meaningful adaptation. The theory's core prediction holds: platforms that
reduce transaction costs attract large user bases and consolidate dominant market  positions.
Where the theory struggles is with the artificial transaction costs generated by algorithmic
design. Platform architecture makes some options visible and frictionless while rendering
others difficult to reach or effectively invisible, giving rise to a deliberate mapping of costs that
Coase and Williamson did not anticipate. Nalchigar et al. (2016), analysing more than half a
million shopping records belonging to 88,637 users from major platforms such as Amazon and
Walmart, found that browsing habits have a strong predictive effect on purchase behavior and
that search effort functions as an independent determinant of e-commerce decisions.
E-commerce must chronically contend with the information asymmetry problems
identified by Akerlof (1970) and Spence (1973). User reviews, seller ratings, platform
guarantees, and third-party certification schemes are concrete signaling mechanisms that have
emerged in response to that problem. Al-Adwan and Yaseen (2023) showed that seller-specific
signals can outperform generalised platform trust in social commerce decisions. Kim and
Krishnan (2015) mapped the relationship between information asymmetry and signal
effectiveness across product categories, finding that consumers respond much more strongly to
signaling mechanisms in high-uncertainty categories.
The network economics and platform theory developed by Katz and Shapiro (1985) and
Rochet and Tirole (2003) offer a powerful account of e -commerce market structure. Network
economics is strong in explaining market structure, yet consumer psychology remains  an area
where this framework does not reach sufficient depth. How network effects translate into
consumer commitment, and how platforms exploit that commitment while degrading service
quality, are questions that behavioral economics and signaling theory must answer. Each
theoretical framework captures something real about digital consumption, yet none has a
sufficiently wide explanatory reach. The gaps in how effort costs, psychological mechanisms,
algorithm-driven steering, and social dynamics are handled are the points the convenience
economy model aims to fill.

Discussion and The Proposed Model
The comparative analysis in the preceding sections leads to an original conceptual framework
that this study names the convenience economy. In an e -commerce environment, consumer
decisions are not organized around price minimisation as the sole criterion but a round
minimising total effort cost, a construct that encompasses price, time, cognitive load, physical
friction, and emotional burden together. The five dimensions of this cost are defined as follows:
price, time (search, waiting, delivery), cognitive load  (decision complexity and uncertainty),
physical friction (platform navigation and payment), and emotional burden (distrust, regret
anxiety, returns concern). The model draws on behavioral economics to explain individual
decisions and on transaction cost theory to explain friction, and the two traditions complement
each other in ways that neither can achieve independently.
The role of algorithms within the model deserves closer examination. Recommendation
systems lower search cost while simultaneously eliminating alternatives the consumer never
encounters. What appears from the user's perspective as effort cost minimisation may in practice
confine the consumer to a choice set entirely determined by the platform. Ribeiro et al. (2025)
systematically documented precisely this tension, finding that algorithms both facilitate and
structure the decision process. Anoop and Rahman (2025), synthesising 25 years of research

meta-analytically, confirmed that platform design factors have a meaningful effect  size on
impulse buying (ESr = 0.362). D'Souza et al. (2024) showed that visual design features affect
planned and impulse purchase decisions in different ways. Liu et al. (2026), in a comprehensive
review of big data and consumer behavior, note that the m ethodological framework needed to
study these dynamics is itself rapidly maturing. The convenience economy model treats this
tension as an externality arising not from the consumer's choice behavior but from the design
of the preference environment, and this distinction constitutes the most fundamental point at
which the model diverges from the rational cost-minimisation argument.
The convenience economy model generates a set of testable propositions about the
relationships among its components.
Proposition 1: There is a statistically significant inverse relationship between time cost
(delivery duration and uncertainty) and purchase probability, independent of price sensitivity.
Proposition 2: Algorithm-driven filtering narrows the consumer's perceived choice set,
and this effect is more pronounced in contexts carrying high product uncertainty.
Proposition 3: The relative weights of total effort cost components vary systematically
by product category. Emotional and cognitive cost is decisive in high -value purchases while
time cost comes to the fore in routine shopping.
Proposition 4: The effect of platform trust signals on purchase decisions increases
disproportionately in product categories carrying high information asymmetry.
Proposition 5: The adoption  of digital payment instruments increases purchase
frequency through the mental accounting mechanism, and this effect is more pronounced
among consumer segments with strong cash-payment habits.
Reading the framework against the findings reviewed in this study, several conclusions
stand out. Price retains some weight in consumer decisions in e-commerce but is no longer the
sole determinant. The classical price-demand relationship continues to operate in e-commerce
but is now situated within a web of interactions encompassing delivery time, convenience
perception, trust, and algorithm-driven steering. Katawetawaraks and Wang (2011) showed that
the factors influencing online shopping decisions form a complex structure in which
convenience, product infor mation, and price operate simultaneously. Singh and Basu (2023)
concluded that multi -variable explanatory frameworks are a necessity in this field, not a
preference.

Limitations And Future Research
The most significant methodological limitation of the study is the absence of original field data.
The convenience economy model is a theoretical synthesis grounded  in the existing empirical
literature; it has not been subjected to scale development, confirmatory factor analysis, or
experimental testing, and is presented as a framework on which future empirical work can build.
The most central of the open questions i s how total effort cost can be operationalised and
measured across different product categories, cultural contexts, and consumer demographics.
The research designs proposed in the method section offer concrete pathways for addressing
these questions.
The second limitation is that the empirical sources drawn upon are predominantly
oriented toward developed economies. Consumer behavior in the rapidly growing markets of
Africa (52.6%), Southeast Asia (18.6%), the Middle East, and Latin America (12.2%) remains
underrepresented, and the cross-cultural comparison of Pham Thi et al. (2023) and the Ghana
study of Amofah and Chai (2022) illustrate how substantially behavior patterns can differ across
contexts. The third limitation is temporal in character: the integration of large language models
and recommendation systems into customer service channels is transforming behavioral
dynamics at a pace that makes any empirical picture provisional. Asdecker (2021), Dias et al.
(2022), and Tsai et al. (2024) document how delivery infrastructure, access convenience, and
automated fulfilment interact with consumer adoption, signaling that these dynamics will
continue to evolve as technology matures.

CONCLUSION
The rise of e-commerce has brought a fundamental transformation in the logic through which
consumers reach decisions, and existing theoretical frameworks struggle to account for that
transformation in full. The classical model was built on an analytical architecture in which price
is the sole coordinator, perfect information is the foundational assumption, and effort is treated
as an invisible cost. This architecture retained its functional relevance in the pre -digital retail
environment, but as e -commerce brought structural change, the framework began to  lose
explanatory power. Each of the theoretical traditions examined captures a particular dimension
of what is happening, yet none grasps the whole on its own.
The convenience economy model proposed in this study diverges from prevailing
theories in several fundamental respects. Unlike classical and neoclassical approaches, it
positions price as one of five components of total effort cost rather than the sole det erminant.
Unlike behavioral economics, it places not individual cognitive biases but their interaction with
platform design at the centre of the analytical framework. Unlike transaction cost theory, it
incorporates not only real market frictions but also the artificial costs generated by algorithmic
design. Unlike signaling theory, it explains how trust signals affect not only product uncertainty
but the overall effort balance. These divergences indicate that the model makes an original
contribution to the literature, but these claims require confirmation through empirical testing.
The convenience economy model places total effort cost at the centre of the consumer's
decision process. The five dimensions of this cost - price, time, cognitive load, physical friction,
and emotional burden - cannot be disentangled from one another in practi ce, and the model is
built not around any one of them as the dominant variable but around their interaction. Drawing
on behavioral economics to explain individual decisions, on transaction cost theory to explain
friction, and on signaling theory to analyse  trust mechanisms in combination makes this
integrative structure possible.
At the heart of the conclusions that emerge from this framework lie two structural
tensions that future research must attend to with particular care. The first is the tension between
convenience and consumer autonomy: platforms reduce effort costs through recommendation
systems while simultaneously narrowing the choice set and steering consumers toward
commercially optimal outcomes. The model defines this tension as an externality arising not
from the consumer's choice behavior but from the design of the preference environment, and
this definition theoretically resolves the apparent contradiction with the rational cost -
minimisation argument. The second is the tension between individ ual convenience and

collective sustainability: frictionless consumption is associated with higher purchase volumes,
more frequent returns, and more intensive logistics activity, all of which carry direct
environmental costs even when individual transactions feel effortless. Both tensions point
directly at the regulatory questions around algorithmic transparency, default design, and digital
payment architecture that are active in current policy debates.
Measuring how the weightings of the model's components vary across different product
categories, cultural contexts, and consumer profiles constitutes the core of the research agenda
this study opens. Emotional cost may be decisive in a high -value electronics purchase while
time cost comes to the fore in a routine grocery order. The quantitative instruments needed to
test these propositions range from conjoint analysis to structural equation modelling and agent-
based simulation, and the method section has set out these designs concretely. Cross -cultural
comparative research targeting rapidly growing e-commerce markets in South Asia, the Middle
East, Africa, and Latin America appears to be the priority area for testing the model's universal
validity.
