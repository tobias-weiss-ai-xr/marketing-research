Abstract. Against the background of digital economy reshaping adolescent consumption
,this paper explores how peer influence and herd mentality in adolescents' smart product
consumption are grounded in social identity and dual-process theories. Peer influence
operates through informational and normative pathways and promotes conformist
purchasing by strengthening adolescents' need for group belonging. Previous studies
identify four major motives underlying conformity consumption, while peer cues may also
trigger impulsive purchasing. The research reveals two-sided effects: peer digital
interactions boost social skills, risk management, and digital literacy, while triggering
interpersonal comparison, financial stress, and material-oriented self-evaluation. Loneliness
and unclear self-concept aggravate blind consumption, and previous research suggests that
if-then plans may help restrain impulsive conformity. Cross-cultural research has narrow
sampling and lacks long-term tracking. This paper integrates fragmented theories and offers
practical rational consumption suggestions for families, brands, and educational institutions.
This paper combines different research ideas and gives practical advice to parents, schools
and smart product companies to encourage sensible consumption among teenagers.
Keywords: peer influence, herd mentality, adolescent consumer, social identity
1. Introduction
1.1. Background of adolescent consumer behavior in the digital economy era
Digital media reshapes teen consumption. As the most influential consumption socialization agent
peers have become important among adolescents who rely on purchasing to gain group identity [1];
digital instalment tools inspire peer-driven impulsive and conformist spending, even online loan
herding exists among students [2]. However, current research unites offline and online peer
interactions rarely in order to explain herd consumption logic, leaving research gaps.

1.2. The role of peer influence and herd mentality in adolescent consumption
Peer influence is divided into informational and normative pathways [1]. Digital social comparison
fuels herd mentality, and dual-process theory proves peers can trigger automatic impulsive buying
beyond deliberate norm compliance [3]. Teenagers consume popular goods to avoid social
exclusion; the internal mechanism linking peer contact to blind conformity remains underexplored.
1.3. Research objectives and contributions
This study sorts out existing theories concerning peer influence and adolescent herd consumption
systematically. On this basis, it constructs a complete theoretical model explaining peer-triggered
conformist consumption grounded in social identity theory and the dual-process model, and finally
targets intervention and teenagers' guidance strategies [3].
Theoretically, this study integrates scattered peer and herd consumption research, distinguishes
rational and irrational herding, and supplements meta-analysis findings [1]. It provides references
for families, schools, and platforms to curb teen blind consumption by using implementable
intervention schemes.
2. Literature review and theoretical framework
2.1. Peer influence and consumer behavior
Consumer socialization theory confirms peers dominate symbolic, trendy consumption over parents
[1]. Peer influence includes information sharing and conformity pressure; online peers exert no less
influence than offline friends. An additional boundary condition is that single companions generate
closer psychological distance and higher consumption willingness, while loneliness strengthens
sensitivity to peer atmosphere [4]. Most studies only verify peer effects without exploring their
irrational conformity shifting.
2.2. Herd mentality and conformity behavior
Herding splits into information-based rational imitation and identity-driven irrational blind
following [5]. Digital algorithms amplify group consumption trends, creating bandwagon effects
such as student peer loan overspending [2]. The dual-process model explains that peer cues trigger
conscious normative compliance and unconscious impulsive imitation; if-then plans effectively
restrain such impulsive conformity [3].
2.3. Social identity and group belonging in adolescent consumption
Adolescents use consumption to mark in-group belonging [5]. Teens with vague self-concept and
strong interdependent self-perception are more susceptible to peer pressure and conspicuous
consumption. Lonely youths have stronger demands for shared consumption to ease isolation [4].
Few studies take group belonging as a mediator connecting peer influence and herd behavior.
2.4. Research framework
The core logical chain of this research is constructed as digital peer influence driving adolescents'
herd consumption by stimulating their demand for group belonging. Within this model, the sense of

3. The influence mechanism of peer influence and herd mentality on adolescent smart product
consumption
3.1. Peer influence as a driver of consumption motivation
Peer influence constitutes the core trigger of adolescents' consumption willingness for intelligent
electronic products, which can be divided into two well-established dimensions in social influence
theory: informational influence and normative influence. The two pathways jointly activate
adolescents' willingness to follow their peers and generate herd consumption of smart devices.
3.1.1. Informational influence
Adolescents are in a stage of insufficient product cognition and limited practical consumption
experience for smart devices. Lacking objective evaluation standards to judge product quality,
functional advantages, and trend attributes, they tend to take peers' consumption choices as reliable
reference information to simplify their decision-making process and reduce perceived shopping
risks. When most classmates wear the same brand of smart watches, adolescents will automatically
regard such products as trendy and technologically advanced, and further form positive subjective
judgments on the product's overall value, thus generating purchase intentions driven by information
imitation.
From the empirical evidence of college student live-streaming consumption research, individual
characteristics and product recommendations both stimulate consumers' time-saving and convenient
consumption psychology via informational peer cues, and peer group information exerts the
strongest predictive effect on conformity purchasing behaviors. This fully proves that informational
peer cues act as a basic cognitive driver of adolescent herd consumption [6].
3.1.2. Normative influence
Normative influence plays a more dominant and decisive role in stimulating adolescents' herd
consumption compared with informational influence. Different from the cognitive logic of
information reference, normative influence originates from adolescents' innate psychological need
for group belonging; teenagers purchase smart products not merely for the practical functional value
of the equipment, but primarily to comply with implicit peer group norms and avoid social exclusion
within the class or friend circle. Taking Xiaotiancai children's smart watch as a typical representative
case, the product transcends its basic communication and positioning functions and evolves into
multi-layer social symbols among adolescent groups. For instance, a daily communication medium
for in-class peer interaction; a tangible symbol of adolescent social identity; or a basic qualification
to participate in group daily interactions.
Qualitative research on adolescent brand consumption verifies that teenagers rely on branded
commodities to complete self-expression and group integration [7]. Adolescents who fail to own

3.2. Herd mentality and conformity-based purchase decisions
Driven by dual peer influence channels, adolescents gradually form herd mentality, which directly
triggers conformity purchase decisions and generates the classic bandwagon effect in the youth
smart product market. The bandwagon effect refers to the psychological rule that the more
consumers a product has, the stronger the attraction to potential buyers. For adolescent groups with
weak independent consumption judgment, the large number of peers using the same smart watch
forms an obvious herd signal.
Empirical results from live-streaming consumer samples show that herd mentality is mainly
reflected in three psychological tendencies: time-saving convenience, pursuit of low cost, and
vanity-driven comparison [6]. Among them, the comparison psychology stimulated by normative
group pressure has the greatest impact on conformity consumption. Relevant research also indicates
that individuals with high loneliness are more susceptible to group trends and blind follow-up
consumption, as they rely on consistent purchasing to narrow psychological distance with peers [4].
3.3. Social identity and group inclusion as mediating mechanisms
Social identity and the need for group belonging provide an important mechanism linking peer
influence to adolescents' conformity consumption. Adolescent consumption is essentially a process
of self-identity construction through material possessions. Adolescents' self-esteem is closely bound
up with peer acceptance; possessing the same smart watch as classmates helps individuals gain
group recognition and consolidate a sense of belonging [7].
The sense of group belonging bridges peer interaction and conformist purchasing behaviors [6],
serving as the core mediating path of peer influence [8]. Meanwhile, research on conformity
consumption motivation further divides adolescents' conformist purchasing drivers into social
identity motivation, self-identity motivation, status display motivation, and self-esteem enhancement
motivation, all of which are realized through the acquisition of group inclusion by buying popular
smart products. Low-income adolescents show stronger compensatory identity consumption, and
they are more eager to purchase mainstream smart goods to avoid marginalization among peers.
4. Case analysis: children's smart product consumption and social circle formation
Peer conformity is a dominant driver of adolescent consumption, acting through both conscious
norm-following and automatic impulsive reactions. Peers exert stronger effects on teens' brand
attitudes and purchases than parents or media [1]. Four core motives underpin conformity: social
identity, self-expression, status display and self-esteem [8]. Peer presence automatically triggers
impulse buying, even for teens aiming to save money [3]. Cross-cultural data backs this mechanism:
verifying peer conformity shapes adolescents' lifestyles to further alter consumption [9]; adding that
compulsive teen buyers score higher on peer susceptibility and materialism than rational planners
[10]. Peer influence will produce two different outcomes—frugality if groups value moderation, or
hedonic overspending if peers prioritize material goods. As a result, planning and family financial
education are validated effective interventions to weaken negative peer consumption pressure.

4.1. Overview of children's smart product consumption
The consumption logic of children's smart products has completed a clear transformation from
single tool attribute to multi-dimensional social attribute. In the early stage, parents purchased these
products in order to communication and positioning functions, while at present, teenagers regard
smart watches as essential social supplies for daily campus interaction, and peer groups form unified
consumption trends around specific children's smart brands.
4.2. Case study: the social phenomenon of children's smart watches
Xiaotiancai smart watch reflects normative influence among adolescents. Its core competitive
advantage lies in built-in social functions such as class group chat and friend matching rather than
hardware functions. As stated in the theoretical analysis of normative influence [6, 7], the product
has become a prerequisite for teenagers to participate in class social activities; many students
actively request purchase only to avoid being isolated from peer communication.
4.3. How peer influence shapes product acceptance and consumption decisions
Informational influence firstly shapes teenagers' positive evaluation of smart watches by observing
classmates' usage behaviors; then normative influence produces invisible group pressure, and the
mediating effect of group belonging pushes adolescents to make consistent purchase choices.
Studies focusing on student social networks prove that peer consumption has significant spillover
effects, and individuals with unstable self-perception are more likely to follow the consumption
choices of surrounding classmates.
4.4. Positive and negative social impacts
Digital social and consumption activities deliver three key benefits [1, 7]. Shared product
discussions between peers boost adolescents' social interaction. Financial and digital literacy
training raises self-discipline, improving shopping risk safety management. Daily use of social
media platforms cultivates teenagers' practical digital operation skills.
However, Peer conformity brings distinct downsides [3, 8, 10]. Constant display of peers' goods
fuels adolescent interpersonal comparison. The need to fit in creates unnecessary financial and
psychological consumption pressure. Teens judge self-worth and others by material possessions
increasingly, which strengthens material-based identity evaluation.
5. Discussion
5.1. Consumer identity construction through product ownership
Adolescence is the critical formative period of self-concept and values. Due to immature self-
cognition, teenagers rely on tangible commodities such as smart watches to complete social
categorization and self-expression. The four-dimensional structure of conformity consumption
motivation fully explains this logic: consistent smart product purchases help teenagers gain group
recognition, show personal taste and elevate self-worth. Nevertheless, excessive dependence on
material symbols will cultivate materialistic values among young people [8].

5.2. Social pressure and irrational consumption among adolescents
Joint pressure generated by informational and normative peer influence is the core cause of
teenagers' irrational herd consumption. Two boundary variables will strengthen this effect:
individual loneliness and low self-concept clarity [4]. Lonely teenagers have stronger demands for
intimate peer relations, so they are more willing to follow group consumption to shorten
psychological distance with classmates; teenagers with vague self-perception lack independent
judgment and are easily captured by peer consumption trends. If-then planning can serve as a
negative moderator to weaken impulsive conformist consumption induced by peer pressure and
relieve irrational purchasing behaviors [3].
5.3. Implications for parents, companies, and society
Parents need to distinguish teenagers' real functional demands from vanity consumption driven by
peer norms, guide children to establish self-esteem independent of material goods, and alleviate
comparison psychology among adolescents. For Enterprises, children's smart product brands should
balance social function design and rational consumption guidance. Avoid marketing strategies that
hype trendy attributes and stimulate adolescent comparison; launch affordable basic versions to
reduce consumption barriers for ordinary families, and weaken the status symbol attribute of high-
end models. Schools should carry out rational consumption education to help teenagers break the
materialized judgment standard between peers; relevant departments need to standardize children's
electronic product advertising and restrict marketing content that induces herd consumption [6].
6. Conclusion
6.1. Summary of main findings
Peer influence serves as a core driver of adolescent consumption motivation. Previous meta-analytic
research suggests that peers can exert substantial influence on adolescents' purchase attitudes and
behaviors. Herd mentality transforms invisible group pressure into conforming purchases, which
identifies four psychological motives behind herd consumption. Peer cues automatically trigger
impulsive shopping. Trendy digital and daily products become identity symbols for adolescents to
gain peer belonging.
6.2. Theoretical implications
These findings enrich adolescent consumption research. The study connects adolescent consumption
behavior with social identity theory and highlights the role of group belonging in conformity
consumption. Combined with the four-dimensional herd motivation model, the research connects
consumption behaviors to social identity theory, clarifying that teens buy goods to satisfy identity
and self-esteem needs. Meanwhile, it supplements the Theory of Planned Behavior, reasonably
explaining digital media's reshaping effect on youth consumption in the digital era.
6.3. Practical implications
For parents, regular family financial education helps teens build independent consumption judgment
and reduce blind conformity. For enterprises, while peer herding brings marketing chances, they
ought to balance commercial promotion and social responsibility, avoiding stimulating teen

6.4. Limitations and further research
Current studies have narrow sample coverage: Wulandari only surveyed Indonesian teens , and Sans
focused on Chilean youth. They both lack cross-cultural data. Most research relies on one-off

regulation strategies on herd consumption.
